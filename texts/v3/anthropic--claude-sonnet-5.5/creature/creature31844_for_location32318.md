# Who Lives in the Chord Room

## The Singers

There is no name for them, because nothing in the room has ever needed to be told apart from anything else. You might call them choristers. They are the color of old candle wax, each about the length of a thumbnail. They are blind, and the places where eyes would be are smooth plates of shell that have sealed over. They lost sight in the way a rumor loses its source. The eyes were never needed, and then they were not there.

What they have instead is the rest of the body turned into an ear. The forelegs carry small taut drums, oval windows of membrane just below the knee, and these are the sharpest instruments in the cave. The feet hear too, through the stone and through each other. A singer standing on a wall is standing on a sounding board made of its neighbors, and it can feel through its six tarsi which pitch the wall is short of. It does not decide anything. The lack simply passes up its legs, the hind leg lifts, and the ridged plate meets the wing-case edge at the frequency that closes the gap. The gap closes, and the chord holds. The singer's whole life is made of this small motion.

They live long by cricket standards. They are slow and cold, eight degrees all the way through, and their hearts tick over as gently as the water drop. A singer may sit on a single handspan of wall for a year, moving only when a neighbor dies and the harmony calls it half a step up or down. They almost never walk. When they do, it is a shuffle of a few body-lengths, and the chord bends around the walker like a hymn around a cough.

They eat the light. The pale crust on the walls is the pasture, and the singers graze it in an unhurried way, with mandibles like the blades of tiny scissors. They take only what regrows. Their droppings, fine as pollen, fall back into the mat and feed it. The glow is bright because the crickets are there, and the crickets are fed because the glow is bright. Neither side has ever taken more than the other could give back, which is not restraint. It is what happens when two things have been leaning on each other in one place for long enough.

They do not sleep, exactly. They have a lull, a slow dimming of attention, but the lull goes through the crowd in slow, slanting waves, never all at once. At any moment about a third of them are half-dreaming, and their notes hold anyway, propped up by the wakeful ones around them. Nobody has ever heard the chord wake up or go to sleep.

## The Newcomers

A seam in the north wall, thin as a knife cut and dark with damp, is where the new ones come from. Whether they hatch or wriggle out of the rock is unclear. They appear pale as milk, soft as wet paper, with their wing-cases folded and unhardened, and they stand at the mouth of the seam for a little while, feeling. Then they walk, and this is the only purposeful walking in the room. They cross the crowd like a swimmer through a shoal, and the shoal opens for them, because the singers know something is coming. They feel the small lack in the harmony, a hole shaped like one voice, and the newcomer goes straight to it. It settles in, its case hardens over an hour or so, and its first note joins the chord. It is already the right note. Nobody taught it. It was always going to be that note.

Watch a newcomer long enough and you may see the exact moment it stops being a body and becomes a part. Its legs lock and its posture settles. It has been received.

## The Ones Who Do Not Sing

There are also six others, and you find them if you are patient, and if you crawl into the hollow under the shale slab and let your ears recover.

They live in the silence there. They are not crickets, or not anymore. They are longer than the singers, about the length of your smallest finger. They are thin, almost translucent, and their long legs fold under them like the legs of a resting crane. Their antennae are long, longer than their bodies, and they are the only creatures in the room with antennae that can be spread, because they are the only ones with space around them. In the sand hollow the antennae sway very slowly, sampling the air the way you might touch a wall in the dark.

Their wing-cases are worn smooth. The ridged plates on their hind legs, the instruments of the whole chord, have been rubbed flat and bright as the inside of a shell. They were singers once. Some morning long ago, or rather some morning in a very slow life, each of them found no gap to fill. The chord was whole around them. It had no place for one more voice, and the voice would only have been a blur. So they went quiet, and the quiet was noticed, and the crowd let them go, and they walked to the one place in the room with no song in it.

That is how the population stays exact. The room does not crowd because the surplus is quiet. It does not thin because when a singer dies, a newcomer comes from the seam, or one of the quiet ones goes back up to a wall and sings the note that was lost. That happens rarely. When it does, a keeper leaves the hollow and one of the six niches sits empty for a time.

The quiet ones are keepers because they do the work that no singer can. When a singer dies, its body stays gripping the wall, and its neighbors do not move it. It would be a kind of rudeness. The keeper comes, with its long legs stepping high, and eases the husk free. It carries it in its jaws to the nearest patch of crust and presses it in, where the microbes take it slowly and the glow brightens there for a day. Then the keeper returns to the hollow. This takes hours. Nobody hurries and nothing is lost.

They also stack the pebbles. The cairns at the edges of the room are three stones high, always three, and the keepers add and remove them with their forelegs in a pattern nobody could read. Maybe the cairns count something. Maybe they are simply what six patient creatures do with hands and time in a room where nothing else can be moved.

## The Stones

In the six niches of the north wall, six smooth quartz stones sit like eggs. Each was polished by one of the keepers, over what must have been many years, by rubbing it against the flat worn plate of the hind leg. It is the same motion as singing, the leg drawn across the case, but with the stone between them there is no sound, only a very slow smoothing. The keeper holds the stone and rubs it, and the surface takes a shine, and it stops when it stops.

If you tap one gently with a fingernail, it rings. Quartz does that. Each rings at a different pitch, and if you tap all six in order, they sketch the skeleton of the chord: root, fifth, and the four bright overtones the singers build above them. Whether the keepers use the stones to check the chord, or whether the stones are their own quiet way of singing, or whether they polish them only because polishing is what a leg with no song left will do, you cannot tell. The keepers won't say. They cannot say. They are what the chord looks like from the outside, if a chord could have an outside.

## What Else Lives Here

The mat under the crust is alive in numbers beyond counting, a slow city of cells that turn light out of chemistry and keep the room bright. They have no faces, but they are the most faithful inhabitants. They have burned at one intensity for longer than the singers have been singing.

The lichens on the drier ledges belong here too, and the pale liverworts where the drip has been kind. They grow flat and even and go nowhere. And the root hangs in the center, the great slow presence, a tree's finger dipped in the pool. Above it, hundreds of meters up, the tree stands in real weather and does not know that its root has become the pipe on which the crickets hold their loudest note. Somewhere in that tree there is a winter and a summer. Down here the root simply drinks its drop and stays.

## What Kind of Place It Is

If the room can be said to have a mind, it is a mind in which nothing has yet been decided. The singers do not remember. The keepers barely do. The mat glows and the drop falls and the tree drinks, and no one in the room can imagine the rock becoming anything but raw.

But sometimes, in the hollow under the slab, the keepers lift their antennae all together, all six, and hold them still for a long time, as if they had heard something no one else in the room can hear. It might be the far-off sound of water finding its way toward the first real channel. It might be only the stillness itself, the held breath before the change.

When it comes, the singers will feel it in their feet, and the chord will bend to a new note. It will be the same chord, made by the same bodies, and the room will go on, changed, singing.
