# The Set

What lives on the Finished Flats was mostly made to live there, and the count is exact. Three herons to a basin. Two thousand nine hundred and eleven frogs on the long wall of the third terrace, no more and no fewer, however often you go along it touching each one with a finger. Eleven grazers of woven withy walk their slow circuits, each taking one whole day to make its round. Then there are the people, who are the only residents inclined to keep count, and who call themselves the Set.

## The Set

They are about the height of children, or of tall adults, or of nothing in between. The makers were in different moods and did not standardize. Their bodies are hollow burnished clay, warm to the touch like a bowl someone has just drunk from. Every one of them has a deep thumbprint at the breastbone, larger and more decided than a fingerprint. This is the mark where life was pressed in, and the Set touch it as a greeting, laying two fingers on their own and then on yours if you let them. Their eyes are inlaid shell or dark seed. Their hair is twine, and the twine has taken root at the scalp and grows slowly in pale, knotted lengths that some of them braid and some let hang.

Some were made carefully. Some were made fast, with a pinched nose and a slit of a mouth and one arm a little short. A few were only begun, faces that are a smooth thumbed-over blankness where features were meant to go. They are not pitied for this. They are, if anything, listened to more closely, because they are the ones who have kept themselves quiet enough to hear the rest.

Like everything here, they sweat. Beads stand on their foreheads and roll down the shell of the eye, and they wipe them with the flat of the hand in a gesture so habitual it reads as thought.

## Speech

Each of the Set has exactly one sound, a single soft note like a finger circling the rim of a bowl. A name is a note. There are no words in the sense you would mean. Speech is the *order* in which the notes are given, who chimes after whom, who waits, who chimes twice, who leaves a gap. A conversation between six of them on a terrace edge sounds, from a distance, like nothing. You have to stop listening for the frogs. Then the quiet resolves into a slow spacing of tones, unhurried, each one arriving in the hush left by the last, and you understand that the silence of the plain was never empty. It was full of polite argument.

They can say *the water is at the brim* and *the third heron's lifted its foot* and *I am here* and *I am still here* and, rarely, *is anyone*, which is one note held slightly longer and followed by a very long gap. It is the only question they ask, and the gap is the answer they are used to.

## What they do

They keep the count. Each has a post: a wall, a basin, a stretch of causeway. They walk it and touch what is there and confirm that it is as it was. This is not anxious. It is closer to the way a person pats a pocket. The frogs are counted along knotted twine, one knot per frog, and the twine, which has rooted, is kept in loops around the wrist and grows a little each year in a place where there are no years.

They also notice, which is a different thing and rarer. The herons and fish and frogs repeat exactly, but the Set do not. They drift, very slightly. A note comes a hair late. A hand settles on a different finger of the wall. It is as though whoever pressed them into being was thinking of something else with the last of their attention, and the something else got in. The Set are the only things on the plain that are, by the smallest degree, *not finished*, and they are accordingly the only things that can watch the heron's water-drop fall at the same point of the arc and feel a small vertigo about it.

They do not eat. They drink by putting their lips to a basin rim, and the water does not go down.

They do not make things. This is the great restraint of their lives. A shaped thing becomes the thing it is shaped as, and stays. On a ledge on the fourth terrace sits a small bowl, one of them once pinched it out of wet clay for the sake of having something to drink from, and it is full, perfectly and forever, to the lip. Nobody has drunk from it. They walk around it. It is the sort of thing you do not do twice, and it is tended like a grave, or a lesson.

## The cloth

The Set have theories about the sky. The most common is that it is a wet cloth. Anyone who has worked clay knows the practice: when you stop for the night you lay a damp cloth over the work so it will not dry and crack, and it stays soft under there, sweating, held in the moment of your leaving. Nothing here dries. Nothing weathers. The white above is even and pressed and has no sun in it. They say: *we are under the cloth.* They say it the way one says *we are in the house.*

From this comes a spread of beliefs, held gently and without heat. Some think the maker went to fetch more clay and has been a long time. Some think the maker has not left at all, but is so large and so near that the terraces are the maker's knees and the warmth of the ground is the warmth of being held. Some think the cloth is not for the work but for the workers, and that the makers are under it too, quiet, in the same held breath, just not visible, as the Set are not visible to the frogs. And a few think it does not matter and simply keep the count.

Nobody thinks the maker is dead. That is not a shape the clay will take.

## The one who rolls the rope

Far out, where the basins spread until the far shores are lost in white, there is a great half-coiled ring and beside it two handprints each as wide as a door, and beside those, most of the time, sits one of the Set.

She is small and quick-fingered, and her left thumb is wider than her right, as though whoever made her had been interrupted with the thumb still pressing. She has no name that anyone else uses. Her note is low and slightly flat, and she gives it seldom.

Every day, which is the same day, she rolls a rope of clay on the flat of the terrace with both palms until it is as thick as a forearm and as long as the loose rope beside the ring. She holds it up against the other, end to end, checking. Then she lays it down and looks at it for a long time. Then she rolls it back into a ball, and starts again.

She does not join it. Ask why, in the only way you can, by sitting with her and waiting, and the answer that comes is not a refusal but a kind of tenderness. *It was not put down for me.* And also: *if it is finished, then it is finished.* The task is held in its posture, and the whole plain seems to lean on that posture the way a person on a ladder leans on the wall. So she practices, and stays ready, and does not close the ring. She is an apprentice who has been left alone in the workshop and has decided, with great patience, to keep her hands in shape.

Sometimes, when the gap between notes is long, she climbs down into one of the handprints and lies there. It fits her the way a seed lies in a furrow. The clay in it is still soft. It gives a little under her back and closes, very slightly, around her shoulders. The others will tell you, if they tell you anything, that the print is warm, not as sun-warmed things are, but the way a bowl is warm when someone has only just set it down.
