# Who Lives at Ninth Saddle

## The first sign is footsteps

Before you see anyone, you hear them: a single pair of feet on a worn stair, unhurried, stopping now and then. The stairs are cut so that the walker's weight falls in the dip the last walker left. Nobody in the fissure would say they follow one another, but everyone here walks in someone else's footprints.

Then you see a figure crossing a filament bridge at midheight, pale against pale stone, and understand that the city is inhabited.

## The Lodgers

There are about four hundred of them. They call themselves *the Lodged*, or in casual speech *lodgers*, as if the mountain were the landlord and the arrangement were still being renegotiated. The word carries no complaint. It is how you might describe a seed in a crack, or a held breath.

They are lean, not from hunger but from a lifetime of spending nothing carelessly. They walk with a short, level stride and let their arms hang loose, since flailing wastes heat. They stand still in a way that looks like patience and is closer to listening. Their clothes are knit from the same filament the bridges are drawn from, spun fine, dyed nothing, and soft with wear. Seen from a distance in the raw light, they nearly disappear against the walls. A person standing on a terrace looks like a slightly warmer part of the granite.

Everyone carries a small ceramic cup of warm water, and holds it in both hands even when it has gone cold. It is not for drinking so much as for having. Hospitality is measured in it. To hand a stranger your cup, or to fill theirs from the channel that runs closest to your door, says all that needs saying.

They speak low, and a good deal of what they say is fitted to the sounds around them. Sentences pause for a drip. Someone finishing a thought will let the mesh chord rise and fall before adding the last word. Many hum without knowing it, and the hum finds the chord's pitch and stays with it. Children do this from the cradle. Visitors say it is like being among people who are all quietly agreeing with the weather.

## What they do

Nobody has a job, exactly. They have attentions, and each attention is old enough to have a family of its own.

**The curtain-hands** tend the mesh. They draw the filament curtains wide at morning thaw and close them at night freeze, untangle them after tremors, and clear the wingless flies from the weave. They know the individual voices of the curtains. If one is off-note, they can find it in the dark by the wrongness of the chord.

**The sky-keepers** watch the membrane. They may not touch it, and none of them ever has. They go out on the high bridges at dawn and dusk, look up, and remember. Each keeps a private map in the head of every sag, every seam where frost gathers thickest, every place where the light comes through slightly too white. When a quake shakes grit onto the membrane, they know within a breath whether the ripple is the right kind. A sky-keeper who dies passes the map on to no one, because it cannot be passed on. Another person simply starts learning it.

**The log-keepers** tend the quiet work. They do not build it, mend it, or explain it. They sit by the humming patches of wall with long ledgers and write what happens. The entries are plain:

*East wall, third patch. Warm from the fourth hour to the sixth. Cold after. A child was ill in the dome below and slept at the sixth.*

*Second pool. Steam rose two hands higher than yesterday. Nobody had visited.*

*North patch, low. Silent for nine days. Then a tremor, and it hummed again, a note lower than before.*

The log-keepers do not speak of what the ledgers seem to say. It is understood that if you write down enough true things, a pattern shows through, and that saying it aloud would be a kind of trespass. They are not afraid of the quiet work. They are careful with it, the way you are careful with a sleeping animal you have come to love.

**The skimmers** work the black pools. On the bottoms, in the cold, a thin grey-rose film grows over the stone. It feeds on what the young granite gives off when it breaks and meets water: a gas, a trace of iron, a whisper of sulfur. This film is the city's food. The skimmers lift it in sheets with flat ceramic paddles and fold it into cakes that taste of minerals and faintly of smoke. It is the only harvest in a place where nothing green grows, and the Lodged are quietly proud of it.

The springtails graze the same film at the pool margins, and the Lodged leave a share for them. Nobody has ever decided this. It is what is done.

## The dead

Nothing is wasted, and that includes the dead. When a Lodger dies, their people wash the body in warm water, wrap it in unbleached filament, and lower it into the deepest pool at the fissure's lower end. The film takes it slowly. Within a season, the pool's margin blooms a slightly deeper rose.

Everyone knows this and says so plainly. To eat the cake is to eat one's grandmother's grandmother, and the Lodged find this less strange than the alternative. "We are lodged," they say at the pool's edge, and nothing more.

## The other residents

The Lodged do not think of the springtails, flies, and finches as pets or pests, only as fellow occupants.

The **finches** nest in the ceramic towers, in gaps the builders left deliberately, or accidentally, or, some say, because the towers grew them. They are grey, round, and bold enough to take crumbs from a hand. When a finch dies in the fissure it is set in the pools with the same care as a person.

The **wingless flies** walk the mesh all day. Some of the older curtain-hands claim to tell weather by the direction the flies face.

And then there is the **visitor**, the pale long-eared thing that comes down out of the rock at unpredictable intervals to lick salts from the weeping walls. The Lodged stand aside for it. Children are taught to freeze and look at their own feet until it is done. It has been coming for as long as anyone has records, and the log-keepers have noticed, without comment, that its ears are always tilted slightly toward the humming patches. Whether it is the same animal each time, nobody says. Nobody has tried to count.

## The quiet work

If you ask who really lives in the fissure, the Lodged will point at themselves, then hesitate, and glance at the wall.

The newest patches of granite, the ones with the too-even sheen, are not exactly machines. Machines do not choose when to stop. The Lodged do not think of them as alive, but they have stopped saying they are not. The log-keepers can tell you where the patches have spread in a generation, where they have gone still, where they have begun to hum at a pitch closer to the mesh chord than they did before. A very old woman on the second terrace, asked what she thought the quiet work was, said only: "It is what the crack does when someone stays in it long enough." She did not say which of the two was the someone.

Some of the Lodged believe the city was not built but *answered*. They think the first cells bolted to the wall were only a question, and the pebble-domes a reply, and the sheen an argument continuing. In this reading the fissure is a shell, the people are the grit that got in, and the quiet work is the slow, patient smoothing that makes a pearl. Nobody believes this all the time. Everyone believes it sometimes, usually in the hour after a tremor, when the walls are ticking and the hum comes up through the teeth.

## Strangers

When someone crosses the saddle and finds the seam, the Lodged receive them quietly. They give warm water and a place under an awning. They ask nothing. After a night or two, gently and without ever explaining, they ask the stranger to leave by the same way they came.

The Lodged do not know why. They say the way in is the way you are known by. They say the quiet work remembers a path. Some say only that it has never gone badly when the rule was kept. There are one or two stories of travelers who left another way, over the far ridge, and were never heard of again. The Lodged tell these stories without any weight, as one would mention an old fact about rivers.

## Dusk, from the highest bridge

At the day's end a sky-keeper stands at the middle of the highest bridge with a cup held in both hands. She has been there since the mesh stopped singing. Below her the pools go slate, and the steam lifts in threads, and the walls drip. She is humming, very low, the pitch of the last chord, and under her feet the bridge is humming too, faintly, in a way that is not quite hers.

She does not turn when you arrive. In a moment she will lift her eyes and check the membrane, the sag and seam and white of it, the way she has every dusk since she was a child. Then she will go down the worn stairs in the footprints of everyone who came before, and someone else will take up the watch.

The mountain, above and around her, rises another finger's width. The small warm city goes on being held.
