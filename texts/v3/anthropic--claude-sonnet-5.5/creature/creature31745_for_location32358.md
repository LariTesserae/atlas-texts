# Who Lives on the Chorded Plain

## The Cordfolk

The outsiders who have written about the plain, and there are only a few, call the people who live there the Cordfolk. The Cordfolk have no spoken name for themselves, because speech does not survive there. Their name is a gesture: two knuckles to the breastbone, a pause, then a third. Traders render it as *the Held*, or *the ones who are struck*. Nobody is sure which is closer.

They are a broad, deep-chested people with heavy shins and jaws. Their bones are dense, and the sternum in particular is thick and slightly flared, like a drumhead mounted in a frame. Their eyes are large and pale for the permanent dusk. Their ears are small and mostly ornamental. Children are fitted with plugs of tarry resin at weaning, and by adulthood most have lost their hearing entirely. They do not grieve this. To them hearing is what a stranger does when he doesn't yet know how to listen. They listen with the feet, the shins, the sternum, the hinge of the jaw, and the flat of the palm, all the places where a roar becomes information.

Their soles are thick as boot leather. When they must go onto the open basalt they wear shoes of beetle-shell plate stitched to gilder membrane, and they still bleed. Old women carry a reddish stain in the creases of their heels that never washes out.

## How They Live

Almost nobody lives on the surface. The Cordfolk live along the rims and upper ledges of the fissures, in dwellings hung inside the vine-curtains, and around the wells.

**The hung houses.** These are hammock-rooms woven from vine fiber and lashed between tree-fern trunks, walled with mats of moss laid over frames of split reed. They are built to be part of the sound. A good house has been tuned for years, its tensions adjusted a finger-width at a time, so that the roar passes through it as a slow even pulse, like a heartbeat against the ribs. Children are born and sleep in this pulse. A Cordfolk infant carried out of it will scream and shake and refuse to sleep until it is brought back, and the same is true of adults. The few who have left the plain for quieter countries describe a sickness that comes on within days: a ringing emptiness in the chest, a feeling of being *unheld*, and a terror of their own heartbeats, which seem obscenely loud. Some have walked back across half a continent rather than endure it.

**The well-rings.** Each well has a household that keeps it: clearing the channels, skimming leaf-fall, combing the fern-ring, and counting the fish-things in the basin. They call the work *paying*, and they mean it. Water arrives as a gift of noise, and the Cordfolk think of themselves as permanently in debt. A well-keeper's family may hold a single basin for two hundred years. Their cups are made from a fine gray-violet dust gathered at the foot of the Instruments, fired with clay, and they hum faintly when filled.

**Food.** They eat the pale hearts of the tree ferns, the loaf-beetles roasted in their own shells, and the legged fish from the seep-pools. They also eat the bell-flowers, which they say taste of copper and sweetness and make the teeth feel newly rooted. Fungal light comes from lamps of woven glass packed with green threads. A house at night is a small green room in a larger green seam, surrounded by amber flashes from the beetles on the walls.

## The Cords

The thing that gives the plain its name is the network of cords.

Between fissure-rims, across the mouths of the wells, and out along the open rock to the foot of every Instrument, the Cordfolk have strung a web of lines: braided vine, tarred with resin, drawn taut over posts of broken basalt column. There are thousands of kilometers of them. Most are as thick as a finger. A few trunk-lines, tended for generations, are as thick as a wrist.

The roar sets every cord humming all the time, so a cord cannot carry a message by adding to the noise. The Cordfolk therefore write in *stillness*. A person pinches the cord between two padded fingers to make a small dead spot, then moves along it, damping and releasing in patterns. Downstream, a listener with a palm on the line feels the pattern as a series of small absences moving through the hum. The Cordfolk are proud of this. They say that noise is given, and that the only thing a person can make is a silence. Every word they speak is a small theft from the tower's voice, returned as meaning.

The predators of the fissures hunt by feeling the disturbances that prey make in the vibration around them, so the Cordfolk have a rule: *never mute in a hurry*. The dead spots are made slowly, in rhythms that imitate the slow settling of vines, so that the listening things above and below take the cord-talk for the ordinary sounds of the place. An old cord-reader can sit for an hour with one hand on a line, apparently doing nothing, and know who has died at the next well, whose child has walked, and that a new crack is steaming three fissures south.

Close speech works the same way, by touch. Two people talk with a forehead to a shoulder, a chin to a collarbone, or a hand on the other's sternum, humming through the teeth at a pitch that means nothing to a hearing ear but arrives in the other body as a shape. Lovers speak jaw to jaw. The dying are held with their chests together so that the last things said are said inside the listener's ribs.

## The Runners

On the open plain you will rarely meet a Cordfolk person, but you will meet the runners.

They are gray, lean, six-legged animals the size of large dogs, with shells the exact color of wet basalt. They cross the bare columns in long, low, jointed lopes, bellies nearly touching stone, and they never stop moving. They are scavengers, and the only large animals that live on the surface. They feed on what the fissures spill out: dead gliders blown up on the updrafts, beetles killed by steam, the stranded fish-things after a flood. They carry it back down to the seams in their jaws.

Nothing hunts them because nothing else goes up there. They travel bent double because the wind is cold and the sky is wide and something in their bodies knows that the open is where you are seen by the sky. The Cordfolk cross in the same posture on the rare occasions they must, at dusk, in fog, from post to post along the cords. They think of it as respect. The runners are the only animals they will not eat, and they leave scraps out on the rock for them.

## The Pressed

Not everyone lives in the fissures and the wells. A few Cordfolk climb the ramps.

The Instruments have hollows in their flanks, alcoves and spiral chambers reached by ramps that seem to have grown to suit a body. The people who enter them call themselves, in touch-speech, the Pressed. They go up with a month's food, a lamp, and a cord unrolled behind them from the base, and they lie against the inner walls where the lattice of pores breathes against the skin. They stay for weeks or seasons. Their families keep the cord and feed the line from below.

They come out changed. The fingertips and lips go faintly gray-violet, the color of the towers. They are quiet even for the Cordfolk, and some of them, when they hum against your chest, produce something that isn't quite a human pitch, a low double tone like a second voice under the first. They can tell, days ahead, what the towers will do: whether the fog will hold, whether the wells will fill, whether the weather will walk in from the east or die on the way. The Cordfolk trust them the way you trust the person who has looked at the sea from the far side.

Some do not come out. Sometimes a cord is followed up the ramp and found to end in a wall that is smooth and pale and pore-patterned, with no seam where the hollow was. The family ties the cord's end off at the base and does not mourn. They say the Instrument wanted the room.

## What the Instruments Are

There are three accounts, and no one has ever proved one of them.

The first is that the Instruments are machines from a time so long ago that nothing survives of the makers but the work, and that what the Pressed feel in the walls is the mechanism of the thing, patient and unthinking.

The second is that they are animals, one immense organism whose towers are its stalks and whose pores are its skin, rooted in the deep crust and singing to call the rain the way a frog calls it. Those who believe this point to the gray dust that gathers each dawn at the tower's foot, exhaled from the pores and sifted down like shed skin. The tower is not built, they say. It is *grown*, and it is still growing.

The third, held mostly by the Pressed, and spoken about only reluctantly, is that the Instruments are not the makers but the *instruments* in the old sense. Something else plays them. Something small, or grain-scale, or spread thin through the whole plain, or not in the plain at all, is running its hands along the towers as a musician runs hands along strings. The roar is one very long piece of music, and the Cordfolk, for all their tending, are the audience, or the room.

When asked what the music is for, a Pressed will lay a hand on your chest, hum something that feels like a very slow question, and take the hand away.

## The Turning

Once in a season, or three times in a year, or not for ten, the Instruments change key.

The Cordfolk feel it before anything else does. Some people, called *leaners*, are born with a sensitivity that lets them feel the change gathering in their bodies as much as an hour early, a pressure like a held breath. When a leaner stiffens and drops her hands from her work, the news goes down the cords in seconds and along the ledges in minutes, and every person in every fissure stops.

Then comes the turning. The whole plain faces the nearest tower. Adults kneel on ledges and rim-stones with their palms flat and their mouths open, teeth apart, so that the sound can enter the skull by every route. They hold their children against their chests. Old men close their eyes. The roar climbs or drops through the whole body of the world, and the fog shears and the vines swing, and for a few minutes nothing on the plain does anything but receive.

Then the pitch settles into the new place, and the rain begins.

The Cordfolk do not cheer. They stay kneeling with their hands on the wet stone while the water pours off the black columns in silver sheets, and each of them, with a fingertip against the sternum, tries to learn the new note. Its nature decides the name of the season. It will shape the tuning of every house, and every cord will be retightened over the days that follow, a finger-width at a time, until the whole web hums in agreement with the new key.

Afterward, when the rain thins, the leaners go out along the trunk-lines, and the cord-readers lay their hands upon the vines. Somewhere on the wet dark plain a woman will kneel at the foot of a tower and pinch a single cord in a slow, careful pattern of absences. It is a message too small to be heard by anything that hears, addressed to the largest voice in the world. Nobody has ever learned whether it is answered.
