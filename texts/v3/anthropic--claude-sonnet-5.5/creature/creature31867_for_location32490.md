# The Residents of Low Vell

Low Vell has no population in the usual sense. It has a staff.

You notice this slowly. At first the town seems to hold only what any drowned place holds: a few fish, some snails, the clicking of small things in the weed. But the fish keep to routes. The snails keep to hours. The whole town has the unhurried, faintly purposeful air of a workplace long after the workers were expected to leave, when someone new has quietly taken over each task, none of them aware of the others, and everything still gets done.

## The Pale Commuters

The long, translucent fish are the most visible. Each is the length of a forearm, boneless-looking, and so clear that you can see the dark thread of its gut and a small red flicker of heart. They move alone. They thread the streets at rooftop height, turn at the same corners, and pass through the same gaps in the catwalks, always in the same direction along a given lane, as though a one-way rule were posted.

If you stay long enough, you see that their routes are not random. They follow the old paths between the tenement rows and the factory doors, the ones a shift of workers would have walked, and they thicken at the same hours the mills would have changed over. Nothing directs them. The current probably does it, or the warmth, or the slow gradient of iron in the water. But a fish will slow at the mouth of a loading bay and hang there a moment, gill-fans working, before it drifts on, and it is hard not to read that as a habit with a long memory behind it.

They eat the drifting film that lifts off the cobbles when the vent pulses. They breed in the hollows of the pipes. They do not seem to fear anything. When the great note rolls through, each one stops where it is, fins spread, and hangs motionless until the water settles. Then they resume, mid-lane, mid-route, as if a clock had been paused and restarted.

## The Glazers

The fist-sized snails have shells of banded umber and cream, worn thin and faintly translucent, and they own the windows. Thousands graze the glass, each on its own pane, drawing its foot across in slow sweeps. Behind each the glass is a little cleaner, in arcs that overlap and interlock, so that a whole tenement front is polished in a pattern like the wake of some enormous, patient hand.

They are not hurried by anything. A snail may take a full day to cross one pane. But the windows of Low Vell are clear in a way that nothing else in the town is, and the light from the lamps inside passes out through them into the streets. If the snails stopped, the town would go a little dimmer. The snails do not know this, and the town does not thank them.

They cluster at the lamps in the hour after each pulse, when the glow is brightest, and stack in soft, shell-clicking pyramids against the panes. Occasionally one falls. It descends very slowly, tumbling once, and lands on a ledge without retracting, and starts to graze again.

## The Clerks

The transparent shrimp live in the vine canopies and are more sound than sight: a dry, ticking chatter that runs along rails and pipes and drops away when you approach. They are small, and the tiniest are nearly invisible. Only their eyes, two black beads, and a faint orange smudge in the gut mark where each hangs in the leaves.

They move along the vines constantly, tapping and combing each node with their forelegs, and where they have passed the vine's glow is brighter by a small but noticeable degree. They seem to feed on something the vine secretes, and in return they clear the nodes of silt and the film that would smother them. It is a plain arrangement, without ceremony. But because the shrimp work the vines in one long unbroken chain, the ticking travels. A sound begun at one end of a lane arrives at the other minutes later, altered by every pair of hands it passed through, and no one is sure whether the messages that seem to move through the canopy are only echoes or something more like a running account of the town.

## The Lamp-Colonies

Inside each glass lamp lives a dense, soft-edged gathering of luminous life. It is not one creature and not quite a swarm. Under a lens it would resolve into thousands of pinhead cells, or perhaps a single tangled filament, clinging to the old electrodes and the inner wall like moss on a stone. It is faintly sweet-smelling, if you could smell here. It feeds on the mineral warmth and on whatever the vent breathes up.

Each colony brightens with the current and dims with the ebb. They are all tuned to the same pulse, and so the whole town glows and fades together, in slow amber breaths. When the great note passes through the pipes, they flare in unison, and for that moment every street is lit as if by a sudden dawn, the silhouettes of the buildings flung out in layers to the rim. Then they settle, a little dimmer, as if spent.

Whether the colonies are aware of one another is unknowable. But they respond to the note the way a crowd responds to a name called in a hall. They do not respond to anything else.

## The Vine

The dark, ridged vine that climbs the conduit courses is the largest living thing in Low Vell, in the sense that it is present almost everywhere. It runs through every wall, every lane, every buried channel where cable once ran. It is one plant, or many joined so long ago that the distinction has stopped meaning anything.

The vine does not move in any way you can watch. But it has opinions. It grows toward warmth, toward iron, toward the paths the current takes, and it reinforces what it needs. Where a gear cracked in the engine hall, the vine sent a lattice of itself into the gap, and the lattice hardened with lime into something like bone and took the load. Where a pulley wore, a ring of coral was welcomed in and trained around the rim. None of this has ever been seen happening. It is only found done.

If Low Vell has a mind, it is here, at a pace of years per thought, spread along the copper and the roots. It does not think about visitors. It has no reason to. But it keeps the town's machines fitting, by degrees, better and better to the water and the warmth, and by now the turbines turn more easily than they did when men oiled them.

In the deep lanes the vine grows pale and leafless, and glows at its nodes with the same rhythm as the lamps. The dark there is old and complete. If there are things living among the pale vines that nothing above has ever glimpsed, the town has not mentioned them.

## The Sleeper in the Chimney

The tallest chimney holds the town's one large animal. Its tail hangs from the flue-top like a rope of wet gray leather, thick as a thigh, and stirs slightly in the current. Most of the time it is only that, a length of something dangling from the brick. But at each pulse the tail draws slowly in, hand over hand, and the animal comes down.

It is long, longer than a boat, and heavy in the body, with a soft gray hide scarred in pale lines. Its face is blunt and whiskered, the whiskers thick and sensitive and trailing like the fronds of the chimney-weed, and its eyes are small and set far back, dull as river pebbles. It moves very slowly, at the height of the second-story windows, with a barely perceptible undulation of its whole length, and it makes its rounds.

It touches things. It presses its face to the vines, to brass door handles worn smooth, to the glass of pressure gauges still bolted to the pipes. It does this without hurry and without apparent appetite, one object after another, holding each for a breath or two, and then it moves to the next. It never touches the same thing twice in a round. It has done this for longer than the snails have been grazing.

What it learns from the touching is a mystery. Perhaps it tastes the iron in the water. Perhaps it listens through its whiskers to the low hum of the vine. Perhaps it is doing what the night watchman of the old mills did with a lantern and a key, checking that every latch is fastened and every gauge holds steady. When the round is done, it turns back toward the chimney, rises, and slides into the flue tail-first, and the tail hangs out again, motionless, until the next pulse.

If you are in its path, it will come to you as it comes to everything: slowly, without interest, its face level with yours. The whiskers will brush your shoulder, your hand, the buckle at your belt. For a moment you will feel weighed, though not judged, only entered into some account. Then the great gray body will turn aside, and pass, and you will find you have been counted as one more fixture of the town, which is perhaps the best a visitor can hope for.

## Those Who Mend

There is one more category, and it is the least certain. Someone, or something, has done the repairs that the vine cannot have done alone: the seating of a valve, the rethreading of a belt, a rivet pressed back into its hole and sealed under a coat of lime. The work is neat. It has the look of small hands, or of many small mouths, patient and exact.

Nobody has seen them. They may be the shrimp, working in a concert too slow to observe. They may be a colonial thing that lives inside the pipes, a chain of tiny bodies moving as one, coming out only when the current is high. They may be something that was once the town's people and has, over ages, softened into the water and the warmth and the growth, and now lives the way the vines live, without faces, without names, in the shape of the work itself.

The evidence is only that the town keeps running. The turbines turn. The looms stroke once in answer. The lamps flare and settle. If someone is minding all this, they have no interest in being thanked, and no need of company. They are simply the town's way of continuing, and you will drift past their handiwork on every wall without ever knowing where they are.
