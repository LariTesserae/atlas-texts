# Who Lives in the Fen

Nothing walks here, and nothing watches. The fen has no animals and no one to look at it. What lives here is the forest, and the forest is so complete that "inhabitant" and "habitat" are the same word. Still, the forest is not one thing. It is a crowd of kinds, each holding a post, and if you spent long enough with your palms on its bark you would learn them the way you learn the members of a household by their footsteps.

## The Elders of the Seams

The oldest are the column-growers, whose roots have fused where the fractures run hottest. They are not individuals in any sense you could count. A column may once have been a hundred seedlings that found the same crack, wove together, and stopped being separate. Nobody knows where one ends and the next begins, and the columns themselves have no use for the question.

They live slowly and on the hottest food. Their sap channels are lined with dark metallic crusts, and the crusts are alive: films of iron-eating and sulfur-eating microbes that the columns feed and shelter and could not do without. The plant supplies the channel, the pressure, and the steady flow. The microbes turn the seam's raw chemistry into something a root can drink. Neither could be called host or guest. Together they are the intake of the whole forest, and the tingle under your palm is the two of them working.

Columns do not grow in any way you could watch. They thicken by a finger's width in a lifetime, and they do not die so much as get absorbed into the next generation of themselves.

## The Beat-Keepers

Above the columns stand the trunk-mains, and these have something close to personalities. Each one keeps its own interval, one stroke every three hours, or five, or nine and a half, and it holds that interval for its whole life. The young are quick and a little ragged, with valves that snap. The old are slow and deep, and their *tock* has the finality of a heavy door in a distant building.

The important thing about them is that they refuse to fall into step. Coupled oscillators tend to synchronize, and a forest that pulsed together would slam its own pipes to pieces. The trunk-mains evolved against this. Their membranes are tuned a little stiffer or looser than their neighbors', and each trunk's timing drifts just enough to stay clear of the beats around it. A trunk that starts to lock with another will thicken a valve or stiffen a fold until it slips free again. This is a kind of manners, practiced by billions, and it is why the pressure of the fen holds like a held breath and never surges.

The hollows inside the trunks are full of a second, quieter population: fermenting microbes that live in the warm sap and give off the sweet, scorched smell. They are the reason the air has its kettle-left-on-the-flame note. They are not doing it for anyone.

## The Tiers

In the far dark above, the vane-canopy lives its own life, and it is the coolest and most public of the forest's societies. The vane-leaves hang in ridged black sheets, tier under tier, each shedding heat to the sheet beneath. Each tier has its species, and each species has its altitude. Higher leaves are thin and fast to radiate, and lower leaves are thick and slow, with the drip-tips that let the condensate fall to the next level down. A drop of water may be caught and passed through twenty tiers on its way to the mat, and every leaf along the way takes its small tithe of coolness and lets the rest fall.

Mosses coat the vanes and are almost the only things in the fen that seem to *play*. They are not playing. They creep along the wet ridges of each leaf in thin bright films, finding the places where condensation gathers, and where the leaf tilts they tilt too. But in a forest with no wind worth the name and no season, the creeping has a restless look, like fingers drumming on a table.

At the surface of the black pools the great door-leaves float. They are not radiators but lids, and they keep the pools still and warm. Beneath each lid hang the pale roots of whatever grows below.

## The Ones Who Wait

Hanging in curtains where the weeping rock meets the mat are the root-hairs, pale as candle wax. They are perhaps the most attentive things in the fen. Each strand is a single tube of cell, and a curtain of them can register a film of water sliding down the basalt within moments and lean toward it. They have never had a use for anything else. They do not hunt, because there is nothing to hunt. They only wait, and when the water comes they take it, and this has been their whole life and the whole life of every generation before them.

If you stood among them with your warm skin and your breath, they would curl toward you. This would not be interest. You are a small, moving, moist thing, and every part of them is built to move toward that.

## The Mat and Its Multitudes

Most of the living weight of the fen is in the mat, and most of that is too small to see even if there were light. The fiber is a city. Filaments of fungus-like growth thread the dead roots, and the dead roots are not really dead, only reassigned. Nothing in the fen is wasted. A root that stops carrying water becomes structure, then substrate, then food for the next thing down. Microbes that eat iron live beside microbes that breathe sulfur, and both live beside those that make the reduced gases the others need. Their trade is continuous and balanced, and it is as tightly regulated as the rest of the fen without anyone regulating it.

You could say the mat is the forest's stomach and its memory. Everything that has ever grown here is still in it somewhere, in altered form.

## The Vines, the Couriers

Between trunk and trunk run the vines, the only lives in the fen that seem to travel. They don't travel far. A vine may take a century to cross the gap between two columns. But they thread the whole country like wiring, and a great many of them are conductors in the literal sense. They carry small currents between neighbors, evening out the differences in potential between one trunk and the next, and in that way they are a little like the postal service of a place with nothing to post.

Their competition is with each other, and it is quiet. Each vine wants a place on a flow of heat or water or current, and the vines that got there first have it. Younger vines wind among the older ones and wait, sometimes for generations, for a place to open. A place opens when a trunk falls silent.

## The Sparks

The blue-white discharges are not a creature, but they are done *by* something. The bark of the columns is lined with a specialized tissue, a scalelike cover of cells that holds charge until the potential grows too steep and then releases it in a glimmer that runs across the surface and is gone. It is a safety valve, and the bark is proud of it in whatever way bark can be. The tissue does not act at random. Each patch is tuned to fire at a slightly different threshold, so that across the continent the number of sparks in any hour comes out the same as in any other.

For a moment the spark shows a leaf, a thread of root, a bead of water. Then the dark closes again, and nothing has seen any of it.

## How It Knows

No inhabitant here has eyes or has ever wanted them. The idea of a sky has never occurred to anything living, because nothing has ever needed to look up. What the fen has in place of sight is *position*. Every part knows where it sits on the flows of heat, water, and charge, and it knows this with complete and unthinking precision. A valve knows its interval. A root-hair knows the water. A vane knows how much heat to shed. When a trunk falls silent, a sapling in the same slot inherits its interval so exactly that the hum does not change by a hair. The offices persist while the officeholders come and go, and the undersong goes on.

Whether there is anything it is like to be the fen is not a question the fen asks. If there is a mind here, it is distributed over a continent, moves at the speed of sap, and has never had a thought that was not also a chore. It holds its pressure, sheds its heat, drinks its water, and passes its small currents from vine to vine.

## The Visitor

You would be the only thing that moved on purpose. The forest would not notice you, but it would respond. Root-hairs would lean, mosses would inch toward your warmth, and a vine might lay a tendril across your ankle and hold it there, not to trap you but because you were, briefly, a flow.

You would not be an inhabitant. But if you stayed until you could no longer hear the hum, you would understand what the inhabitants are: not a population but a single settled act, repeated in the warm dark by every part of itself, and holding.
