# The Tenants of the Cleft

No one lives in the Foundry Cleft in the sense of looking back at you. Nothing here has eyes, and nothing breathes on a schedule of its own. The residents are the plants. They do not hurry, but they keep up with the cleft, which is a harder thing than hurrying. If you stand in the roar long enough, you begin to see that the whole place is a workforce and the ground is the employer.

## The Tree Ferns

The tree ferns are the old hands, and by the standards of the cleft they are very old, which means a few decades. Their trunks are black, thick as a thigh, matted with a fiber that holds water like felt. They stand in loose crowds on the warm floor, and each crown overlaps its neighbors until the canopy is a single shivering roof. They never stop moving. The ground hum runs up through the trunks and the steam wind lifts the fronds, so the whole stand trembles in a flicker that has nothing to do with any breeze you can feel on your face.

Underneath them is the undercroft, which is the closest thing the cleft has to a settled neighborhood. It is lightless, dripping, and warm as a laundry. The dead fronds hang down in brown collars and the trunks are furred with moss to shoulder height. The ferns have bet everything on the floor staying warm. Some will lose. When the heat drifts away, the outer trunks go first. The fronds brown from the tips inward, the crown sags, and within a season the tree is a black post leaking peat. The ones nearer the new heat come up green behind it. It is not exactly a march, but from above it would look like one.

## The Silver Columns

The rosette plants stand waist-high among the ferns, single stems crowned with tufts of silver-felted leaves. They look like sentries, but they are more like condensers. The felt catches cloud and steam, beads it, and sheds it in a steady drip, so each plant stands in its own small rain. Beneath every one is a ring of dark, sodden ground where nothing else can quite take hold, and the rosette seems to like it that way. Its dead leaves are never dropped. They hang down the stem in a brown skirt, layer over layer, a record of every year it has lived. Some plants wear skirts that reach the mud.

In the dim orange from the fissure the silver leaves catch a little light and hold it. Among the black and the rust they are the only pale things that are not mineral, and a line of them along a ridge of scoria looks like a procession stopped mid-step.

## The Red-Barked Knots

The shrubs are the middle class and the most stubborn. They have twisted themselves into knots against wind and heat, so a bush no taller than your chest may have a trunk that coils twice around itself before it branches. The bark is red and peels in paper sheets that curl away and fall into the mud, where they lie like discarded permits. The leaves are small and leathery and give nothing away. The shrubs grow where the ferns do not, in the cracks and on the shattered slopes, at the edge of where heat becomes cold and the mist thins. They take the worst ground and hold it. When a slab drops from the wall with a crack and a long clatter, the shrubs below are usually the ones that end up half buried and still alive, sending up new stems through the gravel.

## The Moss

The moss is the population. The other plants are individuals you can count. The moss is a tide. It lies in deep sponge cushions on every horizontal surface, hangs in long wet beards from every branch, and climbs the basalt columns in bands, higher where the steam reaches and thinner where it does not. Near a fumarole it is a green so intense it seems to glow against the black, a violent color in a place that has otherwise given up on color. Farther out it dulls to olive and then to a brownish gray, and the gradient tells you where the heat has been recently and where it is leaving.

The moss does the slow work. It holds the water that would otherwise run off the scoria. It makes the first soil on rock that has had no time to make any. It catches the falling ash and grit and turns it into something a fern spore can land on. Every other plant in the cleft is standing on what the moss prepared. If the ferns are the old hands, the moss is the ground crew that was here before the hands arrived.

## The Crusts and Films

At the margins live the small, patient ones. The lichens spread in rust orange and mustard on the sulfur-splashed stones, forming rings that widen a fraction of a finger's width in a year, if the stone stays put, which it seldom does. The liverworts coat the seeps in a slick green film that shines wetly even in the near dark. These plants ask almost nothing. They take a rock, a trickle, and the faintest warmth, and they make a life of it. A tremor can shear the stone from under them and they start again on the broken face.

## How They Live

There are no seasons in the ordinary sense, only the drift of the heat. That is the shift schedule. A patch that is frosted and bitter in the morning may be a warm fog bank by afternoon, and the plants respond as they can. The moss greens within days. Fern croziers uncoil within weeks. A shrub pushes new leaves on the warm side of its knot while the cold side stays bare. Nothing is decided in advance. The whole community is a slow reflex, and the heat is the nerve it answers.

They do not move, but they are always changing address. Spores drift on the steam wind and settle on ground that is warm this year. Seeds fall and take root where a new vent has opened, ringed with yellow sulfur crystals like sugar, and by the time the vent has died the seedlings are already tall enough to survive the cooling. Where the heat withdraws, the growth browns and rots into black peat, and the peat feeds whatever comes up in the next flush, a few meters or a few hundred paces on. It is a closed economy. Nothing is wasted, and nothing is kept.

## The Pipe

There is one thing in the cleft that the plants have not made and are slowly taking. The iron wellhead pipe leans out of its mound of moss, flanged, bolted, rusted to the color of scoria, with a seam that still leaks a thin whistle of steam. Ferns grow from its flange bolts. Moss has climbed its length. It is the only straight line in the place, and it is being read back into the landscape one spore at a time. In a few years it will be a mossy lump with a hole in it, and then a lump.

Perhaps it is the closest the cleft has to a grave marker, for a project that failed to tap the heat. The plants succeeded where the engineers did not. They have tapped it entirely, all of it, every drift and pulse.

## What Waits

Nothing here is permanent, least of all the tenants. The fissure will seal one day. The jets will falter, the hammering will thin to a drip, and the ground will stop trembling. The moss will brown at the edges first, then the ferns, then the silver columns will stand with their felt gone dull and dry. Peat will pile up deep and black.

But it will not be empty. The shrubs will hold longest, twisted and red, and the seeds that fell in the last good years will come up in the peat. A cold, quiet forest will stand on the ridge in the dark, made of the plants that learned to live in the noise, and it will be the only trace that the cleft ever roared. For now they are still at work, shaking in their own wet weather, and the whole shift is on.
