# Who Lives in the Clamor Flats

Nothing here is quiet, so the first inhabitants to describe are the ones who are never silent for long. The frogs come first. After them come the things that eat the frogs, and the things that eat those. The people come somewhere near the middle, and they are the only ones who keep records.

## The frogs, who are the weather

There are hundreds of kinds, and the people know perhaps ninety by ear. The smallest is a fingernail of translucent green that sits on the underside of a leaf and produces a single, needle-fine tick. The largest is a brown, wide-mouthed thing the size of a loaf, wedged in the mouth of a warm fissure. It answers the whole basin with a hollow *tonk*, and the sound seems to come up out of the rock itself.

The frogs do not call at random. A species listens to its neighbors, waits, and joins in. Because each kind has a preferred distance and rhythm, the calls build into pulses that cross the basin in slow, visible waves, visible in the way the fireflies and glow-fungi seem to flicker in time with them. The people say the frogs are a single animal thinking. A frog at the edge of a wave is not choosing to call. It is being passed along.

Their silence is a message too. A few heartbeats before a tremor, a whole region of chorus goes out like a snuffed lamp. The frogs feel it in their feet, in the wet skin of their bellies, in the mud they sit in. The people feel it in the gap where the sound was.

## The people

Perhaps two hundred of them live in the basin, in bands of eight or twelve or twenty, and no band stays anywhere long. They are lean and low-slung, and they walk with their knees always a little bent, setting each foot down flat and testing it with the heel before they put weight on it. On the loose basalt plates this comes to feel like the only sensible way to walk. The plates clink under them, and the sound carries. A stranger walks loudly. A person of the Flats clinks softly, or not at all.

They are scarred in a particular way: fine white lines across the palms and the outer edges of the feet, from years of touching things that cut. Their skin is rubbed daily with a paste of ash and tree resin against the biting insects, which gives them a dull grey sheen, and where the paste wears off, the dew beads on them like it does on the ferns. Their hair is cropped or bound tight. Trailing hair in the hammock ropes, in the vines, in the spider-silks that hang between trunks, is a nuisance at best.

Their eyes are ordinary, and they use them with care. Near a vent they keep one eye shut, so that if it flares, the shut eye still has its dark. They call this "keeping a lamp banked." A child who forgets is cuffed, gently, and the lesson is not repeated often.

### How they speak

The chorus is too loud for voices, so they have arranged a language in three registers, none of which depends on being heard in the ordinary way.

At close range they talk with their hands. In the dark, which is most of the time, the hands go into each other's palms: a signing pressed into the skin, fast and light, so that two people sitting shoulder to shoulder can hold a conversation the whole basin cannot overhear. In the lit places by a hearth or a vent, the hands move in the air, and the signs are larger and more relaxed. A joke told in the dark can be told only to one person at a time, and this has made them a people of confidences.

At a middle range they whistle, in pitches placed above or below the chorus, in the narrow gaps where nothing else sings. Every band has whistles the others know and some they don't. A whistle is a name, a warning, a *come here*, a *not there*, a *slab moved*.

At long range they tap, hollow wood on hollow wood, low and slow, a pitch the insects do not fill. Sound moves through a trunk faster and farther than through the air, and a good tapper can send a message through five or six trees to a band a long walk away. The tapping is almost a second language, a kind of speech that lives in the forest's own timber.

Nobody shouts. The one who tries, a visitor once in a great while, gets nothing back but a puzzled look, and perhaps a cord thrown around a wrist to hold them steady until they stop.

### The hearing

They have learned to hear the way one reads a page: not every letter, but the shape of it. A band moving at night is listening for the things that are missing. A gap where the clack-frogs should be is a heavy animal moving through. A rise in the whistling trills at the wrong hour means the fog is thickening. When the low drone of one certain beetle stops, it is almost always because a snake is on the trunk.

The old ones in a band are often the best listeners, and often the worst hearers. After decades of noise, the high registers wear away. But what they lose in the ear they have gained in the feet, the palms, the sternum. An old woman sitting with her hand on a warm slab and her eyes closed can tell you that a tremor is coming before the blue bulbs have swelled. The band walks with her in the middle, or she walks at the head with a hand trailing the ground, and no one thinks this strange. Deafness of the ear is called "an old ear," and the person who has it is being honored, not pitied.

### Where they sleep and what they carry

By day they may walk, forage, dig, and knap. At night they sleep in hammocks of woven bark cord slung three or four body-lengths above the litter, out of reach of the ants and the millipedes and whatever else comes through in the dark. Each hammock has a tether, and a sleeper, especially a child, is tied by a cord at the wrist to the trunk. Anyone who wakes and finds the cord slack and the sleeper gone rings the wooden clapper hung by the fire.

Their hearths are stacked basalt slabs under leaf-thatch overhangs. The fire must be tended all night, since the damp will smother it in an hour of neglect. Someone always sits by it. That person is the watch. They are also the first to feel the ground.

What they carry: obsidian blades chipped from the glass flows and worn in cord slings at the hip, digging sticks fire-hardened to a dark gloss, bone needles, nets, resin-sealed gourds for water, light stone-tipped spears. Little else. What a band cannot carry, the Flats will eat. A tool left on the ground grows fungus by morning. A basket left on a rock is furred with white by the second day. The people mostly do not make things to last; they make things to be remade.

### The cords

The one thing they make to keep is the tally-cord.

Each route through the Flats has its own cord, made of strands twisted from bark fiber and knotted along the length, and the knots are the map. A single knot means a slab that rocks. A double knot means a warm fissure to cross, and its distance. A knot with a small bone bead is a new vent. A knot with a tuft of red fiber is a place where someone died. A flat loop is a fresh crack. The cords are hung on pegs at the mouths of the routes, and anyone can read them by touch, even in the dark. They are worn smooth and dark from handling, and a very old cord feels like polished wood.

When a path changes, the cord-keeper of whichever band finds out first ties a new knot, or cuts out an old one and replaces it. Cords are always being mended. A cord that has not been touched in a season is a cord no one trusts.

The people scratch a few marks on bark and stone too: an arrow, a stroke-count, a crossed line for *do not go on*. This is as near as they come to writing, and it is enough. Everything else they say, they say to a face or to a palm.

### Children, and how they are taught

A child of the Flats learns first to be still. Then to be still and listen. Then to be still, listen, and name what stopped.

A game the young ones play, tied by cords to a trunk while the adults work below, is called something like *finding the hole*. One child hums, or taps, or hisses, and the others, eyes closed, try to say which frog stopped, which insect changed, where the hush began. The winner is whoever hears the absence first. This is treated as more important than any other game, and adults will sometimes stop their work and join in.

### The dead

The dead are wrapped in bark-cord nets and hoisted into the high canopy, in the crotch of a great buttress-rooted tree, and left. By evening the net is empty. The fungi, the ants, the scavenger birds with bare heads, the bats: all of them have a share. This is not sorrow, exactly. It is a return of the body to a place that will not wait.

A knot is tied in the cord of the route the person walked most. It is red, and it stays.

## What lives near them, and what they call it

**The slate-hides.** Heavy, low-slung grazers push through the ferns along the warm fissures at a walk that shakes the ground faintly, so the people learn to read them by the paper-leaf vines curling in a line ahead of their path. The people leave them entirely alone in the daytime and follow them at a distance at night, because where a slate-hide feeds, the ground is stable enough for a heavy animal. They say a slate-hide has never fallen through. This is not strictly true.

**The dark cats.** Long-bodied, low, coats the color of wet basalt, eyes that catch the vent-glow and throw it back as twin coals. The cats hunt by the same principle the people live by: they listen to the gaps. A cat waits at the edge of a chorus until a frog stops, and there it strikes. The people give the cats one of their oldest whistles, a long falling note, and a band that hears it will tie off all the hammock cords and sit very still.

**The small loud packs.** Quick, wiry, half the size of the cats, and twice as noisy. They go through the forest floor in shrieking crowds that sound like a hundred rusted hinges. Their alarms send everything else scattering. The people watch them closely, since a pack that turns abruptly means a cat, and a pack that scatters means something worse.

**The rusty gate.** Somewhere in the canopy a creature makes a sound like an iron gate dragged over stone, over and over, at any hour, with total confidence. No one has ever seen it clearly. The children have a name for it that is also a sort of joke, and to be told "you sound like the gate" is a mild insult and a mild compliment, meaning: *you are very sure, and you are wrong.*

**The pale ones in the hot pools.** Blind, many-legged, translucent as a fingernail, they emerge from the water only when the tremors start, and drink at the edge, and go back down. They are not feared. They are used. If you see them come up, you have perhaps a minute. Some bands keep a pool near camp for this reason and tend it, and think of its pale tenants almost as kin.

**The groaning thing.** Somewhere in the wet places, in the low, warm hollows where the fog pools deepest, something large makes a sound like a very long exhale through a wooden pipe. No one has described it. No one has claimed to see it. The people avoid the hollows where it is heard and, when they must pass one, tap three times on the nearest trunk, slowly, without any expectation of an answer. It is not a prayer. It is a courtesy.

## A band at the turn to morning

In the last dark hour a band of about twelve gathers at its hearth under the thatch. The fog is thickest now and voices seem to come from behind. The watch has been awake all night, feeding the fire, one hand flat on a warm slab. Two of the children have been tied to a trunk and are sleeping with their mouths open. The old woman with her hand on the stone has not moved for an hour.

Then she taps twice, sharply, on the slab.

Everyone is up. The frogs to the east have gone quiet, a wedge of silence spreading through the sound like a drop of water spreading through cloth, and the blue bulbs at the base of the trunk are swelling. Hands go into palms. Someone unties the children, someone kicks apart the fire, someone else is already on the first rung of a climbing vine. Twelve people move without a word in the noise and the dark.

The tremor comes and goes. It is a small one. Every sound stops for perhaps four seconds, and in it you can hear the drip, the hiss of the vent, the pulse in twelve throats. Then one frog tries a note, and the whole basin roars back.

The cord-keeper is already walking toward the peg at the route's mouth with a length of bark-cord in her hand. Out in the middle of the basin, where the ground is newest, a new crack has opened. She will not know where until she has walked there and looked. When she has, she will tie a flat loop, and the path will be different by exactly one knot, and the band will go on.
