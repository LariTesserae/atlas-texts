# The Tenants

You will not see them. That is not the same as their being absent, and the difference is the first thing the plateau teaches.

Travelers call them the Tenants, because they keep the place as tenants keep a house that belongs to someone older. They are a people, a whole people, thousands strong or a handful. The count is one of the things that has worn off. They live here in every sense but simultaneity.

## Out of Step

The plateau's shadows lag their objects. The Tenants lag further, and each of them lags by a different amount. One is a few breaths behind the present, so that you walk through the place she has just left and find the cobbles warm from her feet. Another is an afternoon behind. Another is a season ahead, or a generation, and is only now walking toward the doorway you are standing in.

So the Tenants never quite meet. They don't meet each other and they don't meet you. What each of them meets is the consequence of the others: the porridge, the swept step, the lamp already lit. The city is not empty. It is a place where everyone is present and no two people are present *together*.

Sometimes the edge of one shows. At dusk a long shadow crosses the street carrying a bowl, and there is no one to cast it. The person will arrive an hour later, or arrived yesterday, and the shadow has only now caught up with the light. Once in a while you feel a doorway occupied, the way you feel a held breath, and step aside for someone who is not yet, or no longer, there.

## The Tarns

The black tarns are the one surface slow enough to hold them. Water that still and that cold takes an impression the way clay does. Kneel at the edge and look, and the reflection will not always show the sky above you. It shows a sky from some other hour, and sometimes it shows figures standing on the bank behind you.

They are broad and low, built for weather. Their hands are large and flat-fingered, with thick pads at the thumb, made for the hammerstone and the press of clay. Their faces are the strangest part. They are not disfigured, but *smoothed*, as if some long attrition had rounded them the way the mountains were rounded. The brow, the nose, and the corners of the mouth have lost their edges without losing their meaning. The eyes remain sharp, and they look at you the way you look at a room someone has just left: attentive to what was there, patient about what returns.

They dress in felt, since there is no wool, pressed from the silver hair of the felt-columns. It is heavy and pale and slightly damp, and it carries a bead of fog on every fiber. They wear copper at the wrists and throat, beaten thin and green. Their hair, if the reflections can be trusted, is braided with sedge cord and worn long, and it hangs all one way like the sedge.

If you turn from the water, no one is there. If you turn back, the reflection is only the sky.

## The Relay

Everything they do is done across the gaps between them. This is the whole art of their life, and it resembles courtesy more than anything else.

The porridge is the plainest example. It is set out steaming beside a wooden spoon because someone once set it down *for whoever comes next*. To a person a day ahead, or a year behind, "just now" is a long stretch of time, and the bowl stays hot across the whole of it. It is not a trick. Its warmth is a promise kept at the pace of the one who made it. When you eat, you are the next one. You are the person it was for, and you will find yourself grateful to someone who is, by their own clock, still stirring the pot.

The etiquette, learned slowly by those who stay, is simple. You eat what is set out and you wash the bowl in the gutter, where the water runs clear. You turn the bowl rim-down on the sill to say *received*. You do not leave it dirty, because someone will need the bowl at some point.

The clay outside the potters' quarter is a longer conversation. A palm presses in, and then fingers smooth the edge. Hours or seasons later another hand answers with a thumb-stroke, a small correction, or a small flourish. A vessel is made by twenty potters who have never been in the same hour. The tools shift on the benches because a hand has moved them, some hand for whom it is already or not yet the moment. The drill-bow turns half a stroke because someone is partway through a stroke.

Nothing is wasted in this. Each task is set down where the next person can pick it up, and the city goes on being built because the work is passed hand to hand across time.

## The Cairns

The cairns are how they answer travelers, and they are the closest the Tenants come to speech.

You build a small pile of stones at a crossing, and you come back and it has become an *arch*, which means *come through*. Or it has become a *hand*, five stones fanned open, which means *wait* or *welcome*, and the two are the same word. Or it has become a *spiral*, which seems to mean *down*, or *deeper*, or *stay*. It is never the same twice, and it is never wrong. What you left was a question, and whoever restacked it read the question and answered in the only grammar they have, which is placement.

Many travelers have left a cairn out of idleness and been startled to find that it *meant* something. It is like discovering that a word you said aloud in an empty room was heard.

## Below

The oldest of them live lowest. The city is built in strata, each house floored over by the next, and the lag deepens with the depth. Toward the bottom, where the lamps are still lit in the black compaction, the Tenants are so far behind that the city above is not yet built. The taps of the hammerstone heard through the gratings come from people who are still raising the first house. To them the granite is new, the hearth-stones are unblackened, and the plateau is a place they are just beginning to settle.

They do not know about us, or about the upper city, or about the moss growing over their futures. They tap patiently, one course of stone at a time, and the tapping travels up through the layers and arrives here as a faint rhythm with no direction, the sound of the city's own foundation being laid.

This is why the city grows a course of stone each season with no visible mason. The masons are at the bottom of the time they occupy, and the stone they lay is laid *up* through everything. They are the oldest tenants and the most industrious, and the hardest of all to reach.

## The Columns

Travelers disagree about the felt-columns, and it is the most beautiful disagreement on the plateau.

The columns stand a dozen paces apart, spaced with a courtesy that looks surveyed. Each drips at its base into its own dark ring. Some say it is only a plant, a fog-catcher, and that its spacing is a fact about competition for water. Others say that the Tenants who have lagged so far that they have nearly stopped, who have slowed until they stand in one place through whole seasons, are gathered in the plants. The fog beads on the silver hair as it once beaded on their felt. The dripping is the slow tick of a life at last so unhurried that a single drop is an hour.

The felt they wear is pressed from the same hair. If the second view is true, then they clothe themselves in their own future, and the columns are the place they go when the work has been passed on and there is finally nothing left to hand to anyone.

Nobody has proved it. But the columns are never cut, and they are never burned for their wood or stripped bare. When the Tenants take hair for felt they take a little and never from one column twice, as one might trim a sleeping elder's beard. That much is evident from the trunks, which show the same faint shearing, patient and evenly rotated.

## The Weather

They keep the rain the way other peoples keep animals. There is no evidence they own it, but the columns of rain wander the swells like herds and are never found wandering far. Sedge-cord lines run out from the terraces to stone stakes on the slopes, and no one knows what they were strung to hold. The early rain that darkens the moss under a clear sky and the late rain that falls under a dry noon are the same rain, some travelers claim, being *walked home*: a shower that missed its hour, led gently by the cord to the time it was owed.

A people who never had a beast to tend would tend the one wild thing the country offered. It is a plausible sort of gentleness.

## To Be a Guest

Stay long enough and you begin to feel attended rather than watched. There is no gaze here. There is only the sense that the place has been arranged with you in mind, though the arranger is somewhere else in time and has never heard your name. The steps are swept before you climb them. The lamp in the guest-house is lit an hour before dark. When you leave a bowl unwashed, it is washed. When you leave your palm in the clay, another palm, warm and rougher than yours, will have covered it by morning, not to erase it but to hold it.

What you feel, if you let yourself, is not loneliness. It is closer to what a child feels who wakes in a dark house and hears, from some other room, dishes being set down. Someone is up, someone is doing what needs doing, and you are not the reason, though you are included.

## Evening

At dusk the fog climbs out of the swells, and the copper sheets begin to ring. The lamps come on window by window across the city, in the order in which they were first lit, from the oldest hearths to the newest. That order is the one act all the Tenants perform together, and it is the one moment their clocks align. For the length of the lighting they are, for once, *in the same hour*, all thousand windows kindled by a thousand hands across a thousand lags, and the city is briefly, completely inhabited.

If you are quiet, and stand on the terrace of any round house with your bowl rim-down on the sill, you may feel it: a crowd standing shoulder to shoulder in the dusk, facing the same lamps, saying nothing at all.

Then the lag resumes, the hour comes apart, and each of them walks off into their own time. The porridge is set down again, warm, for whoever comes.
