# Those Who Live in the Basin

Everything here is listening to everything else. The animals, the plants, and the thing at the bottom of the water are not separate populations sharing a bowl of rock. They are parts of one long piece of work, and most of them seem to know their place in it.

## The Choir

**The bell-frogs** are the first residents you meet, because they are impossible to miss. They sit in the warm shallows in their thousands, gold and glassy, their throats so thin you can see the pulse of air through them. Each sings a single pitch, and each pitch is a little different, so that together they make the shrill, beaded clatter that fills the gaps between strokes. They are not singing at anything. They are tuning. Watch a single frog over an hour and you will see it drift sharp or flat, catch itself, and slide back to the pitch of its neighbors. The chorus is a kind of communal memory of the correct note.

Their most remarkable gift is the silence. In the last fraction of a second before each stroke, the whole chorus stops. No frog leads and none signals. The cutoff is clean as a dropped blade, and it arrives before the great tone does. They are not reacting to the stroke. They are *expecting* it, with a certainty that reads as faith.

A few are always wrong. A young frog, or a frog that has swallowed a mouthful of grit, will call on into the quiet and get half a second of solo before the bell rings over it. That is usually the last thing it does. The tallers take the mistaken ones, and it is hard not to see this as the basin's method of correction. In a place where timing is the whole law, the frogs that lose the count are removed from the count.

Lately the chorus has a hurry in it. The older frogs are the ones who hurry. They call a shade faster than the drone wants, press into the gaps, and crowd the shallows near the Well-Eye in the hour before dark, packed so tight the water seems to have a gold skin. Nobody has explained this, but the tallers walk more slowly around them than they used to.

**The tallers** are the frogs' wardens and predators, and they are the only animals that seem to keep accounts. They are ash-grey, long in the shank, with a yellow stripe running down the neck like a strip of lamplight. They stand motionless in the wet edges for so long that the curd begins to set around their shins, and then they lift a foot free with a soft kiss of suction and stab once. They do not gulp. They swallow the frog whole and then stand a moment longer, head tilted, as if hearing something inside.

After a kill, or a feeding, or some occasion we cannot read, a taller carries pebbles to a flat place on the true stone and builds. The stones are sorted before they are stacked, the largest at the base and each layer smaller than the last. A finished cairn is a small stepped cone, with the smallest stone always set at the top. Then the bird leaves it forever. They never add to an old cairn, never knock one over, and never eat or nest near one.

If you go from cairn to cairn across the true stone, you may notice the older heaps are tall, ten or twelve courses, and the newer ones are shorter. The newest are hardly heaps at all: three courses, two, a pair of stones with a pebble balanced on top. Whether the tallers are counting something down, or simply running out of a thing to stack, is unclear. It is certain that they walk to the Reeds at dusk and tap their bills against the white stalks in a rhythm that hovers just behind the drone. The old birds tap slower than the young. The old ones also tap for longer, after the young ones have gone.

**The curd-crawlers** are the least regarded and the most useful. They are flat, wide as a hand, and mottled cream and grey, so they vanish against the curd until you see one move. They have many short legs that they work in a rippling wave, and beneath their bodies is a rasping mouth-plate, like a rough tongue, which they drag across the fresh gel. What comes out behind them is a thin, hard, gray-green line: the first stone. They graze the wet edge in slow ranks, thousands abreast, and the ground behind them goes from print-holding to bootproof. It is likely they do not know they are building. They eat the softness and excrete the hardness, and the basin has arranged itself so that this is exactly what it needs.

At each stroke they stop dead and press flat, legs tucked. Afterward they resume, and the trails they leave in the next minute are the straightest of the day.

**The ceiling-drifters** live above it all. They are kite-shaped, translucent, a meter or two across, with trailing filaments that hang like fine wet hair. They ride the updraft in slow spirals, feeding on the condensing droplets and, it seems, on the low edge of the sound. Their bodies are nearly empty: veins, a bruise of denser tissue at the center, a fold of membrane that catches every stroke and rings like a drumhead. When the bell sounds, they flare pale blue at the edges and the light runs around them in a ring.

At dusk they come down in hundreds and settle on the Reeds like moths on a lamp. There they drone. It is not quite a song. It is a sympathetic vibration, the membrane answering the spire, the spire answering the ground, and the ground answering something else. They cling there in the copper dark with their filaments trailing down the white stalks, glowing, and the whole east side of the basin becomes a single pale organ.

## The Wader

In the fog, at the far edge of the wet, something moves that is not any of these.

It is low, wide, and slow, big as a cart. Its back is a curve of dark plating, like an upturned hull, ridged and barnacled with pale curd. It wades. That is the only word for it: it moves through the shallow water on legs you never see, leaving a wake that stiffens behind it. The frogs go silent at its passing, and this silence is different from the silence before the stroke. It is not anticipation. It is deference, or fear, or the ordinary quiet a crowd falls into when a great person enters.

Nobody has seen it clearly, but the pattern of its wandering can be read from the ground. It walks the boundaries where the wet meets the curd, and where it has passed, the gel is already firming. It seems to *press* the water with its weight, and the water sets. It is either a grazer of the softest matter or a gardener of the newest stone. It halts at every stroke, and if you are patient enough in the fog you may see that it halts with its head, or the front of its body, lowered into the water, as though it were drinking the note.

Some who have listened long enough think that the stroke is not only above the wader but *through* it. On the night when the curd shifts and the whole floor groans, the wader is usually near. It may be the machine's hand. It may be the machine's ear, or the machine's child. It may be one of the last of the builders' servants, still working after the work has gone quiet elsewhere.

## The Ones Below

There is something under the Well-Eye. The evidence is not seen but felt: the pale threads that descend into it and do not return, the warmth that rises with each stroke, the way the drone deepens when the water above that black circle is stilled. Whatever lives there has never come up. The stroke is either its voice or the sound of its work, and the basin is its body or its instrument, or perhaps its shell.

The Ribs along the southern edge are warm, and damp, and curved like the frame of a great chest. They may be its earlier form, left behind like a molt. They may be its next. They are not decayed, and they are not dead. Put your palm on one and you will feel, very slightly, a movement like slow breathing.

The two buried domes are stranger still, and more like tools. One hums faintly, a steady note a fraction off the drone. The other blinks its ring of amber lights in a sequence that repeats but never quite closes, a sentence with the last word always changing. Something made these, and something answers them. Whether the answer comes from the wader, or from below, or from the hollows of the Reeds, no visitor has learned.

## The Empty Doorway

The Lintel stands apart, and the animals go around it. The ground inside is unmarked, without a frog track or a bird's print or a crawler's trail. It is the one place in the basin where the stroke arrives muffled, as though the arch held a smaller room inside it. One leg is finished and the other is a stub, and the space between them is waiting.

It is hard not to think that whatever the basin is making, it is making a way out, and that the animals know this and are keeping the threshold clear. The tallers do not build cairns near it. The drifters do not cross its shadow. Even the wader turns aside. It is as if the doorway were already occupied, or reserved for someone who has not yet arrived, or for something that is nearly finished growing and will step through when the last stone sets.

## What They Know

The frogs hurry, the old tallers tap on, and the cairns get shorter. The crawlers press on with their small exact labor, and the drifters descend each night to sing on the white spires, whether or not they understand what they are singing toward. The wader wades on. The thing below is silent between strokes.

Nothing here is a spectator. Each of them is a note in a piece that is close to its last bar, and they are all playing it to the end, in time, on the count.
