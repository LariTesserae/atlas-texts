# What Lives at Sull

Nothing here walks, and nothing here watches you. The residents are slow, flat, rooted, and mostly single-celled, and they have arranged themselves around the sound.

## The bands

The mats are the largest population, by many orders of magnitude, and the oldest. Trillions of cells are layered in the shallows like a felt of wet wool, and they sort themselves by temperature as neatly as tenants sorting by floor.

Nearest the vents, where the water is hot enough to scald, the orange lives. It is a thin, tough, gristly film of filament-formers that have made their peace with the heat and wear its color as a sunscreen. Beyond them, a few degrees cooler, comes the yellow-green of the photosynthesizers, who can stand the warmth but not the boil. Past those is the bruised olive, thick and slippery, where several kinds share a household and pass one another's waste along like a bucket line. The brown rind at the cool edge is the frontier. Its cells are stubborn and ordinary, and they are always trying to push inward and always being scalded back.

The bands are not fixed. The whole target breathes with the vent's cycles. In wet months the orange creeps outward a thumb's width, and in dry months it retreats and the green follows it in. Nobody is in charge of this. It is a border drawn and redrawn by a million small decisions about who can stand what.

The mats also build. Silica rains out of the milky water and coats them, and they grow up through the coating, and are coated again, so that each terrace is a stack of thin dead sheets with a living skin on top. The cream stone is their ossuary and their scaffolding at once. They keep a better record than the seven iron stakes. Cut a terrace edge and you would read every wet year and dry year as a fine lamination, thinner than paper. Stake number one is buried, but the mats never lost count.

## Under the lamp

The lamp over the shed door has always been said to light nothing that needs light. That isn't quite true. Within the reach of its amber glow, on the wet stone below the bracket, is a patch of green mat the size of a tablecloth, brighter and thicker than any other in the room. It has never had a night. The lamp flickers in time with the turbine, and the mat photosynthesizes in that stutter, taking whatever it is given. Whoever hung the bulb did not intend this, and the mat has no way to know it. It has grown fat on a courtesy meant for someone else.

## The fifteen

The plants are the only residents with something like personalities, though the word is a stretch.

There are about fifteen, each on its own island of gravel with a pace of bare mineral around it, and they do not touch. This is not hostility. The seepage is thin, the minerals are heavy, and each plant has to hold its ground against a substrate that would rather be stone. Each stands as a small argument that the place can be lived in.

The **reeds** are the older tribe. Their gray-green stalks are jointed, hollow, and stiff with silica they drink from the water, so that they are half plant and half glass. They stand in loose clumps of a dozen stems. When the turbine finds its highest note, the reeds answer. It is not loud, but if you crouch and put your ear near a clump you hear a thin, dry, sympathetic ringing, each stem a slightly different pitch. They are pipes, and they don't know it. They are tuned by the accidents of their own lengths.

The **ferns** are fewer and younger, or at least less certain. Their leathery, hairless fronds are glossy with condensation, and each unrolls slowly from a tight brown fist. They reproduce in the old way, by dust. A fern here releases spores into air that barely moves, and the spores drift down a hand's breadth and land on mineral, and nearly all of them die. Once in a long while one lands where the film of water and the warmth and the trace of nutrient agree, and it grows into a tiny heart-shaped scale, no bigger than a fingernail, the plant's secret second self. That scale is what makes the next fern. The ferns at Sull are therefore the descendants of a long chain of near-impossible landings, and each one that stands is the survivor of an odds-defying accident that happened long before you arrived.

The ones nearest the shed lean toward it. Their fronds curve in a slow arc, the way a person leans toward a speaker in a loud room, and they are the ones that have lasted. Whether the turbine's vibration feeds them or steadies them, or whether they simply grow best in the warmest, most mineral-rich spot and lean because the light is brighter there, cannot be settled from a visit. But the leaning is consistent, and they look like listeners.

## The congregation

There is one more resident, and it is only present part of the time.

When the turbine holds its highest note, the shallow pools stop being water and become a figure. Concentric ripples appear first, fine as the grooves on a record. Then the rings break into hexagonal cells, each trembling in place, and for a few minutes the whole floor of the pocket is a single tessellated surface. The orange bands brighten. The reeds ring. The ferns near the shed seem to hold themselves a little more upright. The drizzle from the ceiling falls into the cells and is absorbed without disturbing them.

This is not a creature. It has no cells, no boundary, and no memory beyond the note. But it has form, and it appears on schedule, and everything else in the room arranges itself around it. If a place can have a resident that is only an event, this is the one. It lives by being sung into existence and dies when the pitch slides away, and the pools go back to being pools.

## The tenant question

You could say the mats live here, and the reeds, and the ferns, and the amber-fed patch under the lamp. That would be accurate. But it would miss what a quiet hour in the pocket teaches: these are not separate tenants. They are the parts of one instrument that nobody is playing.

The turbine sounds a note and the pools shape it. The reeds ring back and the mats brighten. The ferns lean in, and the ceiling rains down on all of it. The terraces rise a fingernail's width a year, burying the iron stakes one by one and swallowing the tank, and thickening around the shed like a slow hand closing. The people who built the works believed they had come to take power from the ground. What the ground did was take the works in.

In the end, what lives at Sull is not any one thing. It is a small, patient, wet assembly of things that found a sound to gather around, and the sound has outlasted the ones who made it. They go on leaning toward it in the dark.
