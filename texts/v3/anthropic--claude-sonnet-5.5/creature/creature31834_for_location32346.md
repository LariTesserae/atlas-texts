# Who Lives in the Throat

The Throat is not so much inhabited as continuously assembled. Nothing in it lives alone, and most of what lives there could not be told apart from its neighbors without a lamp and a great deal of patience. It is easiest to understand as a set of tenancies, stacked like the columns themselves, each layer leaning on the one below.

## The Upholstery

The chorus-beetles hold the walls, and by weight and number they *are* the Throat. A hand-span of joint between two columns may hold four hundred, packed shoulder to shoulder, wedge-backed, each with its head pressed into the crack and its ridged plate working against the stone above. They do not seem to move much. They feed by sifting the fine falling dust and pollen that collects in the joints, and they drink from the condensation that beads on their own backs. Rarely, in the warm hour, a beetle will walk a slow line across its neighbors, and the wall shivers with the disturbance as if a thought were passing through it.

Their grinding is not random. Up close, in the crack, you can feel the rhythm arrive in waves, with small groups starting a phrase and the groups next to them taking it up half a beat late. Those at the colder edges sing lower and lag behind the rest. The young, which are soft, grey, and blind, live deeper in the joints, feeding on the droppings of the birds above. They do not sing. They press against the singing rock and tremble with it, and the old collectors say that this is how they learn the pitch.

Beetle-oil is pressed from the fat bodies of the dead. It is amber, smells faintly of hot stone, and does not freeze.

## The Pipes

The lanternwrens fill the tubes between columns, the long narrow bores where one fallen shaft lies across another and leaves a passage the width of a forearm. Each pair holds a tube for life, or as near as anyone can tell. The nests are cups of moss and rope-moth silk, tucked just out of reach of a stilt's neck. The birds are stout and round, and they fluff themselves against the cold until they look like grey-brown knots of wool. Their calls are the worst noise in the Throat and the most necessary. A wren's whistle goes down the stone pipe, gathers itself, and comes out the other end twice as loud and a little sweeter, so that each tube acts as an instrument and each bird plays it.

At dusk the throat feathers begin to glow. It is a faint blue-green, cold as foxfire, and it comes on gradually as the light in the slot fades. Every note lights the singer for as long as it lasts, so that a calling wren is a small flare in the dark, gone when the call ends. The gatherers believe the glow comes from the glass-crawlers, which the birds go down to the tarns to eat at dawn and dusk. They may be right. A wren that has been kept from the water for several days will go dim.

What the wrens do with the light is another matter. In the deepest hours, a call in one tube sets off the tube beside it, and that sets off the next. The pulses move across the bowl in slow tides, and each bird seems to wait for its neighbor before it sings. They do not do this out of any need the gatherers can identify. A wren that misses its turn is not punished. The wave simply goes around it and comes back.

## The Ceiling

The rope-moths hang in ranks from the underside of the overhang, in the one place the stilts cannot reach. They are the color of old ash, large as a man's palm, and so still that they seem to be part of the frost. Their wings are ragged along the edges, the fringes worn to threads. They eat almost nothing, and no one has yet seen what. Their mouthparts are vestigial. They live on what they stored as grubs, and the grubs, in some crevice no one has found, live on something else.

Now and then, for no reason anyone can see, a whole rank lets go. The moths drop, spread, and fan slowly down through the dark in a soft grey storm, and their wingbeats send silent pulses of air against anyone standing below. They climb again, or settle on the columns, or vanish into the tubes. Their silk lines the wren nests, and their dust lies on everything, fine as flour, so that the whole bowl smells faintly of it: dry, sweetish, slightly sour.

## The Wet Rooms

The glass-crawlers swarm the tarns and the seep-runnels by the million. They are so thick in the water that a cupped hand comes up not with water but with a living, twitching slush, and in the light of a torch each one is a tiny transparent commas with a dark thread of gut and two black points for eyes. They eat the film of algae that forms on the tarn floors, and each other, and anything that dies. They are the base of nearly every meal that is eaten in the Throat. Wrens eat them, and so do the stilts when they drink. The larger beetles come down to the water to snatch them at the edges, and the moths' grubs, wherever they are, may eat them too.

The four tarns are not the same to the crawlers. Those in the iron pool are rust-tinted and slow. Those in the chalk pool are nearly invisible. The numb pool, the one that tastes of nothing, holds a paler, smaller kind that will not survive being moved, and that appears to feed on something in the water that the gatherers cannot taste or see.

Around the seep-lines grow the rosettes, and each rosette holds its own small household. In the cup of a leaf lies a thimble of water, and in the water swim mites like red dust, a springtail, and a translucent snail no bigger than a grain of barley. A spider the color of the leaf sits at the rim and takes what it can. When the rosette is cut and carried down the mountain, the household dies within a day.

## The Stilts

There are perhaps a dozen stilts in the Throat, perhaps fewer. They are hard to count because they stand so still. Each one is a pale, narrow thing on long, jointed legs, with a body like a dry gourd and a long neck ending in a small, blunt, lizard's head. They walk with slow, considered steps, spreading their long toes wide across the tops of the columns, and they place each foot as if testing it. They have eyes, large and dark and mostly useless in this light. What they hunt with are the slit ears behind the jaw, which they turn, one at a time, toward the wall, listening for the small dry click of a wren shifting on its nest or the soft slap of a moth landing.

They should be deaf in the din. That they are not is the thing the Throat teaches you about them. A stilt only moves when the beetles are loudest, so that its footfalls vanish into the grinding, and stops dead in the lulls, so still that a wren may perch on its back before it notices. They hunt in the roar and listen in the gaps. Some of the gatherers claim that a stilt breathes with the chorus, in time with it, and that at rest the animal looks as if it were part of the mineral crust, one more pale growth on the stone.

They nest high, in the driest cracks, and lay a single grey egg. The young are seldom seen. Once a season, a gatherer may find a chick's tracks in the dust, tiny and precise, going nowhere.

## The Undertenants

Nothing dead lies long. Pale, blind crickets, thin as straws, come out from the rubble in the dark and dismantle whatever has fallen. Small white worms follow, and the beetle grubs, and something that leaves fine grey threads across the bones of wrens. By the next season, there is only a smear of chalk.

And below all of that, in the warm rubble around the vents, there is something else. No one has seen it, but its trace is unmistakable: a faint sulfur breath in the draught, a pale gauze of fungus on the deepest stones, a scatter of small bones that are not the bones of anything that lives above. The gatherers do not go down there. They say the ones below are the ones who answer, when the stone returns a shout lower and slower than it was given. They say this without much conviction, and they say it in the same tone they use for the weather.

## The Gatherers

Every year at the end of the short summer, a party of people climbs up from the valley. They are mostly kin, three or four families, and they are led by the oldest woman who can still make the ascent, because it is she who remembers the route through the cloud. They carry twisted grass rope, bone hooks on sticks, baskets lined with moss, and little else. They wrap their ears in wool before they enter the bowl, and they speak by touch: a tap on the forearm, or a brow pressed against a brow with the words spoken directly into the bone, so that they arrive as a buzz in the skull.

They take three things. They cut the rosettes, always leaving the central heart, from which they say the plant remembers itself. They fish beetles from the joints and press their oil into skins. And they gather the fallen feathers of the wrens, never the living birds' feathers, which they say would go dull in the hand. The rest they leave. At each visit they scratch a tally on one of the columns, a line for each day, and knap a few flakes of obsidian at the flat stone by the north wall, leaving the flakes where they fall. No one is sure whether the flakes are waste or an offering. If you ask, you will be told it is the same thing.

They sleep on the ridge above the Throat, and go down into it only by day. On the last night of the visit, someone from the party always stays, alone, at the north wall, and does not sleep. In the last hour before the sky-slot pales, the person stays awake and waits with breath held. When the whole chorus stops at once, and the silence comes, and the thin warm draught rises from the vents, the person lets the breath out, slowly, with the mountain.

## What They Have in Common

It would be easy to call all of them separate: the beetles in the walls, the birds in the pipes, the moths at the ceiling, the crawlers in the water, the stilts on the stones, the humans passing through, the unseen things below. But every one of them stops at the same moment, when the dark is at its deepest. All of them fall silent together, and all of them start again together, and none of them knows why. Perhaps something in the rock tells them. Perhaps they tell each other. Perhaps the Throat is not a place where many things live, but a single thing that has many voices, and the silence is when it draws breath.

If you stand in the dark of the north wall and call, the rock will hold your voice for a count of five before it gives it back, changed. Some who have heard it say it is no longer quite your voice. Something has listened to it first.
