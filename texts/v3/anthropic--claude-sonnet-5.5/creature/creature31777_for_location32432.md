# Who Lives on the Drowned Shelf

## The First Tenants

The oldest residents are the ones you take for upholstery. The bacterial mats came before the forest and prepared the ground for it, and they still hold it. They are layered like pastry: a violet skin on top that drinks the light, a green stratum beneath that drinks what the violet lets through, and below that a rust-colored band that lives on the vent water alone, on sulfur and iron, in permanent dusk. A thumbnail of mat holds more individuals than there are people in the lowland cities the Shelf has never heard of. They do not move, or they move as a tide moves, by being more in one place this year and less in another.

They are also the reason the water smells faintly of struck flint and warm coins to anything that can smell it. Every other resident lives inside their chemistry.

## The Standing Ones

The reed-trees are the largest inhabitants, and the plainest word for what they do is *keep house*. Their stands hold the water still enough for silt to settle, shade the ferns from the brightest light, and give the snails a place to climb. Each stalk lives perhaps forty years. Because the bladders are filled and emptied daily, a reed-tree has done something like fifteen thousand breaths by the time it dies. It dies standing, and it stays standing a long while afterward, gray and hollow, until a bladder-knot rots through and the whole length lowers itself into the ferns without a sound. Where a big one has fallen, a clearing opens, and in the clearing the light comes down in a column. Within a season the clearing is a nursery of seedlings competing for it.

Veil-ferns are shorter-lived and more mercurial. They are the ones that flush red. Up close it is not a dramatic thing: a pigment migrating outward through the fronds, a slow flooding, each cell passing the color to the next like a whisper down a line. If you lay a hand on a fern in the middle of the change you feel nothing, but the fronds on either side of your hand will already have turned, and the ones beyond will follow. The change does not seem to care about you, or the current, or the light. It moves at the speed of something being *told*.

The stone-seeds are the shy pioneers, and each is an act of small violence. A seed lands on black glass and secretes an acid so mild it takes a year to make a pit the width of a fingertip. The pit is exactly round and exactly as deep as the seed's root is long. In time the pits merge and the glass becomes lace. The stone-seeds die in the lace they made, and the ferns and mosses root in what they leave. They will never see the forest that follows them.

## The Grazers

The pale eel-things are called grazers, though what they do is closer to *tasting the forest for news*. They are about an arm long, blind or nearly so, with a soft mouth ringed in fine bristles. They drift along the ferns in twos and threes, lay the bristles against a frond, and hold there, sometimes for many minutes, before moving on. They eat very little. Most of what they take in seems to be information: a grazer that has touched a fern in the early hours of a red tide will move, afterward, in straighter lines and with more purpose, as if it had been given directions.

They are slow, gentle, and unafraid, and mostly nothing hunts them. They breed in the warmest fissures, where the water is almost too hot to tolerate, and lay strings of eggs in the silica chimneys. The hatchlings are not pale but nearly black, and they spend their first weeks in the sand.

## The Stone-Colored

The armored many-legged things are of the same color as the rock because they are, in a sense, made of it: each grows its shell from the silica and basalt dust it eats, and the shell takes the tone of whatever flow it was born on. A rust-colored one that wanders onto jade is visible for a season, until it molts and comes out jade. They walk with a deliberate, rocking gait on eight or ten legs, and they are the closest thing the Shelf has to a predator, though what they hunt is mites and thread-worms, and what they mostly do is *stand still*. A stone-colored animal can stand motionless for four days. They are not sleeping. They are listening, through their legs, to the stone.

They are the ones who hear the groan from the caldera before it comes. A day before, the whole population turns at once in the same direction, and every leg goes still.

## The Small Ones

Everything else is small, and everything else is nearly everyone. The pinprick swimmers cluster around the gas bladders in shimmering haloes, feeding on the film of bacteria that gathers there. When the bladders release at evening the haloes are scattered and reassemble within minutes. Snails ride the reed-stems, grazing the algae, and leave in their wake fine tracks that catch the light. Mites work the mats. Filter-feeders comb the plume-edges with fans finer than eyelashes. A cupful of water from above the cushion-mosses holds more distinct kinds of life than the sea beyond the cliffs holds species of fish.

None of them is ever alone, and none of them is ever more than a few thousand of its own kind from a neighbor. They live at density, in soft crowds, and their whole world smells of one another.

## The Glider

Once a season the sheet-animal comes across the light. It enters over the eastern rim and crosses the plateau in a little under an hour, a translucent square of living membrane a body-length wide. It is so thin the sunlight passes through with hardly a change, so the shadow it casts is faint and colorless, but the threads it trails are longer than the animal itself, and they sweep the upper water in slow arcs. Everything below turns to watch, or seems to. The pinprick swimmers dive. The grazers stop tasting. For a few minutes the reed-trees stand as still as they ever stand.

No one has seen it eat, or breed, or die. It comes, and it goes over the western rim, and the forest resumes. It may be the only creature on the Shelf that visits from somewhere else.

## The Combers

Then there are the ones in the sand.

The ripples are the only evidence: long, regular, hand-high ridges in the pale drifts, curving in whorls around the vents and made by no current. In the newest drifts, they are sharp. In the older ones they are worn nearly smooth, and then re-cut, sharp again, as if the sand had been raked over during the night.

Whatever does this lives under the sand and comes to the surface only in the dark hours, if it comes at all. From the few times something has been felt, it is not large. It is not an eel or a shell-bearer. It is something *threaded*. Digging into a drift beside a caldera, one finds a fine white mesh running through the sand a hand's depth down, thin as hair and thick as felt, and it goes everywhere. It runs beneath the ferns and up into their roots. It runs under the boulders. It enters the fissures and takes heat from them. Cut it and it bleeds nothing, but the sand shudders in its rows for an hour after, and the ferns nearby, the next day, flush pink at their tips.

The comb-ridges are not made with a tool. They are what the sand does when the threads underneath it tighten and relax in sequence: a slow peristalsis, wave after wave, passing under the surface the way a thought passes through a body. The grazers, tasting the ferns, are tasting the messages it has left there. The red tides are its speech. The rings around the lone boulders are its manners, a courteous clearing, a spacing it prefers. The spore-clouds after the tides are its one great act of giving, a snow of the forest's own seed sent up and out to whatever will have it.

It is not an animal and not a plant, and not a colony in the way the sponges are colonies. It may be the forest itself, or the part of the forest that remembers. It has no face and no name, and it is sixty kilometers across, or it is nothing but a rumor of pale threads, and both are equally likely. It does not know it is the oldest thing on the plateau. It doesn't know the plateau is young. It is a slow attention, moving through the sand toward warmth, toward light, toward the low groan from the caldera which it may have learned to answer.

## On the Black Islands

At the rim, where the peaks break into air, a different small society lives on the spray-wet stone: low mosses tough as leather, fern-mats that shrivel and revive with every wave of mist, and a scatter of wingless, dark-shelled insects that graze the mosses, having crossed the surface film long ago in some forgotten flotation of eggs. They live in cold, thin light, and they see the sky, the only ones on the Shelf who do. They have no idea of the lake beneath them. They know only that the stone is wet and the wind is sharp, and that at dusk, offshore, ropes of bubbles break the surface in a soft rain, and the water for a moment smells of warm iron, and something under it seems to breathe out.

They have no word for it, and they do not need one. They lift their heads. Then the wind takes the smell, and they go back to the moss.
