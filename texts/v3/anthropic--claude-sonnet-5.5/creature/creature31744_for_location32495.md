# The Lodgers of the Flats

You will not see them at first. The plumes and the haze hide them, and they have no reason to stand up and be seen. What you notice is a small, wrong regularity in the landscape: a low dome the same cream as the crust, with a thread of steam rising from a hole in its crown that isn't a vent. Then another dome, and another, nine or ten in all, set along the dry side of a terrace lip, just outside the arc of the drizzle. The ground under them is warm enough that nothing is ever lit. Cooking happens in the pools.

They call themselves nothing you could say aloud over the roar. Outsiders who have passed through, gull-hunters and salt-traders, call them the Lodgers, because they act like people who rent a room in a house so large that they have stopped asking who owns it.

## Bodies

They are a pale, close-made people, and most of them are small. The glow of the plumes gives them a permanent squint, and the skin at the corners of their eyes is creased as if by a smile they aren't making. The air is wet, and the mineral mist has made their hair stiff. The older ones wear it in short crusted spikes with a faint white bloom, like a stone left in a kettle. Their teeth are good. Their knuckles are enlarged from years of tapping on rock.

They dress in felt. On the cool terraces the pale microbial ropes hang from the sinter lips like wax, and the Lodgers cut them, wash them in a mid-warm pool, and press them flat under stones until they dry into a stiff, waxy sheet, faintly orange or faintly green. It smells of nothing once it is cured. Cut and stitched with gull sinew, it makes a serviceable coat that keeps out the drizzle. The colour stays. A family that works the lime-green terraces will be green at the elbows all its life.

They move with a peculiar economy. There is no hurry in it and no idling either. If you watch a Lodger cross the ring of Stacks, you will see that she doesn't hesitate at the edge. She walks in as the last plume in the sequence is falling, passes between two chimneys in the interval before the wave returns, and is out the far side before the next *chuff* has gone off. She isn't counting. Her feet know the wave. Children learn the crossing before they learn to speak, held by the wrist and carried through the gaps, then walked through them, then let go.

## The Speech of Close Distances

A voice is no use here. It leaves the mouth and is swallowed by the roar within an arm's length. So the Lodgers talk with their hands, and with their mouths cupped at each other's ears, and no other way. All speech is private. There are no announcements, no calling across a pool, no shouted warnings. To say anything to anyone you have to walk up to them and touch them. The result is a people who are unusually gentle in their bearing, since every word they've ever received arrived with a hand on the shoulder and someone's breath against the side of the head.

They also talk in the ground. A flat palm laid on a certain terrace carries a knock through the crust that can be read a hundred paces off, and the sinter has its own long vowels. But this is for emergencies only: a child gone past the ring, a gull-egg cache raided, a stranger. Ordinary talk is close, warm, and quiet even in the middle of the loudest thing on earth.

A sentence, for them, ends on the Drum. Not always, but properly. A person who has something to say lifts a hand, waits for the *thud* to pass through the sternum, and begins in the eleven seconds that follow. Long tellings are counted out in strokes. A story is "four strokes long" or "a full day of strokes." They have no other unit of duration. If you ask a Lodger how old she is, she may put her palm flat on your chest and press it once, rhythmically, meaning: *the same as the Drum, and older*.

## What They Do

The engine needs nothing. That is the first thing to understand, and the hardest for a visitor to accept. Nobody tends the Stacks. Nobody clears the throats or greases the Drum's flap. The governor governs. The Lodgers have no work to do to keep the world in order, because the world was ordered before they arrived and will be after they go.

So their days are made of ordinary small matters. They gather the pale ropes and cure them. They net the grain-sized grazers out of the mid-warm pools with felt scoops, dry them, and grind them into a grey-green flour that tastes of iron and salt. They know which basin cooks a gull's egg to a soft set and which to a hard one, and the pools are named accordingly: *Soft, Hard, Slow, Bitter*. They collect the sinter itself, carve it into bowls, and rub the bowls smooth with their palms. In the cool afternoons they walk out to the gull-edges, the cool margins where the grey birds stand with beaks opening on silence, and they move among them without fear, because there are gulls enough and the birds have never learned to hate them.

Part of every day goes to the drizzle. There is an arc of ground where the cooled exhaust falls, always on the same line, and the Lodgers stand in it as a matter of routine, faces up, eyes shut, letting the warm mist bead on their skin. It smells of flint and boiled eggs, and to them it smells of home. Their children are washed in it. Their dead are, in a sense, washed in it too.

## The Dead

There is a basin on the eastern terraces, long and shaped like a leaf, where the water runs hardest with silica. The Lodgers lay their dead in it, wrapped in cured felt, with the face uncovered. Over some years the rim knobs grow inward and the water clouds, and eventually the person is a soft white swelling under the surface, then a shape under crust, then just a slight rise in a terrace lip. The Flats do not hurry. Nobody has seen the process finish, since the oldest laid-down are older than any grandmother's grandmother could account for. But the terraces along that side are a little rounder than the others, and a Lodger walking there will step carefully and touch her chest, once, with two fingers.

The same crust is digesting the boiler on the northern margin, and the Lodgers make no distinction. All that lies in the Flats becomes the Flats.

## The Rivets

This is the one thing the Lodgers keep that is not part of the pattern. It is, in fact, a kind of history.

On the old boiler drum by the slanted casing, the mineral has been rising for centuries, and the row of rivet-heads that once ran along its seam has been going under it one at a time. A child is taken to the boiler at a certain age, her hand is laid on the rivets, and she is told the number. It is always some number in the low sixties. Every few generations it changes. A rivet vanishes under the sinter, and a new number is passed down.

The keeper of the rivets is chosen from those who show a talent for stillness. She checks the row each season with the pad of her thumb. When one is lost, the whole community gathers, which is itself an event, since they otherwise never gather at all. Each person in turn goes to the boiler and puts an ear against it, and it rings in sympathy with the Drum. Then they go home. It is the nearest thing they have to news.

Some of the Lodgers privately dread the last rivet. Others long for it. Nobody says why. To speak of it, you would have to lay a hand on someone's shoulder and press your lips to their ear, and the words that come out would then be private forever, which is a lot to ask of a word.

## The Loose Bearing

Every so often a child is born whose heart will not fall in with the Drum. It skips, or runs quick, or lags by a hair. Elsewhere this would be a fault. Here it is a sort of vocation.

The Lodgers call such a person a *bearing*, after the loose knock in the ground. The bearings are not cast out, and they are not honoured either. They are given the edges. They tend the gull-margins, walk the long routes to the far coast, deal with the salt-traders and the drifters who wander in, and carry the news of the outside world back over the terraces, hand to shoulder, ear to mouth. Their off-time bodies can bear the trader's speech, the shouting and the changing weather. They are the Flats' only link with any place where things happen.

Most bearings eventually settle. A few leave and do not return. Those who come back have a look about them: they walk slowly and cover their ears with their palms, and then, when the Drum thuds through the ground into their feet, they stop and shut their eyes and weep. It is the silence outside, they say, which is unbearable. It makes a noise of its own. For months after leaving, they heard the Drum in it. Then they stopped hearing it, and that was worse.

## Visitors

Sometimes a stranger comes, lean and hollow-eyed, who cannot forget the world before the basin. The Lodgers watch such a person cross the terraces with a courteous, unhurried interest. They let them sit at the cool pools. They let them go and press an ear to the boiler.

And the stranger listens, and weeps or doesn't, and stays a day or a season or a year. If they stay long enough, and few do, a Lodger will walk up beside them one afternoon and lay a hand between their shoulder blades and say nothing. There is nothing to say. But the hand rests there for eleven seconds, then eleven more, and the stranger, whose heart has been fighting the Drum, feels it fall in.

That is how a Lodger is made.

## What They Are

They are not guardians of the machine, or worshippers of it, or its prisoners. They are the small warm things that have learned to live in a very large, very old clock, and have decided that this is not a diminishment. They are the only part of the Flats that ages, and they are proud of it in a quiet way. Everything here turns as it always has, and they alone are permitted to be late, and early, and loose, and to end.

They do end, and they know it. It is the only thing in the basin that the Drum doesn't govern. In the eleven seconds after a Lodger dies, the others gather round her with their hands on her shoulders, and wait for the thud, and let it go through them all.
