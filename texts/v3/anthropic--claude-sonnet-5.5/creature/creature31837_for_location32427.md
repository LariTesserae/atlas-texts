# The Tenants of the Drowned Bench

Nothing lives here, at the scale you are used to. No eye in the basin will turn to follow you and nothing will flee. But the water is not empty, and the stillness is not unattended. There are three kinds of tenant, and they differ mostly in how long they take to answer you.

## The makers of rust

The first tenants are the reason the walls are the color they are. They are single cells, so small that a thousand across would not span the width of a stonewort joint, and they live in the orange felt itself. They are iron-eaters. They take the dissolved metal that seeps from the ore, pass it through their bodies, and set it down again as rust, one grain at a time, in the stalks and ribbons that make up the felt's soft, scalloped shelves. The felt is not a deposit. It is a slow excretion, a century of small digestions.

They do not know the winch house from the wall. The iron frame is simply a richer vein, and they have thickened over it and softened its corners because it feeds them well. Wherever the felt is heaviest, they are most at home. Wherever it is thin, on the vertical faces, they are sparse and hungry and take their time.

Among them move a few larger, stranger cells, single-celled algae that have made peace with the acid. They carry green in their bodies and a faint oil-sheen on their surfaces, and this is the gleam you catch in the filament lawn on the sunny ledges. Around them, in the floc, drift a handful of rotifers, each a glassy wheel-mouthed speck that turns and turns in a drop of water and eats the algae and the bacteria without hurry. You would need a lens and a lamp to find them. They are the whole of the basin's small-animal life, and there are perhaps a few thousand of them in the entire bowl, each one alone in a volume of water the size of a room.

## The gardeners' rows

The second tenant is not a creature but an arrangement, and it is easy to mistake for one. The stonewort tufts stand a hand's width across and spaced far apart, like plantings in a monastery garden. The neat line of stonewort along the top of each rail is too straight to be chance, though chance is the whole explanation: the rail's metal edge holds a thin seam of cleaner water where the felt cannot settle, and the spores that fall there find bare purchase and take. Something took hold along a line of iron, and the line became a row, and the row became a habit. Nobody planted it. But the basin is the sort of place where accident, given a thousand years and no interruption, comes to look exactly like care.

The moss rosettes on the boulder tops follow the same logic. They cling to the cleanest, highest surfaces, and it looks like learned caution, though what they have really learned is only the arithmetic of light and silt. The bucket-vase, with its rim of rust and its cushion of moss, is the pleasantest of these accidents, and the most deceptive. You will want to think someone arranged it.

## The clear one

The third tenant is the one you will not see until you have been there for a long time, and then you will see it only as a fault in the view.

It is a body of water that has become somebody. Where a groundwater seep rises from its pinhole in the silt, the bead of colder water spreads into a thin lens and flattens out, and the lens seems to look back like a lidded eye. That is not a trick of the light. It is the way the clear one looks. Its body is made of a very cold, very pure jelly, nearly the same density and the same refractive index as the water around it. It is a sheet a few paces across and a hand thick, or it is a slow column, or it is a pair of joined lobes; it changes, but not quickly. You find it only by the way the rust-colored floc bends around it, and by the way the ripple-nets on the benches seem, for a moment, to pass through a window that is very slightly the wrong shape.

It lives on the seeps. The water that rises from the rock carries dissolved iron and sulfur in a more raw, more energetic form than anything in the general stillness, and the clear one takes it in through its whole surface, as a leaf takes light. It is a chemical grazer, and the seeps are its pasture. This is why the white crusts around each pinhole are so neat and so thin: it eats at their edges, keeping them from growing thick.

It moves, but it never touches the floor. This is the first thing you will understand about it, and it explains the plain of wet-cement silt that holds the groove and the scattered stones and has never been crawled across by anything at all. The clear one hangs a hand above the sediment, neutrally buoyant, and travels by the slightest changes in its own density, sinking a little here and rising a little there, so that it drifts from seep to seep the way a cloud shadow crosses a field. Its speed is a pace or two in a day. It has never left a footprint, and it has never disturbed a grain.

It does, however, gather up disturbances, and this is the second thing you will understand.

## What it does about you

When you enter the basin, you shed floc. Your fins or your fingers lift the felt, the rust-colored threads hang where they were left, and the still water holds your passing like handwriting. The clear one notices this within the hour. It cannot see you; it has nothing that would do for an eye, except the lens-shape at its edge that catches light the way a pupil does. But it feels the change in the water, the sudden richness of suspended iron, and it comes, at its own pace, and drifts through your threads and takes them in. Over the course of your stay, the disturbance you leave grows smaller, and it is not settling alone that does this.

That is why the basin looks, when you leave, as if no one had come. You had thought the water closed over you by itself. It does, but with help.

It is not kind, and it is not hostile. It has no interest in you as a visitor. Your floc is the richest meal it will have in a decade, and it eats the way a tide comes in. If you hold very still, and let a thread drift, you may watch the thread thin and vanish at one end, drawn into a place where the water is slightly denser, slightly colder, slightly closer to a thought.

## The knock

Sometimes, in the long hush, you hear the low, muffled knock like a distant door, a slab of crust letting go of a wall and settling a hand's width lower. Most of these are only the crust. But not all. The clear one, when it is startled, or when it turns, or when it has been sitting too long against a warm patch of iron and pulls itself free, will make the same sound, because it is a soft body against a hard one and the water carries the shock a long way. It is the only voice the basin has, and it is used almost never.

## Where it rests

It has a favorite place, and it is the pump housing. The squat casing lies on its side in the lowest corner, one end open like a mouth, the interior filled with pleated mineral like the inside of a shell. Something has always seemed to be at home in there. The seeps are strongest at the low corner, and the pleats have made a warm hollow of it, and the clear one lies in the open end of the casing for weeks at a time, its body filling the opening so exactly that the mouth of the machine seems, from a distance, to be glazed over with a pane of green glass.

It does not know what the pump was. It does not know that its failure is the origin of everything here: the flooding, the century of leaching, the acid that killed the small things, the clarity that lets you see sixty paces. It only knows that this is where the water is coldest and richest, and that it is home, and that nothing has ever come to take it away.

Time has nothing to do in the basin, and so the clear one has nothing to do but be there. It is the one thing in the place that does that on purpose.
