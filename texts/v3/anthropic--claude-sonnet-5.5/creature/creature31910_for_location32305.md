# Those Who Keep the Beat

The first thing to say about the people is that they walk wrong, by the standards of anywhere else. They lift the knee a little too high and set the foot down flat and wide, then wait a half-breath for the ground to decide about them. Children learn it before they learn to talk. A visitor from the lowlands can be picked out across a crowded square by the way they stride, striking the paving as if it were going to answer back. In the cities the paving does answer, faintly, in the same slow *whump* as everything else. The people have stopped noticing. They stand a little wider than they need to, and their chests are deep and barrelled from the thin air, and their hands are stained at the cuticle with a permanent green that no lye touches.

They are not one people. They are the descendants of surveyors, boilermakers, peat-diggers, deserters, and a religious order that came up for the isolation and stayed for the pumps. But two centuries of the same air and the same din have made them alike in ways they would deny. They lean close when they talk. They touch a sleeve before speaking. They sleep badly in silence, and on the rare night when a pump is shut down for repair, whole districts lie awake without knowing why, and by morning the tea shops are full of people who look faintly seasick.

## The Hammermen

The pump-tenders are the aristocracy, though no one calls them that. They hold their shifts in families, and they are known by the pump they serve, not by name. A woman is *Third Beam, Ashgate*, and her son will be *Third Beam* after her. They oil, they listen, they lay a palm on the great cylinder housings the way a farmer lays a hand on a horse. Most of them go a little deaf in the ordinary way, and a little more sensitive in another way that has no name. They can tell by the vibration in a floor whether a distant pump is a hair off its stroke. Some claim to feel a stoppage a full minute before it happens.

Hammermen do not sing at work, but they hum, one low note held under the machinery, and each city's crews hum a different pitch. In the eastern cities, where the sound arrives before the piston moves, the tenders have learned to work by the heard beat and ignore what their eyes report. They say that trusting the eye is how you lose a hand. They sit with their backs to the beam.

## The Second Ledger

Every city keeps clerks called the Reconcilers, though they reconcile nothing. They keep two ledgers, the Seen and the Heard, in parallel columns on ruled paper, and they are careful never to draw a line between the columns. A rumour says that the one clerk who did once, in a fit of tidiness, was found the next morning sitting quietly at his desk, entirely well, unable to tell whether it was that morning or the previous one. He was pensioned. The ledgers were re-ruled.

Because counting is dangerous, the clerks use tallies of matched things: a pebble laid beside each peat block, a bead moved along a wire for each stack, and a second person doing the same, and then the two arrangements compared by eye. If they look alike, the count is accepted. Nobody ever says the number aloud. Ask a baker how many loaves and she will say *a rack, and a bit*, and you will know exactly what she means. Ask a census-taker and he will simply point, sweeping his arm slowly across the street, and write down something private.

Coachmen charge for both directions of any road, since one way takes a day and a half and the other two hours and nobody can predict which is which. The coachmen have a saying: *the road knows who's paying.*

## The Cutters

Out beyond the drying racks, where the mat is deep and the smoke has soaked into the roots, live the peat-cutters. They spend weeks on the open plain, strapped into flat woven sedge shoes as wide as trays, moving in slow lines with long-handled spades. They cut blocks, stack them on sledges, and pull them back across the tide. They are quieter than the city people, and lean, and walk with no lift in the knee, more of a glide. They read the ridges of the mat the way sailors read swell. They know when a column of upward rain is coming by the smell, which is like a struck flint, and they make camp at the edge of the wet ring and lie down in it, faces turned to the sky, and let the drenched moss soak into their clothes. It is considered good luck and, more to the point, it is the only way they get clean.

The cutters do not count either, but they have a vocabulary of stacked stones. At each camp a cairn grows of small granite chips carried from the domes. No one knows what the cairns record. If you ask, a cutter will touch the top stone and say, *This is how tall it was.*

Sometimes, cutting deep, they hit something in the old layers: a dark seam, a smell like cellars, a patch where the peat has arranged itself into a fine, almost woven texture. They stop when that happens. They close the cut and pack it with fresh moss, and they never dig that line again. The company has never asked them to.

## The Landlords

Ask anyone in the cities who lives on the plateau and they will say the trees, and they mean it precisely. The great woody sedges are not scenery. They are the ones who let the cities stand. The people speak of them the way tenants speak of a distant, patient landlord, with wariness and a kind of affection. Rent is paid in iron. Every spring, children go from trunk to trunk with bags of nails and old bolts, pressing them into the fluted bark. The trees close over them by autumn. Nobody has told them to do it; it is simply the custom, and the wood is tinted rust-red where the offerings have been longest.

The trees do have a pulse. Put an ear to the bark in the small hours and you can hear the sap rising, a slow wet ticking, and it does not match the pumps. It runs a fraction behind, and in the eastern cities a fraction ahead. The old women who sweep the plazas say the trees are keeping their own ledger.

## Small Lives

The pale grazers cross the plain with an unhurried, folding gait, each leg unhinging in one joint too many. They never seem to sink. They stop sometimes in the shadow of a dome and stand for hours, unmoving, their breath making soft plumes. The cutters leave them alone and the grazers return the courtesy. Occasionally one wanders into a city's outskirts at dusk, walks down a street of iron and brick as if it were a meadow, and out the other side, and nobody stops it.

The moths cluster on foundry walls at night, hundreds of them, wings shut and lichen-grey, drinking the soot off the warm brick. When one is disturbed and opens its wings, the undersides are a violent, wet, luminous blue, and the whole wall flashes and settles. Children are forbidden to catch them. Children do anyway, and let them go, and carry the blue dust on their fingers for days.

And the bird. It is small and dark and unremarkable to look at, and it nests in the hollow chimney-trunks, in the pale fibre that will not burn. It sings two notes, a falling minor third, in the gap between one pump-stroke and the next. In the west it sings exactly in the gap. In the east it sings a little before the gap, so that the pump stroke seems to answer it. Hammermen of the eastern cities are known to stop work and listen, unashamed, and to say afterward that they didn't hear anything.

## The Long Lodgers

In the oldest districts, where the alleys run further than the buildings allow and the pump sound does not enter, people say there are lodgers. Nobody has met one. But rooms in those alleys are rented, and the rent is left in a tin on a windowsill, and in the morning the tin is empty and a folded square of grey cloth sits in its place. A lamp is lit in a window where there is no window. Washing hangs on a line in the still air, dry, perfectly dry, its shadows a little too long. Those who walk the alleys for the sake of the quiet report a feeling of being politely made room for.

It is possible the lodgers are only what happens when a place forgets its own dimensions. It is also possible they are the reason for the forgetting.

## The Ground

And beneath the cities, the trees, the trains, and the walkers, the mat itself, which is the oldest resident and the only one truly at home. It has been laying itself down since before there was a word for laying down. Every footprint fills back in. Every smoke-plume is absorbed. Every pump-stroke is a word spoken into it, and it has learned the sound. It tides, and the tides carry seed, and the seed finds the sooted places at the roots of the cities and grows there quietly. It is not hostile. It does not have to be.

The people know this without saying so. It is in how they set their feet down, and in the hum the hammermen keep under their breath, and in the two notes of the small dark bird. All of it is one long courteous answer to the ground, given in the hope that it will go on taking the answer as sufficient.
