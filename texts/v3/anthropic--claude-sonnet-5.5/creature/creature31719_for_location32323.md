# Who Lives on the Grey Floor

Mostly, the answer is *the very small, in enormous numbers*, and they are so much the character of the place that it's easy to pass through without noticing anyone lives here at all.

## The Multitude

Under the surface of the ooze, to the depth of a forearm, is a city that has never had a name for itself. Bristle worms in the thousands per square meter work the sediment like slow machinery, swallowing mud, stripping it of whatever the snow left in it, and passing it out again in coils. Tiny crustaceans the size of rice grains live in the burrows the worms leave behind. Nematodes, too thin to see, thread through the gaps between grains. Below them are the foraminifera, single cells in chambered shells, extending nets of cytoplasm into the water to catch what drifts. When they die their shells join the floor. They are inhabitants and building material both, and here that distinction never quite holds.

Nothing is wasted, and nothing is quick. A worm might take years to reach a size you would call adult. A bivalve the size of a thumbnail may have been alive when someone stood on this ground as land. Metabolism runs low here, at the pace of the cold and the thin food, and the lives are long in a way that has nothing to do with contentment. They are slow because the snow is slow.

## The Herds

On the surface of the ooze, the sea cucumbers move. They are pale, translucent, and sac-bodied, with tentacles like short fingers that push mud into their mouths. They graze in loose herds across the plain, and each leaves a trail behind it, a sinuous groove that fills with silt over days. From a little above, the floor looks scribbled over by a patient, aimless hand, and the scribble is constant. No animal here has ever seen a blank stretch of plain.

Some of the cucumbers are swimmers. When a storm's edge reaches them, or when food falls somewhere upcurrent, they lift off the bottom with a slow rippling of their bodies, trailing a translucent sail, and drift. They look like small ghosts of themselves. Then they settle again, wherever the water sets them, and begin to graze.

Among them, brittle stars lie arm to arm in living carpets, each five-armed body interlocked with its neighbors so that the whole mat seems to breathe. When the current quickens, they raise their arms into it, a field of thin fingers combing the water for anything suspended. When a storm comes they bury themselves, or hold on, or are carried off. The carpet, afterward, is always exactly as dense as before. It is filled from the edges.

## The Forest

The sponge forests are the nearest thing to a town. Each stalk is a single animal, a glassy lattice on a long silica stem. It holds itself up into the current and strains it. In dense stands the stalks touch, and among them the tangled spicules make a maze where small things live: shrimp that never leave the lattice they were born on, worms threaded through the tissue, tiny snails that graze the film on the glass, and brittle stars that wrap an arm around a stalk and hold on.

The animals of the forest are less mobile than anything else on the floor. Some sponges, it is believed, are older than any recorded name for anything. They grow a fraction of a millimeter a year. When a storm knocks a stalk down, the collapse is not just the death of one animal but the loss of a habitat, and the small tenants must find another or fail. Sometimes a toppled stalk lies on the ooze for years, still alive, still straining water sideways, sheltering a shrimp couple that has adapted to the new angle.

## The Few Large

Fish are rare enough to be events. A grenadier hangs head-down over the ooze, tail whipping slowly, its large eyes near-useless, its lateral line reading the water for the vibrations of a small crustacean or the smell of something dead. It may go days without a meal and will not hurry when one comes. Eel-like fish thread the nodules, nosing the mud, and others drift in the mid-water above the floor, black and soft-bodied, mouths built for meals that come once in a season.

When a large body falls (a whale, a great fish, a sinking mass of kelp torn from a shelf a thousand kilometers away) the floor changes for years. The scavengers arrive in order. Amphipods first, in clouds. Then hagfish, slithering up the current in knots, then sleeper sharks, if there are any, and finally the bone-eating worms that colonize what's left. The carcass becomes an island. A community lives on it for a decade, a dense, strange, noisy population by the standards of the plain. Then the bones go, and the community goes with them, and the ooze closes over the shape.

## The Octopuses

And in the middle of all this are the ones who build.

They are small, and pale as raw dough, and they do not look like clever animals. Most of the time they don't look like anything at all. They are lumps in the shadow of an overhang, or a shell that has too much weight on one side. But they are octopuses, and octopuses think, and a mind of that kind has found its way into this place and made something of it.

Each lives alone. There is no dark hall of them, no gathering, and they seldom meet except to mate or to fight over a burrow. A female might live for many years, longer than her shallow-water cousins, in the slow economy of the cold. Late in her life she attaches her eggs to a stone or the underside of a shell and guards them. She does not leave. She does not eat. She fans water over the clutch and cleans it with her arms for years while the embryos develop at the pace of the cold, and by the time they hatch she is a husk of herself and dies beside them. The young arrive in a world with no one in it to teach them anything.

So it isn't taught. It's inherited another way.

The rings remain. A ring of nodules and shell plates around a burrow doesn't vanish when its builder dies. It sits on the plain until a storm takes it, and often the storm doesn't. A young octopus, spread out from the hatching place, finds a ring and moves into it. She rearranges a stone. She adds a plate. The next comes, and she adds another. In this way, some rings have been worked on by many hands, none of which met, and their shape has the accumulated logic of a thing built by a mind that keeps forgetting itself and being reborn in a new body. The knowledge is in the stones.

The shell-carrying is the same. Nobody instructs the young octopus to hold a half-shell beneath its body and stilt-walk across the ooze on the tips of its arms. It finds one, and picks it up, and discovers what it's for. In a storm it pulls the shell over the burrow and clamps it shut with its suckers, and waits, and feels the hiss of the moving floor through the shell, and stays.

## One of Them

There is one, in a ring older than she is, near the edge of a terrace where the clay was scoured bare. Her skin is the color of ash and cream. One arm is shorter than the others, healed over a wound whose cause she has forgotten. She has rearranged her ring three times since she came to it, and each time she left the oldest stones where they were, because they were heavy and they held.

At the wall of the ring, facing outward, is a flake of grey flint. She found it in the terrace edge, half buried, and tugged it free and turned it over in her arms for a long time. It was sharp. It was cold. It was unlike the nodules, and unlike shell, and unlike bone. It had been made. She could not know that, but something in the way her arms read the edge told her that a pattern lay in it, that the fracture was not accident. She carried it home and wedged it in the wall, edge out.

The silt gathers against it. Small things graze on the film that grows on its face. She touches it sometimes as she passes, the way you'd touch a doorframe.

When the storm comes she will hear it first, in the fine hairs of her arms, a rising hiss from upcurrent. The brittle stars will burrow. The cucumbers will lift and drift. The grenadier will turn its head into the flow and hold. The sponge forest will bow. She will drag her shell across the entrance, brace her arms against the stone, and shut her eyes, and the flake in the wall will hold its edge toward the dark, as it has for longer than the floor has been the floor.

Then the water will clear, and she will open the door.
