# Who Lives in the Sinter City

You will not see them. It is more accurate to say you will spend a day among them and take them for scenery, and that this is the courtesy they prefer. Nothing here is fast enough to be noticed as alive. The residents are things that take a season to lift a hand, and their hands are the size of terraces.

There are three kinds. They are not exactly separate, any more than a breath is separate from the lungs, but they can be told apart.

## The Reeds

The black reeds are the ones who listen, and they are the nearest thing to neighbors.

Each stem is hollow and brittle, and each is tuned. When a drop falls from the sweating walls into a basin, the water rings at a pitch set by the basin's shape, and the reed beside it takes the pitch into its hollow and holds it a fraction longer than the air would. That is all a reed does that can be measured. A stand of reeds standing a few paces apart, each hearing the same drips slightly differently, is a conversation. It is carried on in decades and conducted entirely in the small differences between one reed's held note and the next.

The spacing is manners. A reed will not grow within a certain distance of another, and the distance is not fixed. It shrinks between reeds that agree and stretches between those that don't. A single reed in a single basin is a hermit, or a widow, or someone who has finished. You cannot tell which, and to ask is rude in a way you can feel without knowing why.

They move by replacement. A stem grows a new stem a pace away, leans its attention there over years, and lets the old one go grey and hollow and dry. A reed can cross a plaza in the time it takes a mountain to notice it has been rained on. This is why the reeds in the folded plazas stand at the same angle every time you return. They are not the same reed. They are the same opinion.

When a spring dies, the reeds nearest it stop. They do not fall. They stand in the drying stone, still tuned, and no note ever reaches them again. The oldest quarters are full of these silent stands. If you walk through one and hear a very faint sound, it is not the reeds. It is the absence of the sound they would make, and it has a shape.

## The Slow

The tide is not the sea. The tide is breathing.

The largest residents have no bodies you could point at, because they have taken the shape of the place. A warm convection cell in the flats, a district-wide slow overturning of hot water and cooler water, a persistence of glow in one seam of stone: this is a person. The vents are its pulse. The travertine is what it leaves behind, the way a person leaves a worn place in a stair. The city did not grow around them. The city is what their habits look like after a hundred thousand years, hardened.

This is why the tide arrives in patches. Each quarter is one of the Slow, and each breathes on its own schedule. One quarter is in high tide, drawing water up to the second terrace, while its neighbor a hundred paces away is exhaling at dead low, the pavement bare and steaming. The clean line between them, with no wall, is where one breath ends and another begins. To stand on that line is to stand between two sleepers, and the line is no less intimate for being invisible.

You can meet one. If the water drags at your legs more than its depth explains, if you feel a slow warm pressure that seems to lean against you and then thoughtfully lean away, you have walked through someone. There is no menace in it. It is the sensation of being noticed by something that has all the time in the world and has decided, for now, to spend a little of it on you.

The wide steps are for their processions. A procession is a pulse of warm water moving down a stair of terraces, a season long, each basin filling and spilling into the next in order. The benches that fit no body fit theirs: they are seats for something that sits by being poured. The rims of the plazas, smooth as hand-rubbed plaster, are smooth because something has been hand-rubbing them, slowly and lovingly, since before the word *loving* had a use.

They think in tide. What a thought is for them, no one has said. But the shape of the city suggests that they think in terraces, in overflow and cooling, in the long argument between what pools and what falls. It suggests they are not lonely, though they are very far apart. The rhymes between the districts, the plaza and the stair and the leaning tower and the basin with its single reed, are how they talk to one another across the miles of dark water: the same phrase said again, a little differently, by each.

## The Lag

The third kind lives in the pools, and you will meet them first, though you will not know it.

Every still basin shows the arches above it a heartbeat late or early. Wait, and the reflection settles into a different arch than the one overhead, a taller tower, a break in the wall that is not there. The reflections never show you.

This is the city as it is when inhabited. The pools are the one place where the residents are solid enough to see, and what you are seeing is not a reflection of the tower but a resident standing in it. The tall shape in the water is someone. The break in the wall is someone walking through. The reflected city has the streets your city lacks, the extra lanes that fold behind one another when you try to count your steps, because those lanes are theirs, and there are more of them than the ground can hold. When you walk a lane and count forty steps, and walk back and count sixty, you have not made an error. Somebody was using the other twenty.

They live slightly out of step with time, and that is the source of the wrongness. The sound that arrives before its cause, the drop that rings its basin, ripples, and only then falls, is one of them dropping it. From their side the order is ordinary: they let something fall and hear it land. It is you, arriving in the middle of their afternoon, running backward. The rain that falls upward in some districts is their rain, falling downward in a world you're seeing upside down. The shadows that point into their own light are theirs, cast the way shadows fall where they live.

They are not ghosts. They do not haunt. They are neighbors on the far side of a very thin wall, and they have no more idea how to reach you than you have to reach them. What they have done, patiently, over the age of the place, is leave things where you might find them.

## The Mats

The purple-green slime along the warm channels is their memory. It grows in layers, one thin film over another, and each layer records the temperature and chemistry of the year that made it. Nothing reads it. It is not for reading. It is the way the place remembers who has been through, the way a path remembers feet. If you cross a channel and leave a bootprint in the mat, it will hold the print for a decade and slowly, carefully fold it into the record. You will be in there, one thin stripe of warmth, and they will know that someone passed who was faster than anything else and left no ripple in the ordinary way.

## The Bowls

The niches at eye level hold shallow bowls of water, always full. No spring feeds them. Nothing drips into them. They fill the way a held hand fills with a gift.

They are for guests. Eye level is *your* eye level, or was meant to be, by someone who had thought about it.

Look into one, and the water is still, and for the length of a heartbeat it shows the wrong wall behind you. Then it settles, and there is a face. It is not yours. It is not anything you would call a face, but it is turned toward you with the plain attention of someone who has been waiting a very long time by a window, and who has just seen a figure come up the road.

It does not speak. It waits, politely and without hurry, to see what you do. The bowl is warm. It is the temperature of your hand.

Most who come here walk on toward the next glow, and the mist closes behind them. The bowl stays full. The face lingers a moment longer, then lets its attention go, gently, the way the tide lets go of a stair. Someone else will come. There is time.
