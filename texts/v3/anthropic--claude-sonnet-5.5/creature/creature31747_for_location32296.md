# Who Lives on the Plateau

Most of what lives here is not people. The plateau is crowded with life, and nearly all of it is small, warm-blooded, and loud, crammed into the few places that hold heat and water. Flies rise off the thaw seeps in black veils. Pikas whistle from the silt. The shaggy herds pass in their slow grunting lines, and the wolves and ravens follow. All of these creatures know the vents' timetables better than any visitor could hope to, and for most of the plateau's history they were the only tenants.

But there are also people. Not many: a few hundred across the whole plateau, in bands of fifteen or twenty. The lowlanders who have heard of them call them **Scarpfolk**. Among themselves they use a word that means roughly *those on shift*.

## The Body

They are built for the air. They are broad through the ribs, with deep barrel chests and a slow, heavy stride that never hurries, because hurrying is paid for in breath. Their lips and nail beds are dark, and their cheeks flush purple-red in the cold. Their blood is thick and their hearts are large. Children are taught to breathe before they are taught to speak: in for a count, hold, out for a longer count. They call this *keeping the ledger*. You can hear it around a camp at night, a dozen people breathing in slow unison, like a bellows worked by many hands.

Their skin is weathered to leather. Their eyes are narrow and heavy-lidded against the glare of the hard little sun and the ink of the sky. In the bright hours they wear visors of slit bone that leave only a thin bar of vision, so they look out of the world through a mail slot. They dress in the hides of the shaggy grazers, hair turned inward, sewn with sinew, and over that they wear mantles of felted fur that shed sleet. Their hands are always the first thing to go cold. They keep them tucked in their armpits and carry small warm stones from the vent margins in their sleeves.

## No Building

They build nothing. This is the first thing a visitor notices and the last thing a visitor understands. There is no wall, no hearth ring, no cairn, no shrine. The ground shifts. A scarp that was a step last year is a cliff now, and a slab that was a roof may be a rockfall by spring. Anything set down on the plateau is undone or buried. So the Scarpfolk set nothing down.

What they have instead is **knowledge**, and it is enormous. Every person carries the schedule of the vents in their head: which throw every forty minutes, which sulk for half a day and then let go, which hiss for weeks and then abruptly stop. Each vent has a name and a temperament, discussed like a neighbor. *Old Valve is early today. The Twins are quarreling. Kettle has not drawn breath since dawn, so be careful of her.* They read the ground the way a foreman reads a floor, by sound and by the temperature of the air, by the color of the rime and the direction the plume leans. They cross the terraces along routes passed hand to hand over generations, stepping only on the old thick crust where it will bear weight, and they can do it in fog by counting steps.

## Shelter and the Gas

For shelter they use what is already made. In the lee of a boulder heap, where blocks lean together, they make roofs of hide stretched across the angular gaps. Better than this is the warm ground: shelves of terrace stone kept blood-warm by seeping water, where a body can lie at night with the cold pressing down from above and the earth pressing up from below.

But the warm hollows are also where the fog settles at dusk, and in the fog, in the low places, the ground breathes out things that cannot be smelled once the nose has gone dull. Sulfur gas and heavier, quieter gases pool in basins and kill in the dark. Here the Scarpfolk's oldest custom comes in. Each band carries a few small **finches** in woven sedge cages, the only creatures they keep and the only thing they carry that they make. At dusk the cage is lowered into a chosen hollow. If the bird sings and shifts and preens, the band sleeps. If it goes quiet, they gather their things without discussion and climb to the ridge shoulders above the fog, where the air is thin and cold and clean, and sleep sitting up, in a huddle, against the wind.

A hush in a hollow is the most frightening thing they know. They speak of it in whispers, if they speak of it at all.

## The Day's Round

The band's day follows the weather like a shift schedule.

**Morning.** In the brittle clear cold, they gather at the ice curtains on the shaded scarps. They chip glassy sheets off the seeping rock into skin bags and let them soften against their bodies for drinking. This is the best water on the plateau: cold, clean, faintly mineral. Then they eat, mostly cold: dried pika meat, fat rendered from the herds, eggs from the waders' marshes carried in moss.

**Midday.** They travel. The clouds are building, the light is sharp, and the ground is still firm. They cross the gravel floors in single file, spaced out, each person a few dozen paces from the next, moving at the count of the breath.

**Afternoon.** When the sleet comes, they stop wherever they are and crouch under their mantles, backs to the wind, and wait it out. The gray veils pass over. They do not talk during the sleet. This is when they sew, or count, or sleep with their eyes open.

**Evening.** When the cloud tears in the west and the low sun turns the wet scarps to hammered copper, everyone stops, stands, and looks. This is the only hour they seem to consider holy, though they would not use that word. The band gathers, tells the day, mends what needs mending, and cooks. Meat wrapped in sedge goes into a hot pool at the vent margin, and there is a small ceremony of not burning your hand.

**Night.** Then the fog, then the frost, and the cages, and the climb if the birds are silent.

## The Herds, the Wolves, the Ravens

The Scarpfolk live at the edges of the herds. They walk with the grazers for weeks, keeping to the sedge flats and the warm springs, and take the old, the lame, and the calves that fall behind. They do not chase. They wait, as the wolves wait, and there is an understanding, never spoken, of distance. The wolves keep to one flank and the people to another. Neither crosses the herd. When a kill is made, the ravens come in a black rushing, and the people take what they can carry and leave the rest, and no one fights over it.

They speak to the ravens. It is not clear that they are understood, but the birds follow them, and a raven circling high over a certain ridge tells them something about a carcass, a wolf, or a cat. Every band has a name for its own ravens.

## Language, Water, and Weather

Their language is thick with words for water in its states and its transitions, more words than for any other subject. There is a word for water that has not yet decided to freeze, and another for ice that has begun to sweat. There is a word for mist rising from a pool at dawn, and another for the mist that rises from a pool that is dying. They have a whole grammar of *becoming*: water into ice, ice into steam, steam into stone. A person's life is spoken of the same way. A child is *not yet set*. An adult is *running*. An old person is *going to rime*.

## The Dead

When someone dies, the band carries the body to a terrace, one of the slow-spilling tiers of rust and ochre, where mineral water is running. They lay it in a shallow basin, arranged as if sleeping, and leave. Over the years the water crusts over the body in bright films. The face and hands go first, then the whole shape, until the person is a low orange swelling in the terrace, indistinguishable from the stone. A visitor who walks the tiers of the great terraces may mistake these mounds for formations. There are a great many of them.

To the Scarpfolk this is not burial. It is *going into the product*. The plateau makes stone, and they give it material. Nothing is built for the dead, and nothing is marked, because the place is already doing the work. They believe, or say they believe, that the terraces are getting a little brighter every year.

## What They Fear

They do not fear the cold. They do not fear the herds, and they only mildly fear the wolves. They fear:

- **A vent that goes silent.** It means the plumbing has changed. Somewhere the fault has moved and the heat has gone elsewhere, possibly beneath their feet. When a vent they know falls quiet, the band packs and leaves in an hour, without argument.
- **A hush in a hollow**, as above.
- **The lean cat**, seen so rarely that each sighting is told for years. They do not hunt it. They do not speak its name aloud.
- **The soft country**, the low land below, which they have heard of. Trees. Soil. Places where the ground stays put. They are not sure that such a place is good for people.

## A Moment

Late in the day, a woman of the band stands on a terrace, visor pushed up on her forehead, watching the copper light run along the weeping cliff. Behind her the herd is a low shifting darkness on the flats, steaming, grunting. A vent she has known since childhood throws its column skyward with a sound like a huge valve giving way, and the hot rain drifts across the terraces, crusting and hissing. She counts her breaths without noticing. In, hold, out. She is not thinking of anything. She stands the way one stands in a workshop where everything is running, slightly aware that she is standing on something that is still being made, and that she, in her time, will be part of it.

Then the fog lifts out of the basin. She goes to see about the finches.
