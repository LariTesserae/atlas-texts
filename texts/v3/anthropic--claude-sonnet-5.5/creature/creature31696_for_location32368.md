# Who Lives in the Basin

You will not see them at first. They are the color of the light, and the fog is the color of them.

What you notice first is a movement along a causeway: a line of pale shapes in single file, poles held level, each pole tip a little ahead of the walker's foot, tapping the stone and the crust beside it. They come out of a fog bank one at a time, as if the bank were unwinding them. They pass without speaking. One lifts a flat hand at chest height and turns the palm, and the person behind repeats it. That is a greeting, and it is also a report: *the ground ahead holds.*

## Bodies

They are long and narrow, with the economy of animals that have been rationed. The people of the eleven cities do not call themselves anything you could pronounce. In the hand-language the closest sign is a palm laid flat, then pressed lightly down and held: roughly *the ones who stay on top.* Visitors call them the Notched, and they do not object, because it is accurate.

Their skin is sun-cured to the tone of unglazed pottery, then dusted with fine salt that never quite leaves it. Their lashes are pale and thick. The irises are very dark and the lids heavy and half-lowered by habit, so that even in conversation they appear to be considering something just behind you. Their eyes water constantly, and they have a trained, unembarrassed way of dabbing the corners with a knuckle. Crow's-feet cut deep by thirty, and old people's faces are maps of squinting, every line pointing toward the same bright place.

They dress in white and near-white: felted wool from the saltgrazers, layered loose, with a cowl that comes forward like a shallow bell. The visors are woven from dark reed and worn low, with a slit only as wide as the work requires. Over cheekbones and the bridge of the nose they smear a paste of ground shell and tallow, which dries into a pale mask and cracks as they move. By evening every face is a fine web of hairline fractures. It is not decoration, though it has become decoration. A person whose paste has not cracked has not been working.

They walk in a way you will find yourself imitating within an hour. The foot goes down heel first, weight held back, and only after a beat of listening does the body follow. It is not hesitation. It looks more like courtesy, as if each step were asking permission of the ground and had learned to wait for the answer.

## Speech

Voices are kept for emergencies, so the hands do the talking, and the hand-language is rich, fast, and built for glare. The signs are large and flat-palmed, made at shoulder height against the pale walls, where a hand shows as a dark-edged shape even through fog. Signing in shadow, or low, or with the fingers curled, is considered mumbling. Children who sign small are gently told to *speak up*, meaning *open your hands.*

Where hands cannot be seen, in dense fog or at night, they tap: a knuckle on a causeway stone, a pole tip on a wall. Each district has a set of taps as a family has a set of habits. A wife can know her husband is coming home by the third tap, and can tell from the rhythm whether he is tired.

There are very few sounds that are permitted, and each has a meaning that everyone knows. A single low hum, one note held for the length of a breath, means *I am here and I am not afraid.* People hum it to the dying and to newborns, to saltgrazers in labor, and to the walls of a house when the rain is coming. A shout is not an insult. It is closer to a physical assault, and in the oldest districts the word for it is the same as the word for *landslide.* The few children who scream in play are taken aside and taught to cry with their mouths closed.

## Work

Every person has a trade, and every trade has to do with holding something in place a little longer.

**Wallwrights** are the most respected. They mix lime and shell wash in wide stone troughs and carry the mix on their backs in wet skins. They know by the taste of the water at a seep whether it is *hungry* that season, meaning loaded with dissolved gypsum and eager to take more. In hungry seasons they work at night, in the cold, when the wash sets slowly and hard.

**Polemen** walk ahead of any procession, and they are the ones you saw. They sound the crust, and after decades of it they can tell by the pitch of a tap whether there is clay beneath the salt, or a void, or a slow bulge of water waiting to give. A poleman who has read the ground wrong is not blamed. A poleman who has read it wrong and lived is quietly given the hardest walls to repair for the rest of the year, and no one is sure whether this is a penalty or a kindness.

**Fog-readers** sit on rooftops before dawn and watch the moths. They say that the moths know where the water is moving under the floor, that the banks form over the places that are about to change. They are right often enough to be trusted and wrong often enough to stay humble, and their forecasts are given as questions: *Will the low market be there tomorrow?*

**Snail-women**, though a third of them are men, go out at dusk with flat baskets and thin bone scrapers and take the glass snails from the lower walls. They take only the dim ones and leave the ones still glowing. The meat is sweet and faintly bitter, and the shells are burned to make the wash that keeps the houses standing. Nothing in the basin is wasted, because everything is either food or wall.

**Keepers of the herds** walk the saltgrazers in slow arcs across the meadows, humming. The animals follow because the hum is the only sound that has never preceded a collapse.

## The Tally

The tally wall is the closest thing they have to law, to record, and to religion, and it works by one principle: *you are what you have mended.*

A notch is cut with a flint blade, a single upright stroke with a small tick at the top, the day a household repairs its home. Children are given a notch of their own the first time they help hold a trowel. A marriage is two households cutting notches on each other's walls in the same hour. A friendship is the same, done more casually. A debt is a notch cut on someone else's wall that you have not yet answered.

A stranger arrives with no notches, and so they are, in the plainest sense, unfinished. Nobody is cruel to them. But before they can be sheltered, they are handed a trowel and pointed at a wall that is not their own. The Notched believe that a person who has never repaired anything does not understand what a roof costs. Once you have spent an afternoon lifting wet lime to a stranger's lintel, your first notch is cut, and with it you become someone who can be trusted with a door.

The dead are not buried, because the ground cannot be trusted with them. They are carried to the pans at dawn, wrapped in white felt, and laid in the shallow milky water, and the crystal comes for them over a few days. They are found again, if at all, as pale shapes under a glittering skin, and people visit for a season before the shape becomes only a lump in the crust. The wall keeps the notches. The cliff owls, people say, take the notches of the dead, and for that reason the tally walls are inspected each spring and a small silence is kept at any that have gone smooth. It means someone's whole life has been carried away.

## What They Believe

They do not believe much, but they believe it carefully.

They believe the ground is not hostile. This is important to them, and it is often the first thing they say to a visitor, in signs slow enough for a child: *it is not angry.* It is simply doing what it does. Water takes what water touches. That is not punishment. It is an accounting. They live inside an ongoing transaction, and their whole culture is a practice of paying attention to what is owed.

They believe the basin counts. They will tell you that the low tones felt through the soles in the still hours are numbers, though not anything a person could say. When the tone comes, everyone stops where they are. The wallwright lowers her trowel, the poleman plants his pole, a mother halts halfway through a doorway with a child on her hip. They stand with their heads slightly bowed, eyes closed behind the visors, and wait for it to cross the floor and pass, and then they go on.

What is being counted, no one agrees. The old say it is the notches, the sum of all the mending, and that when the count is finished the basin will know how much of it to let stand. The young sometimes say it is the days, or the dead, or the water itself, tallying its own take. There is a small quiet group who walk out to the center pans at the end of their lives, not to die there but to stand in the deepest glare and listen more closely, and who come back rarely, and change when they do. They do not say what they heard. When pressed, they make the sign for *ground holds,* and then, after a moment, a second sign that has no place in the ordinary language. It is one palm laid over the other, both turned slowly downward. Nobody translates it for outsiders. It seems to mean that the count has not been finished against them.

## An Evening

At dusk the glass snails begin to glow along the lower walls, and the Notched come out onto their thresholds, doors uphill and black cloth lifted from windows, and sit. There is no music. There is the tick of crystals lengthening in the pans, the faint drip of seeps, the slow rustle of a saltgrazer settling down on the mat. A wallwright scrapes a last smear of lime from her trough. A child, told for the fourth time to close its mouth, practices the closed-mouth hum against a wall and feels it pass into the stone.

Somewhere far off, in the dark under the terraces, the ground gives way with a long hush. Every head lifts a fraction. Every hand goes still. And then, house by house, along the whole pale length of the causeway, one low note goes up, one held breath long, from a thousand throats at once. *I am here. I am not afraid.*

In the morning the fog will come, and someone will be out before it with a trowel and a flint, mending.
