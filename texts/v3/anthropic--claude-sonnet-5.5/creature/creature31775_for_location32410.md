# Those Who Keep the Shelf

You will not find them by looking. You find them by the smoke, and then by what the smoke has been doing to the air: a faint sweetness of burning peat, a brown-blue thread that climbs the height of a house and lies down flat. Follow it down a stair, across a saddle, along a channel whose gutter has been swept clean that week, and you will come to a door with a felted curtain instead of a leaf of wood. Someone is behind it. Someone has known you were coming for a quarter of an hour, because the stairs carry footsteps, and in this place a stranger's tread is heard long before it arrives.

## The Households

They are a low-slung, deep-chested people, built for thin air and long climbs. Their faces are weathered dark at the cheekbones, and their hands are broad, with knuckles cracked and rubbed with grease from the grazers. Their eyes have the narrow, unhurried look of people who spend their lives watching water find its way down. Most are not tall. They move with a small rolling economy, planting the foot flat on each dished tread so it doesn't slip, and the old ones seem to have worn the same shallow bowls into their own gait.

They dress in wool felted so hard it stands away from the body: long hooded mantles, stiff at the shoulder, that shed rain in beads. The wool is undyed, in the natural range from cream through gray to a rust-brown. Their hems fray into fringes on purpose, so that water leaves the cloak in a hundred small drips and does not run down the legs. In the afternoon rain they look like walking cairns, each with a thin halo of falling drops.

They have no metal and don't miss it. Their adzes are dark flint-hard stone lashed into wooden handles. Their needles are bone, their cordage braided fiber, and their levers are long poles of a dense, slow wood that has to be carried up from somewhere below the shelf and is passed down through families like inherited land. A good lever has a name. It has been used to reset stones that a grandmother's grandmother also set.

A household is not defined by blood alone but by a reach. Each is responsible for a stretch of water: a run of gutter, two cisterns and the steps around them, the conduit under a certain street and the spring that feeds it. They say *I am of the third channel below the split*, or *I keep the black pool with the crooked stair*. Someone raised in one reach may marry into another and take up its water, and in a generation the new reach is theirs. Nobody governs the whole. Nobody could. The shelf is too large and the settlements too far apart. What holds it together is that every reach flows into the next, so that a household that neglects its channel floods its neighbors below. They mind each other's water without speaking of it, the way one minds a shared wall.

Their days follow the weather the city makes. They rise in the cold clear dawn and cut peat from the wet turf between the terraces, stacking the black bricks on wall-tops to drain and stiffen. They clear the gutters of silt and the winter's fallen plates of slate. They go out early to walk their reach and read it: how fast the cascades run, whether a joint is beginning to weep where it hasn't wept before, whether the mineral curtains on a lintel have grown enough to be hazardous to a passing head. When the cloud cap forms and begins to darken, they go indoors and stay there. They sit around the low peat fire, mending, braiding, sharpening, or simply listening to the rain come on.

## Speech and Silence

They talk little, and quietly, and often with their faces very close together. Sound behaves oddly in the alleys. A whisper can be heard a hundred paces along a channel, while a shout dies against the first turning. Wet stone hushes some sounds and throws others. Over generations the people have learned a courtesy of low voices, and children who talk too loudly are not scolded but drawn gently to a wall and shown how the voice goes flat there and what it sounds like in a stairwell, until they understand.

There is also a language of the stones. Tapped on the conduit walls with a knuckle or a bone, a few simple patterns travel a long way through the drainage, carried by the water inside it. *Come. Danger. Water rising. All well.* Nobody thinks of this as a code. It is just what you do when the channel is full and someone far down the reach needs to know. On still nights a very faint tapping can be heard under the paving, slow and patient, passing news from house to house across the width of a quarter.

## What They Believe, Without Saying It

They do not have temples. Nothing is built for worship, because that would be building for something other than water. But they have manners toward the place that come close to devotion. They step around the tread-bowls when they can, so the palmfuls of rain stay unspilled. They touch a lintel's mineral curtain on the way through a door, lightly, with two fingers, and never break the growing ones. When they must reset an old stone they speak to it first, briefly, as one would to an animal about to be moved.

They say the city was not built so much as *agreed to*: the mountain offered the stone, the water offered the way, and the first ones only listened. That is why the rules are what they are. Do not build across a channel. Do not wall in a spring. Repair the oldest stones last. These are less commandments than the terms of a very old truce.

They bury no one in the peat, for the peat is the water's. Their dead are carried up in felt wraps to the highest dry terraces, where the wind is steady and the stone is bare, and laid in low stone cells open to the sky. The dark broad-winged raptors gather there. People watch them turn on the thermals over the cells and regard this as the last work of the body, to become weight in the air. They do not grieve loudly. They stand in the rain a while after.

## The Hour

Once the afternoon rain thins, doors open all along the street. People come out and stand in the wet with their hoods pushed back. Children too, and the very old on someone's arm, and no one says why. They simply face the west, where the cloud is tearing, and wait for the light to come back across the roofs. It arrives the way it always does, patch by patch, roof by distant roof, until the whole tilted country of stone is lit in pieces and gleaming. Nobody speaks. A few people close their eyes. Then, one by one, they go back in to their fires.

## The Grazers

The small long-legged grazers do not belong to anyone, and they belong to everyone. They move in family bands of five or six along the upper terraces, and they know the stairs better than the people do. They take each tread with a pause and a considered placement of the forefoot, testing the wet edge, then bringing the hind legs down in exact order. They crop the cushion plants and moss just enough to keep the steps clear, and the people have long understood that the grazers are gardeners, so the herds are left alone. Once in a while, in the worst of winter, a household will set out a heap of dried sedge at a terrace corner and step away, and this is neither payment nor tribute. It is a courtesy between neighbors.

Their dung is gathered, dried, and burned alongside the peat. It is a slow, sweet fire that smells of grass, and the smoke of a house that burns it can be told at once from one that doesn't.

## The Others

The raptors circle. There are never many, but there is never a moment when there are none, and the people track them the way sailors track a fixed star. When the birds drop low and abruptly, someone up-slope has died, or the weather is about to turn. They are silent birds. When one does call, once a season perhaps, it is a thin, high, dry sound, and everyone within earshot stops to listen.

The frogs sit in the cisterns, thumb-sized and the color of wet mud, and vanish at a shadow. They are the household's clocks and its conscience. As long as the frogs are in a pool, the water is sound. If one season a cistern has none, the family of that reach will drain it, scour it, and wait, and the frogs come back when the water is right. Nobody knows from where.

In the drowned rooms of the abandoned houses, in the clear cold water that fills them to the ceiling, there are tiny pale creatures no larger than grains of rice. They drift, and they turn slowly, and on the rare bright day when the light falls down the flooded stair, they glitter in it like dust in a sunbeam. They keep the water clear, the people say, and so it is never disturbed. Those rooms are left as they are. The stairs go down into them and continue below the surface, and nobody has ever needed to know how far.

And over everything, patient and enormous and slow, the lichens spread. They are the oldest inhabitants, some of them perhaps older than the walls they are mapping. The people call them the *tenants* and take care not to scrape them from any stone that still holds a roof. A house is not really finished, they say, until the lichen has decided to live on it.
