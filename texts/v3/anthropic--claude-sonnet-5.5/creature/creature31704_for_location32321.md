# The Halves

Almost no one who has stood in the bowl has known that it was inhabited. The inhabitants do not hide. They are simply the same color as everything else and hold as still as the stone.

## What they look like

They are people, or were, or are what people become when they stay somewhere long enough. They are small and long-limbed, with skin the buff of the limestone and a fine pale bloom over it, the same powder that comes away on your fingers from the wall. Their hair is thin and blond and grows in clumps, like the tufts along the rim. Their eyes are wide-set and dark, and they blink so seldom that the lids seem to have forgotten how. Their hands are the strangest part: broad, soft-fingered, and permanently cool, with palms worn smooth and slightly hollowed, as if from long use as cups.

Most of the day they sit in the alcoves with their backs against the rust-and-ochre streaks. Moss has begun to band the shoulders of the oldest. No one minds. It is considered a kind of belonging.

## Why they are called the Halves

They live in pairs. No one is ever alone in the bowl, and no one is ever quite whole.

In each pair, one is the **sitter** and one is the **walker**. The sitter stays in the alcove and keeps still. The walker rises at long intervals, crosses the silt, and does the small work of the place. That work is touching the drip cups to keep their rings bright, brushing the scale-lip of the pool, and laying a palm against each seep to check that it still weeps. Then the walker returns and the pair change places. This happens perhaps once in a day, or in a season. They do not measure it. The change is made without speech. One simply rises as the other settles, and each takes the warmth the other left on the stone.

They do not think of themselves as two people. Each thinks of the other as the part of themselves that happens to be elsewhere at the moment. A walker who is far from the alcove is not lonely. A walker is only the sitter's other half, out in the world.

There are as many pairs as there are ferns in the largest alcoves, about a dozen, and the number has not changed. Nobody is born and nobody arrives. When one of a pair grows very old, the two stop trading places. The sitter stays and the walker stays, side by side, and over time they lean into one another and into the wall. Their outlines soften and the bloom takes them. Later a new pair is found sitting in the same alcove, and no one asks where they came from. The sunken stones of the southern wall may be all that remains of the ones who were set down first.

## What they eat and drink

They do not eat as other creatures do. They drink from the cup-stone, always by halves. A person takes half of what the hollow holds, waits while the seep refills it, and then the partner takes the other half. The cup-stone's level has never changed because it has never been drunk to the bottom or left unshared. The pool is not drunk from at all. They regard it as the center of the trade, not as something that can be taken from.

For substance they take the pale powder from the walls, dampened on the tongue, and the leathery leaves of the crooked tree, chewed a long time and never swallowed whole. They say the tree's leaves taste of coins and rain. They have never seen rain, but they know its taste from the tree.

They make no fire and have no memory of any. The word they use for warmth is the same word they use for company.

## What they make and keep

They are not builders now. They tend what their earlier selves made. They keep the moss from creeping past the damp line, and they keep the lintel exactly as out of level as it is, because it was set that way. They believe the tilt is part of the doorway's meaning, though none of them can say what the meaning is. Sometimes a sitter rises, walks to the threshold, and stands under it without entering. Then the sitter goes back. The gesture is complete, and it is enough.

The scratches over the largest alcove are made in groups of three, and they are still being made, so slowly that the mineral bloom seals each group before the next is begun. The three are the two halves and the water between them. The Halves do not say this. They only make the marks when the light is raking and the mood in the alcove is unhurried. It takes a walker a long afternoon to scratch a single stroke. Nobody counts them or reads them. The marks are not a tally but a form of sitting.

## How they speak

Rarely, and mostly through the drips. Each pair has taken one of the cups as its own voice and keeps the interval of its drip in mind, the soft *tuk*, the higher *tik*, the wet round *plum*. When a Half wishes to speak, it hums so low that the note falls between the drips. Two pairs in conversation sound like two drips slowly drifting into and out of step. A visitor hears only the quiet. A visitor who listens for a very long time may notice that the quiet is holding a conversation.

They do have a few spoken words, dry and soft, breathed more than voiced. The most used is a word for *enough*.

## How they meet you

If you come down into the bowl, you will be noticed long before you notice anyone. The sitters turn their eyes, and no more. They will not be alarmed. You are a kind of weather. If you sit long enough, a walker may cross the silt and stop a pace or two away, and hold out one cupped palm with a finger-depth of clear water in it. You are meant to drink half and return the rest.

If you drink all of it, nothing dreadful happens. The walker only looks at the empty palm for a while. Then, with great patience, the walker goes to the seep and waits for it to fill again. Afterward the walker does not come back to you.

## What they are, perhaps

Some might say they are the bowl's own keeping, the way a place holds its shape by having someone sit in it. The seeps feed the pool and the air takes it back, and the Halves feed and are fed in the same trade: half taken, half given, and the whole left where it was. Whether they came to the bowl or the bowl made them is not something they consider. They are the last thing in the place that could have moved and chose not to, and they consider this the whole of their work.

When you climb out over the pale rim, past the blond tufts and the rings of lichen, you may look back. A shape you took for a boulder in an alcove may turn its head. Or it may not. From the rim, in that dim shell-gold light, there is no telling one half from the other, or either from the stone.
