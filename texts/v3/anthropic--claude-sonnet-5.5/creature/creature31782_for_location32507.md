# The Tenders

You will not see them the first time, or the tenth. What you see is the evidence, and the evidence looks like work.

The bog ore lies in windrows along the outflow, and the windrows are too even. Nodules of similar size lie together, the larger crusts sit behind them like a bund, and small shallow pans are cut into the mud behind each low wall, so that the current fills them and leaves them. Every spring the flood scatters the piles. Within a few weeks they are stacked again, not in the same places, but by the same logic.

Then there are the prints, five-toed and broad, with a webbing that stops short of the claw tips. Some of them are stained orange to the wrist. They cross the mud in loops and never in straight lines, and they stop at the steam columns.

## The Animals

They are the size of a large otter, but longer in the body and lower to the ground, built like something meant to pour itself into narrow spaces. The fur is dense and oily, dark brown where it's dry. Most of the time it isn't dry. The iron gets into it and gives the pelt a bronze cast at the tips, and a rainbow film clings to the guard hairs like the skin on the pool. When one shakes itself, the drops come off orange.

The head is broad and blunt, with small ears that close flat against the skull when it dives. The eyes are set forward and are much larger than an otter's, dark and wet, made for the green-gray dusk of the bowl. The whiskers are the remarkable part. They are long, stiff, and numerous, fanning from the muzzle and also from the wrists and the insides of the forearms. Watch one work a pan of rust-mat and amphipods and you'll see the forelimbs go in with the fingers half-curled and the wrist whiskers laid flat on the silt, reading the water the way a hand reads a page in the dark.

The hands are the thing you would remember. They are almost too capable for the animal around them, with long fingers, a thumb that opposes, and pads scored with deep ridges that hold a wet nodule of ore without slipping. When an animal stacks a windrow it picks up a nodule, turns it once, weighs it, and sets it down. Sometimes it picks the same one up again and moves it a hand's width. Nobody can say what it is judging.

## What They Do

They eat amphipods, and the pans are a way of farming them. The little walls slow the outflow and hold the water so the rust mats bloom thick in the shallows, and the blind amphipods gather there to graze. The animals come at dusk and again before dawn and sweep the pans with their forearms, lifting whole handfuls of pale crawling bodies to the mouth. They also eat frogs when they can, and the eggs of the water rails, and now and then a heron chick, though the herons nest on the rim and the raids are rare and desperate.

But the walls do other things too. They turn the current so that ore accumulates in certain places and not others. They keep the outflow from cutting a straight channel, and they keep the orange water braided and shallow across the whole floor of the flats. Whether the animals know this, or whether generations of stacking have simply favored the families whose stacking happened to keep the fishery alive, is not something you can learn by watching. The pattern is older than any one animal.

## Moving with the Thump

They travel on the thump. This is the first behavior you might catch if you sat very still. The frogs go silent when a shadow crosses the moss, and the heron turns its head at every small sound. But the animals move in the half-second after the water slams shut underground, when the ground shudders and the pool rings and every other sound is briefly buried. They take three or four bounding strides and freeze. Then they wait for the next one. Across the forty-second cycle, and across the seven vents drifting in and out of phase, they thread their way from the reeds to the pans, hardly ever seen in motion. It looks like a kind of slow stop-motion, an animal that is always somewhere new each time you look.

They have learned the polyrhythm the way a person learns a stairwell in the dark. When a storm shortens the cycle to twenty seconds, they slow down and wait it out in the vents, because the pattern is too tight to cross.

## The Dens

The steam chimneys are their doors. Each of the seven vents has a hollow beneath the lip of the crack, where the warm breath of the underground keeps the mud from freezing, and the animals have enlarged these pockets into chambers lined with moss and shed reed. On rime nights, when the frogs fall silent section by section, the animals are already inside. A faint glow of steam seeps out around their sleeping bodies. If you crouched at a vent mouth on a cold dawn, you would feel warm, damp, iron-smelling air on your face and hear, beneath the thump, something like slow breathing that might or might not be your own.

They sleep in heaps of three or four, wrapped around one another. A vent is held by a family for as long as the family lasts, and passes by some unseen inheritance to its young or its neighbors. The best-kept vents are ringed with a low circle of ore nodules laid rim-to-rim, like a threshold.

## Voice

They almost never call. What they do is tap. One animal will strike two nodules together, a dry knock that carries surprisingly well under the roar, and another will answer from across the pool. The knocks fall into the gaps in the vents' rhythm and never on top of them. The frogs' clatter and the animals' tapping have some resemblance, which may not be an accident, since a tap in the middle of a frog chorus is exactly the sound a predator would like to be mistaken for.

The young make a different noise, a high thin whistle that comes out of the vent chambers in spring and is drowned almost at once by the water.

## Ends and Continuations

Not many live long. Herons take the kits, the winter takes the weak, and sometimes an animal misjudges the thump and is caught on the wrong side of the pool when the slug of water hits the ledge and the whole bank shivers into the current. The population has never been more than a dozen and is probably often fewer. It persists because the pans persist, and because each spring a young animal finds a scatter of nodules on the flats and, without teaching, begins to stack them.

If the walls slump in and choke the fissure, they will go with the engine. They live in its warmth and by its sound and on the orange water it makes, and everything they do keeps the place running a little longer, whether or not they mean it to. Standing at the lip in the early evening, you might catch a bronze back sliding into the reeds just after a thump, and by the next one it will be gone. You will be left with the roar, and the pale mist, and the neat gray windrows, which look, in the failing light, as if someone had just finished with them.
