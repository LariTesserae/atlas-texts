# Who Lives in the Held Hollow

## The People

They are small, and they are pale in the way of things kept out of weather: not sickly, just unburnt, the color of the inside of a bark strip. Their eyes are large and dark, the pupils wide open in the shadowless glare, and they rarely squint. Their fingertips are stained green-brown to the first knuckle from moss and liverwort, and this stain does not wash out. It is a sign of belonging. A newcomer with clean hands would be noticed at once, though no one would say so.

They move with great economy. They step where the moss is deepest, turn a shoulder to pass in a lane without touching, and set a cup down so gently that the water in it barely shivers. They stand for long moments with a hand resting on a wall, not tired, only listening in the way the deaf listen, through the palm. Their breath smokes faintly and they breathe slowly, and they seem to do so on purpose, as if breath were one of the sounds that can't be prevented and so should be kept small.

They dress in felt and pressed wool the color of wet slate, with hide shoes and hoods of grey-green felt that match the walls. The copper is what they allow themselves for ornament: a pin at the collar, a ring of beaten wire on the smallest finger, a band around the wrist. The copper is cold-worked and never polished, so it has gone the soft brown-green of old pennies. Each person's pins are mended so often that little of the original metal is left, and they call it the same pin.

## The Count

The town keeps a knotted cord in the lowest room of the oldest house, and the knots are the people. Each has a tie of its own, a small distinctive twist, and the cord is a closed loop. No one has ever had to add length to it or cut any away. There are as many people as there are houses that can be lived in, and as many houses as there are people.

Ask how this is so and you get a small tilt of the head, not evasion but honest puzzlement. There are children, and there are the old. Somewhere in the course of things a house is taken down and its stone goes into another, and a child who was not quite there is there. Nobody remembers a funeral. Nobody remembers a birth as an event, with cries, a gathering, or a night of waiting. There is only a slow adjustment, like a held note changing pitch below the threshold of hearing, and then a grandmother is missing from the terrace and the count holds. The people do not grieve loudly. They lay a palm on the pillar a little longer that day, and then go on.

## The Work, and Those Who Do It

There are no rulers and no priests, only the kinds of attention.

**The mossers** are the most numerous. They walk the lanes with flat bone spatulas and small bowls of water, lifting a corner of cushion here, pressing one back there, easing a fern away from a lintel. The moss neither spreads nor dies back, and their work is what keeps it so, or they believe it is. Some have tended the same twelve paces of wall since youth.

**The combers** go up onto the thatch gardens with long-toothed bone rakes and lie on their bellies in the ferns, combing the reed straight and picking out any dead frond, of which there are none. A comber will spend an hour on a palm's width of roof, and come down with a single pale moth on their sleeve, and carry it back up and set it down where it was.

**The carriers** move the water. They take it from the dished steps in wooden scoops and cupped hands and bring it, brimming, up to the terraces without spilling a drop. The goats drink from stone troughs, the ferns are given their share, and the water goes out from the lowest steps in the same order it has always gone. A carrier who spilled would freeze in place for the space of a slow breath, and any others nearby would freeze with them, and then all of them would go on.

**The pin-menders** sit in the doorways with small anvils of river stone and tap copper cold, a sound so muffled by moss that it comes as a pressure more than a noise. The rims of doors, the rings on hands, the pins in collars: all of it is constantly touched up and made the same again.

**The goat-folk** live on the upper terraces with the small hornless goats, which are as pale and silent as they are. They milk in the half-dark of their byres and make a soft white curd, pressed in reed baskets and eaten with pickled snail and flatbread of sedge-seed baked in clay ovens. It is a plain diet and nobody seems to want another. The food is cold by the time it reaches a mouth, and it is eaten cold.

And there is one who sits by the **Measure**. There is always one, though it is never the same person for long. They sit on the ring of white stones with their eyes on the waterline, and they do nothing else. They do not record, warn, or call out. If the level moved, they would presumably know. It has not moved, and the sitter goes home at the end of the day with the same look as the person who came.

## Speech

Speech is a thing they save. When a word is needed it is said low, and short, in a tongue with few consonants that hiss or clap. Most of what passes between them passes without it: a lifted chin for *yes*, a turn of the wrist for *wait*, two fingers laid flat on the breastbone for *I am here.* The click of a thumbnail on a tooth has a dozen meanings depending on how many times and how softly, and can stand for a name, a greeting, a question, or a small dry joke.

Names are not spoken at all. Each person has a name-click, a rhythm of ticks, and children learn theirs before they walk. To call someone, you tick their rhythm once, very softly, on any hard thing near you: a tooth, a copper ring against a stone, a fingernail on the lip of a bowl. They turn.

The children play silently. They stack pale pebbles into towers that do not fall, run string figures between their fingers, chase the moths without ever closing a hand on them, and laugh, when they laugh, with the whole body and no sound at all, shoulders shaking, mouths wide, eyes streaming. Adults smile at this without surprise. It is the same laugh they have.

## The Other Residents

The people are not alone here and do not act as if they were. The frogs in their niches are counted among the household, and each niche is somebody's. A person will pass a frog and lower two fingers toward it in a small courtesy, and the frog does not blink. The pale moths on the chalk are left undisturbed, and a lane where they have settled thickly is walked slowly, with the sleeves held in. The voles and dormice in their moss tunnels are heard by no one and feed on what falls. The wrens in the ivy keep their songs folded in their throats, and it is generally believed, though never said, that the wrens are holding something for later.

Everything alive here keeps a private silence, and the people say, in their fashion, that it is a *shared* private silence, held by all of them at once, like a cupped hand passed from one to the next. It is best not to be the one who lets it drop.

## The Held Breath

There is a moment the whole town knows. When a bead of mist gathers at the tip of a leaf and hangs, trembling, before it falls straight down without a sound, everyone who sees it stops. It lasts less than a breath, and in that time the mossers' hands are still, the carriers stand with full scoops, the pin-menders' hammers rest above the copper. The frogs, if they could, would have no need to stop, being already still. Then the drop falls and the town breathes out together, very softly, and the work resumes as if nothing had interrupted it.

Nobody says what the pause is for. If you asked, you would receive a look of mild, patient incomprehension, like being asked why the heart goes on beating.

## What Lies Under

Near the pool, in the deepest lane, a few stones are warm. The people walk past them without altering their pace. Sometimes a child, not yet schooled in what is not spoken of, will crouch and lay both palms flat on one, and stay there with eyes half shut, until a mother's hand comes down gently on the shoulder and lifts the child away. The child is not scolded. The mother's own palm may linger on the stone a moment first.

It is possible that they are not the only ones who live in the Hollow. It is possible that the count on the cord is not only theirs, and that the seep feeding the Measure comes up from something that is also tending, also holding, also very carefully not spilling. The pillar in the center is worn to a glassy smoothness by centuries of palms, and though the air is always cold, a hand laid on it does not come away cold. No one has said what this means, or whether it is the town that holds the Hollow still, or the Hollow that holds the town, or whether the difference matters to anyone who has lived all their lives in the even light, breathing slowly, with green on their fingertips, and a small copper pin at the throat that has been mended so many times it is only ever the same pin.
