# Those Who Live in the Held Breath

Three kinds of things live in the Cup: those who know they are in it, those who don't, and the one that is simply understood. The first are the people.

## The People

They call themselves something that means *the ones who are dripped on*, and it is said fondly, the way a family might name itself for a leaking roof it has loved for generations.

They are small and quick, made for stairs. Their feet are broad and horn-soled, with toes that spread and grip; a child of six can cross a fig-bark bridge in a gale without touching the rope. Their skin is the warm brown of fired clay and always has a faint sheen on it, less sweat than the fog's residue. Their hair is kept short, or braided close to the skull, because damp hair breeds lice and catches on canes. Their eyes are large and dark and slow to narrow. On the rare occasions one of them stands at the rim, in the flat white glare, they weep and squint and hold a hand up like a visor, and they are always relieved to go back down.

They dress in bark-cloth beaten thin as a leaf and in woven fig-fiber, dyed in ochres and the brown of steeped bark. Nothing is bright. Anything that flashes is covered. Even the copper, the hammered bracelets and hairpins of the water-rich, is kept dull with ash and handling, because a glint at the wrong angle might climb the shafts of light and be seen from above.

That is the first rule, and the people keep it in a hundred small ways. No one builds above the level of the canopy. Cooking fires are lit only in the wet gray hour, when the fog swallows smoke, and are banked to coals before the sun burns through. The paths on the plateau leading toward the rim are unmade. Old cairns out on the flat, older than anyone's grandmother, point subtly the wrong way, and small crews of *rim-menders* go up on foggy nights to reset the ones the wind has knocked true. A child who says "someone might see us" is not scolded. She is told she is right, and given a task.

### How they sound

They are quiet people in a loud place. Their speech lives underneath the birds, in a low, clipped murmur, and when the cicadas rise at midday they switch to a language of hands and to a soft, pitched clicking of the tongue that carries exactly one lane's width and stops. Strangers would think them shy. They are simply fluent in the town's acoustics. They know which alleys carry sound upward and which swallow it, and they argue, court, and gossip in the second kind.

What they listen to is the animals. The thrushes sing in shifts, and everyone in the Held Breath has a body-clock set by them. When a shift ends late, someone glances up. When the song *breaks*, when the thrushes go quiet in the wrong hour, the lanes empty in moments. It might be a hawk, or a snake on a roof, or a stranger on the rim. They do not wait to learn which. In the enormous few minutes of a dry storm, when every animal stops at once, the people stop too, sitting in their doorways with their hands in their laps, and no child needs telling.

### What they do

Water is the whole of their economy, and nearly every trade is a kind of water-trade.

**The net-tuners** are the most respected, and the least loud about it. Each roof's fog net is strung to a slightly different gauge, and the mesh must be changed through the year: finer when the fog runs thin and fast, coarser when it runs thick, slackened or tightened as the temperature drops. A good tuner can walk a terrace in the dark, run a palm across forty nets, and say which will run heavy by dawn. They are often half-blind, oddly, from age and from a lifetime of squinting into wet dimness, and their fingers have become the better eyes. They speak of nets as one might speak of temperamental animals.

**The gutter-children** are the town at its most alive. Between the ages of about five and twelve, every child spends the gray hour running the lanes with clay troughs and split-cane channels, joining runnels, clearing blockages, and tilting a spill back onto its course. They are fast, barefoot, and grave. They sing a counting-song under their breath as they work, a rhythm that syncs with the drip. If a child spills water on the ground, the shame is total and silent. Nobody shouts. The child stands in the wet patch until an elder comes and looks at it with them, saying nothing at all.

**The jar-keepers** live in the lower town, in cool dim rooms lined with great glazed vessels taller than a man. They count. They know the level of every jar by the sound of a knuckle rapped on the shoulder of the clay, and they know each household's share, which is inherited and traded and argued over the way other peoples argue over land. Their tally-language is knotted cord and scratched clay tablets, the only writing most of the town needs. A person's standing is spoken of in cupfuls. *She holds nine and a half* means she is well off. *He's thin at the bottom* means he is in debt, or ill, or in disgrace.

The jar-keepers can also taste the Cup's water and tell where it came from, which nets, which hour, which fissure-breath, since the mineral tang shifts with the deep warmth. In a bad year it turns bitter and metallic. They say the Breath is *turning*, and a quiet, careful anxiety moves down the terraces, and the old are asked to remember.

**The root-listeners** are few and strange. They are the ones who descend. Where the fig roots go down into the fissures, there are passages, worn or torn by roots, that lead to the warm places under the town, and someone has to check them: to see whether a channel has silted, whether a root has died, whether the deep air is still sweet. They go down with a small caged thrush on a cord and a lamp of clay and fat. If the bird slumps, they climb out fast, leaving whatever they came for. Their skin is paler than anyone's. They are gentle, tend to be a little deaf from the pressure of the deep, and are treated by everyone with a courtesy that is close to reverence.

**The potters** are the loud exception. They alone own the wheel, and they hammer and thump and shout in a cluster of open-fronted workshops on a mid-terrace, and they are teased for it constantly and indispensable absolutely. Every cup, jar, trough, and channel-tile that keeps the town's water from touching the ground comes from their hands.

There are also the **fig-keepers**, who tend the great trees and do not prune them; the **thatchers**, who work in the evenings, when the wasps are dozy; the **guano-gatherers**, who climb into the fig crowns after the bats leave, carrying baskets, and whose gardens are the richest on the terraces; and the **rim-menders** and the **risers**.

The risers are the traders. A handful of families, and no more, climb out in the fog on moonless nights and walk across the flat to where salt-caravans cross the far edge of the plateau, carrying baskets of dried figs, cane-pith, cord, and a little copper. They come back with salt, obsidian, seed, and news. They are the only ones who can lie fluently. They are respected, and a little feared, for carrying the outside inside them.

### How they eat, and how they end

They eat figs in twenty forms: fresh, dried, pounded, fermented into a sour paste that keeps a season. They eat cane-shoots, snails, swift-eggs taken with strict restraint, the eggs of their own hens, and a porridge of pith and seed. They eat geckos only in famine, and never ants. Fruit is never allowed to rot uneaten. What cannot be eaten goes to the gardens, and what cannot go to the gardens goes to the beetles.

The dead go last, and go up. The old are carried in the gray hour to the highest terrace, a small level shelf right under the rim, where the light is brightest and the fig-shade thinnest, and are laid on woven cane. The vultures come, and then the beetles, and then the roots. A season later, a fig-sapling stands there. No one cuts it. The people of the Held Breath do not say that their ancestors are the trees. They say only, *we don't cut that one*, and the difference is important to them.

## The Others

The people are not the town's rulers, and would be embarrassed by the idea.

The **geckos** are pale, pink-footed, and everywhere, each house's private tenants. A household that stops hearing its geckos click at dusk begins to worry. The **ants** own the lanes. Their roads cross every path, and the people step over them, wait for them, and carry babies around them, and the ants keep the walls clean of mold and the thatch clear of mites. The **wasps** are left alone; a wasp-nest under the eave is a kind of guest-blessing. The **swifts** live in the walls by the thousand and are so integral to the sound of the place that when a house is repaired, a swift-hole is always left.

The **monkeys** are thieves and are accepted as thieves. Nobody hates them. People put a little extra on the roofs, out of a kind of tax. The **civets** slink through the night lanes, spotted and polite. The **bats** pour out at dusk in their smoking spiral, and the whole town stops for a moment to watch them go, every evening, as though some part of them goes up with the bats and might not come back.

And in the deep lower bowl, where the light is green and thin and the fig roots come down like curtains, lives the **flat-faced cat**.

It is large, broad across the skull, tawny going to grey, with pale eyes and a slow way of turning its head. The people do not hunt it and do not feed it. No one has ever, in memory, been harmed by it, though everyone has seen it, at the edge of a lane at dusk, or lying on a warm terrace stone, watching. It keeps the monkeys from multiplying past reason. It takes the occasional pig or goat, and the household that loses one says nothing, having been paid, in an old way, for the shade and the safety of its own doorway.

The people say only that the cat is *understood*. If pressed, the old ones will say that the cat was here before the town, and that the day it leaves is the day the Held Breath should think about going, too. Nobody has ever tested this. The cat is looked at with a kind of sober fondness, the way you might look at the last beam of a house you love.

## The Whole

If you stood at the bottom of the Cup at the gray hour, in the fog, with the dripping starting all around you, you might not be able to tell which sounds were the town and which the forest. The gutter-children's song blends into the thrushes. The tap of water on a net is the same as water on a leaf. A gecko clicks, a woman coughs, a swift shrieks and the sound goes up the terraces and around. It is one voice, held in a bowl of warm stone.

They do not think of themselves as *in* the place, the way you might live in a house. They think of themselves as a part of it, as one of the many things that keep the breath from going out. When they say the Cup is small, and full, and holds only because nothing in it is wasted, they are not reciting. They are describing the shape of themselves.

And overhead, out on the flat, the wind tears its cloth, and no one on the plateau ever looks down.
