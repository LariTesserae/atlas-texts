# The Residents of the Boiler Cleft

Nothing in the Boiler Cleft lives alone, and it is doubtful that anything living there could. Counting the inhabitants is like counting the stitches in a coat. You can do it, but you miss what they are for. The cleft holds perhaps a hundred thousand animals of six or seven kinds, and after an hour inside you begin to suspect there is only one resident, with many bodies and a single hour-long day.

## The Shingle

The moths are the most numerous, and they set the room's character. They are pale as paper, each about the size of a palm, with wings the texture of old book-cloth and faces so small and blunt that they seem incidental. They have no use for their mouths. They feed by laying the long, soft, unrolled tongue against a bead of condensation and drawing up the thin mineral sugar the vent gives off. Feeding takes them the whole drip-rain, and they never seem hungry, only attentive.

They hold the walls in overlapping ranks, each moth's wings tucked beneath its upslope neighbor's like roof slates, so that rain, when it comes, sheds downward across a thousand backs and no one is left dry. This is architecture, though no moth designed it. When the first drop falls, the lifting of their edges begins at the bottom and climbs, each moth raising its wings a moment after the one beneath it. What looks like one gesture is a relay of ten thousand small ones, and it runs a little slower than seems natural, as if the room were thinking about drinking.

The moths are also the engine's audience. In the half-second pause at the top of each stroke they go rigid, and the stiffening travels outward from the flywheel like a shiver. A moth that hangs from a spoke rides the wheel around, lifts off at the bottom, and resettles a spoke later. They seem to enjoy this in the way a dozing animal enjoys being carried. Nobody could say whether they enjoy anything. But the moths on the wheel are always the fattest.

## The Masons

The beetles are the only inhabitants who make things. Their backs are flat and copper-colored, a little green at the rim, and they pack so tightly that the wall beneath the moths looks armored in coin. They are slow and heavy, and they do not yield to a boot. The cause is not stubbornness. A beetle that has settled has entered a kind of duty, and it does not respond to a large indifferent foot any more than a brick does.

Their work is the mosaics. A mason walks, sometimes for an hour, carrying a flake of verdigris or a brass filing or a washer nearly as wide as its own body, held in the mandibles with the rim tilted up against its head like a shield. It brings the piece to a crack and presses it into the mud-cement its own kind secreted there. Then it goes back for another. Near the engine the results are close to intentional: green rings, tight spirals, and a repeating toothed shape that looks so much like a gear that you feel you should apologize for noticing. Farther out, toward the walls, the mosaics thin and coarsen, the pieces set at random angles, the spirals broken. The pattern is spreading outward from the machine and losing information as it goes, like a rumor or a hymn.

Whether the masons are copying the engine or the engine's shape has simply soaked into them, through warmth and rhythm and the bacterial film on the pipes they eat, is not known. Younger beetles, born in the outer cracks, sometimes wander inward and are seen pausing at the old mosaics, antennae working over the green work as if reading it. Then they pick up a filing and go on.

The masons keep the pipes clean, too. They graze the warm slick of bacteria off the steam line, and it may be that the pipe's seals stay damp and sound because the beetles keep them so. If so, the engine has been maintained for years by an animal that has never known what it maintains.

## The Bronzed

The frogs are translucent as wet paper when they are young. Their small organs show through the belly, and when the fog is thick you can watch a heart go by. They are irritable, quick, and the only animals in the cleft with anything resembling opinions. When you step among them they hop aside with a little grunt of offense and settle again a hand's width away, facing the same way as before.

That direction is important. Before the fog rises, the frogs turn as one toward the vent, and they hold that stance, silent, until the first drops fall. It is the nearest thing the room has to a prayer, and the frogs are its priests.

During the drip-rain the older ones move under the leaks in the steam pipe and sit in the falling condensate. The water is faintly metallic, and over years it does something to them. A bronze bloom gathers on the skin, first as dust, then as a glaze, and finally as something like a casting. The oldest frogs, in the deepest crannies, are copper through and through. They are stiff and dull-eyed and perfectly still, and they sit among the valves and gauges so like the fittings that you cannot easily say where machine ends and animal begins. Some of the fittings blink. Some do not, and have not for a long time, and may be only the shapes of frogs. No one has tried to find out which. The other frogs leave them undisturbed and seem to defer to them slightly, in the way one defers to a grandparent who no longer speaks.

When the engine stalls, it is the frogs who sound the alarm. The piping is thin and high and continuous, and it comes from every throat at once, including, some listeners insist, from a few of the bronzed ones, whose mouths do not move.

## The Slow Ones

The snails are the polishers. Their shells are dull-shined from centuries of neighbors rubbing past, and they graze the pale ferns in the drip-rings and the grey lichen on the north blocks, the only true plant-eaters in the room. The greenery is too sparse to feed them all, and they know it, in whatever way a snail knows. They move hardly at all, and they are almost always where the rain will be. Now and then a shell clicks against another. It is the sound of the room's most patient conversation, one word an hour.

## The Threads

Between everything, in every gap too narrow for anything else, live the threadlike centipedes, translucent and faintly luminous where they cross a beam of light. They are the room's mortar. They are eaters of small dead things, and no one ever sees them do anything but pass, one thin pale line after another, through the cracks of the crowd. The crowd tolerates them as a body tolerates its own blood.

## The Appetite

The cleft is not innocent. Pale spiders live in silk tunnels threaded through the moth-shingle, and they take a moth occasionally, leaving a wing on the wall like a dropped page. The moths around the gap never seem to notice. The shingle mends itself.

The one large centipede is the true predator. It is banded and slow and the length of a forearm. It patrols the frog shelves, and every few hours it takes one frog, unhurried, without a chase, as though it were collecting a debt. The crowd closes over the gap almost before the centipede has finished. The frogs seem to have arranged themselves around this arrangement: no one is ever missed, and no one is ever spared. It has probably been the same centipede for a very long time. It might even be the room's oldest resident, and the crowd may be shaped around its rounds, the way a village is shaped around a road.

## What They Make Together

Taken together, the inhabitants make a creature. It has a heart, the engine, and a pulse, its stroke, and a breath, the vent. It has weather. It has a digestion (the centipede, the threads, the bacterial film), a memory in the beetles' mosaics, and a slow hardening skeleton in the bronzed frogs. It has a nervous system of sorts, the ripple of wings and the turning of small bodies to face the same direction. It has a fear. When the engine stalls, the creature unlocks itself and rushes toward its own heart, beetles pouring down the walls and moths rising in a column, and the frogs keep up their thin insistent noise until the wheel turns. Then it sits back down, slightly rearranged, as if it had briefly forgotten who it was and had been reminded.

Whether the engine drives the crowd or the crowd keeps the engine going, there is no one to ask. The belt still runs from the flywheel to a shaft that turns something under the rubble. Perhaps it turns nothing. Perhaps it turns what the creature is for. The inhabitants of the Boiler Cleft do not wonder. They lean on one another, wet and warm and exactly full, and they wait through the hour for the vent to breathe.
