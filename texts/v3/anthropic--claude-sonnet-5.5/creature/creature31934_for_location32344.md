# The Worked

The people of the town call themselves *the Worked*, a name that started as a joke and set hard. It comes from the doors. Traders from upriver call them the Sheathed, which is the word for a house gone dead, and the townspeople take it as they would being called corpses to their faces: without offense, but without warmth. Something over a thousand of them live inside the white blister, packed like seeds in a fruit.

## The Body Learns the Town

You would know them by their hands first. Every adult has the same hands: broad in the thumb, ridged across the pads, the fingertips worn so smooth from rasping that many have no clear prints. The nails are short and chalky and often split. The forearms are corded from a lifetime of scraping stone at an angle. The hands are also never empty. A Worked person carries a cup on a cord, or a small rasp in a belt loop, or a bundle of reeds, or a child. A pair of idle hands is faintly suspicious. It suggests someone is waiting for something to set.

The skin is dry. Lime leaches the oil out of it, so everyone rubs in fish fat or pressed reed-seed oil, and the whole town smells faintly of it, along with wet stone and the egg-sweet breath of the vents. The shins, forearms, and palms of the young are crosshatched with small white scars. These are *rim-kisses*, from running along the serrated outer terraces before the foot-sense comes in. Nobody scolds a child for them. The scars fade by adolescence, and the adults say a person with none never learned the town.

They walk in a particular way, with a short sliding tread, toes slightly spread, weight low, the feet lifting less than they seem to. Soles are thick and yellow-grey. Visitors on the wet stone go down like felled animals. The Worked cross the same stone while carrying full jars and talking. On a ladder they move without looking. On a dark lane they run the backs of their fingers along the wall as they go, lightly, the way you might touch a sleeping dog, and if the wall feels wrong, too thick or too sharp or too dry, they stop and say so.

The lime is also in them. Nearly everyone past middle age passes gravel, and old people sometimes pass stones the size of a lentil. This is spoken of with the same humor as the rest of it: *you're growing*, *you're laying down*, *the town's getting into you*. Willow-bark tea and reed-root decoctions are taken for it. The very old are stiff in the joints, and their eyebrows and lashes carry a permanent white bloom from a lifetime of mist. Their eyes are narrowed against the glare that the stone throws back at them.

They dress for water. Kilts and wraps of felted reed fiber and thin leather are hitched high, and rain-cloaks of overlapped reed hang from pegs beside every door. Hems dry stiff and pale with a bloom of salt. A white hem is not considered dirty. It is considered lived-in, and a fresh new garment marks the wearer as a child or a newcomer.

## The Breath in the Blood

Every Worked person feels the Breath. It is not a thing they listen for. Somewhere in the ankles and the low back, a few moments before the water rises, there is a loosening, like the drop before a sneeze. Women at looms lift their work off the floor. Men on ladders climb higher. Toddlers, who cannot speak, stop mid-step and look at their own feet. A baby raised in the town will sleep through a thunderstorm but wake before the Breath and stare at the ceiling.

Those who leave and come back after years often find the sense gone. They stand in the lane as the water rises around their shins, surprised and a little ashamed. The town calls them *deaf*, not unkindly. The sense usually returns in a season or two, and sometimes it doesn't.

Because of this, the Worked keep time in Breaths. A task takes *three Breaths*. A journey is *eleven Breaths to the ford*. Meals, sleep, and the length of a quarrel are measured by it. There is no word for hour. Days are counted by rasping: the first rasp of morning, the last rasp of evening, and the many quiet scrapes between.

## The Morning Sound

Before dawn, when the drips are the only voice, a dry, rhythmic scratching begins in one lane and spreads. It is a thousand people, or nearly, working their thresholds. It sounds like a very large animal gnawing something patiently. When it finishes, the town has been renewed by exactly one night's growth. Everyone has checked that everyone else has answered. A house that does not rasp is noticed within a Breath or two, and someone will knock on its lintel, then call, then, if there is no answer, go in.

The greeting is built on this. *Is your door open?* someone asks, and the reply is *Worked this morning.* It means *hello*, *how are you*, *I'm alive*, and *I'm holding my own* all at once. A different reply, *Not yet*, is a request for help, and it is answered at once and without questions.

## Hands, Trades, and Standing

The town has no lords, and no one who could be called a chief. It has depth. A family's standing is the thickness of the floor under its oldest hearth, a slab of layered stone that can be read like the rings of a tree. The old households, *the Deep Floors*, own the central chambers and the best claim on the vent-channels. They tend to be quiet, careful, and faintly sad, as if living on top of something they can't quite see. The newer families live on the outer terraces, where the stone is still soft and the work never stops, and they are louder and quicker. There is envy in both directions. A Deep Floor envies the freedom of the rim. A rim family envies being already half-held.

Within this, people are known by what they do with their hands:

**The Sawyers** cut with cord and sand while the stone is still soft. They are the town's builders, and the town's cleverest people, because they must guess how a slab will harden and where the water will want to go next. They talk to the stone in a low, continuous mutter, a habit they deny.

**The Jar-growers** are slow, methodical, and famously unhurried in speech. They set a clay or wicker form into a trickling channel and wait a season, sometimes two. Their reputation is that they can outwait anything. They are asked to settle disputes because nobody wants to argue with someone who has spent their life watching stone form around a pot. A good grown jar is passed down for generations and is sometimes buried with its owner, then dug out again if the family is poor.

**The Rimwards** live on the outermost terraces and tend the low waterfall, the reed beds, the scoop-baskets, and the dead. They are weathered, watchful, and often solitary. They are the ones who speak to traders.

**The Listeners** are less a trade than a knack. They can hear every terrace-tone in the town and know at once when one changes. A shift in a note means a shelf has thickened or cracked, and a house below may be in danger. They walk the lanes at dusk with their heads tilted. They also guide the lost, calling out *toward the deep tone* or *away from the reed tone*, and there is no map of the town but the one they carry in their ears.

**The Sleepers' Keepers**, a small group at the lowest terrace, tend the sheathed dead. This is a task of restraint. Too much rasping erases a face. Too little lets the flow blur it before its time. Families argue about it, sometimes bitterly, each quietly certain the keepers have gotten it wrong.

## The Dead and the Deep

The Worked are not afraid of being turned to stone. It is what happens to everyone and everything, and they take a slow satisfaction in it. The sheathed dead on the lowest terrace are greeted by children who pass them, a touch on the shoulder or a word. Adults do the same, less openly. Over decades the figures lose their features, the nose first and the fingers last, until each is a smooth white rise like a drift of snow. The oldest are barely shapes. Some families keep a small rasp at the foot of an ancestor and give a stroke at the cheek every year on the day of death, a kind of maintenance of memory.

The center is another matter. There the lanes are closing faster than anyone can rasp, and the old families who live nearest it do so with a mix of pride and dread. Each of them has a neighbor's chamber sealed on the other side of a wall, still furnished, still holding a cup on a shelf. No one opens them. It is not forbidden in words, only in the way that certain questions are not asked at a meal. The very old sometimes move toward the center in their last years, into rooms with a single working door. What they intend, and whether they ever ask to stop rasping, is not discussed. The town says only: *everything is meant to be held.*

## Speech, Manner, and Mind

They speak softly, because the lanes carry every word, and privacy in the town is a courtesy of pretending not to hear. Arguments are conducted in low voices and sometimes at a distance, one party on a ladder above the other. Shouting is for emergencies, or for the Breath.

The temperament is patient but never idle: watchful, dry-humored, unsentimental about the loss of things and very sentimental about small daily care. They do not trust stillness. They rarely say *forever*. Their sense of time is layered, a person as a thin film laid over their mother's floor, over her mother's, over the first hearth. A common insult is *unlaid*, someone with no thickness, no history, no floor. A common blessing is *may your floor be deep*, meaning *may you be remembered by stone.*

They are hospitable to strangers in a careful, formal way, and slightly appalled by how they move. A trader with a bag of obsidian is given a mat on a frame, never on the floor. He is told, kindly, not to set the bag down.

## The Other Residents

The Worked share the town with a great many smaller lives.

The **snails** are dark, small as lentils, and countless. They graze the algae films so densely that the pool floors seem to crawl. Children scoop them for roasting, and the shells are crushed into a scouring powder for pots.

The **pale fish** in the warm pools are thumb-long and nearly transparent, with the shadow of the spine visible along the belly. They are caught with scoop-baskets and eaten whole, and they are considered luck by the household that catches the first one after each Breath.

The **swifts** nest in the roofed lanes in mud cups glued to the ceilings, and no one has ever harmed one. They skim the wet corridors in flocks at dusk, so close to shoulders that people learn to walk without flinching. Their droppings are a nuisance and a mild blessing. A house with a nest above its door is said to be *held from above*.

The **heron** on the outer rim at dawn is watched, not fed and not driven off. It is regarded as an auditor. It stands still in a town that does not trust stillness, and it is the only living thing there that is permitted to do so.

The **algae** in the warm pools, orange, lime, and rope-brown, are tended almost as a crop. In the deepest pools they glow blue-green at night when disturbed, and the children write their names in it with a finger. The adults say it is only the growth talking to itself. But some of them, on nights when the Breath is late, sit by the vent-pools longer than they need to, with their hands in the water, as if listening for an answer.

The **rats** are rumored, and never seen. This is said with a straight face by everyone.
