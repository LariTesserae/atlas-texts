# The Lodged

The people of the vug call themselves the Lodged, as a splinter is lodged in a thumb or a seed in a crack. They mean it plainly. They are not proud of it, and they are not ashamed. It is only the correct word for how they sit in the mountain: wedged, held, and always slightly at risk of being pushed out by growth.

## The body the vug made

Generations of thick air, thin air, and stooping have given them a particular build. They are short and deep-chested, with wide ribs that flare like bellows and shoulders rounded from years of ducking under crystal. The legs are strong and bowed from stair-climbing. The hands are the thing you notice. They are large for the body, broad across the knuckles, and glazed at the fingertips with a fine grey sheen where quartz dust has worked into the skin and never left. A Lodged child of ten can hang from a girder by three fingers and talk while doing it.

They are pale, since no sun has touched them in eleven generations, but it is not the pallor of sickness. It is the color of candle wax, and under the lamps it takes on the rust and brass of everything else. Their eyes are large and very dark, the pupils slow to close. In the furnace flare they squint and turn their faces aside with a gesture so common that it has become a courtesy, a small bow to the fire. Many have a faint sheen at the back of the eye, and when a lamp swings past you may catch a pair of them looking at you from a catwalk, gold for a moment, then gone.

Their lungs are their weakness. The dust of the vug is fine, sharp, and endless, and by middle age most carry what they call the *chalk*, a dry, patient cough that is the sound of aging here. You can tell a person's years from the cough alone. Children have none. The old have a low ratcheting rasp, and it is so familiar that grandchildren find it comforting. They fall asleep to it.

They are scarred, and they wear the scars openly. Quartz opens skin cleanly. Every adult has a pale crosshatch on forearms and shins, and a good scar is a kind of résumé. Because pigment is imported and dear, the only color the Lodged can afford to put on their bodies is soot mixed with mite-wax, pricked into the skin with a fishbone needle. The tattoos are small and dark and mostly on the inside of the wrist, where the forearm is touched before a sentence. They are usually a mark of trade, a tiny gear, a bellows, a key, or a single line for the number of heaves survived.

## Names and shadows

A child is named for the trade of the parent who holds it first, and for the crystal beneath which it was born. *Isk of the Low Smoky. Tamra Wirecut, Fifth Fluorite.* Over a lifetime the second name changes several times, and the change is the only ceremony most people get. Marriage, apprenticeship, a bad accident, or a great kindness can each trade one name for another.

They also believe that everyone has two shadows, and they are the only people who could. The quartz throws them, and the Lodged say the second shadow is the one who came before, an ancestor leaning the other way to steady you. They do not step on each other's shadows. When someone dies, the second shadow is said to stay behind in the crystal, and on some walls you can point to a shape cast by a lamp that no longer has a body to belong to. Nobody removes these lamps.

## How they talk

They speak low, close, and slowly, with a hand on your forearm to open the sentence. The gesture is so ingrained that a Lodged person speaking to a stranger without it will be seen as either furious or lying. Before you say a thing, you touch, and the touch means: *this voice is mine, and it is here.* The reply is a squeeze or a small nod. It is one of the reasons the city feels so tender. Every conversation begins with contact.

Across gaps, over chasms, and from one level to another, where voices don't carry, they use lamp-talk. A cage-lamp with a copper shutter is flicked in patterns, quick and fluent, and whole arguments are conducted in blinks across the dark. Children learn it before speech. If you sit on a high walkway during the busy hours, the cavern seems to twitch and murmur all around you, points of light stuttering to one another across the void.

Their spoken language has few words for weather and many for pressure. They have eleven ways to say *tight*, and each means something different about a wall, a chest, a seam, or a debt.

## What they do

Everyone works, and work is sorted by depth.

The **deep-crews** tend the furnaces and the fumarole works. They are the strongest, the most scarred, and the most fatalistic. They wear leather aprons stitched over felt and carry long iron hooks for the furnace doors. They joke in a rough, cheerful, gallows way and are said to be the only people in the city who laugh loudly.

The **mite-wives**, who are not all wives and not all women, manage the foundry floors. They walk barefoot through the red carpet of furnace-mites, harvesting the waxy paste with flat brass scrapers and singing to keep the colonies calm. They insist the mites listen. Nobody has proved otherwise.

The **wirecutters** work the diamond wire that carves streets through quartz. It is quiet, precise, dangerous work, and they are the closest thing the Lodged have to priests. They know which crystals are bearing and which are ornament, and they read stress the way other people read faces. A wirecutter's apprenticeship lasts twenty years, and the last lesson is to refuse a job.

The **drivers** ride the haulers, murmuring to them all day in a language of hums and clicks. They love their animals in a slightly embarrassed way and would deny it. A driver whose hauler dies wears a strip of its hide on the left wrist for a year.

The **bellows-watch** is everyone. Every adult keeps a shift at the great pumps, and the rota is as sacred as anything. You will see a master smith and a scrawny apprentice side by side at the treadles at the dead hour of the cycle, breathing in time, not speaking. The pressure does not care who you are.

The **cistern-wardens** wear their keys on cords around the neck, worn smooth and dark from touch. They are the nearest thing to magistrates. They hear disputes at the cisterns, where the brass reflects your face back at you distorted, which is thought to encourage honesty.

The **gong-keepers** live in the signal-lofts, and they are the strangest of the trades. They spend their lives listening. They do not talk much, having no need. They can tell from the ring of the crystal whether a fracture is a hairline or a fault, and in an emergency they strike the great bronze once, exactly, and the entire city stops moving.

## The unspoken creed

The Lodged are not religious in a way outsiders would recognize. They have no gods and few stories. They have habits so old that they function as faith.

They keep the bellows fed. They count the water. They leave the roof crystals alone. And they do not whistle near the vents.

That last is the strangest. Ask why, and you get shrugs, a grin, a change of subject. But there are the **vent-sitters**, a handful of old men and women who spend their shifts on stools beside the deep fumaroles with a hand flat on the warm iron, doing nothing. They are fed by the community and excused from the bellows-watch. They say they are listening. If you press them, they say the vents have a pitch, and that the pitch is sometimes a little different from what the physics would give it, and that in the hours before a heave it drifts toward something like a note held in the throat by a person about to speak.

They do not say what they think it is. They say only that it is unwise to give it an answer before it has finished asking.

## Food, drink, and the only allowed waste

They eat mite-paste, sweet and faintly metallic, pressed into cakes, fried, fermented, whipped into foam, and pounded with dried moth-wing into a grey flour that tastes of chalk and honey. They eat ratchet-lizard on feast days. They drink the bitter condensate and, when they can, mite-spirit, a clear, fiery liquor distilled in copper coils in the back of every workshop. The spirit is legal and, in moderation, encouraged. Water is not a thing you spill, or waste, or splash. A Lodged child who knocks over a cup is not scolded. The child is held very still, and the adults look at the puddle in silence for a moment, the way one might look at a wound.

There is one exception. When a Lodged person dies, the body is carried, by relatives on their shoulders, through the length of the city to the raw crystal at the far end, where the lamps do not reach. It is laid down among the untouched spires. Then the family pours out one full measure of their own ration, freely, onto the clear quartz, and walks away. It is the only water in the vug that is ever deliberately given to the mountain. The mist rises and vanishes. The mourners do not look back. They return by another way, by the high catwalks, with their collars up.

## The heave

Twice a year the city holds on. The heave comes up from below like a slow cough in the ground, the lamps swing, the crystals sing in their broken choir, and everyone seizes the nearest solid thing. Children are lashed to their parents. Haulers kneel and moan. The Lodged sit together in knots on the catwalks and in the doorways, arms linked, and pass around the mite-spirit and sing very quietly. The songs are old and shapeless, hummed more than sung, and they follow the pitch of the crystals, so that what you hear is not a human melody but a duet between people and the mountain, each adjusting to the other.

Someone always cries. Someone always laughs. Someone tells the story of the heave when the whole third gallery came away and no one died, and someone else corrects the number of survivors, and everyone hushes them. And when the shaking stops, they do not cheer. They sit for a while longer, hands on one another's forearms, feeling the pulse under the skin, and then they go back to work. The scaffolds are already going up.

## The ones who leave, and the ones who come

Very occasionally an outsider arrives through the Slot: a trader with sacks of salt and seed and dyestuff, wheezing and giddy from the ridge, stumbling through the hide curtains with frost in his beard. The Lodged are patient with him. They sit him down, put a cup of thin bitter water in his hands, and wait for his breath to steady. They think outsiders are very loud, very fast, and oddly naked, with their voices flung out into the air without a hand to anchor them. They buy his paint. They hold the little pots to the lamp and watch the color, blue or green or red, like a flame in a jar. Then they put them away for a wedding or a burial, and use them once.

The ones who leave, the exiles and the restless, are said to go out along the ridge. They do not come back. Their names are said once at the next gong, and after that their second shadows are left to look after themselves.

## The last quiet

At the edge of the city, where the lamps stop, there is a spot where the Lodged sometimes go alone. It is not a shrine. There is no marker. It is only a place where the crystal is clear and cold, and where the noise of the town has thinned to nothing. They stand there with a hand on the wall and listen. The mountain rises. The quartz ticks, once, then again. Somewhere behind them, a long way off, the gong sounds and rolls and fades, and the low tone of the turbines returns. They turn around and walk back toward the light, their two shadows swinging ahead of them, the one they were born with and the one that came to hold them up.
