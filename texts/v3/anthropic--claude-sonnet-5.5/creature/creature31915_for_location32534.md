# Who Lives in the Lowfold

Begin with the smallest residents, because they are the ones you will believe in.

## The moths and the waders

The pale moths, broad as a palm, are born in the ribbed domes half sunk in the sand. Their larvae feed on the sweet metallic note in the air and grow slowly, in the dark, in the warm hollows of the seamless walls. Nobody has seen a moth die. In the last weeks of their lives they drift toward the tower bases at dusk and hang on the amber glow with their wings open, and the light seems to go into them. Later their wings are found on the black sand, translucent and emptied, weighing nothing, as if the tower had drunk them back.

The waders in the pools are patient in a way that looks like manners. They are long-legged and the color of wet slate, and each holds its territory of shallows by standing in it. They fish by stillness and then by a sudden speed the eye can't follow. What they catch is small and pale and rises from the deep water, and this is the odd part: the fish come up from below, not in from the sides. The waders never step past the ringing ferns into the dark middle of the pool. They face it while they stand, and their heads tilt a few degrees, the same few degrees as everything else.

## The grazers

The tall grey grazers cross the basin in single file. They are the only large animals, and they seem to be the only ones who understand the terms of the place. They stop when you stop. They walk the reed fields, whose stalks lean toward the towers, and they eat only the stalks that lean the wrong way. Their dung is dark and dense, and the reeds grow well from it. They come near no tower, and they cross only where the ground is cold under their feet. They walk the way people walk past a house where someone is sleeping.

If you follow them for a day, you learn that they do not move at random. Over a season they make a very slow, very wide circle around the central plain, and in the season the circle closes they lie down in the reeds at its farthest point and do not eat. Then the ground hums, and the towers go silent, and the grazers lift their heads together, all of them, and hold them up until it ends.

## The tenders

Something keeps the causeways. That much is plain from the trimmed edges of the succulent mats, the cleared drains, and the leaves swept into neat drifts against the arcade walls. The work is done in the dim hours, and nothing is left behind, not a footprint, not a dropped leaf.

What you may glimpse is a low ripple in the air, a shimmer like heat over a road. It moves along the ground in a slow, deliberate line and vanishes at a tower door. If you were patient, and did not look straight at it, you might learn a few things.

They are not invisible. They are *unlit*. Their bodies are made of the same matte material as the towers, worked thin and pliable, and in this light they can't be seen any more than a hole can. The ripple is only the warmth of their passing, the pavement giving up what it had taken from them. They are low and many-limbed, or perhaps they are one long thing moving in sections. They work at a pace that leaves no room for haste, and they are not, in any sense that matters to them, servants.

They don't repair the steps. Nobody repairs them. The white scar that closes across a cracked stair at dusk is the tenders' true work, though no one has ever watched it done. It is a matter of pressure and patience, a thousand hands laid on the seam. Then they go, and the closing looks like the world's own doing.

They are the most numerous inhabitants of the Lowfold, and they have no name for themselves, because they have no need to be told apart. If one were removed the others would not miss it. They would simply close over the gap, as the step closes.

## The towers

Here the description grows uncertain. It is not clear that the towers are *dwellings*. It is not clear that they are not.

Every tower has a door, low and narrow, at the level of the ground, and it is never closed. Warmth comes out of these doors in the evening, and the amber light climbs the inside walls from the base. At the hour when they glow you can see, if you stand close, that the light is not a lamp. It has a grain, a slow current in it, a rising and settling like the movement of a sleeper's breath. The tenders enter at dusk and are not seen to leave, and the glow begins soon after.

One reading is that the towers are hives, and the tenders their makers. Another is that the towers are the living part and the tenders their cells, their attendants, the way a reef is tended by what it shelters. A third is the strangest and the most persuasive. The towers are *listeners*. Their tilt, the tone each one gives to the wind, the cold that draws water from the air, all of it is the shape of an ear turned inward. Each tower was grown, or agreed to be here, to attend to one thing, and the glow at dusk is what attending looks like from outside.

When two towers sound together and the air thickens between them, the hair lifts on your arms because you have stepped across a line of attention. You are for a moment between the listener and what it hears.

## The walkers

The pavement is warm where someone has walked. This is true of the fresh paths, and it means the tenders, and the grazers on their crossings, and the occasional traveler who did not know the rules.

The oldest paths are warm always. They lead to the plain of glass, and they are warm because something still walks them. It walks them all the time and has done so since before the tenders' oldest memory, before the moths, before the basin finished opening. It is not seen because it is not passing *over* the ground. It moves within the warmth, and the warmth is the trace.

The tenders do not walk the old paths. When a tenders' line meets one, it bends and follows beside it, a hand's width away, the way the ivy keeps a hand's width from the wall.

## What turns

At the center is the glass plain, and under the glass something turns.

It is not a reflection. It is very far down, deeper than the pools, though the pools are connected to it and their cold is its cold. The water that gathers at the tower bases in the night runs down the canals, into the fissures, into the black pools, and down through them past the point where a pebble stops being heard. The dew is a kind of offering, or a kind of drinking. It is unclear which. The city condenses the sky and pours it down a long throat, and something at the bottom turns a little more slowly for it, or a little faster.

It is the oldest inhabitant, and the only one for whom the word *inhabit* is true without qualification. Everything else in the Lowfold is arranged around it: the tilt of the towers, the walking of the old paths, the circle of the grazers, the tilt of the waders' heads. The tenders keep the roads clear so it can be approached, though it has never asked to be.

Its nature is not something the place will say. It might be a sleeper. It might be a seed, and the basin its first opening. It might be the crust's own slow attention, the young land noticing itself. When the ground hums and the towers fall silent, it is the moment when the thing beneath turns over, and the whole city holds its breath, and every listener listens hardest.

## What you are

You, standing at the edge of the plain at the lit hour, are an inhabitant too, for as long as you stay. The rules of the place already apply to you. Your distances are honest only when you walk alone. Whoever looked back will arrive late. Your footprints will be warm for an hour, and the tenders will be careful not to step in them.

The towers lean toward the center, and after a while you notice that you are leaning too.
