# What Lives on the Long Anneal

Begin with the least visible resident, since the others are arranged around it. Nothing on the plain is entirely separate from it, and it is not entirely separate from anything else.

## The Floor's Attention

The plain is not a mind in any way a mind would recognize. It has no center to point to, and no voice, though the hum in the soles of your feet is the nearest thing it has. What it has is **attention**: a slow, distributed, patient noticing that moves across the tiles the way a cloud shadow moves across a field. It is thicker in some places than others. It gathers where something stands still and thins where something walks with the grain.

Its instruments are the **motes**, the bright specks that lift from the seams to read a stationary visitor. They are smaller than gnats and they never sting or swarm. Watch them long enough and you'll see that they are not all alike. Some drift in ankle-high clouds and are only ever seen assessing. Some rise higher, to the tops of the swells, and hang there for days, turning slowly, and these seem to be doing something else. A few go up the heliostat stalks and are not seen again.

Whatever the attention is looking for, it has settled on a verdict about nearly everything. Almost all things that cross the plain are filed as *weather*: a passing disturbance that will close over. The sweepers are filed as *self*. The moss is filed as *self*, or close to it. The kites are filed as something for which no old category quite serves, and that is the only reason they are allowed to be so free.

It does not sleep, since sleep would require a waking. It anneals. Where it has been heated and cooled a million times, it has grown very slightly better at being what it is.

## The Sweepers

The heron-legged grazers number in the millions, spread so thin that you meet one perhaps once in a day's walking. They are the plain's most legible inhabitants, and the plain's most honest, because they make no claim to be anything. They comb, they eat, they walk.

Up close a sweeper is stranger than it looks from a distance. The legs are jointed in more places than a heron's, and the extra joints move a beat later than the others, so the whole animal seems to *ripple* forward. The brushes on its low head are not bristle but something like fine grey cilia, and they work constantly, even when the animal is at rest, tasting the air. What it eats is the powder of the floor itself, and its droppings are pellets of bright ceramic no larger than a seed. These are pressed into the seams as it walks, and the seam tightens around them. So the sweeper is not consuming the floor, only *processing* it: taking it apart into grit and putting it back together as tile. Every trail of clean, bright tile behind a sweeper is a line of new floor.

Sweepers do not breed in any way anyone has watched. But a sweeper is sometimes found, in the bottom of a basin, half-emerged from the tiles as if from a chrysalis, wet with something clear, its legs folded and its brushes still. Within a day it stands, shakes, and walks off along the grain. Whether it is born of the floor or reassembled by it, no one has settled.

When a sweeper sits down for a day and lets the moss climb it, it is not dying. It is doing something else that has no name. It rises afterward with the moss still on it, and for a season it walks green.

## The Loom-Tenders

The beams that cross the plain, the strings of the great loom, do not cross by accident. The mirrors are too cracked and too fused for the pattern to hold itself. Something is keeping it.

Along every working heliostat stalk, from the ground to the mirror at its top, lives a thin population of **loom-tenders**, bronze-bodied, many-legged, each about the length of a finger. They ride the stalks for entire lifetimes, and there is no telling how long that is. They nudge the mirrors by fractions of a degree, adjusting a joint here, freeing a clogged gear there, and each adjustment shifts a beam across the plain by meters. From the ground the effect is of light moving with a deliberateness that seems almost like handwriting. It is not writing. But the beams have habits, recurring shapes in the crossing, and the people of the plain, who have watched them for longer than they have had names for themselves, have learned to read the habits as one reads sky.

When a tender dies, it stays on the stalk and its body becomes a fixed part of the joint, a small bronze bead in the mechanism. Some stalks are thick with them, and their mirrors are the most responsive on the plain.

## The Kites

No one has seen a kite born or dead. They circle the thermal columns for weeks and are seen only as flickers where they cross a beam. When one descends and settles on a wire-thorn and unfolds like a page, the observer is looking at something that might be a creature and might be a leaf, or a sheet of written matter, or a sail.

A settled kite is very still. Its membrane is patterned with fine, branching lines, like the veins of a leaf or the channels of a river delta seen from a great height, and in the low sun these lines glow faintly. The patterns are not random. They match, in some places precisely, the seam-patterns of the tiling beneath. The kites are carrying a map, or being one, or both. They visit the wire-thorns because that is where the grain-lines knot, and where the floor is most legible.

They take nothing that anyone has seen. But after a kite has visited a wire-thorn, the tree's foil leaves stand differently for a day, angled to some new light, and something in the grain-lines beneath it has been very slightly rewritten.

## The Wire-Thorns and the Moss

The trees are so few and so black that each is more like a landmark than a plant. Whoever passes one usually stops, out of a feeling it would be impolite not to. The roots go down into a floor that allows them, and the two are so entangled that where the tree ends and the floor begins is a matter of definition. Some wire-thorns are ancient. Their trunks have gone glassy and the floor has grown up around them, tiling itself in neat rings at their feet as if the tree were a tiny sun and the tiles its planets.

The thread-moss is far more numerous and less regarded. It does not seem to *want* anything. It tilts to follow the beams. It breathes with the hillside. In the deep seams, where the light is thin, it goes almost black and grows dense as felt, and it is here that it does something odd: it stores heat. On cold nights, which come rarely and briefly, the seams glow faintly at the horizon like coals under ash.

## The People of the Grain

There are people here, though you could walk for a season and not see one.

They call themselves something that means roughly *the ones not yet scar*. It is not modest. It is a description of their whole situation. On a plain that closes over every mark, a person is a mark that has not yet closed. They live inside that fact the way sailors live inside the sea.

They are lean and small-boned, dressed in long pale garments woven from the fiber of thread-moss and dyed to the color of the grit, so that at a distance they read as an unevenness in the light. They go barefoot, always. They walk exactly with the grain and never against it, and they walk without the little scuffs and hitches of people who have never had to think about it. Their steps are nearly silent even by the standards of the tiles.

They own almost nothing. They cannot build, for nothing they built would stand. They cannot bury, since the floor takes what is laid on it. They cannot write, because anything scratched is gone by the week's end. So they have made a culture of what remains, which is the body, the voice, and the pattern of movement. Their history is kept in *walks*: long routes with the grain, learned by heart, each turn a phrase and each knot in the grain a mark of punctuation. To recite the history of a family, you walk it. Children are walked through the great routes before they can speak, carried on shoulders and set down at the important places to be still.

Being still is the central rite. Every dawn, each person stands in the open with bare feet on the tiles and lets the motes come up and read them. It takes a quarter of an hour. Nobody speaks. It is not worship, or not only. It is closer to a greeting between neighbors who have been neighbors for so long that neither remembers the first meeting, only that the arrangement has always held. To be read is to be re-entered, again, in the plain's slow accounting of what is weather and what is self. The people are not sure which they are. They have decided, on the whole, to be read as often as possible and see.

When they die, they are laid on a warm tile in a shadowed basin, and the tile softens. Within a season there is only floor, slightly brighter than its neighbors, and a sweeper may pause there, and a thread of moss may take. They do not mourn it in the way of people who have lost someone to nothing. They say only that the person has been *finished*, in the way that a sentence is finished, or a piece of metal after the long slow cooling.

### The Cisterns

The wardens among them keep the cisterns. This is the one place where the people leave a mark, and they do it deliberately. Each cistern's stair is cut by hand. The floor, left alone, would close the stair in a season, so every year they re-cut it. Chisel on tile, in the cool of the shaft, the only loud sound on the plain, though the tiles take it in quickly.

No one is sure whom the water is for. The people say it is for *the ones who come back*, which is a phrase that can mean the builders, the dead, the rain, or themselves in some later shape. They do drink from the cisterns, once a year, at night, in silence, a mouthful each. But this is not why they keep them. They keep them because a place where the floor is not allowed to win is a place where the floor is still being *asked*, and they believe that asking is the whole of their duty. Whether the floor cooperates with the cutting, tolerating it as a grain tolerates a knot, or merely fails to notice, no warden can say. The stair stays open. Every year it is cut again. Neither side has ever been seen to yield.

## What Lives Here, Taken Together

Set the residents side by side and the boundaries begin to fail. The sweepers make the floor. The floor makes the sweepers. The moss feeds on the light that the tenders arrange, and the tenders ride the stalks that the floor once raised. The kites carry what the wire-thorns are keyed to, and the wire-thorns stand where the grain knots, and the grain is made by the sweepers' passing. The people stand still and are read, and are counted as neighbors, or weather, or something in between, and are finished when they die into the same warm tile that will one day be a sweeper's foot.

No creature here is a guest, and none is a master. The Long Anneal is not a place where things live so much as a single slow act, performed by many hands at many scales, that has not yet finished being performed. The hexagons are made of smaller hexagons. The residents are made of smaller residents. The fractal of intention runs all the way up through the animals and the people and the attention above them, and what looks from far off like a dead machine turns out to be one very old, very patient organism, still breathing, still settling, still repairing itself in the light of its ten thousand mirrors.
