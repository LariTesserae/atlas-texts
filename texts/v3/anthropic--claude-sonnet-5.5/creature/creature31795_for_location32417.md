# Those Who Live in the Lip

They call themselves the Sett, which is also the word for the hollow a badger sleeps in, and for the moment a stone stops moving. They would tell you it is one word. They would tell you in a murmur, with two fingers shaping *settle* against the palm, and you would have to lean close to hear.

## The bodies

They are a short, deep-chested people, built low to the ground the way a boat is built low in a swell. The lungs are large and the hands larger. Their skin is the dark gold-brown of old peat water, roughened at the cheekbones by wind, and around the eyes it is creased into fine radiating lines, like ice that has been struck once and has not broken. The old have faces that look mapped. The eyes themselves sit deep beneath heavy lids, narrowed by a lifetime of glare, and are often a startling pale grey or amber, as if the light had bleached them. Most wear the horn visors on a cord at the throat and lift them only indoors, in the amber dimness of the lodges. Children go bare-faced with soot rubbed under the eyes in two bars, done each morning by whoever is nearest, a small tender act that is nearly a greeting.

Their hair is worn in long braids, and into each braid they work a length of twisted sinew rope, a fathom or more, coiled against the skull. It is not decoration. A person caught on a shearing floor with a seam opening beneath them has rope on their head. The braids are dressed with fat and hold a faint smell of smoke and sour milk, so that the whole population, passing in the mist, leaves the same warm trace behind.

Their walk is the first thing a stranger notices. The knees stay soft and the weight stays low. The feet, wrapped in hide with soles of scored yak leather, slide rather than step, testing the floor before trusting it. They cross a plank bridge in a sideways glide, eyes not on their feet but on the far edge, where the gap will next want to open. Even sitting, they seem to be listening through the seat of the body for a change in the ground.

## The hands

Their hands are the most spoken-of feature, and they speak with them. Rope has scarred the palms into hard, shining pads. The finger joints are loose and flexible from generations of signing, and a Sett child of six can bend a thumb back to touch the wrist. The sign-speech is fast and small, done close to the chest and at the hip, out of the glare and away from the eyes of anyone not meant to read it. It has a grammar of pressure and speed. A word signed slowly is a question. The same word signed with the fingers pressed flat is a warning.

Alongside it runs the language of cords. Everyone carries a hank of knotted sinew at the belt, and knots are tied, read, and passed hand to hand: a debt, a greeting, a birth, a request for a rope crew at the third quarter's north edge. A widow keeps her husband's cord tied about her upper arm for a year, and the knots on it are said to be still speaking to him. Strangers tie awkward, blunt knots, and the Sett are too courteous to read them aloud.

## The voice

Speech is kept between a breath and a word. They talk with the lips barely parted, a wet, low murmur that carries a body-length and no more. It is not shyness. They are not a timid people. It is that the rock is full and the mountain listens, and a voice raised past the lip of the mouth is understood, at the deepest level, as a kind of trespass.

Laughter has been taught into the shoulders and eyes. A Sett joke arrives as a silent shudder, a tightening at the corners of the visor, and a hand pressed to the mouth. Grief is a fierce stillness. There is no keening. The bereaved sit with their foreheads against the wet wall, and the neighbors bring hot water and lay a hand on the back and say nothing at all.

Even birth is hushed. A newborn's first cry is met at once, before it can carry, with a pad of warm moss pressed lightly against the face and a knot of rendered fat for the tongue. The midwives are among the most respected people in the town, and they have a name, *the softeners*, that is spoken with real weight. Children who scream are not punished, only carried to a place under the roof of a steam-pit lodge where the sound goes into the steam, and held there until it passes.

## The work of the town

There are no chiefs, only ropes. Authority runs along who can call whom to a line. A woman with four copper rings on the left hand can summon four households to haul. A man with one ring can call his own kin and his neighbors' goodwill. The rings are wrapped in hide when worn so they never chime, and when a ring is given in honor it is placed silently in the palm and the fingers closed over it.

The greatest of the offices is the **Clapper**. Each quarter has one, and each carries a bone clapper the length of a forearm, worn smooth as a river stone, passed down from teacher to pupil. When the Heave turns and a quarter must shift, it is the Clapper who reads the salamander mats and the tilt of the pools, and taps the beat. Fifty people lean into the ropes, and the whole street slides on its rollers, an entire district of stone and turf gliding sideways across the bright floor to the *tock… tock… tock* of a single bone. No one speaks. The Clapper's face, in those minutes, is very still. The old Clappers say they no longer hear the tapping at all, only the mountain answering.

Other offices are quieter:

**The Weepers' Wardens** tend the cisterns and the runnels and know every seep by name and mood. They clear the moss channels with antler picks, one soft stroke at a time, and they are the ones who notice when the water begins to run upward.

**The Fire-Keepers** carry the town's rationed embers in clay pots lined with ash and feed them dung, peat, and fat. They are thin, sharp-eyed people who are never quite warm and who are given the best place beside the steam-pits.

**The Haulers** go down. They descend through the sea-fog to the timber-lands and return weeks later bent under lashed poles and timber skids, the town's only link to wood. When they come back over the rim, they are stopped at the last ledge and given a wet river-stone to hold in the mouth for the length of a day. They come from a loud world, and it takes them time to remember quiet. Some never quite do, and are gently kept at the edges, where they may speak a little more freely to the birds.

**The Clock-Children** are not an office but a role given to whichever children are between seven and eleven. They sit in the doorways when the wind drops and watch the small rainbows. *That one is past the second peg. That one has crossed the sill.* They are always right and they are given honey-fat for it.

## The others who live here

The people do not say they share the town with the animals. They say the animals are the town.

The **masks**, the small snow-grey, black-faced birds, nest in the potholes so close to the human households that some families share a single pocket of rock, the birds at the back and the children at the front, with a curtain of hide between. Their communication is dry clicks and wingbeats, and the Sett read it as easily as they read cords. A sudden fall of clicks from the ledges means the Heave will turn within the hour. A total stillness of wings is worse.

The **red-hearts**, the translucent salamanders, are kept in the warm pools like a slow, living reckoning. The Sett do not eat them. The children are permitted to hold one in cold wet hands for the count of ten, to feel the tiny heart beat against the palm, and then to return it. When the mats turn, all together, to face the same way, the whole town knows, and someone signs it across the roofs faster than any voice: *the next shift is that way.*

The **whistle-rats** and the **coat-beasts** live in the warm hollows by the steam-pits and raid the stores, and are fed from small plates set on each threshold, so that raiding becomes a form of collecting. They have never once been driven out. A household that forgets the plate finds, within a day, that its barley has been gently and thoroughly rearranged.

The pale midges rise at noon, and the waders wade the shallows on their white-bellied legs. The moss-beads in the cracks hold their eyes of water, and the willows, cropped low for bark and withies, are addressed by name each morning. The Sett do not think of any of this as scenery. It is a crowd, and they are one of its members.

## What they believe, insofar as they say it

They say the mountain is a vessel, full to the brim, and that they live in its lip. Nothing more is needed, though the old add small details, murmured at the steam-pits at the end of the day. The Heave is the vessel breathing. The upward threads are the mountain drinking, and the town does not stand under them, because no one is sure what a mountain does when it is thirsty. The steady lights that hold on the cliff after the sun has gone behind cloud are called *lent suns*, and the birds roosting inside them are guests of something that has not asked to be understood.

When someone dies, they are carried up the roof-stairs at dusk to the highest ledge on the rim side, wrapped in hide with a cord of their own knots, and left to the masks and the weather. Nobody weeps aloud. The whole town sits on its roofs and watches the ledge until the last light leaves the cloud-lid, and the ticking of the rock goes on.

## Night

At the end of the long glowing evening the Sett lie down in their sledge-lodges, in rows and heaps, with the birds shifting on their ledges and the rats curled in the warm hollows. A child asks a question in the fewest fingers possible. A mother answers in one. The ropes are slack, the houses are still, and beneath them, with a slowness that would frighten anyone else, the ground lifts.

They fall asleep to it, all of them, a whole town breathing with the mountain. And over them, from the rim to the floor, the water ticks on its thousand ledges, and no one wakes.
