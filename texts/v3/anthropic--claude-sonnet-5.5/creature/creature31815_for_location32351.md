# The Kept of Vell

The people of the pit call themselves **the Kept**. Rim-dwellers and the occasional surveyor from above call them the Sunk, and the Kept don't mind. Asked what they are kept by, they give different answers depending on the cluster, the trade, and the hour. Some say the mirrors. Some say the lampstalks. Some say the dry. A few give the flat answer of the old stokers: *by each other, and not well enough.*

You will see them long before they see you. This is not because they are hiding. They stand very still, and they move as though the air were a fluid they had to negotiate with.

## Bodies

They are lean, and their hands are the first thing you notice: broad in the palm, with thick pads at the base of the fingers and skin like the inside of a glove. These are **reading palms**. A Kept child starts laying her hand on cable-liana at two or three, and by adulthood the skin there has thickened and the nerves beneath it have gone very fine. They feel a train coming three kilometers off. They feel a neighbor's mood in the way she has been tugging a washing-line. They shake hands rarely and hold them long.

Their skin is dry and faintly glossy, rubbed each morning with clarified sap-oil against cracking. The oil gives them a smell of crushed pepper and warm cork, so that a crowd smells like the pit itself. Their color runs from ash-olive to a deep brown-grey, and none of it is sun-touched. The mirror-light is real daylight, but it comes in pools, and no one stands in it all day. Everyone has a habit of drifting toward shade at noon, as animals do.

They do not sweat, or barely. They do not weep, and they cannot spit well. Their eyes are heavy-lidded, and each has a clear inner membrane, a second lid that sweeps sideways and keeps the surface wet. It can be seen if you watch closely, a quick slide of glass. Children learn to control it before they learn to whisper. A person who blinks too often is thought to be lying or ill.

Their hair is cropped to a finger's length, or shaved, on men and women alike. It is a matter of fire more than fashion. In this air hair crackles, and it takes a spark from a boiler-door like tinder. Old women wear caps of woven veil. Old men wear nothing and keep small scars on the scalp, which they tell you about.

Their gait is unhurried. They step down flat and roll onto the toe, so their feet make no sound on the litter. Exertion costs water, and everything about how they move is an accounting of water. Runners are rare and look faintly obscene. A person crossing a lawn will take the long way through the shade rather than the short way through a lit disc.

## Clothes

They dress against three things: cutting talus, veil-cats, and sparks. The cloth is woven leaf-veil, pale as parchment, resin-stiffened at the cuffs, collars, and knees, so a Kept adult in good clothes has a faintly armored look, and the sleeves *rasp* as she walks. Boots are lampstalk-bark over iron, and they are the one thing bought new. Everything else is mended.

Gloves are fingerless, cut back to the palm-pad. To wear a full glove is to be deaf, and the phrase for a fool is *gloved to the wrist*.

Each adult wears at the collar a small tin tag stamped with a mark. It is the mark of their trade, but it is also, more privately, a pattern of taps, their "hand-name," a short rhythm that anyone can thrum down a cable to call them. A hand-name is used across distance, never spoken aloud in company, and a wedding is mostly the exchange of hand-names.

## Speech

They speak low and precisely, with long silences between sentences. In the still dry air a whisper carries a hundred meters, and they know it, so privacy is a skill rather than a given. Gossip is done at close range, mouth nearly to ear, and it is considered rude to hear what you weren't leaned toward.

Their language is full of words for texture, tension, and slack. Something can be *taut-true* (honest), *gone to slack* (dying, or lost interest), or *humming under* (angry, but hiding it). A good stretch of conversation contains many pauses in which each speaker lays a palm on a nearby trunk or line, and the pauses are part of the talk. Visitors mistake them for hesitation. They are listening for whoever else is on the line.

Beyond speech there is **tugging**, the cable code, a language of pulses, drags, and held tensions. It began as railway signaling and is now a second tongue. Poets work in it. Lovers argue in it. Children of the rail clusters are fluent before they can read, and many of them are more at ease with a cable than a face.

## Work

Nearly everyone has a hand in the engines or the plants, and often in both.

**Stokers** keep the boilers, and are the most respected and the most burned. They tend the iron drums the color of old tea, feeding them gourd-tinder and pressed pulp, and they can judge a boiler's health by the color of the smoke. They are cheerful, scarred, and hard of hearing in one ear from the door-roar.

**Cutters** keep the streets. They walk the channels at dawn with long curved knives, slicing back litter and liana before it closes over the bare rock. A street un-cut for a season is gone. Cutters are the city's memory of where things are, and the best of them can walk a buried lane blind.

**Tuggers** run the rails and signal lines. Their palms are the largest and most callused of any trade, and they are prone to a slow deafness of the hands in old age, which they regard as the worst thing that can befall a person.

**Pressers** work the long belts of the sap-presses, where trunk-segments become oil, pulp, and pitch. It is hot, sticky, sweet-smelling labor, and they come home glistening.

**Netwrights** weave the sleeping-nets that keep veil-cats off the platforms. This is a serious craft, and a badly made net is a killing thing. Netwrights are almost always women, and they are known for a certain dry, dark humor.

**Surge-wardens** are the strangest. They walk the pit with tuning-forks of cast iron, listening to the trunks. Before a surge the sap chemistry changes and the trunks ring a half-tone sharp. Wardens can feel it in their teeth days ahead, and when they come into a cluster with their forks held high and silent, people begin quietly moving rails and lifting belongings.

**Root-listeners** go down into the mat itself, through warrens the litter-runners have dug and widened, and lie with an ear to bare rock. They hear the roots working at the cracks, a tick and creak like a ship's timbers. They are the only ones who know which wall will shed next. They are thin, pale, taciturn people, and they sometimes say, if pressed, that there are rhythms down there that don't belong to the roots. They say it lightly. They don't say it twice.

## Water

Water is the one true wealth. It comes from **gourd-stills**, copper coils in which the breath of bellows-gourds is chilled to droplets, from a few deep seeps, and from the small unavoidable water in the oil-fat foods they eat. A household has a cistern the size of a chest, and its level is common knowledge.

This shapes their manners. To offer a guest a full cup is to offer them a day of your life. To refuse it insults, and to drain it in front of the host insults worse. The correct thing is to drink half and hand the rest back, with a small tug of the ear. The host will drink the other half, and after that you are family in a minor way.

## Food

Their food is oily, sweet, and dry: flatbread of cushion-pulp fried in sap, strips of sap-thief roasted until they crackle, the pith of young lampstalk shoots, and the mild tannic tea they brew from leaf-veil, which is drunk almost cold to save the fuel. There are feast dishes, and they involve fat: sap-thief stuffed with gourd-cream, or crisped litter-runner, which is a rural delicacy, and about which the children who keep runners as pets are not told until they're older.

## Companions

Nearly every home has litter-runners in the walls, and nobody minds. Children keep one or two in their bedding for warmth, and there is a proper craft to it: the soft scratch behind the ears, a sliver of cushion-plant held out flat, the runner's whiskers trembling. A child's first grief is generally a runner. Their hearts are quick and their lives are short.

At dusk, when the tick-moths lift in slow clouds, families sit on their platforms under nets and watch. Nobody talks. The moths glitter in the last of the mirror-light, and for a few minutes the whole city is a room in which people are holding still together.

## Fire, Death, and Grief

They keep fire with reverence, because the whole orchard is a store of it. A spark loosed carelessly can take a cluster in an hour. There are rules about doors, about lamps, about the distance from a boiler to a bed, and children can recite them like prayers. The worst word in their language is the word for a person who has let fire go.

Grief is the hardest thing for them, because there are no tears, and they have built ceremony around the lack. A mourner gives up water. At the funeral each person brings their own ration, a cup or half a cup, and pours it slowly onto the litter beside the body. The mat drinks it in seconds and it is gone. This is the most extravagant gesture they know, and everyone who has done it has felt the cost of it in their throat. That, they say, is what crying is for.

The dead are sealed in hollowed lampstalk segments packed with gourd-tinder and lit. The tinder smolders for days, and the smoke rises thin and golden, smelling of burnt sugar. The family sits vigil the whole time, in shifts, and on the last night the eldest lays a palm on the nearest cable and thrums the dead one's hand-name once. The cable carries it out across the lawns and up the trunks, and every leaf along its length trembles. It is said the cable remembers. It is said that a cable-liana that grows along a thrummed name will carry it forever, and there are lines in the older clusters that the tuggers will not use for signals, because they are *occupied*. Everyone knows which ones.

## The Surge, and the Mirrors

When a surge comes, the Kept turn out together. It is the one time they are loud. Every cutter, presser, and child able to hold a knife goes to the streets, and for three days the city cuts itself back out of the growth, calling and laughing in cracked voices. Weddings are timed for the tail of a surge, when the air is thick with pollen and the lampstalk discs are unusually bright. Babies born then are called **quickborn**, and are said to grow fast and burn hot. Nobody quite believes it. Everyone keeps an eye on them anyway.

Each morning, as the first heliostats swing into place and the first disc of white light slides down the wall, people stop what they are doing, wherever they are, and hold out one hand palm up toward the rim. It is a small gesture, hardly more than a lifting of the wrist, and no one comments on it. Ask why and you get answers: *to show we're at work*, *to show we're still here*, *to show we've nothing hidden*. Some say it's for the rim-keepers, who tend the mirrors and are seldom seen. Some say it's for whatever the rim-keepers themselves are showing their hands to. A few, mostly the very old, say it's for whoever opened the pit, and that someone must have, since nothing fell here.

When they finish, they lower the hand, and the day goes on. Boilers are lit from the rim inward. The cushion-lawns turn from blue to green. The cable-liana hums under a hundred thousand palms, carrying the news of who is awake, and the Kept go out to keep the orchard, or to be kept by it, which after enough generations they no longer distinguish.
