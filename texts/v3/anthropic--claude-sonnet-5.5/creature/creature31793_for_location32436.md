# What Lives in the Cellar

Nothing here has a face, and nothing has ever walked the floor. Still, the room is occupied. It is occupied the way a house is by a family that has been living in it so long that no one remembers who arrived first.

There are three kinds of tenant, and they cannot be pulled apart. Even to count them is to draw lines the room doesn't draw.

## The Green

Most of what lives here is plant, and most of the plant is under your feet.

The mat is one body, or nearly. Roots knit into roots, and the older moss has long since fused its rhizoids into a single felt that runs from the eastern stair to the northwest thicket without a seam. When a frond dies on one side of the chamber, the sugars it held are gone from the dead tissue within a season and turn up as new growth thirty paces away. Nothing is wasted because nothing is separate. Ask which fern is which and the room has no answer. A fern is just where the mat has chosen, for now, to lift itself into the air.

The lifting is deliberate. The pale-tipped ferns in their crowded ranks are the mat's reaching organs, and they reach with terrible patience. A crozier commits to unrolling in autumn and finishes the following one. In between it may stall, or stop for a year because the rime came in wrong, or lean a few degrees toward a pore that has begun to seep again. Watch one for an hour and you will see nothing. Come back in a decade and you will see a decision.

The root-hair curtains on the ceiling are its ears. They hang combed and beaded, and they register what passes through the air: a change of pressure, the warmth of a body, a breath. When you squeeze through the crack, the curtains nearest it shiver, and the shiver travels along their length in a slow wave, then down into the mat and outward. It reaches the far wall in about an hour. By then you may have gone.

The silver cushions in the wet hollows are something like its memory, or its ledger. They are the densest tissue in the room and the slowest. Each one is a mound of thousands of tiny upright stems, and the stems carry a pattern of thickened and thinned growth, ring on ring, that records every hard winter, every long thaw, every change in the lattice's pulse. Nothing reads these records. They are kept anyway, as a habit, in the way some people keep every letter.

## The Lattice

The second tenant is not alive in any way that biology would accept. It is not dead either.

It lies under everything, and its work has not stopped in all the time it has been running. It lifts heat. It lifts dissolved minerals. It lifts something subtler, small structured charges that arrive at each hexagonal pore in patterns, never quite the same twice. The roots meet those patterns and answer in chemistry, with a sugar released here, a mineral withheld there, and the lattice adjusts.

Whether this is conversation is a fair question, and the room seems to have no interest in settling it. The lattice was built to do something, and the doing has worn thin with age. Its purposes have frayed into routines. It still sends the pulses, but it no longer seems to know what they were for. The plants have taken those pulses, which were instructions to a vanished caretaker or occupant, and made themselves into the thing that answers. They answer imperfectly, and the lattice, imperfectly, accepts. Each has spent so long shaping itself around the other's gaps that the gaps are now the relationship.

The rods glow because the lattice can no longer keep from glowing at its edges. It is a leak of the ancient signaling, cold and blue-green, faint as a memory of a language. The ferns nearest the rods have grown a little taller and paler than the rest. They lean in, as if listening.

## The Vigil

The third thing that lives here is harder to name. It is a shared habit, older than either partner's present form, and it centers on the cell.

The cell is the warmest place in the room, and the warmth is not incidental. The lattice sends more heat there than anywhere else. Every cycle, without exception, the pulse rises and ends at that low door. The plants, for their part, have built up moss on the walls in a thick, even coat, unbroken and carefully maintained, as if swaddling. The door is ajar, and no root has ever grown across the gap. The bench-like ledge inside is worn smooth. No root or moss has touched it either. It is the one surface in the chamber that stays bare of everything except warmth.

Something once lay there. Whatever it was, the lattice still expects it, and the green, which has no way to understand expectation, has learned to keep the place as it is kept for a sleeper: warm, dry, quiet, unoccupied. Neither knows what it is waiting for. Neither has stopped. Perhaps this is what the two of them are, when you strip away the roots and pores and pulses: a long, unhurried act of keeping faith with someone who did not come back.

The ring may be part of the same story. It is bare, and the lattice's hum vanishes inside it. Perhaps it was made so that whoever once stood there could have silence from the thing that was tending them. The green respects it. Roots that reach its edge turn aside without touching, as they would at a doorstep.

## How It Knows You

It does not know you quickly.

When you enter, the curtains shiver, and the shiver goes into the mat. Over the next days a few things change. The pores near the crack seep slightly more. The ferns nearest to where you knelt turn their new fronds a few degrees toward that spot. The silver cushions add a thin band to their record, a mark for a warm-blooded visitor who came for an hour and left. Then the mat stills, and the moss closes over the print of your hand.

If you come back after a year, it will not greet you. It will not remember you in any way that has a face or a name. But the band is in the cushion, and the ferns have already bent, and the pulse that rises toward the cell will, that season, arrive at the door a moment slower than before, as though something in the dark had paused to check whether it was you.

Then it will go on. Water moves down, warmth moves up, the roots feed the lattice and the lattice feeds the roots. In the dark the green grows a fingernail's depth in a century, and it keeps the bed warm.
