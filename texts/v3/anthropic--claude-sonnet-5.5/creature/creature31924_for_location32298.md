# Those Who Keep Still

## The People

They call themselves something that has no sound. It is a knot, a loop pulled through itself and drawn tight, and if you asked what it meant they would show you the same loop made in a strand of wet fern-thread and let it dry. *The ones who are held.* Or perhaps *the ones who hold*. The knot does not say which, and they have never felt the need to.

They are a slight people, long in the hand and narrow in the shoulder, with skin the color of the underside of a leaf. Generations of green-gray light have left them pale, and in the hour before sunrise their faces take on a faint cast from the glowing plants. Their eyes are wide and dark, with irises so large that in the dimness there is hardly any white. Their feet are the most remarkable part of them. They go barefoot or in soles of thin scraped hide, and the skin on their feet is thick but not dull, like the pad of a fingertip. A grown person can stand on the root-mat with eyes closed and tell you whether the pulse that just passed was a grazer, a child, or a slab of scree letting go somewhere far off. They can tell you how far, and whether it was afraid.

They breathe carefully, through the nose where they can. When they must speak aloud, the voice comes out as a dry rustle, a sound like a page turned in the next room. The old ones do it best. The young ones, being young, sometimes forget and let a real syllable escape, and every adult in reach will turn and look at them, without anger, the way you would look at a child who had dropped a cup.

## Names and Hands

A person receives a name at the first frost of the child's life, if there is one, or at the first season the pools skin over at the edges. Elders tie it into a cord in three knots: one for the mother, one for the place in the cleft where the birth happened, and one for the first thing the child touched with intention. Someone born by the third pool who first reached for a moth's wing has a name that means something like *third water, soft dust*. It is worn at the wrist and read by fingertip. To say someone's name aloud would be strange, like calling out their organs. To greet them you find their wrist and press.

Because the hands do all the talking, the hands are everything. They are scarred and quick and strangely expressive. A conversation across a gap of fern is a flicker of fingers in the half-light, and a stranger would take it for a kind of shadow-play. Joy is a quick flutter of the fingers against the palm. Grief is a hand held flat and pressed to the chest without moving. There is a gesture for *I am hungry* that also means *the moss is thin this year*, and another for *I am afraid* that can also mean *the wall is warm*. They are never sure which of these meanings the speaker intended and have long since made peace with it.

## Trades

Nobody has one occupation for life, but people lean, and the leaning becomes a name for the kind of person they are.

**The wristkeepers** carry the town's memory. They wear cords from elbow to knuckle, hundreds of them, a whole history of births and repairs and debts and weather, and they can read them in the dark. A wristkeeper walks slowly and is never hurried. When two households dispute the water rights to a seam, the wristkeeper is asked to run her fingers down her arm until she finds the knot.

**The floor-listeners** lie on the mat at dawn with their cheeks pressed to the moss, sometimes for hours, and feel the signals of the night settle. They know when the grazers have shifted their herds, when the spiders have moved a net, when a predator has crossed the floor. Their hair is always damp and their skin always smells faintly of iron and earth. Most of them are quiet even by the standards of the town. Some have said, with their hands and very slowly, that they no longer entirely feel the difference between what is beneath them and what is inside them.

**The water-tenders** know the cleft's seams the way a herder knows a flock. They lay strips of split fern trunk to guide a thread of condensation toward a house, and they clear the drip-lines of moss when the flow chokes. They ask the water where it wants to go and follow it, and only then do they build anything.

**The lamp-hands** carry hooded lanterns made from veined leaves that glow, held in hide and blinked with the fingers. A lamp-hand on a platform can send a message the length of the cleft in the time it takes to draw twenty breaths. Their code is close kin to the flank-light of the translucent-throated lizards, and the lizards have been known to answer it.

**The moth-tenders** are the strangest of all. They climb the walls at dusk with padded hands and lie flat against the stone among the sleeping sheets, close enough to feel the small wind of each breath. They do not harvest the moths so much as accompany them. When the sheets lift, the tenders lift with them, hanging from vine-rope harnesses, rising a little way into the spiral before the fall of the tether stops them. They come back down with their faces wet and their eyes shining, and they will not say what they felt. They only press a palm to their chests.

## Children, and Death

Children are born into hush. A woman in labor is attended by a ring of women and men who breathe with her, exactly, in rhythm, and the birth is a synchronized inhale and exhale that spreads out through the house and the neighboring houses and, some say, into the root-mat itself. The infant's first cry is caught in a hand-cupped hide before it can reach the walls. It is the only sound of its kind the child will be allowed for years.

They learn to move before they learn to speak. They learn to place a foot so the mat takes it without a ripple. They learn to open a door without a sound, to hold a bowl so it does not ring, to breathe through a sneeze. Their games are games of stillness, and the best players can stand so long in a shaft of light that a moth will settle on the shoulder and fall asleep.

The dead are given to the moss. The body is wrapped in fern fiber and carried, in complete quiet, to a shelf of wall high enough that the snails do not reach it. There is no lament, because a lament would be a cry and the cry belongs to something else. What the mourners do instead is stand still. Each person picks a leaf and watches it until it has dripped a hundred times, and that is the length of the grief. Then they touch their wrists to the wrists of the others, and one of the wristkeepers ties a knot for the dead and hangs the cord on the wall beside the ledge. Over the centuries those cords have rotted into a dark lace across the stone, and the moss has grown into it, and no one can any longer say which threads are memory and which are green.

## The Cry

One person in each generation is raised for the cry.

She is chosen young, usually a child who is quiet even among the quiet, and she is taken to a platform near the rim where the air is thinner and the cold is sharp. She lives there apart. She does not speak, or whisper, or hum, or sigh. She is fed and clothed and kept warm, and her voice is kept for exactly one purpose: on the day the mountain shakes, she stands at the lip of the fissure and lets go.

No one has heard it in living memory. The wristkeepers say it has been used six times since the town began, and the cords for those days are long and blackened and knotted many times over. When the cry goes out it is said to come back from three walls at once, and the animals go still, the moths lift all together into a wall of wings, and the people drop what they are holding and climb. Everyone in the cleft, human or not, understands what it means. The cry-keeper is treated with a tenderness that is close to awe, and a little pity. She is the one person allowed no voice so that all the others can keep theirs.

## The Other Inhabitant

There is something else in the cleft, and the people are careful about how they speak of it. They hold their hands flat, palm down, and move them slowly outward, which means *the thing that settles*.

It is not a creature, quite. It is felt more than seen. The floor-listeners know it as a very long pulse in the root-mat, slower than any animal's step, slower than the drip of the fog. It comes perhaps once a night, perhaps less often, and it does not travel along the mat as the grazers' steps do. It seems to rise from beneath, from under the mat and under the scree and under the black pools, from somewhere deeper than the rock has been broken. It passes through the whole length of the cleft at once, and everything alive pauses a fraction of a breath. The snails stop, the moth sheets tremble, the glowing plants flare and then dim.

Some of the older listeners believe it is the mountain itself, still learning to breathe. Some believe it is a thing that lives in the wound, a slow animal older than the fracture that waits in the deep dark for the stone to finish knitting. A few, very quietly, believe that the pulse is an answer. They say it comes back a little differently each night, a shade longer or shorter, as though something were listening to what the cleft says and learning how to say it back.

Nobody tries to reply. That is the oldest rule of the town, older than the knots. You do not stamp, or drum, or call. You lie on the moss with your cheek against the cold and feel it go through you, and you hold your breath along with the moths and the snails and the tall gray birds on their ledges. Then it passes, and the drip resumes, and the light along the leaf-veins comes back, and the whole silent multitude of the place breathes out at once, a soft sound like fog moving over stone.

Then the hands start to move again, and someone ties a knot.
