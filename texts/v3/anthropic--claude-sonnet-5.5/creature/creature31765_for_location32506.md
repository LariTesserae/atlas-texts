# What Lives on the Shelf

Only one kind of thing lives here, and it takes some time to see that it is one kind of thing. It presents as a hundred: stalk, vane, root-mat, heap, seep. But these are organs, and the creature is the **stand**. A stand is a basin of green, some hundreds of vanes on jointed stalks, joined below the rock by a felted mat of root that may reach across a hectare. The stalks are not individuals in the sense that a tree in a forest is. They are closer to fingers. The stand is the animal, if animal is the word, and it does not have another word yet.

## The Body

**The stalks** are the visible workings. Each is a jointed tube, collared at intervals, its walls translucent enough at the joints that you can watch the amber fluid climb in slow surges and see the sorting happen: dark suspended particles drifting down through a chain of chambers, pale grains dropping out at the last. A stalk is a column of small decisions. It has no brain, but it has a great many valves, and the valves are very good at their work. Each closes when it should and opens when it should, over and over, at a tempo you could set a clock by. Every stalk is slightly different in height, as a hand's fingers differ, but the collars are spaced by a rule that never varies.

**The vanes** are broad, ribbed, cupped, and copper-tipped. They are what the stand shows to the sky. They hold their angle to the sun with great steadiness, and now and then, at intervals too regular to be chance, a whole bank will dip a degree or two and return, all together, as though the basin had exhaled. The sun does not move on the shelf, and the stand does not seem to know that. It rehearses a day that does not come. Each small tilt and reset is the whole arc of morning to evening compressed into a single breath, made again and again and never finished. I do not think it is mistaken. I think it is faithful to something older than this noon.

**The root-mat** is where the stand mostly lives, and you will never see it whole. Where a scarp cuts through a stand's ground you can see it in section: a dense pale felt, fine as hair, threading every vesicle and hairline crack in the glassy rock, thickest around the seams where the amber fluid seeps. It is the stand's stomach and its memory, if it has a memory. It reads the rock in a way no eye could, tasting the grain of it, and it decides where the next root goes. This is the one place in the whole plateau where anything seems to be *deciding*.

## The Heap

Beside each stalk sits a cone of gray-white pellets. It looks like waste, and it is not. The heap is part of the stand, an organ worn on the outside. The stalk sheds pellets onto it, and at its base the root-mat draws them slowly back in, so nothing is really discarded and nothing really accumulates. The cone holds its height because the stand holds it there. That balance is the closest thing the creature has to a heartbeat. If you pushed a heap over, kicked the pellets across the grit, the stand would not react in any way you could see. But return in a day, or in a year, and the cone would stand again at its height, neat, with the same slope, because the sorting and reabsorbing would never have stopped.

Some of the pellets, I suspect, are how a stand is born. The dark seams under the terraces are full of them, and at the far edge of a stand you may find a single small stalk not yet ringed with collars, standing in a drift of pale grains, its first vane still furled like a fist. These are the only young things I have seen. They are not many. A stand does not spread, because each basin is already full to its edge, and a new stalk can rise only where an old one has been reabsorbed. Birth and ending here are the same event, one stalk folding into the mat as another unfolds from the pellet. The stand as a whole does not age. Its parts turn over like the water in a river, and the river stays where it is.

## How It Lives

It does not eat and it is not eaten. It does not grow toward anything, since every basin it could fill is filled. It does not compete with its neighbors, because a kilometer of bare rock lies between stands, and it seems to want nothing on the far side of that rock. Its whole economy is a loop: fluid up through the seam, minerals sorted out in the stalks, pellets shed, pellets drawn back, the vane dipping and returning. Nothing leaves the loop and nothing enters it except light and the slow amber seep. The stand is not moving toward anything. It is *keeping*.

I hesitate to call that a life, and I hesitate to call it anything else. It has appetite without hunger and rhythm without hurry. It has something like attention, the reaching of the root-mat and the patient answering tilt of the vanes, but the attention has only one object, the light, and the light never changes. So the attention rests. I have stood among the stalks and felt a thing I can only describe as being in the presence of something *content*, though the word is too warm. It is closer to the contentment of a machine that runs exactly as it was made to, except that nothing made it.

## What It Knows

It knows the sun and the seam. It knows the grain of the rock, root by root. It does not know you. The vanes turn very slightly as you pass, and it is easy to take that for notice, but the turning is the ordinary small adjustment of their day-rehearsal, and you happen to be walking through it. Your shadow crosses a bank and the whole bank dips a degree, and this does not mean it saw you. You have only fallen across the workings of something else's noon.

It does not know the edge of the terrace either, not in the way we would. But its green stops at the foot of each riser as though it had been told. The likeliest reason is plain: the fluid seeps only in the basins, and the risers are dry, and the stand goes exactly as far as the seam allows and no farther. That is not respect. It is the shape of the creature's own need, which is its whole knowledge of the world.

## Toward Evening

There is no evening, and the stand does not wait for one. It ticks and thrums and lets its valves seat with their small click-and-sigh. The vanes dip and return. The heaps hold their height in the level amber light. Between the stands the cut rock lies bright and sharp and untouched, and the stands sit in it like islands, each one whole, each one a single creature that has never been hungry, never been young for long, and never been old at all.

If there is a word for what it is doing, the word is *abiding*. It abides in the moment of its own making, tending the exact size of its own heap, turning a degree toward a sun that does not move, and turning back.
