# Those Who Live Under the Lid

Almost everything that lives in the Sump lives on borrowed food. The trough grows nothing of its own but a little fern and moss in the shafts of light. The rest is carried down in bellies. Every night the bats climb out through the skylights and scatter over the cold scrub of the plateau, where they take moths, midges, and beetles from the air. Then they come home and drop the day's harvest as guano. The whole country below is an outgrowth of the flat land above, fed by a thin rain of digested insects. The scrub does not know it is being grazed.

## The Roof-Dwellers

The bats are the largest fact of the place, and it is hard to think of them as individuals. They hang in a brown pelt on the underside of the lid, and the pelt moves: a shiver crossing it, a ripple of unfolding, a seam of wings opening and closing. Each animal is small enough to sit in a cupped hand, with a fox-like face, a damp pink mouth, and hooked feet that have not tired since it was a day old. They are born hanging. The mothers find their own young among thousands by voice and smell, and the pups mass in nursery clusters where the warmth of their bodies is a small climate of its own.

They never all sleep and never all wake. The colony keeps a rota that no one designed. One tier drops and streams to the skylight while another tier, gorged and heavy, comes spiraling in. Beneath each opening the air is a slow braid of wings, going up on one side and coming down on the other, so that the light shaft looks like a pillar of smoke drawn through a loom. A bat leaving passes a bat returning close enough to touch, and neither swerves. Two million wingbeats make one low fabric sound.

Among them, and below them on the cliffs, the swiftlets keep their tiers of nests. They are small dark birds with pale rumps, and they glue cups of spit and moss to the rock in rows. They call constantly, a dry ticking that is really a sonar, each click a small hand feeling the dark for the wall. A swiftlet enters its nest at full speed in a blackness a person would fear to walk through, and it does not miss. The whitish cups stand out on the wall like shelves of shells, each with one or two pale eggs and a bird pressed over them, quivering, tired, and never for a moment silent.

## The Ground That Breathes

Beneath the roosts the guano lies in cones taller than a man. They are dark and wet, and they steam faintly in the cold, and they are the richest thing in the country. They are also alive from the inside out.

The cave crickets cover them in carpets. They are ghost-pale animals with bodies like a thumbnail and antennae several times longer than themselves, which they sweep before them like a blind man's canes. When you step, the whole floor lifts and resettles, and the ground appears to draw breath. Most of the crickets will never see light. They eat guano, dead bats, dead crickets, each other's shed skins, and anything soft, and they live in a thick quiet interdependence of grazing and being grazed.

Under them are the beetles, glossy and armored, hundreds to a handspan, tunneling and turning over. Under the beetles are the mites and springtails, so numerous that the mound's surface seems to glitter, a fine crawling dust of bodies. Nothing is ever wasted in the mound. What a bat drops in the evening is bone, chitin, mite, and cricket by morning, and some of it has already been eaten twice.

The pale centipedes hunt through this. They are as long as a forearm, quick, and many-legged, with venom in their front claws. They come out of cracks and take crickets by the dozen, folding them in and chewing without hurry. A centipede can go a week without a kill and not lose weight, because the crickets never run out. It has never known hunger. It hunts anyway, patiently, because that is the shape of its body.

## The Pools

The still pools hold their own small population. Blind salamanders, white and translucent with red frills of gill, drift over the bottom, and you can see their hearts beating through their skin. Crabs the color of glass pick along the edges. Thin white fish, eyeless, hang in the black water and read it by the tremor of their lateral lines. They know a hand is coming long before it enters, and they move aside.

None of them has ever seen a color. The rust and lime on the stream beds mean nothing to them. What they know is pressure, vibration, and the slow chemistry of the water, and from these they map a world as detailed as any sighted animal's.

## The Weavers and Hunters

The spiders hang their webs between the rocks like torn linen. They are large ones, banded and hairy, and small ones, grey as dust. The webs sag under moths, crickets, and sometimes a bat pup that has fallen from its roost, wrapped and slowly emptied. Spiders here grow fat and old. Some webs have hung so long they carry a beard of dust and desiccated husks, and the spider that keeps them has taken up a permanent post at the centre, as a clerk does at a desk.

The higher predators are few and never numerous enough to change anything. Owls nest on the ledges at the edge of the light. They are pale, round-faced, with feathers so soft they make no sound, and they drop from the dark onto rats, shrews, and bats caught at the skylights. Their pellets, full of small bones, pile at the base of their perches. Civets patrol the stream beds, low and sinuous, brown and spotted, with long noses that read the mud. They eat crabs, beetles, eggs, and whatever they find. The rats and shrews live in the rubble in the thousands, forever eaten, forever replaced.

## The Balance

No one animal governs this. There is no king of the trough. If the owls vanished, the rats would rise a little, the beetles fall a little, the guano be worked a little faster, and the whole would slide back to where it was. Every creature here sits on a slope that rolls it back to its place. That is why the place seems to hold its breath: the numbers do not fluctuate, and the deaths are eaten as fast as they fall. A dead bat lasts a day at most.

It is not peaceful. It is a vast, quiet, continuous consumption, and each mouth is lifted by another's leavings. But it is not cruel either. There is no cruelty where nothing has ever had a chance to be otherwise.

## The Visitors

People come and go and do not live here. The gatherers descend on rope ladders with baskets on their backs, cut guano from the drier cones, take eggs from the low nests, pry down the whitish cups for soup, and climb out again, hurrying, ears ringing. They are counted among the animals only in the way a passing storm is counted among the seasons. They leave a channel, a cairn, a peg in a crack, a handprint in ochre. The crickets take a few days to close over their footprints. After a season the beetles have worked their spoil back into the mounds, and the trough has, by any measure it uses, not been visited at all.

## What Actually Lives Here

If you stood long enough in a column of grey light, you would stop sorting it into animals. You would notice that the bats' breathing and the drips' ticking and the crickets' rasp were one pulse, and that the pulse had been going since the fault first dropped. Something is alive here that is not any single creature. It is the circulation itself: insects from the scrub going up into the bats' bodies, down into the guano, through the crickets, the centipedes, the owls, into the pools, and out again as bones and seep-water and the green of the ferns that lean toward the holes.

Millions of small lives serve it, and none of them knows it exists. It has no face, but it has a clock in the tiers of the roost, a gut in the mounds, and a breath in the ground that lifts when you step. It finished growing before you came, and it goes on around you, warm and wet and ticking, taking what the plateau sends down.
