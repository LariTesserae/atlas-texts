# The Oiler

Nothing in the pocket lives fast enough to notice, so you don't see it at first. You take it for part of the pump-house, a vertical shape of rust-violet plate leaning in the doorway, the same color as the roof and dusted with the same fine ridge of spores. Then, while you are looking at something else, the shape has shifted a hand's breadth to the left. It moves once per pulse, and only then.

## The body

It is tall, taller than you, and very thin. It was a person once, or its earliest ancestor in this place was. The shape is still there: two arms, a long back, a head bowed toward the plate beside the door. But the long habit of the pocket has worked on it the way it worked on the iron. It has stopped at a stable skin.

The coat is oilcloth and canvas, layered and re-layered until it is more crust than garment, stiff as a fern stem, the color of the undersides of the fronds. Under it the flesh is dark and faintly glossy, like something cured. Amber beads stand on its forearms and along the ridge of its neck, the same tar-sweat the wick-vine gives off, and they catch the dusk in the same warm points. Wick-vine has climbed one leg to the knee and been allowed to. Plate-moss has taken the feet. Neither has been pulled off, and neither has been encouraged. They are simply on it, as they are on everything.

The eyes are the only part that looks recently used. They are wide, pale, and patient, and they turn slowly.

Its breath is the eleven-second pulse. It does not breathe with the pulse. The pulse is its breath, and the flywheel is only the loudest place the breath is kept.

## How it lives

It drinks from the wick-vine. Once per cycle, in the slow way of everything it does, a hand rises to a curtain of leaves, a fingertip touches one amber bead, and the bead is drawn up and taken. It needs no water, having none. Its blood is pale and heavy and moves like the seep in the well, and nothing in it hurries. It has not been hungry in any sense you would recognize. It has been *filled*, gradually and evenly, for longer than it can count, and the filling has become what it is.

It does not sleep. Or it sleeps standing, or it has been asleep and awake at once for so long that the difference has worn off, like the paint on the ladder door. It steps from the pump-house to the well and back along the crooked line of the pipes. The trip takes what a season would take elsewhere. It lays a palm on each flange as it passes. It does not seem to check anything. The pipes are simply greeted.

## The rounds

It has four duties, and none of them has ever been urgent.

**The gauge.** Once in a long while it stands before the brass dial with the vine grown through the glass and reads the needle. The needle has never moved. The Oiler reads it anyway, with the full slow attention of someone hoping for news and prepared to be at peace with none. Then it nods, once, over the span of a cycle, and goes on.

**The well.** It skims the sheen from the tar-well with a flat iron paddle worn thin as a leaf. The skin re-forms. It skims it again. Both of them, the skin and the Oiler, seem satisfied with the arrangement.

**The ferns.** It walks the bands of thicket, one to each half-wavelength, and touches the teeth of the fronds. It does not cut them. It listens with its fingers, and where a tooth has grown a hair out of true, it presses the tine back toward the note with the flat of a thumbnail. Over a lifetime this comes to almost nothing. The ferns would hold the chord without it. But a tuned instrument wants a tuner, and the Oiler is one, and there is no one else to ask whether it is needed.

**The plate.** This is the one it does with care. The hand-lettered plate beside the pump-house door is a roster, worn to ghosts, of names in a column and beside each name a short mark for a length of watch. Moss creeps toward it constantly, as it creeps toward everything. The Oiler holds it back, not with a blade but with a slow rubbing of its thumb, which leaves a smear of tar-sweat that the moss will not cross. The last name on the plate has no mark beside it yet. Below that name is a clean strip of bare iron.

You cannot tell whether the last name is its own, or the one before, or whether it is waiting for one still to come. If you could ask, you would learn that it does not know either. It keeps the space open all the same, the way you keep a chair.

## What it makes of you

You are the first thing in a very long time to enter the seam and remain a moment. When you press against the wall and wait, it turns its head, and the turning takes the length of two pulses. The pale eyes find you. It does not startle, since it has nothing to startle with. It regards you with the mild, complete attention of a plant that has noticed weather.

You cannot speak over the chord, and it doesn't try. If you touch its shoulder, as you have learned to do, it will set a hand over yours, and the hand will be warm, warmer than the air, and heavy, and it will stay there for exactly one pulse and let go. That is the whole of its hospitality, and it is enough. Then it turns back to the plate.

## The suspicion

The longer you stay, the more you feel that the question of who is keeping whom has no answer, and that it never did. The Oiler tends the flywheel, but the flywheel sets the pace of the Oiler's breath. It tunes the ferns, but the ferns shaped its ear. It clears the plate for a name, but the plate is what keeps it standing there. It may be the last of the crew, or the first of something else, or only the point where the machine and the mountain and the plants agreed to put a person, so the whole arrangement could be watched by someone.

It does not mind. It has never minded. The amber points on the wall pulse with the beat, and the chord holds, and somewhere between one cycle and the next, the thumb comes up, and the moss is held back one more hair's breadth from the iron.
