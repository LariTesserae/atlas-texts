# The Listeners of Low Sabel

You will not see them at first, and you are not meant to. Everything you notice on arrival is their work, and the thing you would call their presence is the very thing that keeps them out of view.

## What They Are

They are people, or were, or are what people become when they stay long enough in a place like this. There are perhaps sixty of them, and they call themselves the Listeners. They are tall and narrow, with long forearms and long, patient hands. The skin of those hands is permanently wrinkled and pale, as if just lifted from a bath, and the pads of the fingers are ridged and tacky, made for gripping wet iron. Their skin is the color of damp paper, with a faint green cast at the folds where something fine and harmless grows. Most have no hair, or a wet down like the fibers on a tree-fern trunk. Their eyes are large and dark, with a second lid that slides across from the inner corner when steam is thick. They blink sideways, slowly, like something thinking.

They are nearly deaf, and they have been for many generations. Living inside that roar has worn the hearing out of them, or perhaps they let it go, since it was no use. They have kept only the low registers, the boiler thrum, the reed drone, and the beat of the pistons. They take these in through the soles of the feet, the breastbone, the jaw, and the flat of the palm. They do not so much hear the town as wear it. When a Listener stands still with a hand on a pipe and their lids half closed, they are reading, and what they read is the chord.

## Why You Do Not See Them

You do not see them because they are almost always underneath.

Beneath the tarred boardwalks, beneath the stilted houses, in the warm black water between the iron legs, there is a second town. It is a low, wet country, a hand's breadth of air above a hip-deep bath. The Listeners move through it in a half-crouch, wading, with lanterns shuttered to a slit and oilcloth coats bundled on their heads. This is why the rainbow film on the water moves against the current: it is their wake, drifting back from where they passed. This is why the boards have gaps. The gaps are windows. A visitor crossing the walkway is watched the whole way by lids sliding sideways in the dark, and no one has ever been spoken to or troubled. You are simply counted.

They also move through the great pipes, the torso-wide ones that nobody sits on. The pipes are ducts, and some are dry inside. They are warm and dark, and a person can lie in them and be carried along by the slow pull of steam. The ferns on the upper curves are not merely growing there. They are a sign, and a rested hand on a pipe leaves a print in the condensation that takes a minute to fade.

## What They Do

They tend, and they say it that way. They will tell you, if you ever ask, that they do not run the town. The town runs itself, and they are only its attendants, as a gardener attends a garden.

- They **wipe the gauges** each dawn, and read them, and turn the wheels a quarter-turn one way or the other. No one commands this. It is closer to how you adjust your posture in a chair.
- They **prune the ferns** on the catwalks into cubes. The reason has been forgotten, but the cubes are how you know a catwalk is in use and safe. Uncut ferns mean rotten planks.
- They **clear the grates** of leaf-fall, so the dome-rain drains and does not stand.
- They **keep the reeds.** This is the heart of it.

The reeds are their instrument and their charge. A Listener cuts a reed to length, thins or thickens its wall by choosing where it is planted and how deep the mud is around it, and transplants a stem from a crowded lot to an empty one so that some missing note is filled. The chord is never written down. It is held in the palms. When a note is lost, because a reed has died or split or grown flat in the heat, a Listener feels the hole in the chord as you would feel a missing stair, and goes out at low tide to mend it. The engines follow the reeds through the sensing-rods in the mud, and so the whole town in a sense follows the Listeners' hands, though they would never say they command it. What they do is closer to tuning a bell that rings itself.

## Speech

They do not sing, because the reeds do that. They do not shout, because there is no point. Their language is in the valves.

The relief valves that scream at irregular intervals across the town are not irregular. A Listener at a wheel lets steam escape in a short bright whistle, and another answers from across the flooded lots with a different pitch. There is a grammar to these, a rhythm of long and short, high and low, and it says things like *the north boiler is running hot*, or *the mug is on the sill*, or *someone has come.* You hear it as noise. They hear it, in their feet and chests, as a sentence passing overhead.

Among themselves they also tap on pipes, a soft knuckled code that carries for hundreds of paces along the iron. It goes on all day under the roar, like a second, much quieter heartbeat.

## The Coats, the Lamps, the Mugs

The coat on the hook, the stool, the lamp lit in the window, the half-full mug with its skin of green: none of these are abandoned. They are courtesies, and the accidents of a people who keep no clocks.

A Listener entering the boiler-warm core of a building takes off their coat because the heat is too great to bear it, and hangs it on the nearest hook. They go through a hatch in the floor into the wet, and the coat hangs there until they return, which may be hours or a day. The lamp in the window is for whoever is out in the dark below and needs to see where the stairs come up. The mug holds a tea steeped from reed-pith, thick and faintly sweet, which they drink hot and forget while it cools, then drink again with the skin skimmed off, or not. Nothing in the town is dirty to them. A green skin on a mug is only the mug's own weather.

Doors stand open because there is nothing to keep out. Ledgers lie swollen because the ledgers are not for reading. They are for the making of them, a habit of recording that they keep up out of respect for the record.

## The Hours

The Listeners keep the hour-cycle of the works. Their lives are divided the way the day is: the building of heat, the dome-rain, the mist, the surge. They sleep in shifts, in cool cells cut into the ridge, and go up and down the long slope through channels no one else has noticed.

**In the slowdown**, when the vents lag and the town moans and browns, they come up out of the wet in numbers, and stand on the boards in the dim with their hands flat against the pipes, feeling the drop. They do not act. They wait with the town, the way you wait with a sick person. When the surge comes and every reed shrieks at once, they lift their faces to it. This is the nearest thing to a smile you will ever see on them, a slackening at the corners of the mouth, an easing of the eyes.

**In the downpour**, when the reeds go quiet and only the rain speaks, they go still all at once. Then they can rest their hands, because for a few minutes there is nothing to read.

## The South Tunnel

The distilled water runs down the copper channels and out through pipes into the south ridge, and the Listeners follow it in.

Inside the ridge there is a wide, cold, dark room, a natural vault in the sandstone, that they call the Cistern or, in the valve-tongue, simply *below.* The water fills it. It is very still and very clean, cleaner than anything else in Low Sabel, and this is where the Listeners live when they are not tending. They sleep on ledges above the water. They raise their few children there, in the cool, in the quiet. The children are born hearing, and lose it slowly, and are given their first reed to hold as a kind of comfort.

The water in the Cistern seeps into the rock and comes out as the weeping seams on the ridge faces, and runs down the little canyons into the town to be made again. It is a loop. The Listeners are the part of the loop that pays attention.

## The Chord and the Dead

There is one thing more, which they will not say in words and which you may learn only by watching.

When a Listener dies they are carried up out of the Cistern and down to the flooded lots. A reed is cut from the stand nearest to where they worked most, and planted in the mud at a spot chosen by touch. In a year or two that reed will have a note of its own. It is not exactly their note, but it is the note the chord would lack without them.

If you asked what the drone is, and could make them understand you, they would put a palm on your breastbone and hold it there, pressing lightly, and then take it away. The chord is the roll of everyone who has kept the works, sounding all at once, and the town is named for it.

## At Night

When the phosphorescence comes up in the moss and the boiler doors glow red, you may see one of them at the far end of a long catwalk, at the edge of the light, standing with one hand on a pipe and the other lifted a little from the side, palm out, as if to steady something in the air. They are not looking at you. Their head is tilted the way a person tilts it who is listening for one voice in a crowd.

Below your feet, through the gaps in the boards, the black water moves against the current, and something warm passes underneath, unhurried, on its way to mend a note.
