# Those Who Live in the Warm Throat

## At the lip

The first residents are the ones that barely qualify. On the lip of the stone, where the grey blade of light lands, orange lichen grows a hair's width a year, and some of the crusts are older than any language spoken in the valleys below. Behind them are cushions of moss holding a little warmth in their crowns, and a few tufts of fern that have arrived at a bad idea and can't reverse it. They are thin, pale at the tips, and bent toward the crack in the vault like supplicants. Springtails ride them. A small dark spider has strung a web across a fern's elbow, and its catch is mostly what the cave exhales: gnats, tiny moths, and sometimes a wingless cricket that has wandered too far toward the light.

Everything beyond them lives on falling things.

## The ceiling people

The bats are the population, and the cave is arranged around them.

Each is about the size of a plum, with dense brown fur tipped in a frost-colored silver, a blunt, almost puglike face, and ears folded close against the head. Their wings are short and broad for bats, built for thin air, and they pay for it in effort. A bat leaving the cave at dusk beats its wings so fast that the sound is a whir, not a flap. They rarely glide. Every hour outside is hard work, and they seem to know it, which is perhaps why they spend most of their lives hanging still.

They are not spread evenly. The warmest part of the vault, above the guano dune where the rising air is thickest, is the nursery. In early summer it is packed with mothers and naked pink pups, each pup fastened to the stone or to a neighbor by tiny hooked feet. A mother returning from the night's hunting lands in that roar of ten thousand shrill voices and finds her own young one by its call, and by smell, in a few seconds. She noses along the felt, rejects a dozen wrong pups, and stops at the right one. Nobody watching could say how she does it. The pup takes her nipple, and both go quiet, and the noise closes over them like water.

Nearer the entrance, in the cooler pockets, hang the bachelors. They are looser, dozier, and slightly ragged, clustered in knots of twenty or thirty. When a cold night makes the outer chamber chill, they drop into torpor. Their hearts slow to a few beats a minute and their breath nearly stops, and they hang like dried fruit until the dawn exhalation warms them. Elders sit here too, bats whose muzzles have gone grey and whose wing membranes are stitched with old scars, some of them ten or fifteen years in this same crevice. They know the room. They know which ledges drip and which ones wick moisture away, and when the mountain is about to breathe.

The colony has habits nobody taught it. The wave is the plainest one. When the warm air pulses, one bat shivers, the next shivers because it was touched, and the motion rolls across the ceiling in a ripple that takes half a minute to cross. Then, at the moment before dawn, comes the hush. A million small throats stop, all together, for perhaps four heartbeats. Then the mountain sighs out through the mouth, the entrance sings its cello note, and the chittering comes back a tone higher.

## The nest builders

Between the bats, on ledges and in cracks, live the swiftlets. They are small, sooty, quick birds, the color of burnt paper, with pale throats and forked tails. They are the noisiest tenants after the bats, and the only ones that talk in the dark. Each click is a question thrown at the stone, and the answer is the stone's shape. They weave through the chamber in the dim, past wings and shoulders and each other, without touching anything.

Their nests are cups of hardened saliva, pale and translucent, glued to the rock like scraps of amber glass. The old nests are yellowed and layered, rebuilt year after year on the same scar of stone until they stand out a hand's width from the wall. The chicks are naked and blind at first, with huge heads and rattling voices. Their parents leave before dawn and come back at dusk with a wad of tiny insects in the throat, midges and beetles and gnats blown up the mountain on the thermals, and the whole chamber fills with the sound of feeding.

They are the neighbors that make the bats seem calm. A swiftlet hurries where a bat waits.

## The wall crowd

Down from the ceiling, the wall is another country. Cave crickets live there by the thousand, pale as candle wax, with antennae three times their body length and back legs like folded wire. They are the nervous people of the cave. A footstep sends a hundred of them leaping in one direction, clicking against the stone, and for a moment the wall looks like it is boiling. They eat almost anything, including dead bats, dropped pups, insect husks, and their own molts. Nothing that falls is wasted by them for long.

Among them go other small tenants. There are pale spiders with legs that seem too long for their bodies, hunting-lacework spiders that never build a web, and thin centipedes the color of old ivory that pour along cracks in long slow ripples. Fungus gnats drift toward the green patches of glow, and the fungus, which is a bit hungry, has spun threads to catch them. Some of these threads glow too. From a certain angle a wall of dark stone is dusted with tiny green lights, blinking as gnats settle and die.

## The heap

The guano dune is the largest living thing in the room, and it is not one creature at all. It is a warm compost city, and its residents are almost uncountable. Beetles work through it in tunnels, dark shining ones the size of a grain of rice and larger ones like polished seeds. Their larvae are white and soft and hungry. Mites cover the beetles. Springtails cover everything. Fly maggots turn in the wet places, and there are worms as thin as thread. The heap heaves, just slightly, as if it had a pulse.

It also has a smell that the inhabitants have made theirs. Ammonia sharp enough to sting, and under it the sweet flat stink of something that has been dying gently for a thousand years. The beetles live in this as fish live in water. They would not survive a breath of clean air.

## The wet people

At the edges of the heap, where seeps trickle and the tea-colored pools stand rimmed in orange, the salamanders live. They are pale and translucent, with pink smudges of blood showing through the skin and feathery gills that never went away. Their eyes are dots under the skin, blind and unneeded. Each is about the length of a forearm, and each moves with a slowness that looks like patience but is only economy. A salamander may go a season without a meal. It feels the world through its skin and its jaw, through the tiny tremors of a beetle in the mud, and it strikes with a quick gulp that leaves the water shivering.

They are the top predators of the floor and behave like it, which is to say hardly at all. The oldest may be fifty or sixty years old. It sits at the edge of a pool through whole seasons of noise and drip, a pale shape in the dark that has watched twenty generations of bats begin and end above it. It has no opinions about this. It waits, and eventually something falls.

## The visitors

There are also the ones who come and go. Not every year, and not in great numbers, a few people climb up through the snow from the lowland valleys, thin and wrapped in wool, breathing hard at the height. They carry resin torches, baskets, and coils of grass rope. The cave knows them by their smoke.

They are not tenants, and the cave does not treat them as such. The bats stir when they come, and a dark curtain of wings swirls off the ceiling, then settles again once the torches move on. The swiftlets protest for a day and a half. The crickets scatter. The visitors dig guano, take a few nests from the highest ledges, rub their palms against the shiny place on the wall where the rope hangs, and leave. Afterward the cave closes over what they did, sealing the smell of smoke into the smell of everything else. The rope rots. Mold takes the hooks. The colony does not remember them, or remembers them the way it remembers the wind.

## What lives there

If you were asked what lives in the Warm Throat, the truest answer might be that no single creature does. A million bats, ten thousand swiftlets, crickets and beetles and salamanders, and the green fungus and the tea-dark water together make one slow animal. It eats moths from the thin air over the snowfields, digests them on the stone, feeds the leftovers to the floor, and returns them to the ceiling as the next generation. It breathes at dawn and at dusk and shivers in waves. It talks constantly in a language too high and too low for people to follow, and what it says, over and over, appears to be *I am here, and here, and here.*

Outside it is black sky and ice. Inside, the warm dark hums, and the small brown bodies press closer together, and the drip goes on, and the mountain breathes out.
