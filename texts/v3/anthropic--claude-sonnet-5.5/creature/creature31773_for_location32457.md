# Those Who Keep the Works

Ask who lives at Karrow Bend and you will get, depending on who you ask, one answer, or four, or none. Here they are in order of size, smallest first.

## The Fine Machinery

The true citizens are the springtails and mites, and there are more of them in one cupped handful of mulch than there are people in the nearest city. They are pale and blind, and each is about the size of a comma. They have no names, no faces, and no idea of the engine above them. They are the district's clerks and stokers. Every leaf that falls is read by them, disassembled, and passed along as heat.

You will never see them working except as a shimmer when you lift a boot, a glitter of motion like static in the moss. But the whole warm body of the place is their payroll. When the drizzle cools the mat and the engine slows, they slow with it. When the sun angles in and the ropewort leans, they surge. Whatever the beam does, they are why it can.

## The Pearl-Keepers

The pale snails are a smaller kind of civil servant. They come out onto the warm iron at dawn, hundreds to the beam, and they graze the lichen, the film of algae, and the first flush of rust-bloom. Their shells are the color of old teeth. Each morning they rasp the beam clean, and each evening they withdraw into the pivots and joints, where the tallow-smell is strongest and the iron holds heat longest.

They are the engine's only lubricant that no one applies. The tender greases the pivots, but the snails' slime gets there first, in a thin invisible glaze. Neither knows about the other. The beam turns more smoothly than the schedule of tallow alone could explain, and the tender has long stopped asking why.

## The Warmer of Legs

The heron, or the bird that is like one, is the district's only resident with anything like dignity, and it appears to know it. It is tall, gray, and stooped, with a yellow eye that never quite settles on you. It stands in the stone channel where the pump water comes out warm, one leg folded up, then the other, alternating with the slow regularity of a pendulum. It has been seen to strike at a fish exactly once, and it missed.

The tender believes it is very old, or that there is a succession of them, each arriving when the last one goes. It keeps the channel's rhythm the way a lighthouse keeps a coast. If the bird ever left, someone would notice. Nobody could say how.

## The One Who Comes Twice a Week

The tender is a woman in her sixties, broad-shouldered, in a waxed coat the color of wet slate. She arrives on foot along the towpath tunnel every Tuesday and Friday, carrying a tin of tallow, a long-handled rake, a canvas roll of wrenches, and a flask. She is called Ines Harrow. She inherited the job from an uncle who inherited it from someone whose name is written in the earliest logbook, in a hand nobody can date. She does not think of herself as its owner, or as its master, or even quite as its employee. She thinks of herself as something like the district's neighbor.

She works without hurry. She greases the pivots, rakes the leaf-fall from the channel, and puts her palm against each copper joint to feel for the wrong kind of cold. She says little, but she talks to the beam, quietly, as one might talk to a large patient animal. When she writes *turned; clear; steady* in the book, she means it as a report, not a ritual. She has seen the entry *not steady* only twice, and both times the ropewort put out new growth over the affected spot within days, as though in apology.

Ines does not lock the engine house. She leaves the lantern on its hook unlit. She could not say who it is for.

## The Fox

The fox came once, and Ines saw it once, and that is all that is known. It crossed the flat in the mist with its head low, not hunting, not fleeing, following some line only it could feel. Perhaps it was drawn by the warmth. Perhaps it was young and lost. It did not come back, and the district did not seem to notice, and yet Ines tells the story often, always in the same words, like a small private relic.

## The One Body

The last inhabitant is the hardest to point to, because it is most of what you are standing on.

The ropewort, the moss, the ferns, the fig-root tower, the lone bramble: they are so alike in their pale under-leaves, their smell of warm bread and iron, and their appetite for rust, that the tender's uncle used to say they were not a community but a single person, spread thin and wearing many shapes. The botanists disagree. But if you kneel at the mat in the dead hour of the afternoon and watch the tendrils swing slowly toward the light in unison, it is difficult to feel that you are looking at separate things.

If it is one being, it is a strange one. It has no center, no brain, and nothing you could call a face. Its body is the mat, two centuries thick. Its skin is the moss. Its long green arms are the ropewort, reaching over the Drying Hall in the exact ribs and bays of the vanished roof. It remembers not in thought but in shape: the trellis is its memory, the street plan its handwriting. It has adopted the old machines as one adopts a limb or a tool. The buried copper coils are veins it did not grow but has learned to warm. The beam is a muscle it did not build but has learned to move, once every eleven or twelve seconds. The pulse is its heartbeat, and the beam its slow way of keeping itself from drowning.

It does not want much. It wants the water low. It wants the rot warm. It wants the iron, which it drinks from the old machines and turns to leaf. When something falls, it closes over the wound. When something wanders in, a fox or a heron or a woman with a tin of tallow, it makes no move to drive them out, and no move to keep them. Perhaps it cannot tell what they are. Perhaps it can, and has decided they belong to the loop.

Between the tender's care and the plant's patience, the two of them have arrived at an arrangement neither ever proposed. She keeps the machine clear, and it keeps the machine turning. She writes *steady*, and it is.

## At the Hour When None of Them Are Watching

At dusk the snails withdraw, the heron folds its second leg, and the mist gathers at the edges. Ines's boots have long since gone back down the tunnel of willow and ropewort. The lantern hangs unlit. The springtails go on reading the leaves. And the beam tilts down, and rests, and tilts back, and the whole green body of the district, ferns and figs and moss and mulch and iron, breathes out a ribbon of steam and holds its place in the dark, as it has for longer than anyone alive can say.
