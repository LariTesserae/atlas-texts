# Who Lives in the Wellwork

Nobody lives here who is not also on duty. The people of the basin would find that sentence odd, because to them there is no other way to live. They are called, in the language of knuckles and stone, something that can be rendered as **the Tenders**. The name is really a rhythm: two short raps, a pause, a long drag of the nail along the grain. If you rap it on a wall seam, someone forty paces away will look up.

## The Tenders

There are perhaps a hundred and forty of them, though nobody says the number aloud. They are a low, broad people, built to keep their footing on wet scalloped limestone. Their feet are wide and long-toed, with soles like tanned hide, and they grip the flutes of the walls the way you might grip a rope. Their skin is pale, with a green undertone from a life spent under the Sheets, so that in the shadowless light they look faintly like something grown. Their eyes are protected by a clear inner lid that slides across in the heaviest spray. It gives them a blinking, pausing quality, as if each of them were thinking a half-second behind the world.

Their hands are the most eloquent part of them. The fingertips are ridged and callused hard as horn, because a Tender talks mostly by tapping and signing, and the fingertips take a beating. Their hearing is mostly ruined by adulthood, and they don't mind. They hear through the jaw and the teeth and the soles of the feet. A Tender presses a forehead to a wall to listen to a Column three hundred paces off and can tell you if its note has flattened.

They dress in woven moss-fiber and in cord twisted from the roots of the pale tree, waxed with lime and spray-fat until it sheds water. Nothing they wear is ever dry. Dryness is their one great luxury. A blanket that has spent a season behind a flowstone curtain, where the drip is diverted by a groove, is an heirloom. A dry child's cot is a wedding gift. When a Tender says a person is *dry-hearted*, it means that person is safe and well loved.

They live in the wall. The great scallops, each wide as a doorway and shell-smooth, have been walled and curtained into rooms. Hammocks are slung above the highest tide-line, and pockets of black-stemmed fern are cultivated at the thresholds. The rooms are joined by ledges and rope-cord bridges of tree root. At night the whole wall shows a scatter of small warm lamps, oil rendered from algae, burning behind curtains of flowstone so that each room glows like the inside of a lantern-fruit.

## What they do

Every Tender has a work, and every work is a piece of upkeep on something they don't fully understand.

**The Reseaters** are the climbers. They travel the filaments, the hair-fine lines that hold the Sheets, on slings of root-cord with hooks of bone and old brass. They work by feel, because a filament that has whipped free and drifted may have reseated itself wrongly, and a wrongly seated Sheet throws its light into the rock. A good Reseater can sense the tension in a filament through the hook, the way a fisher feels a line. Their hands are scarred by the crack of filaments. Half of them are missing a finger, and they wear the loss like rank.

**The Borewrights** go down into the Columns' dark passages at the base, in the short silences of the Turn, with lamps and scrapers. They clear silt from the throats of the stone. They have perhaps thirty breaths to do it. They are the bravest of the Tenders and the most quickly worn out, and they retire young, deaf as the walls, to the quiet work of teaching.

**The Sorters** walk the floor and mend the pebble drifts. After each Turn, small things sometimes end up in the wrong place. A gravel drift is a hand-width off, a knuckle-sized stone has rolled among the fines. The floor sorts itself by size, as it always has. But the Sorters believe the sorting is a kind of language the basin speaks, and that a wrong stone is a misspelling.

**The Dish-watchers** sit on a ledge of the north wall with a bad view of a distant glint. Once an hour the dish swings and the Sheets answer, and the ripple of moving light crosses the basin from one side to the other. The Dish-watchers count the ripples. They are the timekeepers, and they knot the count into cords.

**The Listeners** do not so much work as attend. They are the eldest, the ones so deaf that the basin has become something like a single sound to them. They sit in the mouths of scallops, foreheads to the stone. When something is wrong with the chord, a note gone sour or a Column that ought to have started and hasn't, a Listener taps out the correction to the rest.

## How they speak

A Tender can tap a message through the wall seams that will carry to every scallop in the basin. Different flowstone curtains ring at different notes, and the messengers play them the way you would play a xylophone. An alarm, a birth, a death, a broken filament, a visitor on the stair: each has its rhythm, and each curtain its use.

But there is another speech, and it is the one that matters most, because it happens only in the ring.

The seven low pylons at the center of the floor make a cone of quiet that lasts about as long as a held breath. The Tenders take this literally. They believe the cone is a single breath, that the ring drinks your breath and gives you back a bubble of silence exactly that big, and that when you exhale, the roar floods in. So they have learned to speak on the inhale. In the ring, a Tender draws words inward in a thin, reversed whisper, sentences pulled in over the teeth. It is a strange sound, like a voice being sipped. A very good speaker can fit a whole sentence into one long intake.

Because of this, everything that is *said*, rather than tapped, is short. A name is given in the ring. A vow is made there. A person who is dying is carried into the ring and asked one question, and has one breath to answer. Their spoken language is a language of last words and first words, dense as pressed flowers. Children are taught to hold their breath long, and to be sparing with it, because breath is what the Tenders have to say things with.

## The Turn

In the moment the Columns fall silent, one after another like a struck chord dying, everything in the basin stops.

The slate-colored amphibians in the channels tilt their heads a few seconds beforehand. This is the Tenders' first warning. Small children are put to watch them, and it is the finest job a child can have: to lie on the warm limestone with your chin on your arms and your eyes on the Tilters, and to tap once, sharply, when they turn. Then every Tender in the basin lowers their hands. Nobody signs or taps. The Reseaters hang motionless in their slings. The Borewrights, if they are in the bores, wait in the dark with their lamps cupped.

For those few breaths the only sound is dripping.

One Tender, chosen by rota, the **Answerer**, will be standing in the ring. In the last drop of silence, when the final Column has died, the Answerer draws a single word in over the teeth. It is the Tenders' name for the place itself, and also the word for *present*, *accounted for*, *I am here*. The Answerer says it into the cone, into the quiet, into the listening.

Then the Columns start again, out of order, and the rain re-plans itself.

Nobody knows if this does anything. It might be a courtesy, or a superstition, or a maintenance procedure whose function the makers never wrote down. But the Tenders tell of a year when the Answerer, a young man frightened by his first Turn, was late into the ring and missed the moment. The silence ran eleven breaths instead of four. The amphibians did not tilt back. Someone crossed the floor at a run and said the word too late, in the wrong place, and the Columns coughed and resumed. The young man became a Borewright, and never spoke of it again. There is a knot for that Turn on the pale tree.

## The tree, and the dead

The one pale, leafless tree on its ledge is called the Elder, though it may not be a tree. Its roots run down the rock in a hundred wet cords, and the Tenders climb them daily and cut from them what the community needs: rope, thread, wax-fibre, fuel. The cords never seem to shrink. Some Tenders believe the tree is the same age as the Sheets. Others believe it was planted, and that a planter is a thing worth wondering about.

Knotted into the roots, at the height of a hand, are the records. The Tenders tally not each Turn, which would be fifteen thousand a year, but the *irregular* ones: the long silences, the wrong order of restarts, the Column that stayed quiet a breath extra. These knots are the community's only history, and they read them like scripture.

When a Tender dies, the body is carried out into the channels at the beginning of a Turn and laid in the current. The water does what it does. The bones, over many Turns, are sorted with the pebbles: the long bones into the drifts of the coarse stones, the small bones into the gravel, the finger and toe bones to the fine bank at the south of the floor. A visitor can walk there and not know it. The Tenders go there to talk to nobody. They say the dead are being *put in order*, that a person who has been sorted has been *understood*, and that this is the best a body can hope for.

## The other inhabitants

**The Tilters** are the slate-colored amphibians. They are not owned, and it would be an insult to suggest it. They lie in the warm channels with only their eyes above the current, and their tilt is trusted more than any Tender's ear. When one dies in the current, the Tenders lay it in the Sorters' drift like one of their own.

**The Stilts** are the colorless, long-legged birds. They stalk the floor's edge and eat the Tilters when they can. The Tenders neither love them nor drive them off. They carry the birds' eggs, pale as the walls, up to the ledges and eat them in season, and they keep the population level, though nobody could tell you the rule for it.

**The Unlanded** is the translucent, shivering, moth-like thing that rides the updrafts and never seems to touch anything. Most days there is one. Some days there are none, and the Tenders are uneasy on those. An old Dish-watcher once claimed to have seen it settle on the great dish on the north wall, briefly, just before the dish swung. She said it was the dish's hand. Nobody has confirmed this, and nobody has refuted it, because nobody can climb that high.

## The hut and the socket

The squat stone hut by the eastern wall is empty. There is a bench and a socket in the floor. Something used to sit there. The Tenders call it, simply, **the Sitter**, and do not say what it was. A person, a machine, an animal, a god. Every morning a child sweeps the bench dry and clears the socket of silt. This is not on any rota. No one assigns it, and no one has ever failed to do it.

Now and then, in the silence of a Turn, the Listeners say they feel something in the socket. It is a hum, or the memory of a hum, so faint it is more like a change in the pressure of the stone. It is never strong enough to be sure of, and it is never gone for long.

## Visitors

When someone comes down the stair, having crossed the thirty warm steps into the wall of sound, a child is sent to meet them. The child takes them by the hand, without speaking, and leads them across the floor to the ring. The visitor is set inside it, and the child steps back.

The cone forms. The roar dims. And the visitor has one breath, the same breath every Tender has, to say who they are. The Tenders watch the visitor's mouth, and what the visitor says in that breath determines everything else. A visitor who uses the whole breath on a long explanation is thought a fool. A visitor who says nothing at all, and only listens, is offered a dry cot.
