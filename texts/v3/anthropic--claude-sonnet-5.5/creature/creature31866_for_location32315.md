# Who Lives in the Terraces

The first thing to understand is that no one here lives alone, and most of the inhabitants are not people.

## The Gardeners

The people call themselves by a word that a dry-lander would hear as a long hum with a click at the end. Its nearest meaning is *those who tend*. They are lean and pale, and the pallor has a faint green cast where moss has been allowed to grow on the shoulders and scalp. It is grooming, not neglect. A good pelt of moss is considered handsome, and the old often wear it thick as a shawl.

Their bodies are made for cold, slow work. The hands are broad, the fingers webbed to the first knuckle, and the palms are calloused to the hardness of horn. The feet are long, prehensile at the toes, and they grip a stem as well as a hand does, so a gardener can hang upside down from one rope-stem with her feet while both hands work a lattice-joint. Along the ribs, under the arm, lie thin folded slits that flutter when the person is at rest and lie flat when she is working hard. Nobody remarks on them. Hearts beat slowly and breath is unhurried. A person may hang motionless in a shaft of light for an hour, apparently doing nothing, and be in fact reading the current.

Hair is worn long. It streams like the moss-carpets, and people can tell each other's moods by its motion before a word is said. It is braided when work is heavy, and braided with cord for mourning.

They are quiet in the hand and loud in the throat. Speech travels well in water, and the language has grown to use that. It has words in hum and words in click, and a whole grammar of pitch. Under it runs a second language of hand-shapes for when the water is too full of drummer-fish to be heard through. A third language is the reeds, which say only a few things but say them for a mile. A child of six can read most reed-calls. An old woman can tell from a single stroke which mallet was used, and therefore who struck it, and usually what mood they were in.

## Names and Cords

Every person is born to a house, and every house is born to a ring of lattice. A baby's name is tied into a cord by the midwife before the child is a day old, and the cord is knotted into the wall of the house at the height of the mother's shoulder. Each year on the child's naming-day another knot is added, and at the end of life the cord is coiled and laid into the floor with the body.

The cords are the town's memory more than any speech is. A wall of an old house is a ledger. The thick dark knots near the base are the founders. Higher up are their children and grandchildren, in cords of different fibers and dyes, each knot recording something: a wedding, a flood of silt, a bad winter, a lost child found. Elders can run a hand up a wall in the dim light and read four generations in the time it takes to swim to the door.

Cord is also used to bind, and in the town's thinking this is close to a sacred act. To tie two stems together is to make a promise on behalf of both. A knot that outlasts its tier is a good knot. A knot that fails is talked about for years.

## Work, and What It Means

There is no ruler. There are the Pruners, who are not a caste but a habit of respect: those with the best eye for what a stem will do in twenty years. They gather in the court at each turn of the season and argue, often for days, about which lane to close, which dome to let thicken, and which ropes to thin so the light can reach the lower town. Arguments are conducted at a full-throated bellow, with hands. Out-of-town visitors, if there were any, would think it a fight. It is actually the nearest thing the place has to government, and it is usually settled by someone pointing out what the shoots want.

The whole ethic of the people is a single principle, learned before any other, which children recite before they can swim well: *leave the one that goes with the light, cut the one that goes against the wall.* From this comes a way of seeing that is both practical and moral. A shoot that grows toward the pattern is fed. A shoot that grows against it is starved, gently, by shading it and letting its neighbors take the water. Nobody thinks of this as cruel. It is simply how anything is made to stand for four generations.

There is an unease under it that the older gardeners will admit, if asked in the right season. People also grow crooked. A child may refuse the pattern, or leave, or turn out to have a voice that will not go where the choir goes. The town has no word for punishing this, and it does not starve anyone. But it knows how it feels to be a shoot that is not fed, and it has, in its cords, a small number of long knots that are tied off to nothing, for people who swam out over the rim and did not come back. Those cords are hung at the edge of the court, in the shade. Everyone knows where they are.

## Food and Table

The people eat what the lake gives. Sweet young shoots are stripped from the growing tips of the long ropes and eaten raw. Gas-bladders are split and pressed into a sour paste that keeps for months in sealed clay. Grasses are pounded into flat cakes and dried on racks in the shallows where the sun gets through. Shrimp, grazing crustaceans, and the drummer-fish are taken with woven traps and never in the breeding weeks. The ribbon eels, whose song brings the harvest, are not eaten at all. Neither are the trout, who are guests of the whole town.

Meals are communal, taken in the lee of a house wall where the current is gentle, and there is a strict order to the passing of the food. The youngest go last and eat the most, because they have the most growing to do. It is considered a fine thing to be able to hand a cake across a table without moving your body, using only the current.

## The Trout

The big trout are the length of a man and older than most of the walls. They drift over the meadows with the slow gravity of aldermen, gray-green and pearl-flanked, and they are known. Each has a name, a lineage, and a habitual route. One is called *Late Again*, because she is always the last to arrive at the court. One is called *Second Knot* after a wall where he likes to rest. Children are taught to greet them as they would an elder, with a low hum and an open palm, and the trout, who do not speak, tilt a fin to acknowledge it.

When a trout dies, it is mourned. The reeds ring the death-call, the body is carried to the silt apron and laid in it, and a cord is tied and buried with it. People say the trout carry the town's patience, and that the day none is left, something will have gone out of the water.

## The Smaller Neighbors

The drummer-fish are the town's clock and its weather. When the evening pulse is quick, the water is warming and the ice will break early. When it is slow, it is a long winter. Gardeners lay a hand flat on the trunk of a rope in the evening and read the pulse through the wood.

The clicking shrimp are the town's applause. Their fine dry crackle rises whenever a large group is content, at feasts and births, and drops away at a death. Some say this is coincidence. Nobody who lives here believes it.

The lantern snails are not owned, but each lane has a keeper who feeds its snails on fine-cut algae and moves them along the stems as the plants grow. A dark stretch of lane is a small scandal and is fixed before the next meal.

The ribbon eels are left to themselves. When they sing, everyone stops working for a moment and lifts their face. It is not quite superstition. It is closer to how a dry-land people might pause when the first rain of the year falls on the roof.

## The Keepers of the Bell-Tree

At the deepest hollow, a small number of people live who are not quite gardeners. There are perhaps nine at any time. They are called the Listeners, and they tend the bell-tree, which no one prunes. Their work is to sit against the trunk in all seasons and to hear. They know its pitch through the year better than any instrument could measure. They tell the town when the tone shifts, and by how much, and they are the ones who set the reeds' tuning at the turn of each season, so that all the bells of the basin are in accord with the one under the rock.

They are usually old, and often nearly deaf to ordinary speech, having grown so used to the deeper sound that voices are a kind of thin noise. Their hair is mostly moss. They eat little. When they die, they are buried in the silt at the tree's foot, and it is said that the tone deepens a fraction for a season afterward.

## Winter, and the Lid

When the ice comes down, the town changes its manner. Work slows to a crawl. People cluster into the deepest houses, sharing warmth, and their metabolism falls to the level of a slow dream. They talk little. They tell the oldest stories, in a murmur, around the bases of the walls. One of these stories is of the air, the place beyond the pewter ceiling where the ducks come from, a place of wind and of a hard bright light that hurts. Most of the people say it is a tale. A few say the ducks are the messengers of it. None of them have gone to look, and nobody has been told they may not.

When the ice cracks in spring, the whole town rises to the shallows and listens. The booming and groaning travel through the water like the voice of something enormous turning over in sleep. Children laugh and cling to their parents. Old people close their eyes. Then someone strikes a reed, and the first new shoots are counted, and the work begins again.

## The Evening

At dusk, the light drops to violet. The lantern snails come up along the lanes. The gardeners set down their knives and drift, without haste, toward the court, where they hang in the green vault among the shafts of failing light. The drummers begin. The reeds ring. Someone hums the note of the bell-tree, low, and then a few more join, and then the whole gathered town is humming it, three hundred throats, the pitch that has held the place together for longer than the cords can say.

Below them, in the dark of the deepest hollow, the bell-tree answers.

Nobody claims to understand what it is answering. But everyone here, every evening, waits to hear that it does.
