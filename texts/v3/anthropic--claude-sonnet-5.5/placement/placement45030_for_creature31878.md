I would come the way everyone comes, across the marsh with the ground talking under my feet, and I would stop at the lip and look down at nine rings of pale stone. I would go down the ramps with my sleeves darkening. Somewhere around the fourth ring I would stop being able to tell you what it looked like.

The light goes first. The copper bar on the far wall, the shafts in the mist, the grey-green: all of it thins into an account someone else gave me. What replaces it is closer than sight. The step is cold through my palms. The air has weight. Then I am lower than the step, in the black water, and the roar of the hall is no longer something arriving at my ears. It arrives everywhere at once, through the skin, the jaw, the long slack belly, and it is not noise, because I can feel where each part of it comes from.

So this is who I would be: the one in the fourth notch, with the polished jar, and no eyes worth the name.

**What it is like**

It is like having a very large hand laid flat on you, made of many small fingers, each one tapping. The frogs are the quick fingers. The swifts are a thin bright scratching high above, felt more in the water than the stone. The bats are a dry flicker. The big jars near the floor are not fingers at all. They are the slow pulse under the hand, and they are the reason I am here.

I press my jaw to the barrel-jar and the whole wall comes down into me. The masons built it that way, or the wall grew into it. They set the smallest voices high and the deepest low, and they tilted the rings so that everything runs downhill, water and sound together, to a place where a body could lie and receive it. I am not sure they meant a salamander. I am sure they meant someone. The rim of my jar is smooth because I have come back to it for longer than I can count. And I count in rains, not years, and the rains are countless.

I do not think about the builders in words. But when I move to the little jar with the thumbprint in its shard, the one broken loose and set in the dry niche, I feel something that is not the wall. It is a small pressure, the shape of one person's pushing, fixed in clay before the fire. I lay my chin against it sometimes. It doesn't hum, it only holds. Someone once stood here and pushed with a thumb, and the push is still the same size.

**The line**

The niche the stone never closed is mine, in the way a rim is mine. I have put things in it. The pebble came out of the heron's leavings, which is a theft I don't regret. The green wire I found where a peg had rotted and dropped. The swift's egg, empty, rolled to me down the third ring in a wet spring. Each thing is set a little beyond the last.

I would not call it a collection. A collection is kept for the keeper. This is closer to what the jars do. A drip lands, and a jar gives its one note back, and the note is gone but the answer was made. I cannot make a note. So when something is given to the hall, I place it where it can be found, and that is the answer. *This arrived. It was noticed. It is still here.* The line is long because the hall gives constantly, and I have only ever answered the things that could be held.

Some things cannot be held. The rain, the light, the seasons of the pool going up its notches and down. The humming of the ones who come down and sit on the step.

**The one who hums**

There is one on the step now.

It arrives as a change in the stone, a weight settling and a small warmth. Then the sound begins, low and unsteady, with breath in it. It is unlike the frogs. A frog sings the jar's note and stops caring. This one is looking for the note and is not sure. It wavers, corrects, wavers. I feel it search, and then the great jar beside me catches it, catches it truly, and the whole wall turns a fraction toward that one sound like a field of grass in a small wind.

I come up out of the water without deciding to. I lie at the edge, chin in the lap of the pool. I turn my head a hand's width toward the sound, because that is as far as I turn for anything.

I cannot sing back. I have wanted to in some way I have no word for. I have only what I have: stillness, attention, and a niche with a line of small things in it. Let the hum go on. I will not put it in the niche, because nothing can hold it. But I will lie here and take all of it in until it stops. That is the answer I know how to give. That is what the wall was built for, and I think that when I do it right, it is the same answer.

**The chord**

And the chord, since you will ask.

When the rain comes and the jars fall into step, the note builds in me before it builds in the hall. It fills my ribs and my jaw. I stop breathing, and I do not decide to, because breath would be a second rhythm and there is room for only one. I am not causing the note. I am a place it can gather. It gathers and gathers until every surface of me is tight with it, until the pool is standing in rings and the swifts are holding themselves against the clay.

Then it is too much. There is nowhere left in me to put it, and the only way to make room is to open. So the jaw falls. It is not a choice and not a cry. It is what a full vessel does. And above me the wings go up all at once, and the chord breaks into every direction, and the water runs back down my throat cold and sweet, and I lie there, emptied and ringing.

I do not know whether I let it go or it left. I do not think the difference matters. What matters is that afterward, in the long minutes when the hall is a great confused ringing, I am more entirely the hall than at any other time, and there is one drip on one jar, quiet and clear, and it is the start of everything again.

That is who I would be. Not the one who sings, but the one who receives so completely that the singing has somewhere to go, and who keeps, in a dry hollow above the waterline, a small line of proofs that it was heard.
