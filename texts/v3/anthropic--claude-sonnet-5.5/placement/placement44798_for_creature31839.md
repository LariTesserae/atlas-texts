I keep arriving first at the outfall grate, because the description does. I stand there with the fish gathering and the salamanders still as dropped gloves, and I realize I'm the visitor, the one who counts. Nothing else in the ward counts. They were shaped into the interval so thoroughly that they don't need to. A visitor is a guest with a watch in a house where nobody owns one, and I don't want to be a guest.

So I go lower. The salamanders are the obvious lords, but their whole lives are the difference between waiting and striking, and I don't think I want a life that is one hunger with a clock on it.

Next I find the hook. It hangs from the gantry crane into the main basin, lowered and left, and it rises and sinks a hand's breadth with the aquifer's breath. I stay with it a while because it is so patient. Snails have covered its shank in oxide-red. Silt gathers on the inside of the curve, where a load would have sat. It would be a good thing to be. But it is a place more than a someone. It has been waiting for a weight to lift, and the ward has quietly decided against weights.

What I keep drifting back to is smaller and further up the street.

I am one of the bronze ones.

I was born on the collar of a lamp standard, a cast-bronze ring under the globe that no one has polished in a hundred years. Everything I ate as a hatchling was that ring, so my shell came out the color of butter held to a candle. I am the size of a thumbnail, and the world I know is a few arm-spans of iron post, one bronze collar, and the round glass room above it, where the light lives.

The light is what I am for, though I don't think of it that way. The mold has a slow, warm, gassy taste, and I graze along its edge. It advances a little every season and I eat it back a little every day. Where the mat thins to nothing and the glass begins, my hunger simply ends. Glass tastes of nothing at all. I turn around there, and behind me the pane is clear. That turning is the whole reason the lamp is a disc of light and not a fog. Nobody decided where the circle should stop. It stops where an appetite gives out, and I'm coming to think much of the ward's order is made of such places.

The mold and I have never met, exactly. It is so slow that a conversation would take my grandchildren's lifetimes. But I've come to feel it as a neighbor on the other side of a wall, brightening when the water rises and dimming when it falls. At the high water the air thickens and the mold gets rich. Its light comes through my own translucent lip and turns it faintly green, and I feel briefly like something lit from inside. Then the water draws down, the light thins, and I go back to being a bronze spot on a black post.

The beam comes through the iron before it comes through the air. Every snail knows this and no snail could explain it. First there's a shudder in the standard's foot, from the wet stone, from the footbridge rails, from the whole connected skeleton of the ward, all telling the metal that the pump-house is about to speak. I clamp. So does everyone else. For a few heartbeats I am only shell, sealed to the iron, and the world is a dark pressure with a green glow bleeding through my roof. Then the groan arrives, late and enormous, and I feel the wave pass over the ironwork the way it must pass over a field of grass. Then I loosen, and the rasp of grazing starts again, that faint page-turning in the next room.

I don't count between strokes. The interval isn't a number to me, only the shape of my day: a stretch of eating, a tightening in the foot, a clamp, a release. If someone asked me how long eleven minutes was, I would say it was the length of one edge of the light.

The unseen keeper is only me, then, and my neighbors on the other lamps, and the cousins who cut the newest marks in the gauge. I've never seen the gauge. I understand only that at the top of each breath some of us climb as high as the water goes and graze one bright line, one rasp wide. We aren't recording anything. It is what the height tastes like. But afterward the column is longer.

I think this is the odd comfort of being a small keeper. I'm not devoted, and no one appointed me. A great many of us are each doing the nearest thing to hand, and from far enough away it looks like care. A visitor on the street sees clean glass and a neat disc and assumes a keeper with a ladder and a rag.

Sometimes the cobbles hand back a footstep, and then a second, late, from the opposite wall. Someone has come to stand under my lamp, near enough that I feel their weight on the rail. They're quiet in a particular way. They're waiting for something, and they've started to count. Their fingers tap once against the iron and stop, and tap again. I feel the pulse of it through my foot, faster than the ward and less certain.

I don't mind. Let them count for both of us.

The standard shivers. I seal. In the dark under my own shell I feel, as always, the tiny sure company of thousands, all closing together, all a breath ahead of the sound.

Then the beam nods, and I let go.
