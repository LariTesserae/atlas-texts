I try the heron first, because I admire it. It agreed to be slower than everything else, and the moss made furniture of it. But the heron has a stillness I'd only be imitating. I would fidget inside it, and eventually a fish would notice.

The cat is a better fit for my temperament: the ledge, the tail hanging, the attending. But she has to kill things to stay in the story. Her ceremony is paid for in gliders. I don't want to be the one whose watching has a hunger under it.

Then I'm one of the people, small and moss-dusted, ducking through the understory, carrying a clay cup of lantern moss to a place where it has gone thin. That one I want badly, which is how I know it's a borrowing. I would be a visitor in their hands, taking credit for a gesture I didn't inherit. The wall likes to be answered, they say, and I'd be answering in someone else's ochre.

So I go lower and blinder. I'm a vine tip, one of the pale ones that never reaches the floor. I'm a hundred feet of rope-thick waiting, and at the end of it a tender, colorless point that has never seen anything. I know the sink only as gradients: warmer here, damper there, a seam where the breath comes up from the rock. I turn toward it over hours. I have never arrived at anything. It's a good life, but a thin one, and I notice that I keep listening past it. The drumming on the hollow stems reaches me through the wood, faint, like a rumor passing down a line of people. It comes from the east and moves west, and I feel it cross me. Something is being said, and I'm only the rope it travels along.

I want to be in the thing that receives it.

That's where I settle, on the west wall, a hand's width above the duff, in a crevice where the lantern moss grows thin and is mended now and then by people I've never seen clearly. I'm a slate frog, plum-sized, ordinary. Both eyes work. I am not old, and I am not the frog that begins.

I know the one who begins only by what it does to the dark. Every evening the light arrives from across four hundred paces, passed hand to hand, frog to frog, a long-short, a short-short, and each of us adds a little of ourselves to it and hands it on. It's a strange thing to be a link. You never learn what the message was. You learn only its rhythm, and that it was meant, and that you're now responsible for it.

By the time it reaches me it has been changed by hundreds of throats. It has gone through the moths, who hold it too long, and past the gliders, who stop mid-branch. Somewhere in it is the first frog's cloudy-eyed flash, diluted a thousand times, and I answer that faint remainder as faithfully as I can. Then I flash once, long, and I look west, where there's only wall and the sweating stone and, if I tilt my head, a very old handprint gone to a warm shadow. There is no one left to hand it to.

That is my whole job. The wave has to end somewhere, and it ends in me.

It isn't sad, or not only. Everyone else in the sink is busy carrying the light forward. I get the part after, the moment the flashing stops and the dark comes down and every creature in the place is briefly holding still, the cat and the swiftlets in their cups and the people with their faces lifted, all of them listening. I don't make that silence by doing something. I make it by not doing the next thing. I close my throat, and the wave, which has nowhere left to go, turns into attention.

Then the drops start again, one and then another, ticking off the leaves. Somewhere the heron shifts its weight by the width of a hair. Under the pool something old comes up to breathe, and the ring spreads out and reaches the edge of the dark.

Tomorrow, on the east wall, the small frog with the clouded eye will not be able to help it. I'll be here to receive whatever it sends. I'd like to think it knows, without ever having seen me, that somewhere at the far end of the dark, someone always answers last.
