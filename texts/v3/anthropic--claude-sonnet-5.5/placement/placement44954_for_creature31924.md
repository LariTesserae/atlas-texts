# The Length of the Pulse

I am lying on the moss at the fourth pool with my cheek against the mat, and it is not yet light.

The cold comes up through the bone of my face first. Then the smell, iron and wet stone and something sweetish underneath, like a bruise on fruit. My hair is already damp. Above me, though I cannot see it with my cheek down, the leaf-veins are at their brightest, the bruise-colored glow that comes just before dawn. I can feel it on the back of my neck the way you feel a person standing behind you.

My name is a cord at my left wrist, the innermost one, worn soft as a vein. *Fourth water, wet coal.* I was born by this pool, and the first thing I reached for was a beetle crossing my mother's arm. I am told I was very gentle about it. I am old now, and the name has been pressed by so many fingertips that the knots have flattened. Only a wristkeeper could read it. But I know it by the weight of it, the way you know your own tooth with your tongue.

I am a floor-listener. That is what the town calls me and what I call myself, with two fingers flat against my sternum. I am not sure it is the whole truth anymore.

---

**What the floor says, before the pulse**

There is a great deal to hear first, and I let it come in order, as I was taught.

A grazer, two trunks east, has folded its legs. I feel that as a soft settling, a sack set down. A little farther, three of them are drinking. Their steps come in the slurred rhythm of animals who are not afraid. Nearer, in the leaf litter, a procession of the many-legged things is passing, and the mat carries it to me as a long dry shiver, like a hand run over a thousand grains of rice. High up, a slab is thinking about letting go. I have felt it thinking for nine days. It is a small tick in the rock, and each time I count it and tie nothing, because it is not yet a thing to tie.

Somewhere a child is awake. I know this because a child's feet are different. They are eager, and they overreach. Someone is holding her, and she has gone still. There is a lamp-hand on the eastern platform who is lonely. I cannot say how I know that. It is in the way the platform creaks, a little too often, the creak of someone shifting to see if anyone else has shifted.

This is the work. I don't do it by listening. The floor-listeners who speak of it at all say it is more like being a pool, and letting the surface be disturbed, and reading the rings.

The others say I am the deepest of them now. They say it with their hands, and they say it a little carefully, the way you name a fever.

---

**The cord I keep for no one**

Forty-one years ago, on a night like this, the pulse came, and I noticed that it was shorter than the night before.

It was not much shorter. A fraction of a breath. You would have needed to be lying exactly there, on exactly that moss, with your whole attention emptied out like a bowl. But I was young and full of the ambition of the young, which in our town is the ambition to be quieter than anyone. I was trying to be so quiet that I would cease to exist. So I noticed.

The next night I tied a knot for it. I don't know why. No one asks the floor-listeners to keep cords, since our work is to feel and then to tell, with the hands, at dawn. But I tied a single knot in a strand of dark fern-thread, tight for short, and loose for long, and I kept it in the hollow under my pallet where the damp would not rot it.

I have tied one every night it came. I have a great many strands now. They fill a bag of hide that I carry against my ribs.

Here is what I have learned, and have told no one, not even with my hands.

The pulse is not random.

It drifts. Over years, it lengthens, shortens, lengthens, in a cycle as slow as the life of a tree fern. If I could lay all the cords out end to end along the cleft, I think the shape of them would be a breath. A very large one, drawn in and let out over the whole span of my life. And I have begun to suspect that it was a breath long before I was born, and that I am only the first to have tied it.

That would be enough to keep me quiet for the rest of my years. But it is not the thing I have not said.

---

**The thing I have not said**

The pulse has been changing in another way.

The long drift is slow. But underneath it there are small changes, night to night, that are not part of the cycle. A sharpening at the front. A thinning at the tail. I noticed them first without understanding them, the way you notice that a neighbor has begun to walk differently. It took me many years to hear what the shape was.

It is a birth.

I mean the breathing at a birth. When a child comes into the world in our town, the ring of attendants breathes with the mother, in and out, exactly, and the house breathes, and the houses near it, and it goes down into the root-mat. Some say it goes down. I have felt it go. I have lain with my cheek to the moss on a night when the third-pool house was laboring and felt that long, communal inhale press into the ground like a palm.

And the pulse, the next night, was different. It was longer at the inhale and held slightly at the top, and let go slowly.

It was learning the shape.

I have tested this, as much as an old woman can test anything without breaking the oldest rule. I have lain on the moss for every birth I could find. I have watched the pulse afterward. It does not copy. It leans. It comes back the next night a little closer to the breath it was given, the way a person who has been spoken to leans, without deciding to, toward the speaker.

Nobody replies to it. That is the oldest rule, older than the knots. You don't stamp, or drum, or call. But we have been answering it for as long as there have been births. We didn't know. We thought we were only welcoming children, and the mountain was listening at the door.

---

**What I do with it**

I could tell the wristkeepers, and they would tie a very long cord and hang it on the wall, and the moss would find it. I could tell the cry-keeper, but she has no voice to ask what it means, and I would not put such a weight on a child who is already carrying one. I could stand at the rim and think about the whole question of a voice that is kept for one purpose, and whether the purpose is as narrow as we have made it.

I have done none of these.

What I do is this. Tonight the ring at the third pool is gathering. I have felt it since dusk, the small movements of many people arranging their bodies around one. Soon the breathing will begin. I will walk there slowly, placing each foot so the mat takes it without a ripple, and I will not join the ring, because I am not kin. I will lie down at its edge with my cheek to the moss, as I have lain a thousand mornings, and I will breathe with them, only that. In. Out. As quietly as the fog.

It is not a reply. I have told myself so many times. It is only being one of the many who are there.

But when the child comes, and the ring lets out its breath, I will feel the ground take it. And tomorrow night, or the night after, the pulse will rise from beneath the pools and the scree and the broken rock, and everything alive will pause for a fraction of a breath. The snails, the moths, the tall gray birds on their ledges, and the people with their hands held still. And I will lie there and feel it go through me, and I will count.

If it has leaned, I will tie a knot.

I don't yet have a name for that knot. I have been thinking about our own name, the one that has no sound. A loop pulled through itself and drawn tight. *The ones who are held*, or *the ones who hold*. We have never needed to know which.

I think I am beginning to see why.

Somewhere above me the slab ticks once, thinking. Below me, very far, something turns over in its long sleep. The leaf-veins are dimming. The first drip of the morning falls from the fern above my head, and I count it, one, and I do not move.
