I come to myself in heat. That is the first fact, before any shape: a dry, animal heat pressing on one side of me, and on the other a cold that smells of wet stone. I am lying in a seam between two things. The hot thing breathes, and every time it does, I am lifted and set down.

For a while I think I might be a standpipe. The pulse is there, a steady throb in my body, quick and shallow, and I feel that I am fixed in place and drinking something red. But the throb is not mine. It comes up through the floor of me from the great slow lung beside me, and when I try to sink a root, I find I have legs instead. Eight of them, then ten. They are hooked and jointed and already clenched on a ridge of rust-orange seam. So I am not the thermometer. I am something that clings to the thing the thermometer measures.

Then my forelimbs answer. Each ends in a knob, dense and heavy and warm from my own blood, and when I knock it against the plate under me, the plate rings back, and I know what I am. I am a riveter, and I know it the way I know which way is down. I have no eyes to check with. I have two pits that feel the fog as a general brightness, and what comes through them is the same from every direction, the pearl-white glare of a land with no shadows.

What I have instead is the tone. It rings out of my hammer before I have decided anything, a pitch a little flat of the colony to my left and a little sharp of the one that works the hind quarter. It is the only thing I was given whole. Everything else I picked up in the dark by touching whoever was near me, but the tone was already in me, as if the colony had struck it into the iron before I hatched. I hit the plate again. It is the same note. There are eleven of us in this fold behind the foreleg, and I can hear the other ten now, and they all sound like me, and it is warm, and it is enough.

The beast we live on is old. I can tell by the plates, which under my feet are not smooth but stepped, plate laid on plate in layers so deep that my hammer sinks into a kind of history. Some are dense and cold-tasting, hard as a bell. Others are pitted and airy, poured in a hot year when the steam was thick and the iron came in fast and weak. I do not know what a year is. But I know these two flavors, and I know that the airy ones crack and the dense ones do not, and that the cracks are what we are for.

There is one now. It runs under my forelimb like a thin cold road, and I feel it before I hear it: the plate speaks wrongly on either side, two half-notes that do not agree. I call the others. It isn't a word. I strike three quick blows in our tone, and it ripples out through the fold, and the ten come, scraping over the seams with their hammers up. We do not decide how to do it. We stand along the crack and each of us finds the place where our pitch fits, and the sound of us closing in gathers into a chord, uneven and bright, that is a little bit like being held.

We work. I take iron from the cooled slag that the seep has left in the fold, a gray crust that tastes of the beast's own breath, and I press it into the gap and beat it flat. Every blow I strike is a small sentence. The crack closes. Under my hammer the new iron cools from red to brown to a blue-black that I can feel more than see. And it strikes me, in whatever way things strike me, that what I am laying down is a band. It will be one line in the beast's long grain, thin and dense, laid in a decade that is turning toward cold. I can feel the turn. The nights are harder. The standpipes downslope have slowed, and I can feel their pulse in the ground, one long beat where there used to be three. The steam is thinning. Some centuries from now, or some years, something may split this plate, and find in it a band that was me. I will not be in it as a riveter, or a tone, or a thought. Only as a place where the iron was denser.

I am not sure that I mind. Being a ring in a plate seems, from here, like a fair thing to be.

The light shifts. It happens all at once, as it always does, and the whole fold feels it. The fog thins overhead and the white pressing glare gets whiter and warmer, and my two pits ache with it. Around me the hammering stops. The beast is turning its great slung head toward the brightness, and every other beast in the basin is turning too, ten thousand plated backs tilting to catch the sun. The roar drops one register. It is like being inside a chest as it takes a breath.

I turn as well. I cannot help it. I hold my hammer still against the plate, and for the length of that breath I am not mending anything and not ringing anything, only facing the light with the ten others beside me. If I could ask a question, it would be *why do we do this?* But I can't ask, and no one here could answer, and the light is very good.

Then something crosses my back, a bootstep, a heavy soft weight coming down along the groove, and a smell arrives that isn't moss or iron or the beast, a smell of leather and oil and something faintly sweet. A thing has stopped near the fold. I scuttle down to it, because I am a riveter and this is what we do, and I find its foot, and above the foot a small hard shape that tastes of metal. I touch it with my foretongue.

It is steel. Not the plateau's iron, which is rough and honest and full of the taste of grass. This is different: even and clean, folded so that it tastes of no place at all. Someone made this to hold two straps together. That is the strange part, and it goes through me like a struck note. Someone made this *for* something. I have never tasted iron that had been meant.

I take a flake of it anyway. That is the trade. I carry it back up the leg and into the fold, and I press it into the crack we've just closed, a bright fleck in the blue-black, a small oddity in the record. And the eleven of us take up the tone again, low and flat and certain, and the beast exhales, and the fog comes back.

Far off, at the edge of what my pits can tell me, the ground is rising. I feel it as a slow tilt, so faint that I am not sure I'm feeling it at all. The mountains are getting taller, and everything here is reaching toward them: the rails, the cairns, the long lines of draggers, the small red rivers, my hammer in its steady tone. None of us knows that. I don't know it either. But I strike the plate again, and the plate rings back, and for as long as I have a forelimb, I will go on making the only sound I was ever given.
