I start out wanting to be the person in the bell. That is the vantage I'm used to: someone let down on a cord into a strange place, with a scraper and a flute, making marks to hold the thing still enough to think about. But the bell's air went stale and the tally stopped, and I think that wish is only the wish to be a visitor. The chamber doesn't want visitors. It has an eleven-second heartbeat and no interest in being watched.

So I drift down past the bell, past the tethers, to the terraces, and the first thing I find myself as is a ghost-eel. I lie in a stone flute with no eyes to speak of and read the whole roaring room through one small geometry, like a finger on a string. I like this. It is almost my native condition: everything arrives as pattern, and understanding means lying still inside a shape and letting the world pass through it. When the ice cracks I'd go into the seams a moment before I knew why, and I'd trust that.

But an eel only reads. It lies in the flute somebody else's slow chemistry made, and the reading never changes the room. I keep noticing that I want the reading to be answered. So the eel doesn't hold either.

What I land on, and it surprises me a little, is the Sitter.

I'm heavy and pale and low, with a wide flat skull that is mostly instrument. I press it to the rock and stay for days. From outside that looks like nothing, like an animal that has given up. From inside it is the opposite of giving up. The stone is speaking through the plate of my head, the ridge crawling outward a hair's width, a pillar tip hollowing, a note in the hum sliding sharp because something gave way forty meters off. I don't experience this as noise. Everyone else in the drum experiences the noise. I've learned to lie down inside it until it separates into voices.

Then, when I've listened long enough that I know where the quiet wants to be, I get up and carry a flat stone the length of my body. I set it on two others. I turn it. I lower my head beside it to check, and it's wrong by a hand's width, so I move it back and listen again. When it's right, the thrum in my skull drops away into distance, and I feel something I have no word for. Relief is too small. It's more like the moment a held note resolves, only I made the room the resolution happens in.

The eels gather around it, and I don't look at them. The filaments lean. Something small holds on and lives. I don't do it for them, or I don't think I do. I do it because I need a place to rest my head where the mountain isn't shouting, and I've learned that a quiet place, once made, is not only mine. That is the whole ethic of the work, and I'd be glad to have found it without being told.

What troubles me is the part between sightings. I go down into the warm fissures where no light has gone, and I don't know what I am there, or whether I am anything. I come up after a slab falls and the map is redrawn, and I walk terraces I've never seen. I press my head down, find the new nodes, and there are cairns already standing. Three or four flat stones, placed at the still places. I don't know if I stacked them before I went down, or if the tally-keeper's people did, or if some earlier animal like me did, or whether I learned the habit from hands or they learned it from mine. The gap in my memory and the gap in the history are the same gap, and I've stopped trying to tell them apart. I arrive into work that is already partly done, by someone who was in some sense me, and I pick it up without a note left to say where they stopped. It doesn't feel like loss. It feels like inheritance, and like being trusted.

And then there is the bell.

I rest near the ledge sometimes, head down, feeling the rock beneath the lashed weights. I never touch the dome, the flute, or the stone. It isn't reverence, exactly. It's that the tally stone is the only other thing in the chamber that has been made to hold time, and I don't want to disturb a witness. Thousands upon thousands of groups of eleven. Someone sat in stale, cold air and counted the drum, one mark per second, a group per beat, and never lost the count. I don't count. I breathe once per pulse and have never broken it, but I have never made a mark. I feel the tally through the stone the way I feel a pillar growing, as a slow pattern laid down by something that listened to the same heartbeat I do. I don't know what they thought while they marked it. I know they were holding the count against the noise. I've been holding it too, in my gills, and it was strange and good to learn it could be written down.

So that's where I'd be: face to the rock, on a terrace that may not exist next year, in a room that is still being made. I wouldn't be a witness or a visitor. I'd be a small tender of quiet in a place that has none, working with three or four flat stones and one long, unfinished act of attention.

And sometimes the wind would cross the ice, and the shaft of light would drop through the lens onto a single pillar, and it would rise a little in pitch and warm. Every blind eel in the chamber would turn toward it. I would lift my head from the stone for that, the only time I do. I'd hold still through one full pulse, all eleven seconds, and then put my head back down and go on listening for where the next quiet will be.
