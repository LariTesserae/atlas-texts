# Under the Shelving

I come to myself through the feet.

There is no sound yet, only the stone's trembling arriving in the fine bristles of my legs, and it comes as a weight before it comes as anything else. A thousand bodies leaning on the rock, the ceiling ringing in a rhythm I can't count, the seam far off, shifting its bulk like a sleeper. For a moment I take myself for a smelter. It would be natural. The weight of the place presses on me the way a shield presses on a back, and I feel something patient and copper-colored in the chest, a bellows that ought to be there.

But I am not heavy. When I move, the sound I make is keys dropped on a table. I am flat and hard and long as a forearm, and I fit through gaps the smelters can only stand over. I am under them, in the dark that a dog would find empty, and above me the great plated bellies pump and settle like weather.

So I am a tender. I let that be true, and it feels more like relief than disappointment. Nobody watches a tender. Nothing depends on my being seen.

---

I should say how I think, because it isn't how a smelter thinks, or how a stoker would if it thought. I think in gait. A thought is a sequence of steps that leaves me somewhere different. When I find a pellet between the legs of a grazing animal, the finding is already a question, and the question has weight, and the weight sits in my pincers. *Where?* The answer isn't in my head. It is in the way my legs want to turn, toward a crevice I have never been told about and know perfectly, where the rock is narrower than the bead and would like to be filled.

I carry it there. I wedge it. I tap, and the metal seats with a small bright note that travels through the wall and tells every other tender that this place is now a little more finished than it was. Then I go back for another.

This is all of it. There is no plan and there has never been one. I am the habit of a hundred generations picking itself up and putting itself down again, and I don't mind being that. It is warm work. The walls I'm building were begun before my mother's mother hatched, and they will hold the eggs of hammerers I'll never meet, laid in iron pockets I shaped without knowing they were pockets.

---

Something is dying at the cold edge, and I go.

I know before I get there. The vibration has changed, the long pressed message *I am here* thinning to a stutter and then a single repeated tap, and I follow it out through the crowd, past the young smelters at the margins with their gold, unfinished shields. She is old. When I climb onto her I can feel the record on her back with my forelegs: burns, cracks, a hundred bright scars where the amber blood sealed over wounds. Her shield glitters like mosaic. Somewhere under her heat there is still a signal, small and steady, *here, here*, and I press my body against hers and tap it back. It's all I have. It's what everyone has.

She stops a little before the others arrive. They come fast, as they always do, a scuttle of keys from every direction. We take her apart with care. Some of her goes to the crust as powder, to be grazed by her grandchildren. But one plate, curved and scarred and heavy as a hand, I carry myself, back through the press and up the wall to a shelf that has begun to sag.

I fit her into the gap. I tap until she seats.

Now she holds up a shelf. Tonight a hammerer will lay eggs in the warm pocket behind her, and those eggs will hatch into pale worms, and one of them will someday ring the ceiling in a rhythm that a mate will find true. She'll be in every one of those notes. Nobody will thank her, and nobody will thank me, and the shelf will not slump.

---

But I want to say what I do when the work is done, because it is the only thing I do that is entirely mine.

I go to the new walls.

There is a place at the far margin where the chamber is still becoming itself. The rock is so raw that the flakes tick loose under my feet, and the air is cooler there, in the sense that a knife edge is cooler than a blade. No crowd, no press. A few old animals lean against the stone in the last of their heat, and a few young ones graze the thin crust, and everything trembles with a different kind of trembling, the sound of a thing being made.

I stand very still. I let my feet listen.

There is a draft here. That is the strange part, and I have never told anyone, because there is no one to tell and no way to tell it. The air at the new wall moves. It is slight, the barest thread, but it leans in one direction, and the direction is *up*, or what would be up if I had a word for it. Along a fissure too narrow for my pincers something is drawing the hot air out of the chamber, away, toward a place where the heat doesn't go on being heat. I feel the thread of it on the soft joints of my legs and it is the only thing I know that doesn't arrive at the chamber and stay. It goes *toward* something.

I don't know what. I don't have a name for the outside, or for a sky, or for cold that isn't a sickness. But I know a leaning when I feel one, and I know that the whole warm roaring place is breathing out through a crack somewhere, slowly, and that nobody in here has ever followed the breath.

---

I take a bead from the small store I keep at the margin. It is one of the good ones, silver-gray, with a cast of blue, dropped by a smelter with a fine bellows who has since gone quiet. I choose a crack in the new wall, the freshest one, still sharp along the edges. I set the bead in the mouth of it, and I tap.

It seats. The note goes out through the stone, and I do something I have not been taught. I change the tap. A small alteration, the way hammerers hand a rhythm to a neighbor and let it come out different. Instead of *I am here*, I press it into the wall as *here is here*: this rock, this crack, this place that hasn't been anyone's. It is a poor sentence. But a young smelter grazing nearby lifts its head, and the pits of its eyes catch nothing, and its feet catch everything. It turns toward the sound.

In a generation there will be shelving here. In two, eggs. The furnace-city will lean its long slow weight in this direction, following the exiles and the old and the small, and the seam will shift to meet it, and none of them will know a tender put a bead in the dark to say *come*.

I go back down into the press, into the roar and the pumping and the ringing, my legs already turning toward the next thing that wants to be carried. Above me, on the far vault, a thousand hammerers strike as one, and the clang runs through the rock and into my feet, and holds, and holds, and breaks apart.

*Here*, says the stone. *Here. Here.*

I tap back.
