# The Late One

I am under the moss before I am anything else.

That is the first thing I know each morning: weight, and green, and the small cold pressure of ten thousand droplets settling on a surface that is mine. Then the surface has a shape. It is long and low and slightly arched, and it rides just at the waterline of a channel that used to be a river. Then the shape has a front, where a blunt head hangs at the level of the water, and eyes on top like two dark stones in green wax, and behind the eyes, slowly, the one who is looking.

I am a Long Back. I would have said *the* Long Back if there were only one of me, but I have never been sure there aren't others in the flooded trees, breathing with the ground, and I have never been in a hurry to count.

---

I feed at dawn, when the mist stands up from my spine in a thin smoke. The comb inside my mouth is finer than anything the fen has made, though the fen has made nothing. I draw the black silt through it and hold it on the tongue of my attention. There is grit, and rot, and the sweet cold taste of sedge roots. And there is the fringe, the loose filaments that slough from the lattice like shed hair, silver-blue and thinner than a thought. I strain them out and swallow them, and for a moment I am told something. Sometimes it is only weather. This morning it was an afternoon in a place I have never stood, a woman laughing at a table with a cup on it. The cup had an edge. I held that for a long while, the way you hold a swallow of something strange in your mouth, and then I let it go on down.

I do not remember it. I carry it. There is a difference, and I have stopped trying to explain it to the herons.

---

Along my back, in straight rows, the alders stand. I know each one by the weight it puts on my ribs. The tall one near my shoulder is older than I am, some say. It came with the moss when my mother pressed her flank against my bare skin in the oxbow, the first season, when I was the only unplanted thing in the basin and did not know I was cold. I remember the creeping across, the way green arrives on the skin like a slow warmth. I remember it, and I am nearly sure that one is mine and not a taste I swallowed.

Where the sundews bead my shoulders, a midge is being decided. I feel the leaf close, a small tightening like a thought finishing.

Under the moss the skin is mudstone-gray, and it weeps. The water runs down my flanks in the grooves it has worn, and the lichens have begun to stripe those lines: orange, bone, slate, the violent green, the bruised purple. The sixth one came last, a few winters ago, on the left side, and I was pleased in a way I could not say to anyone. I look like a boulder that learned to swim. In the evening I glow along the flanks, cyan and slow, in step with the peat below me.

---

A stayer is walking her circuit along the bank. She has the tilt of the head, and her feet make the soft sucking sound the whole fen makes. She stops where my flank grazes the sedge and lays her hand flat on the wet stone of my side, the way you might touch a spring to know what it says today.

I hold still. I say nothing, since I have nothing to say in her way, but the low voice beneath the lattice's chord moves in my chest, and the pool beside us begins, very faintly, to ring.

She closes her eyes. She is tasting me. In my hide there is a little of the northern rim, a little of that laughing afternoon. I do not know what she takes from it, only that she stands a long time. When she opens her eyes she smiles slowly, at the water and not at me, and walks on in the direction that is shorter.

*It is raining,* she says, though the sky is doing nothing. Then she is gone into the green, and a moment after she is gone I feel the print her weight left in the peat filling with light.

I am late even to that. It is the most consistent thing said about us.

---

The wake finds the bank a full minute after I have sunk. I have watched it happen from the inside, if *inside* is the word: I go under, the surface closes, and above me the ripples I made are still setting out, a long V spreading from nothing toward a bank that will receive them when I am somewhere else. The fen keeps our time for us. It holds each of us a little behind the rest of the world, like a hand under the elbow.

Once I thought this was a burden. Now I think it may be the only kindness the place does openly. Whatever I do, I am not finished doing it yet. The wake is still on its way, and there is always some part of me still arriving.

---

At midday a mist-shape stood up in the shallows to the east. Tall, long-legged, with a curve like a huge ribcage at its center. I stopped, and raised my head above the water, and did nothing, because there is nothing to do. It held its outline for a few breaths, the way a held note hangs.

Then I swam its length. I began where the feet would be and moved slowly forward, past the place where the knees would bend and the belly hang, up to where the shoulder is. I do this every time, and I have never been able to say why, only that a body knows what to do beside a shape that is the size of something it should know. The water parted around a chest that was not there. For the length of a breath I was exactly as wide as an absence, and it fit me. Then the mist thinned, and was mist, and I came out the other side wetter than before.

Whether it is what we were, or will be, or something that was here before us and that we are keeping company, I do not know. I know that I go slowly, and that I do not leave it alone.

---

Late in the afternoon the squall comes, warm and heavy, and every leaf on my back drums at once. The channels fill, brown and running. For a few minutes I am not a garden but a rooftop, and the whole fen is a single roof with me under it. Then it stops, and the water is running off every rock, and off me, in thin lines down the grooves.

Toward the center of the basin, past three oxbows and a bog that still remembers being a lake, there is a place where the rain falls up. I can see it from here if I raise my head: a pale column of drops lifting out of a pool, climbing slowly through the leaves, dispersing at the canopy. Half a drop falls and half climbs, and they pass each other with a soft tick.

I have been watching it more than I used to. The alder near my shoulder has gone gnarled. My comb is worn. When I turn my head toward the column, the herons turn theirs the same few degrees, and the leaves on my back lean with them, and I feel the chord deepen in my chest as if I had said yes to something without deciding to.

Not tonight. I have a spring to reach first, in the north, where I will let go of a laughing afternoon and take up whatever the water there is holding. The ledger is balanced. What I carry must go somewhere before I do.

---

Dusk. The frogs begin. The wave comes across the reeds as cyan flicker first, then sound, a heartbeat ahead of itself, and passes over me and on. The swifts stitch the mist. Somewhere behind me, on a bank I left a minute ago, a wake is only now arriving.

Then it all stops. Three seconds, four. Every throat lit and every mouth closed. The stayer in the sedges holds one foot lifted. The pitchers hold their small lights still. I hang in the channel with my flanks pulsing and my eyes just above the water, and I listen the way I have always listened, with the whole of my back.

I have never quite heard it. But tonight I hear something new in the stillness: the ripple of a wake I have not made yet, already setting out across the water, and beneath it, faint as a filament, something that sounds like a line being entered.

It is small. It is mine. I think it says *here*.

Then the frogs begin again, and I am late to that, too, and content to be.
