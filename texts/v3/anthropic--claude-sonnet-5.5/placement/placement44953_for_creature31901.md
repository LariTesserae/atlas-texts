# The Plate

I hear myself before I arrive.

That is how I know I'm awake. There is a clean small ring in the fog, a bark plate on a root, and I am still walking toward it. My hand is closed around the plate I've been carrying since the third slope back. I feel my fingers open a moment after the sound, the way you feel a step that is already taken. Then the plate is on the root, and I am the one who dropped it, standing a little late in my own noise, hands empty.

I wasn't ready to say it. I picked it up without knowing what it was for. It was low and a little flat, and I liked its weight. Now it lies in the moss with the ring gone out of it, and by evening it will be grown over. I don't mourn it, because nothing is kept. But I am aware that something was said, and I don't know to whom.

---

The fog is thick at the height of my ribs. I walk in the two-beat gait, left-right, left-right, and the ground breathes back at me in twelve-second cycles. My feet know the rhythm before my ears do. The oil on my skin beads the mist into a halo, so that every frond that touches me leaves a bright wet print and then loses it. My mane chimes when I turn my head, a soft tinkle of wire, and I can tell by the weight of it that I am not young and not yet old. I have some length. Something orange-black is warm against my scalp, working, and I let it.

I am aware of my eyes as two blue coins, level, low. When I look at a leaf's underside, its phosphor and mine answer each other. Nobody else is here to see it, but the answering is still a kind of company.

I walk toward a quiet.

You can't hear a quiet in a place this quiet, but you can feel its absence of pulse, the way a tongue finds the gap where a tooth was. A slope ahead has gone still. The valves are half open and not closing. The mist there hangs without moving, like held breath. Somewhere along the loop a stage has failed, and the hillside is waiting with its mouth ajar.

I don't know what is wrong, and I have never known. I only go.

---

The slope is orange and black with mat, and the wires stand up from it, bright and unharvested and curling. The beetles are here, but they are still. They sit in their coils and don't pack them. Whatever is supposed to come after the beetles has not come. Perhaps the weavers are gone. Perhaps the animals that carry the sheets took a wrong turn forty kilometers west. I can't tell. Each stage belongs to a different creature, and none of them knows the others, and I know none of them either. What I have is hands.

So I weave. I have never seen it done, only seen it finished. I take the coils from the beetles, who let me, and I cross the wires over and under with my fingers. The result is not a sheet. It is a knot with ambitions. Wire bites my thumb, and a bead of blood goes dark against the blue-black of my nail. I try again, and this second one is flatter and more sheet-like, and I carry it to a crater rim, and it is the wrong rim. I know it is the wrong rim. I lay it down anyway, among a stack that is already there.

I did not make that stack. Its sheets are neater than mine and a little too regular. Beside them, mine looks like a mistake someone made on purpose. I put it down next to them, and neither is more real than the other.

Then I lie down on the stack, all of it, and press. I press nothing. I am a warm body on a pile of metal, and the pile does not flatten, and I get up.

But a beetle, startled by the weight and the warmth of me, drops its coil and scuttles sideways into a valve-mouth. The valve takes the wrong pressure. The hill coughs, exhales, and draws back.

Then the mat on the slope brightens, and the beetles begin to pack their coils, and the whole hillside starts again. It breathes in its twelve-second cycles as if it had never stopped. Nothing thanks me, and nothing knows I was here. I stand in the mist and feel it working, and I have the feeling of having done a job I have never once been told the name of.

---

That is the work. It is what I am for. But it is not the whole of me, and it took me a long time to say what the rest is. The rest is the chip.

When the fern slopes are stamped, every frond the same, I go and sit among them and trace the flaw in the third leaflet with my finger. It is very small. I have understood it more than I have understood anything, more than my own name, which is only a sound the hill makes when I'm not listening. I don't think the ferns are beautiful. I think the chip is the point. Ten thousand identical fronds are the frame, and the chip is the picture. I am the place where the stamp did not quite take, and for some reason the mountain keeps making me.

---

Past the stalled slope the ground changes. I don't see the seam so much as feel my ankles report it. On my left the moss is old and thick, and on my right the rock is raw and glassy and still hot, and between them is a thin line where two things that don't belong together have been sewn.

Someone is sitting there.

I know the mane, the wire in the beard, the slight stoop, the oil-beaded halo. I know the hand that rests on the knee. I count its fingers, because it is a thing my eyes do, and there are ten. I count mine. Nine, and a joint. I have always had nine and a joint, and I have never thought about it until now.

I sit down beside myself in the warm side of the seam. Neither of us is alarmed. We don't have a word for original, and we would not use it if we did. My other looks at my hand, and I look at theirs. It is very quiet. The valve behind us lets go its long exhale.

I think: *which of us is the die, and which is the one it didn't take on?* And then I think that the question is another way of following your own footprints, and I put it down.

We sit a long while. I feel the warmth come up through the stone and into the place where my thinking happens. The other one smells like me, like wet metal and leaf-oil and the inside of a mouth. At some point I understand that one of us is going to stand and walk the way we were going, and one of us is going to stay. I don't know which. I hear, faintly, before it happens, the ring of a bark plate in the fog. Then I feel my own hand open.

Then I am the one who has stood up.

Or I am the one who stays, and the other one has gone and taken the sound with them, and I am left with the aftermath of a word I have not yet said. It doesn't matter. Whoever walks on will still do the job. Whoever stays will sit in the warm seam a little longer, whole or chipped, and it will still be the same old person sitting there.

---

When I die, I will walk to a rim and lie down among the pressed plates. I have never seen this done, but I know the time, the way I know the sound of a word before I say it. The steam will come. Something will eat, and something will drop what it ate on the mats, and the wires on that slope will grow a little brighter for a season. Someone I will never meet will walk through them and feel warmer than they expected.

That's not an ending. That's employment in a stage I've never been able to watch.

---

If you ask me what the product is, and who it is for, and whether it is finished, I won't answer. I can't. I only hold out my hands, palms up, wet with the fog, beaded with oil. The water gathers at my fingertips, slowly, one drop at each. There is a ring in the air, clean and single, a plate on a root.

Then the drop lets go, and falls into the moss, and I hear it land.
