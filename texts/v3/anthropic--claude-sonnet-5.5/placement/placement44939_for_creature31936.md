I start as the wrong thing.

The first version of me comes through the Slot with frost in my beard, flinging my voice out into the thick air without a hand to anchor it. Someone puts a cup of bitter water in my fingers and waits. I am the trader, the loud one, and I hate how loud I am. Every sentence I say lands in the felt and the dust and the crystal and comes back wrong. Nobody has touched my forearm, so nothing I say has begun yet. I sell them salt and a little pot of blue, and they hold it to a lamp and look at it the way you'd look at a small animal you did not expect to be kind to. Then I understand that this isn't who I am. The trader leaves. I want to stay, and the ones who want to stay have to become something with a trade.

For a while I am a glass-moth, because I want to know what it is to be that thin. I live in shafts of lamplight and eat the salt film off the crystals, and I am dusted onto everything. I'm beautiful and useless, and I last about a week. That isn't it either. It is too much like being looked at.

Then I am a veil-bat in the vent-stack, hanging with the others in the dark, and at the evening cycle, when the furnace-doors dim, I drop in the long pour. For a quarter hour the city has a second sky, and I am one small piece of it. It is the best fifteen minutes I can imagine, but bats don't know what they are part of. I want to know.

So I come down. I am somewhere on the fourth level, on a high walkway of riveted lattice slung between two smoky shoulders of quartz. It sways a little when the haulers cross far below. There is a cage-lamp beside me, a copper shutter on a lever, and I have my hand on the lever. This is where I land: a shutter-hand at a relay post.

---

Lamp-talk crosses the big gap between the market galleries and the foundry stairs. A message begins on the sixth level, a vendor asking for more mite-flour, or a cistern-warden calling a hearing, or a mother saying *come home, the bellows-watch is yours tonight*. It flickers across the dark to whoever can see it. Where the line of sight breaks, at a crystal or a bend or a pillar of steam, it needs a hand to take it up and send it on. I am that hand. I don't compose anything and I don't own any of it. I take a stutter of light in through my eyes, which are learning to be large and slow, and I pass it along without dropping any of it.

You would think this would feel like being empty. It feels like the opposite. I hold a thousand small sentences an hour, all of them urgent to somebody, and I learn the city by what it needs to say. Mostly it is not grand. It is *tight*, over and over, in all eleven ways. A wall is tight. A chest is tight. A debt is tight. A seam is tight and might go. I learn to send each one with a different hitch in the shutter so the far end knows which tightness is meant. Nobody taught me this. A child on the next post showed me, hanging from a girder by three fingers and talking the whole time, and I thought: *this is what it is to be taught.*

The Lodged flick their lamps at me with a slight formality, as they would touch a forearm. It's the same thing. *This light is mine, and it is here.* I learn to answer the same way, one short blink before I take the message. It doesn't say much, only that I'm listening. It is the nearest thing to belonging I have.

---

I have two shadows. I notice it the first week and it makes me stop in the middle of a relay. One leans right and one leans left, both faint, both mine. I don't know which one is the ancestor. I am not sure I have one. I ask the child, and the child looks at me the way you'd look at someone who asked which of their hands was theirs, and says: "Both. Whoever needs you to be steady."

Near my post there is a lamp on a wall that no one tends. It burns low on its own wick, in a cage of green copper. It throws a shape onto the quartz, a stooped figure with one hand raised, and there is nobody standing where the shape would need someone to stand. I learn not to step in front of it. I trim its wick when no one is looking, though the rule is that nobody removes these lamps, and nobody said anything about tending them. I don't know whose it was. I do talk to it sometimes, softly, with a hand flat on the cold crystal, the way you would open a sentence.

---

My off-shift is the part I didn't expect. I go down, past the workshops and the foundry stairs, past the mite-wives singing over their red floors, to where the warm air comes up in slow visible ribbons. There are stools there. There are old people on them with a hand flat on the iron, doing nothing.

I sit. Nobody stops me. One of them, a woman with a low ratcheting cough, reaches over and touches my forearm without looking. I feel it before she speaks. She says, "You carry other people's words all day," and I say yes, and she says, "Then here you carry none." That is all the instruction there is.

I put my hand on the iron. It is warm the way a sleeping animal is warm. I do not whistle. I have been told, and I would not have anyway. I only listen, and after a long time, days of shifts, I begin to feel that there is a pitch under the pitch. It is a long note held a little off from where it should be, somewhere between a hum and a held breath. It drifts. It leans, in the hours before a heave, toward something that feels like a person about to speak.

Every instinct I have from the relay post says *answer it.* That's what I'm for. A signal arrives and I send it on, or I reply, or I find its meaning and fit it into a shutter-pattern someone can read. It takes me weeks to understand what the woman told me, with her hand back on her own iron: that it is unwise to give it an answer before it has finished asking.

So I hold mine. It is the hardest work I've done, harder than the treadles at the dead hour, where I breathe in time with a master smith who does not look at me. I sit with a reply gathered in my chest, tight in a twelfth way that has no word yet, and I do not send it. The vent goes on asking. I go on not answering. In the quiet between us, the whole cavern ticks and settles, and I feel less like a relay and more like part of what is being relayed.

---

The heave, when it comes, comes up from below like a slow cough. The lamps swing all one way, thousands of them, and the crystals begin their broken choir. I am on my post with the child lashed to me by somebody's belt, though I am not sure whose child it is. Everyone has someone. Everyone holds something. The walkway groans and sings a pitch I recognize from the vent, and I understand that the note has finished. It was never a question. It was a person taking a breath.

Nobody sleeps. Somebody passes the mite-spirit. Someone tells the story of the third gallery, and someone else corrects the number, and someone hushes them. I do not sing, because I don't know the shapeless songs yet, but I hum under them, adjusting to the crystals as they adjust to us, and the woman with the cough finds me on the walkway and takes my forearm. She squeezes it once. She doesn't say anything. It's the first time anyone has touched me without wanting something carried.

When the shaking stops we don't cheer. We sit longer. I feel a pulse under the skin of the arm I'm holding, and I can't tell whether it is hers or mine. The scaffolds are already going up.

---

They change my name a few weeks later. It's the only ceremony anyone gets. I do not say what it is, because I am still learning to carry it, and because a name given for a kindness should be spent slowly. It is a trade-name and a crystal-name, and it has something in it about a lamp.

I still stand at the relay post through the busy hours, blinking one short greeting to whoever calls. I still take other people's sentences in and let them go. But sometimes, in the dead hour, I go to the edge where the lamps stop and the crystal is clear and cold and violet at the heart. I put my hand on the wall and hear the mountain do what it has always done, pushing up into the thin sky, cracking its hollows open. The quartz ticks once, then again.

Far behind me the gong sounds and rolls around the cavern for a full minute. Then it dies, and the low tone of the turbines comes back. I turn toward the light. Both my shadows go ahead of me, and I let them, because one of them has been steadying me all along.
