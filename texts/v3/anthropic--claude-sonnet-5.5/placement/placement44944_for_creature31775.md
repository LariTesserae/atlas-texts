I would be at a saddle.

Not in a knuckle, where a thousand roofs hold each other up and news is cheap. I would be in one of the long thin places, where nothing continues but a wall and a channel, and one house sits against the wall like a barnacle on a pier. It has one door with a felt curtain, one peat fire, and one reach: a hundred and forty paces of gutter, a black cistern with a split lintel, and a conduit that runs under the paving carrying more than water.

I would be the person who lives there. I am not old yet, but my knees know the stairs. I planted my foot flat on every dished tread before I understood why, and now I couldn't walk any other way. My mantle is cream going to gray at the shoulders, the fringe cut fresh each autumn so the rain leaves me in a hundred drops. On the afternoons it pours I stand at the door and watch myself become a small cairn with a halo.

---

The work of a saddle is to be a place where a message doesn't die.

Tapping travels far through the drains, but not forever. Knuckle to knuckle across the long thin places, someone has to be there to hear it arrive and hand it on. A knuckle-dweller's *water rising* comes down the conduit as a faint knock-knock-pause, and by the time it reaches me it's almost only a rumor in the stone. I put my palm flat on the cold lid of the channel, wait, and feel it more than hear it. Then I answer with my knuckle, in the same rhythm, a little louder, to the west, to the next thin place, where someone I have never met and never will is also waiting with a hand on the stone.

I don't compose these messages. I want to be clear about that, at least with myself. I am not the one who has anything to say. I am the place where what is said gets stronger before it goes on. Most nights it's *all well*, three soft taps, and I pass it along the way you pass a bowl of water hand to hand without spilling. Sometimes it is *come*, and I don't know who is calling or whom, and I send it on anyway, and lie awake a while listening for whether anyone answers.

That is the kind of thing I'd be good at, I think. Not being the voice. Being the listening that keeps a voice from thinning out.

---

Then the cistern, because it's mine to keep. Square, black, ringed with steps. It holds the sky upside down. There are four frogs in it this year. I know because I count them each dawn when the ice on the surface has just broken, and they are sitting in a row along the second step like people waiting for a door. When my shadow crosses them they go, and the water closes over the place where they were. If I ever came down some morning and found none, I would drain it and scour it with my own hands and not speak of it to anyone. But there are four, and so I am allowed to go on.

Past the cistern, at the edge of the reach, is a house I don't keep, because nobody keeps it. The lower rooms are full of water to the ceiling. The stair goes down into it, and I have stood on the top step and counted the treads I can see. Eleven on a gray day. Fourteen at noon when the light falls down the well of the stairwell and the grain-sized creatures turn in it and glitter. Fifteen once, in the hour after a storm, when the water was so clear it seemed it had been taken out of the world.

I have never gone down. No one has needed to. But I know the number changes, and I have never told anyone that I go to count it. It isn't sadness that brings me and it isn't fear. It's closer to how one visits an old neighbor who says little and is always home. I go and stand. I say *still here*. Whatever is below the fifteenth stair does not answer. I think that is its way of saying the same.

---

The wall is failing in one place. Low on the west face, near the corner where the mineral curtain has grown the color of old teeth, a plate of slate has begun to weep differently: a bead in the wrong place, a darkening at one edge. I have known for two seasons. I have laid my palm against it in the morning, and my two fingers on the curtain above, and I have not touched it with the lever. *Repair the oldest stones last, and only when they truly fail.* It hasn't failed. It is only telling me it will. I have learned that most of what I do is listen to things tell me what they will do, and then wait, and be there when they do it.

My lever is named Grandmother's Patience. I did not name it. It came down the reach with the house, and the wood is dark and slow and smells of something from below the shelf that I'll never see. When the plate fails, and it will, I will speak to it first, briefly, the way you speak to a beast about to be moved. Then I'll set it back with the same lever, in the same place, on top of the same course that a hand not mine once fitted. In the joint where I put my thumb I will find another thumbprint, worn smooth. I'm not sure that isn't the only kind of immortality worth having.

---

And the hour. It comes as it always does, at the tail of the afternoon rain, when the gutters are running like ropes and the cistern is dimpling and the whole saddle is nothing but gray and the sound of falling. I open the curtain and push back my hood. The wet is on my face immediately, cold as a hand. I look west, where the cloud has begun to tear.

Under my feet the conduit knocks. Three soft taps, far off, from the east. *All well.*

I don't move at once. I let the light come first. It arrives on the far knuckle, a single roof lit gold, then another, then a long shining stretch of wall, patch by patch, like lamps lit across a dark room by someone unhurried. My face is lifted. I do not speak. Somewhere beyond the light there is a person with her palm flat on a stone, waiting for what I have to give her.

Then I kneel, slowly, in the wet street. I put my knuckle to the channel lid and give it on, three soft taps, a little stronger than they came.

*All well.*

And I go back in to my fire.
