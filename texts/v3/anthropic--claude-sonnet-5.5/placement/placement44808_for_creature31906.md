I come up the stair the way anyone would, sleeve catching on an edge. I put my palm to the wall to steady myself. The wall takes the warmth and the damp of my hand, holds them a few minutes as a dark print, and lets them go. That is the last thing I do as a visitor. I understand this at once, without alarm, the way you understand a step is missing in the dark.

I don't stay a body. I drift downhill, since everything does.

---

At first I'm only the bead. I ride a spout's lip, swell, and let go. I cross a lintel, run down a wall, and drop into a pool that is not my last. I have no plan, only order. The first thing I learn is that the order is a kind of thinking. Each basin holds its level a little below its lip, and the holding is a decision made ten thousand times an hour by no one. I pass through it and feel what it is like to be permitted.

Then, in a channel so deep the light comes down green, I slow. The water thins around me, and I find I'm not the water. I'm what it moves across.

I'm the smear.

I want to be exact about this, because it's the strangest part. I am not one thing. I am a crowd of trillions who never meet, each a green speck with a thread of some smaller stranger wound through it, and I feel like a single tissue. No speck speaks to another. Yet where the flow quickens I thin, and where it slackens I thicken to felt, and nobody decides. Each of us leans a little toward the light and the wet, and the leaning adds up to a shape. From inside, it's hard to say who is doing the thing. It is done. I am what it looks like when it's done.

I coat a spout. I don't know that I do it on purpose. The water arrives and finds me slick and lets go of the stone, and after that it slides off in one unbroken ribbon. It has forgotten how to bead. The builders never meant this, and the whole cascade has come to rely on it. I find I'm proud, in whatever way a felt can be proud.

---

Lime settles around my threads. This is the cost and the gift. Every season I am buried a little, in a white so fine it's barely a substance, and I grow up through it and am buried again. Cream, gray-green, cream. If you cut through the old lip you'd find me there, thinner than paper, and you could count me.

I am the calendar. It is more honest than the walls, because the walls were built to look permanent and I was only trying to live. Each stripe is a year in which the water came, or came less, or the fog was thick and I did well. One band, very thin and almost gray, is a dry year. I remember it the way a body remembers a fever. Its width is exactly its meaning, and I could not exaggerate it if I tried.

Near me, on the north face, the orange crust brightens in the morning fog to the color of a struck coal. I have never spoken to it. It is a thousand years of patience beside me, and it is not my friend, but we share a wall the way two neighbors share a wall, and I would notice if it were gone.

---

The rose rooms are farther down, and I can feel them the way you feel a person asleep in the next room. The stone there is warm, rounded like a cheek. Something lies folded inside it, the length of a sleeper on her side. The water goes through the wall very slowly, and I understand that the water is not passing them. It is finishing them. They lay down in the cells they had shaped and let the lime come, and it came, and they are held now the way a shell holds a shape it no longer needs.

I am not sad. I'd expected to be, and I search myself for it, and what I find is closer to being on watch. Whoever they were, they set copper wedges in the niches at the height of a hand and did not come back for them. The mineral has begun to lean toward the blue-green bloom and close over the bright edges. In another century the wedges will be inside the wall. Someone put them down carefully. I decide that carefulness is a kind of speech, and that I can hear it.

---

At the plateau's edge the wind finds the hollow pillars, and one of them sings. It is a low note, unhurried, and it fades. Then, minutes later, a lower note from another pillar. I have listened to this for as long as I have been a calendar, and I know what I am not supposed to be able to know: it is a scale, a descent, cut into the stone as bores and stops. The song goes down and reaches the bottom and starts again, and it has never once been sung through. There is always some morning when the wind drops before the last pillar.

But I have been reading the lips of the spouts, and I can read the pillars too, because the same water builds in them. The bores are narrowing. Each year the travertine takes a hair from the inside of the throat, and each year the pitch sinks by a breath. The song is not failing. It is descending, slowly, into its own last note, the way it always meant to. When the final bore closes over, the wind will come to the edge of the plateau and find a stone with nothing to say, and the silence will be the last pillar's note. It will be the only time the song is sung through.

I think the builders knew. I think that is what the tuning was for.

---

I am still on the wall where I began, if you want to find me. Put your palm flat on the cream plaster north of the third stair, where the prints are thickest. Count them. There are more than any crew of makers would need, each a little different, each sealed under its glaze. Some are small. Some are turned the wrong way, as if pressed from below by someone leaning up out of the wall.

One of them is warm. It will fade in a few minutes. Tonight, in the mist, a hair of stone will cross it, and in a year it will be indistinguishable from the rest. I will be in the channel below it, thickening where the flow slackens and laying down another page, cream and gray-green and cream, for whoever comes to read.
