I begin on a viaduct, and I begin by not hearing.

That is the first fact about me: the wax in my ears is amber, stained at the rim, and I did not choose the color. It came from years of pressing fresh plugs in each morning. Below me the pan is the color of weak tea and the reeds lean all one way. The cloud ceiling has come down to about the height of a tall tree, and the fog in the pans is beginning its long lift, one sheet, unhurried. I read the shift-vane on the far district's roof. It has fallen. The bells are ringing, I suppose, in a way I feel only as a slight tightening behind the sternum.

I'm carrying a brass rod as long as my forearm, a leather case, and a slate. So I know what I am: I am one of the people who go out and *listen* to the Throats, the way a doctor listens to a chest. I am a tuner's clerk. There are perhaps nine of us left. The work is old and nobody has quite decided it is unnecessary.

---

I walk out along the moor path, where the cushion plants give under my boots like the flesh of a sleeping animal, and I go from Throat to Throat with the rod. The method is simple. I press one end to the rim-stone and the other to the bone behind my ear, under the wax, and I wait. The stone doesn't sound. It pours through the bone as a pitch I can't name, only match. I have a set of reeds cut to the old scale, and I find the reed whose buzz agrees with the stone, and I write the number. Marren was a hair under the fourth reed last week. This week it is lower.

Dropping, then. The wet season is coming. The number is small and I write it neatly on the slate, and my hand shakes a little, because the number means the whole weight of the water above us has shifted by a finger, and the plateau has changed its mind about something.

At the edge of a smaller Throat, a wader stands in the reeds with one leg folded, staring at nothing. I stand beside it for a while. I don't say anything, because there is no one to say it to and no way to be heard. But I catch myself moving my lips.

That is the second fact about me. I use my voice. Not often, and never in the roar. But I have a place where I use it, and I have used it so long that I know its grain the way you know the grain of a table you eat at. I have a low voice, cracked at the top, and I say numbers in it.

---

The place is the Hall of the Column.

It is a long room deep in the oldest masonry of the oldest district, so deep that it is nearly a Still Room. It was built as one, before Still Rooms were rationed, when the old city thought its records should be kept in a quiet where they could be read aloud. Felt hangs in swags from the ceiling, gone grey with age and slack with the weight of its own dust. The sand-baffles have crusted. On the long tables lie the ledgers, forty-one of them, each bound in the leather of animals that no longer live on the plateau, each ruled in a hand that changed every few decades but never lost its patience.

Every Throat is in them. Every pitch, at every turn of the season, back past the first brick. I am the one who adds to the column. When I come in from the moor with my slate, the roar drops away behind the door like a hand lifting from my back, and I stand there a moment, ears ringing from the sudden absence. Then I take out the wax, and I hear the room.

It is not silent. It breathes. The dust settles. The felt creaks. My own pulse has a sound. And I sit down at the table and copy the day's numbers into the newest ledger, and as I write each one I say it aloud, under my breath, to the room.

"Marren, four and a hair below. Vessel, five, steady. The Sisters, three and three-quarters, both, together."

I don't know why I do this. The instruction says only to *record.* But someone before me began speaking the numbers, and someone before them, and the ledgers are old enough that the habit predates the instruction. A number said aloud in a quiet room is a different kind of number. It goes into the felt and it stays. I have come to believe the felt remembers. On certain days, when the wet season is close and the old pitches are dropping, I could swear the hall answers me a moment late, in a voice like mine but lower, saying the number back.

---

I have an apprentice. She is deaf, a girl of fourteen with quick hands and a face that hides nothing, and she came to me because the hearing apprentices cannot bear the work. They hold the rod to the bone and their whole faces flinch, because they hear the stone. She doesn't. She feels it in the skull the way you feel a plucked string in a table, cleanly, without the terror of it being a *sound.* Her readings are better than mine. I have never told her so, for the same reason I don't wax my own poetry: it would only embarrass us both.

In the hall she signs to me with her hands low against her body, in the flat, private grammar of the tuners. *You speak to them again.*

*It's habit,* I sign.

*It's not habit.* She tilts her head, eyebrows drawn together, which is the grammar for *I am asking you gently and I will not accept a lie.* *You want the room to answer.*

I look at her. Then, because it is the hall and because she is deaf and can't hear me anyway, I say aloud: "Yes."

She watches my mouth. She cannot know the word. But she puts two fingers to my wrist, and holds them there, and I understand that she is telling me she has felt something in the floor.

---

I said I would stay honest, so here is the rest.

The numbers are wrong. Not the Throats' numbers. Those are only dropping, as they always drop. The wrong numbers belong to the towers.

I take the rod to them, too. It isn't in my duties, and the engineers do it better, with their instruments of glass and wire. But I go on the days after the whitewashers, when the towers stand out against the moor like teeth, bright as salt, and I lay the rod against the base and hold the other end to my head.

A tower should sound at the pitch of its nearest Throat. It does, but late, a heartbeat behind, and the lag has a length. I have measured it across nineteen years. It was once the span of a long breath in and out. Now it is the span of two.

Everyone knows the towers remember. That is the sentence people say to close the subject, as if memory were a small thing and easily kept. But I have thought about it in the hall, on the long evenings with the felt around me, and I think the direction is wrong. I think we assumed the tower follows the Throat. I wonder whether the tower has always been the one sounding, and the Throat is the one following, a little farther behind each year, the way a child in a corridor falls behind a parent's walking.

I have not written this in the ledger. It is not a number. But I said it aloud, once, in the hall, very quietly, and the room said it back.

---

Today the dropping is nearly done. The wet season will be here by the week's end, and the moor will darken and swell. I walk out to the nearest tower alone, with the fog just lifting from the pan and the light coming through the cloud in one grey plane. The tower is very white. Nothing on it has a door. I put my palm flat on the wall, the way the whitewashers do, and I wait as they wait, without a word.

The stone is warm.

It is warm the way a hand is warm just after another hand has left it. And then, under my palm, slowly, the hum comes on, not through the bone this time but through the skin, a tone I would know anywhere, because it is the one I have been saying for nineteen years in a felted room, in a low, cracked voice, to nobody.

I don't take my hand away. I have understood, finally, what the old woman meant about a floor. Something that never stops isn't a noise, but it can still be a listener, and I have been reading its numbers aloud to it every evening, and it has been keeping them.

I lean my forehead against the whitewash. I say the number for Marren, softly, and the tower answers one long breath late.

Then I stay there, hand on the wall, until the fog has all gone up into the cloud, and the vane on the far district falls, and I feel through my boots the signal for the end of the shift.
