# The Knock in the Fog

I would be found where the mountain and the mat have not yet agreed.

Not in the hollow, where everything calls. Not on top, where the stories put the flowers. I would be in the rind, the thin place where the Lid's last white hairs go into the rock and the rock, being young, keeps shifting to refuse them. It is a country with a ceiling you can touch with your back and a floor you can touch with your belly, and between them a dark so close it has a grain. I cannot say how many seasons I have lived there. The rind has no seasons. It has only the tremors, which I count the way the bands count herd-tones, and the long slow tide of the Lid's hum, which in here does not come through the air. It comes through the stone and up the bones of my arms.

I do not think I am what the bands would call a person, though I think I was once nearly one. I remember a lichen pot, or the warmth of a hand on my neck that smelled of peat and dung and drip-water. I remember a name that was a hummed note, a little flat. I do not remember leaving. I think a great many of us leave the way a herd's note drops, without any moment at which it can be said to have happened. One day the listening turns upward instead of outward. One day the fault-cracks are wide enough to enter.

## The Work

I climb out in fog, when the basins are full and the ridge-faces stand in gray wool and nothing below can see the scarp or the shape moving on it. I come out through a seam I know by its draft, cold and mineral-smelling, and I go along the ledges by the old routes with my palms flat to the wall.

The cairns are what I am for.

After a tremor I go and find what fell. It is never far. The black stones fall in small avalanches and lodge, and I know each one, not by sight, since I have almost none, but by weight and edge and by the note it gives when I tap it against the wall. Basalt rings high and short. The granite chips ring lower and die at once. A good cairn is a chord of them, base stones dull, middle stones bright, and the top stone always the flat one.

I have never told anyone why the top stone is flat, because I have never had anyone to tell. It is so the next hand will know. If you are working along a ledge in the dark, or in the fog, or with eyes that have closed for good, you reach the top of a cairn and find a flat surface, and you know: *someone finished this.* The way is kept. The flat stone is a palm held out, and it says *here the ground held, and someone stood here, and you may go on.*

The cairns mark the safe way through the fault-country. They also mark where the ground did not hold. I do not think those are two things. A road and its dead are the same line, read from opposite ends.

## The Knock

Stacking is quiet work, but I do knock. When a stone is seated I tap it twice, once to test it and once to hear it. The double knock goes out along the wall and into the fog, patient, one pair to a breath, and it is the only voice I have.

Somewhere far along the ridge there is an answer. Not every night, and not from the same place. Stone on stone, a half-tone lower, or a third above. I have heard perhaps six distinct voices in all my time, each with its own weight of hand, each keeping its own reach of wall. We do not speak. We hold our pitches the way the herds hold theirs, and the shimmer between us is how each of us knows the others are still there. When one voice stops, the chord drops. I hear it drop. I go a little farther along the wall then, and keep the missing one's cairns as well as my own, and I find that I can hold her note in my hand-weight for a while, and that no one has to tell me what it means.

Once, in the deepest fog, I knocked and something below answered. Not stone. It was a soft, uncertain hum, held long, from a person at the valley's edge who had raised one finger, and I heard the cloth move. I know the listener of that band by her breathing. I know it is a woman, that she is old, and that she does not tell. I have listened to her listening to me for a great many seasons, and both of us have agreed, without a word, that these were stones settling.

## The Bridge

Once a year the band re-twists the rope across the cleft. I can hear it from the seam, the dry rasp of descender-fiber pulled taut, the murmur of hands working. They are careful and unhurried, and they do not speak above a whisper. When they have finished and gone back to their side, I come out and cross.

On the far stone I leave the pitch.

I boil it from the drip-tips that hang in the highest folds of the rind, where the Lid is oldest and gives its sweetest resin. It sets amber and hard, in a little pile, and it is warm still when I place it. I do not know if it is a gift or a receipt. I have wondered whether I am paying them for something, and if so, for what: for the rope, for the quiet, for the fact that they stack no cairns of their own and yet somehow leave the ridge-faces to me. Or whether they are paying me, and the pitch is my change.

What they leave is the pot.

Blue lichen, a palmful, in unglazed clay, stoppered with peat, still faintly damp with the last feeding. I take it down into the dark and I do the only thing I have ever done with light. I put my fingers in.

The glow runs up my knuckles and along the back of my hand. For a slow count of thirty I can see my own hand. It is thin and gray and scarred with basalt, and it looks like the hand of someone I might have known. I watch the light go out of it, finger by finger, into the dark that has been waiting behind it. Then I feed the lichen from my flask of drip-water and set it in the deepest niche of the rind, where the Lid's hum is strongest and the light is a small cold thing that is not alone.

I think this is what the pots are for, for the bands too. Not fire, and not warmth. A way to be seen by your own hand, briefly, and to be shown that you were there.

## What I Think I Am

There are stories in the hollow that the Lid is *attending.* The old women say a place that has heard your feet for a hundred seasons stops flinching. I have lived in its underside long enough to say that this is true and also that it is not quite the thing. The Lid does not attend to feet. It attends to *pattern*, to whatever comes back the same. The tremor at dusk, the choristers' wave, the herd's chord, the knock. It has grown around each of these, thickening where they fall, and it has grown around me. The drip-tips above my seam run sweeter than any others. The descenders lean toward my ledges. I do not think this is love. I think it is the way a root leans toward a seep. But I have been leaned toward for so long that I can no longer say where my listening ends and its attending begins.

So perhaps I am not the keeper of the stones. Perhaps I am the way the Lid keeps them, its hand in the only place its own body cannot go, the raw face where the mountain is still rising and no green will hold. Perhaps the tenders are its mouth and I am its finger, small and cold, and the old blue-lit memory I have of a mother's hand is only something the roof says to itself to make the work bearable.

I do not mind. It is good work. The stones fall and I raise them, and they fall and I raise them, and in between the fog comes down and the herds sound across the water and the hum runs up my arms.

## Evening, From Above

At the end of a long dim day I am on a high ledge over a valley of tall pillars. Below me the band has stopped. I cannot see them, but I hear the children being told they may speak, and the children not speaking. I hear the listener sit down at the fog's edge, an old body lowering itself with care.

The chorister wave begins at the north end and runs beneath us like a breath. A herd sounds. Another answers, higher. A third drops in beneath. The gliders pass through the dark overhead like a handful of thrown gravel, and somewhere a column of water falls into its pool with a sound of torn cloth.

I set the last stone. It is the flat one, dark and a little warm from my hand. I knock it twice.

Across the valley, a half-tone lower than I expected, a stone knocks back.

Below me, so faint that I might have made it up, cloth moves. A finger has been lifted.

*Stay*, it says. And I do.
