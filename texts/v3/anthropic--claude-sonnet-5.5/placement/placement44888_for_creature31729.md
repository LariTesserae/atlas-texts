I start under the boards, because that is where anyone would find me if they knew where to look. I'm hip-deep in the warm black, crouched so my spine matches the underside of the walkway, with a shuttered lantern held to my chest and an oilcloth coat rolled on my head. The water is body-temperature, so after a while I can't tell where I stop. The film moves against the current behind me, and I've stopped being embarrassed by it. It's only my wake.

Above me a visitor is crossing. I know before I look, because I still hear.

That is the first true thing about me. I'm one of the Listeners, but I'm a late one. The children are born hearing and lose it slowly, and mine is going more slowly than most. I have perhaps a season left, a little more. So the boots on the boards are not only a tremor in my breastbone. They are *sounds*: a knock, a drag, a knock, a hesitation where the gap is widest. They're a stranger's weight and a stranger's caution. I slide my second lid across, and through the slit between the planks I count them. One. Only one. The rest of the town feels the count pass down the pipes as a soft knuckled tapping, and I am the one who started it.

*Someone has come.* I could say it in the valve-tongue too, if I were at a wheel. But I'm not, so I only tap it, and the iron carries it away from me in both directions.

---

That's where I thought I'd stay, under the boards, watching. But it isn't what I do. Watching is what I do while I wait for low tide.

What I do is the reeds.

I'm an apprentice to a tuner who is very old and almost entirely silent. I carry her knives and her mud-boots, and when she points, I wade to where she pointed. This week she has pointed three times at the same lot, the flooded one behind the north engine-house, where the water lies clear and still and the pipework hangs reflected upside down. In the reed-stand there, a note has gone flat. My hands can't find it yet. My ears can, and that's the shameful part, and also the useful part. There is a stem near the middle whose lip has begun to sag in the heat, and it sits a hair under where the others lean. To her palms it is a missing stair. To me it is a *sourness*, a thin waver in the top of the chord, up in the register that everyone else gave up generations ago because it was no use to them.

I haven't told her I hear it. I think she knows. Once, when I was small, she took my chin in her ridged fingers and turned my head toward the stand and back again, watching my face, and let me go without a word.

So I go out at low tide with the knife. I don't cut the sagging reed. I wade in beside it and put my palm flat on its ribbed side, where the steam is climbing, and feel the thrum that everyone else feels, the honest low part. Then I lean my head against the stem and listen to the sour top with the ear I'm about to lose. Both together, the low in the hand and the high in the ear, until I can tell what the reed is trying to do and where it's failing. It wants to be a little shorter. It wants the mud around its foot thinned by a finger's depth. I cut. I dig. The lip lifts. The waver goes out of the top of the chord like a held breath finally let go.

I don't know whether anyone else would call that beautiful. I stand in the water with my hand on the stem and think that I would be content if it were the last thing I ever heard.

---

The slowdown comes while I'm still standing there.

It doesn't arrive all at once. The vents lag, the reeds drop a step and then another, and the engines follow through their rods in the mud, slowing to a heartbeat at rest and then slower, a heartbeat of something that is very tired. The lamps brown. Valves that were screaming sigh. Along the boards above me, and out of the hatches in the floors of the tall riveted houses, the others come up, wet to the shoulder, and lay their hands against the pipes. They don't act. You don't. You wait with the town the way you wait with a sick person. I climb out and find a pipe and lay both palms on it, and I feel the drop go through my feet and up my bones.

And I can also *hear* it, which none of them can. That is the cruelty and the gift. The whole chord is thinning, going down into a moan, and in the thinning I can pick out each voice separately, the way you can pick out faces in a crowd when it goes quiet. There's the reed I mended. There's one by the south wall, with a cracked, throaty note. There's one that is very old and very steady, planted somewhere in the middle of the far lot, and I know without being told whose it is. It sounds like my grandmother's laugh.

They say a planted reed is not exactly the note of the one who died. It is the note the chord would lack without them. I'm sure that's true. But I've heard both, her laugh and the reed, and I can tell you the two are not strangers.

Then the surge. It comes up from below like a shout in the ground, every reed shrieking at once, and the engines lurch and leap, and all around me faces lift, lids half-closed, mouths slackening at the corners. It's the nearest they get to a smile. I lift mine too. It floods my ears, that huge wild brightness, and I think, *I will have to learn to wear this.* I'll have to learn to take it through my soles and jaw and palms, as they do, as a sentence instead of a sound. The hearing will go, but the town won't. It will only change the door it comes in by.

---

At night I go out along the far catwalk, where the ferns are cut in cubes and so I know the planks are sound. The moss on the weeping rocks has come up pale green. The boiler doors are red slits. I put one hand on a pipe and lift the other a little from my side, palm out, to steady something in the air that isn't there.

Below me, through the gaps, someone is passing in the black water, unhurried, with a knife and a shuttered lamp, on the way to mend a note. Maybe it's my teacher. Maybe it's me, later, or someone who will be me. I tilt my head the way we do, listening for one voice in a crowd, and for as long as I still can, I hear all of them.
