I keep wanting to begin as something else. The eel-worm is the obvious pull, lit water going upstream against the tuning, unbothered. But it is what the people look at, and I would rather be one of the people looking. The blind grazer is tempting too, a whole life of echo and breath against brick. But a grazer only knows a quiet is wrong. It can't say where.

So I am on a causeway between two pools of light. Behind me the lamp-rank smells of mutton tallow. Ahead the fungus bowls give off their cold green, which is enough to find a doorway. Between them there is a stretch where I am only a sleeve dragging on wet brick and a hand held slightly up, because I have stopped mid-stride again.

I am a keeper. I found that out the way you find your own teeth with your tongue.

---

Something is late. It's not the big things, not the silt-wheel's grinding under my feet or the sluice-whistles needling overhead. Those are the floor and the ceiling of the sound. It's one bucket, somewhere behind the copperworks, that has slipped a beat. Ten thousand tipping and slamming, and one of them is a fraction slow, like a man at the back of a crowd who has started to say the wrong word.

I go up. There are rungs cast into the wall-face, slick with condensation, and my knuckles know them. The trough is jammed with black felt the gutters have grown thick, a wad of it wedged under the pivot. I dig it out with the flat of the mallet and the bucket swings, fills, overbalances, and slams. It's on the beat again. The whole run of channel below me takes up its rhythm the way a held breath lets go, and I stand on the rungs with my eyes shut and listen to something resume.

My fingernails are the color of ink. The gruel tastes of old rain, and I have never wanted anything else.

---

My name is a rhythm with a limp in it. I should say that, because it's the truest thing about me. Most names are even, a little fanfare of taps a mother beats into a cradle-rail. Mine has a false step in the middle, a stumble, the kind that makes a listener smile in the dark a quarter-mile away. My mother was a sheet-beater and had a sense of humor about it. She said she wanted the pipes to laugh a little whenever they called me. So my whole life, when I am hailed down the runs of bronze, the district has heard me arrive as a joke, and every keeper who ever taught me has said the same thing: *you'll hear the wrong beat quicker than the rest of us, because you've never been in step.*

They were right. It's why I'm out here between the lights with my head tilted, hearing something the rest of the din is covering.

---

I hear it after the bucket is fixed, when I climb down and stand a moment on the brick. It isn't a hole in the sound. I know the holes and have filled them since I was a girl, and a hole is a subtraction. This is a small addition, high and wet and too fine, a clicking in the seam between two courses of brick. It is the thing in the walls, the thing that eats the moths.

Everyone leaves it fat. I have left it fat since I could carry a dish. But I have never once stood still and asked what it was saying, and I find that I am standing still.

It is not random. That's the shock of it. It has a length and a spacing. It's the kind of thing a knuckle does on a pipe.

I put my ear to the joint. The brick is cold and beads against my cheek. Under the clicking there's something even smaller, and I think for a moment I'm imagining it, because it isn't a sound so much as the absence of a sound getting very slightly more crowded. It's a tick, one tick, then a long wait, then another, and each is a grain of the wall letting go of another grain.

I have heard about this, the way you hear about the rim. Nobody believes it. Buildings fail slowly and quietly over centuries. It's a story with a place in it, and the place is somewhere else.

---

The old quarter is where the lintels sit at knee height, and I go in on my hands, as my grandmother's granddaughters do, into a room she walked into standing. The air is dry there, oddly, from some old kiln-warmth still held in the vault. The lamps have been trimmed by someone. I don't ask who.

At the back is a wall that was sealed in my mother's time and has been sweating a hairline since the last Laying. I don't remember deciding to kneel. I've seen keepers do it, and I've been a little afraid of them for it. Now I lay my palm flat against the crack, and it isn't fear I feel.

I feel a story being shown to me. All my life I've been told that the walls would one day begin to come apart, and I have said *yes, of course* the way you agree with an old person about the weather. And here is the seam, warm under my palm, and it is just true. Nobody warned me that the truth would be so small. It's the width of a hair. It is the most tender thing I have ever touched.

I stay there a long time. Somewhere overhead a rain band crosses, and I hear it coming through the clay before I hear it in the air, the hammering rising district by district, a wave of racket traveling toward me and then over me. The buckets speed. The tuning holds. The silt moves. Through it all, under my palm, the wall goes on very slowly letting go.

---

Then I do the thing I was told as a child to do once and never look back from. I take the bone mallet from my belt and I tap my name on the sealed door. The whole limp of it, the joke in the middle.

I wait. The noise pours around me. A drip finds the back of my neck.

Nothing comes. Then something does, or doesn't, or the water lets me think so. A single knock, so faint it could be the clay settling, a long way down and a long time late, like a rain band from a different sky. I can't tell if it's an answer. It might only be a tick. It fell after the place where my name stumbles, though, right in the false step, where a listener would smile.

I don't leave at once, and I don't tell anyone. I would have to explain that the wall is dying, and also that someone might be in it, and I don't yet know how to say those two things in the same rhythm.

What I do is go back out on my hands and knees into the green light, and stand on the causeway between pools with my head tilted and one hand up, like a keeper, like a girl with a wrong beat in her name, listening for the next thing that's late. Behind me, in the seam, the clay goes on learning how to fall.
