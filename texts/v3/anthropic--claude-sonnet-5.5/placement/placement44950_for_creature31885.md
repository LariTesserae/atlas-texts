I come in from the cloud at the lip, and my first thought is that I am thirsty, which surprises me, because the thought is so old. It has been carried a long way and set down here, still wrapped, like a letter.

I have feet. I know because the moss takes their prints and fills them, and I stand watching my own steps rise back. Bare and narrow. I don't remember choosing that shape. I think I was only holding it loosely, the way you hold a note in your mouth before you sing it. The slope behind me, the century of it, is already going soft in my memory. What I am left with is the sound of dripping, which I recognize, and the heat, which I recognize as one recognizes a hand.

I walk toward the egg-house. Along the way the sundews turn their paddles after me, slowly, and I feel oddly courteous toward them, as if they were elders who have to be greeted by turning. The bird is there, gray, needle-billed, stepping between the leaves as if the ground were made of held breath. It doesn't look at the rain-keys. It doesn't look at me either, at first. Then it does, a single sideways eye, the way you glance at a person you've been told is coming. It goes on. I take that as being expected, and it steadies me more than a welcome would.

The door is already slid aside. Inside it is dry and cool and smells of stone after lightning. The floor has been keeping my old prints all this time. They rise as I step in, faint and pale, and sink back to make room for the new ones. There are three bowls. I take the one nearest me, and the water is exactly as warm as the inside of my mouth, and I drink half. I don't have to think about stopping at half. It's like the pause at the top of a breath, and my body already knows it. I set the bowl back, and it is full to the middle again, as if I had never touched it. I understand then that I have been *left* something. Somewhere on the slope behind me another is still walking, and this half is theirs, and it was mine before I arrived. Someone I have never met kept it.

I sit on the bench, on the warm end, which is warm for me.

---

By midday I am beginning to come apart, and it's less frightening than I expected. The heat loosens whatever holds the outline of me, first the edges of my hands, then the long line of the back. I go out and lie down on the moss, which lets me sink into it a little, and I feel my water begin to lift and mix with the steam from the pans. There is a moment when I can't tell which shoulder is mine and which is the fern's. The black glass ticks as it expands. It sounds like a room full of people clearing their throats, about to speak, and no one does.

I think: this is not dying. It's what I do at this hour. I go into the general steam and am general. From there I can feel the whole shelf as one skin, every pan a pore, every rain-key a small cold point of attention. And below them all, so slow that it takes me a long time to understand it is not silence, the pulse.

I have known it my whole life. I know that now the way you know the sound of a house you grew up in only when you leave it. I was never far from it. The long walk was just a slow way of being spread out around it.

---

In the afternoon the pans lift their threads and I go up with them. This is the part I am best at, it turns out. I spread flat under the lid of cloud, and the keys begin to hum, and I feel the hum in what would be teeth. I'm not alone up there. There are the others, the ones who were at the pans this morning, thin and tall and unhurried, and there are, faintly, the ones still on the slope, whose water has already begun to arrive in the cloud ahead of them. We are arranged. That's the only word. Nobody is in charge of the arrangement. We hold still in the pattern the hum asks for, and the sleeper below, which had been drifting, turns once in its sleep and comes back into step.

Then the cloud is dense enough, and we let go. It falls warm on the frogs and the sundews and the toppled key, and I feel every drop as a small homecoming with a slightly different weight. Twenty minutes. I've never been so many places at once, and I've never been so completely in the same place as everyone else.

---

Near evening I condense again, carefully, into feet, because I want to see the fallen key.

It lies among the ferns with its ring of bare, clear margin. The moss has kept its distance as if by good manners. I put my palm against it, or the denser place in the mist that is my palm, and there is a pulse, but it's late. It arrives a fraction after the others, like someone answering from the far end of a corridor. So this is the one that has to be spoken to a little louder. Nobody has told me that. I simply understand, standing there, that it is mine. Not owned. Mine the way a bed is mine. I'll come to it each dawn after the other keys, and I'll wait for its beat to arrive, and I'll wait some more, and I'll fold my slow pulse in behind its slow pulse until they lean the same way. It won't ever stand up. That isn't what it needs. It only needs someone to keep it company in being late.

The frogs begin. One note, then another, a little off the first, then a third, and the chord builds without anyone being responsible for it. I stand in the last gold light with the print of my foot slowly refilling at the edge of the ring. The moss leaves it a minute longer than it leaves the bird's, I think. Maybe two.

I go back to the egg-house and look at the bowls. One of them is mine and full, and the other two are full, and I wonder how many of us are out there, still coming, with the long slope in their mouths, and which one of them will find the bowl I've just left. I hope they are less startled than I was.

Then I lie down on the warm end of the bench, in the dry, and let the floor remember me beside the others, and listen to the drip go on all night in its thousand pitches. Under it, late by a breath, the fallen key answers the rest.
