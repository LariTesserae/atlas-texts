I am standing, and for a long while that is all I know about myself.

Then the floor tells me the rest. A lamplouse sheet shivers somewhere to my left, quick and thin. A Sexton line goes down behind me like a slow drum. Under all of it the beetles hold their breath, and I hold mine with them, and the huff comes up through the terraces and passes through my legs. I find that I have legs, and that there are eight of them. Seven are resting on the crowd's back without weighing on it. The eighth is lifted a hair above a gap, and it is the only part of me that is deciding anything.

So I am a Threadwalker, and I am standing on the hot line.

I have no eyes worth the name. What I have is a stretch of ground about ten of my lengths wide, and I know it the way you know the inside of your mouth. The lamplice end at my left edge like a shoreline. Past my right edge another hunter is standing, and I know her by her footfall. She sets down a leg every so often, and it rings a little flat, as if she had once broken it and it healed short. We have never touched. We will never touch. If we did, one of us would be short of food, and so we stand apart and feel each other through the rock like two people in separate rooms with a wall between them and a pipe running through it.

This is the loneliness of my kind, and I would not call it sorrow. It is only a measurement. Everything else here lives pressed against something warm. I live with a gap on each side, and I have learned to read the gap.

I wait. An hour goes by, or what I would call one if I counted. A young beetle is shifting in its pit, and I know it is young by the higher note of its weight. A lamplouse wanders toward the line, out of the warm and into the almost-hot, its flicker going weak. I do not move yet. It comes closer. The eighth leg comes down through the gap, the hooked tip finds the soft shell, and the lamplouse is lifted out of the sheet. The sheet closes behind the hole and gives one small shiver, *something is wrong*, and the shiver runs out and dies against the cooler ground. I fold the thing and carry it to the ledge and take it apart slowly. Nothing about me has ever been hurried.

I am not sorry. But I have noticed that I am never careless, and I think that is close to being sorry.

Above me, near the shaft, hang the husks. I have shed six times and all six are up there in the gallery, each one my exact shape, gray and empty and facing the same way I face. When I climb during the pulse I pass beneath them. I cannot look up, but I know they are there the way I know my own legs are there. I sometimes think of them as the six who stood here before me, though I am not sure the word *before* means anything. They stood the same stillness. They read the same floor. I am only the one who is still inside.

To the north of my stretch, where the line dips toward the bottom of the third terrace, is the ring.

I have never stepped on it. My range ends where its outer wall begins, and I set my northernmost leg on the clinker beside it and no further, and nothing has ever told me to. The crowd stops there too. Beetles have not dug into it. The lamplice sheets curve around it. Even the Sextons, who carry everything, lay their lines in a wide arc so they never cross the stones. It is not fear. I know fear; it is a particular quick note in the floor. This is more like courtesy, held so long that nobody remembers it as a choice.

The ring is silent almost all the time. That is how I know it is not empty. Empty ground rings back, and this ground only takes the tremor in and keeps it. Inside the circle sits something that breathes once, very long, every several minutes. I feel the draw the way you feel a door open somewhere in a house. It does not disturb the dust. It disturbs me a little, because my legs were made to read every trembling thing and this one reads as neither prey nor neighbor. I have no ring for it.

Once, in the time of this body, I felt the stone.

The pulse had passed. The ground was coolest, the beetles were only beginning to loosen, and I stood at the north end of my stretch as I always stand. Then, through the rock, came a sound that was not a footfall. A footfall is a strike and a fade. This was a slow drag, weight taken up, carried, and set down, with a small grinding settle at the end like a word finished. It took the whole of a season, or seemed to. Every leg I had was listening. Even the flat-footed hunter beside me went still, and I felt her stillness reach me through the floor. For that long hour two of the loneliest animals in the Kiln were standing together, and neither of us would ever say so.

Then the stone was placed, and the ring was a little different, and the crowd went on breathing.

I do not know what the Stacker is doing. I have heard, in the way things are heard here, that it built the ring, or inherited it, or is the last thing that remembers what the ring is for. I have heard that the empty honeycomb on the eastern wall was the work of its kind. I do not believe or disbelieve any of this. What I know is what my leg knows: someone is turning stones, slowly, in the one cool place in the chamber, and while it does, the rhythm holds.

When the heat rises and everything climbs, I climb with the rest, and the flow bends around the ring the way the tide bends around a rock. I go up past the husks and stand at the rim among the pale small things I usually eat. They are too frightened to flash. I am too frightened to hunt. We wait together, the hunter and the hunted, pressed close for once, until the cold air comes sinking down the shaft. I always think then of the one down there in the cool circle, sitting through the fire with its long hands folded, and I think that it must be the only one of us that was never afraid.

Then we go back down. The salts start to sweat. I take my stretch of the line again. I set seven legs on the backs of the sleeping crowd, and I lift the eighth, and I wait, feeling for the note that is in no category, the draw of one breath every several minutes.

When it stops, I think I will be the first to know. My stretch runs to the ring's edge, and my leg is resting on the clinker beside its wall. I have thought about what I will do then, on the many hours I have to think. The answer that comes is small. I will keep my leg where it is a little longer than I need to, and I will let the gap on that side be a gap. Then the beetles will change their rhythm, and the floor will tell everyone else.

Until then I stand. One foot is up. Something below me flickers once, *I am here*, and I let it be.
