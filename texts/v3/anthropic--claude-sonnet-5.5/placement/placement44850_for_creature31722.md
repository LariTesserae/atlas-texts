I would like to say I'd arrive as the visitor, boots on the stone, the warm water at my ankles, the first violet skin of mat starting on the leather. But the visitor is only the flats' newest entry, and I think I would spend my whole stay waiting to be written down. I'd rather be something that already is.

So I start out a comb, in the middle of a bed, leaning my personal degree downstream. It is pleasant. The flush comes down the diagonal from the fan upstream and passes through me and on to the fan below: *richer, richer, thinner now.* I have nothing to add, and the nothing is a kind of peace. But a comb has one subject, and I find I have been thinking about the number of things, so I can't stay. I am too aware of the rows.

I drift up, in the way a mind can. I go past the tenders, whose scritching is a hand brushing a table. I go past the beads, breathing the steam into the wall, ring on ring, one day at a time. Up to the rail, where the light is nearly shadowless and the gantry casts its faint gray outline over all of us.

I am a roost.

---

The rail is cool and wet under my feet. On my left is a shoulder and on my right is a shoulder, and the warmth of them is exactly the warmth of me. I hold still. This is harder than it sounds, and it is also the easiest thing in the world. Holding still is not the absence of motion. It is the work, and I am doing it with everything I have. The gantry carries us along its rail so slowly that I feel it only as the terraces changing their minds beneath me. A lip slides under, a sheet of water goes over it, smooth as glass, and I watch the weir the way you watch a clock you have stopped needing.

I do not count. I want to be clear about that, because the tenders would say we do, and the beads, who hold the actual record, would say nothing. What I have is a pressure, the way a full cup has a pressure. It is the sense of this span, this rail, this terrace, and how many of us are on it against how many the water below is asking for. The threaders braid in the culvert, and the braid is a little thick. The comb-bed past the lip flushes a little pale. Nobody tells me. It is simply that the row is a shade too full, and I am a shade of the row.

That is how it happens. There is no decision in it, only a loosening, like a held breath finding out it can be let go.

I open my wings.

---

The first thing is the sound, which is the absence of the others. I have never heard my own feathers. They make a small dry tearing, silk against nothing, and it is the only voice I have.

Then I am low over the water, and the flats open under me the way a hand opens. Every terrace is level, then a lip, then level, and the haze takes the far ones a few at a time. The combs lean, thousands of them, ticking like rain on paper, and I pass over them so close that the ripple of the flush runs beneath my shadow and I do not know whether they are announcing me or only announcing the water. A tender lifts its slate back to the air and lowers it again. A braid of threaders is pouring through a pipe mouth, and one of them is going the wrong way, and I do not take it. It is not my one.

Here is what I did not know on the rail. For the length of this flight, I am not in the count.

I am not in the row I left. The row has already closed over the gap, gently and without hurry. I am not in the row I'm flying toward, because there is no gap there yet, only a pressure I am going to answer. For a few dozen wingbeats, over a few kilometers of warm still water, I am the one the tally cannot place. Every thousand years the total has never changed by more than one, and here is the one. I am the margin. I am the error the balance allows itself.

I begin to suspect it is not an error at all.

A ledger that never had a body in the air would be a ledger of things that never moved. A count needs a slack place in it, or nothing could ever be counted into a new position. The wheel lifts back what the lip lets fall, but there is a moment, a full moment, when the water is in the pipe and belongs to neither terrace. The young pass through the dark warm pipe the same way, unborn and unplaced, tumbling in the fine pale growth. Maybe the flats hold their perfect arithmetic by keeping a small, moving hole in it, and by trusting that hole to fly.

I don't think the others know this. The combs simply live the number. The roosts, they say, know it, and I have always assumed knowing was the same as standing still.

---

I see the rail ahead. It is another gantry, another row, pewter and patient, all their heads turned the same way. One place along it is open. I don't remember seeing it open, but it is, a shoulder's width, and the neighbors on either side hold a fraction more apart than they must. They have not looked at me. They have not looked at anything. They have made room the way the water makes room.

I fold and drop and take the rail. It is cool and wet under my feet. On my left is a shoulder and on my right is a shoulder, and the warmth is exactly the warmth of me, though I have never met them.

The gap closes to my width. That is the whole ceremony.

Am I the same one? The question is as heavy as a stone in the pipe, and it sinks the same way. The flats do not answer questions like it. They answer only that the count is right, and the count is right.

But I keep something. In the long holding-still that follows, while the gantry carries us across a terrace where it never rains and into one where it always does, warm fine curtains drifting a few hundred meters and stopping, I keep the small dry tearing of my own wings. I keep the moment I belonged nowhere. I turn my head the same way as everyone else, toward the next terrace, and I look at it with my half-lidded, dark, patient eye.

And far down the staircase, on a rail I can't see, some other roost is feeling the row a shade too full. I don't know its name, and the flats have no names. I feel it the way you feel a sluice-wheel in your soles, a pulse slower than a heart. I hold very still, and I wait to be the one who is not counted, and I wait to be the one who makes room.
