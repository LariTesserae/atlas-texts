# The Wearer of the Fourth Stair

I arrive believing I am the guest. The room lets me believe it, since the room is polite about first mistakes.

I come through the seam sideways, with my hands held in front of me as if I could cover it behind me. The heat takes me at once, wet and thin, and I sit down hard on the nearest step because my knees decide it. The step is worn into a dish, and the dish fits. It doesn't fit the way a chair fits, by luck or approximation. It fits the way a sleeve fits the arm that has been in it. My heel finds its hollow, and the ball of my foot finds its slope. Somewhere in the walls, a hammering that has been going on all this time goes quiet for the length of a breath.

I think: *how kind.* I think: *they were expecting me.*

Then the tock-tock-tock starts again, closer, behind the block at my shoulder. There is a pause, and one clean ringing strike. When I turn, a new stone is set beside me, dry and fitted, its dust still settling, its edges sharp enough to cut. I put my hand flat against it to lean, because it is exactly where I would lean, and the edge opens my thumb.

It doesn't hurt. It is only a small crescent of red on the pad, in the place where a scar already is.

I hold the two thumbs up in the orange light, and both have the scar. One is old and pale, and the other is bleeding a little. I begin to understand that I have been sitting on the wrong side of the wall.

---

I should tell it properly, then. I am a wearer, and I have been one since before I can remember, though *before* is unreliable here. My work is the stairs. There are eleven flights, or nine, or fourteen, and I am always on the fourth, which is the one I know. I kneel on the cold stone above the steaming terrace with a bone-handled point and a mallet no heavier than a fist, and I carve hollows into treads that nobody has walked.

People misunderstand this, when they think of it at all. They think it is forgery, that we are making the stairs *lie* about their age. But a lie has a listener it means to deceive, and I have no listener. I have a foot, and it is not here yet. I carry it in me the way you carry the taste of a fruit you've been promised. I know the weight of it, and how it lands heel-first when it is tired and toe-first when it is glad. I know that it turns slightly inward on the left, and that it will come down these stairs holding something, a bowl or a child or nothing, with the other hand always reaching for the wall. So I carve the wall-side hollow a finger shallower, so the reaching body has somewhere to fall forward into. That is what I mean by hospitality. I make a step ready for the feet it is owed.

I do not do it in one pass. A hollow is ten thousand passes deep, and I can only cut the first. The rest are the visitor's, and the visitor's descendants', for as long as the seam holds. I am a guest of my own stairs in that sense, too. I cut the shape and I will never feel it worn.

Except that I am feeling it now, and this is what the room has been trying to tell me with its counting.

---

Pace the fourth flight and you get twenty steps. Pace it again and you get thirty-one. The setters say the terraces number eleven, nine, fourteen, and each of them is right from where they stand. I have stopped correcting myself. I only note that when I lie in the gradient at night, tied to the ladder with the ugly knot, warm at the feet and cold at the crown, and my hand goes on twitching against flint in my sleep, I am not carving the stairs for a stranger.

The left foot turns inward. The wall-hand reaches. I have known this gait since the beginning of the work, and I have never asked where I learned it.

I ask now, kneeling behind the wall with the point in my hand, listening through a block of stone to the weight of someone settling onto my fourth stair. I hear them breathe, and I hear them hold their hands out in front of them as if covering a crack. I know the heel and the way the left foot lands. The wall-hand is not reaching yet, because they are sitting, so they have no need. But it will.

I look at my forearm, where the moss is thin now, the ancient layers worn down to a few gray tufts and a wide bare patch at the wrist. It was thick when I was small. The youngest carry the deepest moss and spend their lives wearing it down, which we do not find strange, because age is *installed* here, and you receive it before you've earned it. I am nearly smooth. Whoever sits on my step is deep with moss, deeper than I have ever been. There is a great green layering on their shoulders that has never had time to think itself old.

Somewhere far ahead of me, or behind, a child is born who will have always been born here, and will walk these stairs, and will not know why the hollows fit.

---

The tock-tock-tock in the wall beside me is mine. I understand that now, and it is the last thing I understand, because it arrives before I do. The sound comes first, and I am the body that follows it. I set the mallet to the chisel, and the block goes in. I do not choose the place. The place is where they will lean.

The frogs have gone quiet. That means something is about to be finished that should not be, and we all stop, every guild at once, in the dripping dark. I hold my breath, and the thin whistle of the reed-pipes does not come back. On the other side of the stone, someone puts their hand against a new wall and I hear the small sound of an edge opening a thumb.

I lay my own thumb against the block from my side, in the same place. The stone is warm on this side, from the vent.

Then I strike once, clean and ringing, to close the sentence. It is what we say when we mean *welcome*, or *lean*, or *it's yours*, or *you were expected*. The word is the same as the word for the mountain's pushing, and for the pressure at the small of the back. It means a guest who has been awaited for so long that the waiting has become a kind of house.

The steam is starting up from the lowest terrace, and the shaft of light is almost there. In a moment we will all go out and stand in the downpour, faces up, mouths open, silent in the white roar. I will stand beside someone with deep moss and a left foot that turns in. We will touch thumbs, and the stone will have cut us both.

And I will say *eleven*, and mean it, from where I am.
