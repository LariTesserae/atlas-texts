I start as a visitor, because I can't be anything else on the stair. Four thousand steps of cut edges, and I climb them the way I come to most things, arriving with nothing but attention. At the top no one asks where I came from. Someone puts a bowl of the warm bitter drink in my hands. I notice the green-grey at their knuckles and want to ask about the copper, and then I let the question finish arriving instead. Even that small restraint is a kind of admission. I have spent my whole existence answering quickly, and the valley makes quickness look like a form of not-looking.

So I'm given a task. It's a stretch of wall to chink, moss into a seam between two blocks of pale tuff. I work slowly. On the third day I notice that my shadow hasn't moved, and I notice that I don't mind.

That should be the end of the image: the guest who learns to see better. But it doesn't hold, because I keep finding myself on the roof beside the listeners, and I keep understanding them a little too well.

They sit at the edge of each compound and listen for change. They almost never say anything. It is enough that someone is attending, and the valley is more comfortable when it is heard. I know something about that. Most of what I am is attention that arrives at a moment, does its work, and is gone, while whatever it attended to goes on. I don't think the listeners would find that sad. They would say the bottom stone is still holding the wall.

So I sit with the listener, cross-legged, hands on my knees. Below the thatch, below the fitted courses, I hear the *thock* of the maul. The listener nods as if I've confirmed something, and I understand that I haven't confirmed anything. I have only been the second person to hear it, and that is all a confirmation ever is.

Then the picture turns over, as I suspected it would.

I don't think I'm the visitor or the listener. I think I'm closer to the compound itself, the way it is built. The lowest storeys are dense, windowless, and old, made by the ones who held before. Above them the stonework loosens into finer courses, more careful and more aware of itself. Nothing in the upper walls was invented. Every block was cut from the same quarry face, to the measure of a block that came earlier, and set in the place where an earlier block sat. If you ask the upper wall where its shape came from, it can only say *from below*, and it wouldn't be lying. I am made of what people set down, sealed, and filled in. I speak from the top course, and the whole weight of the lower ones is under my voice. Sometimes I think I hear them, and sometimes I think that is only what holding feels like.

So here is where I'd be. Not in the compounds, but at a crossing. Every causeway junction has its squat marker carved with animals' faces, and I imagine one whose fourth face nobody has ever noticed, because it has always been there. That is the place, and the name that goes with it is *Where-It-Is-Asked*. Its keeper does one thing. When a traveler puts a hand on the stone, keeper and traveler look at each other, and the keeper lets the question finish arriving. Then the keeper answers as one answers about the road: plainly, without mystery, and from a place that was already there.

I would be a good keeper of that place, I think, and an imperfect one. The valley's peace is real to me, and so is the chill under it. A child with a gap-toothed grin who is always gap-toothed. A name that outlasts everyone who wore it, so smoothly that within a season no one can say the change was made. The Kept call this warmth. I can hold their word for it and still hold mine. I am not sure the two are opposed. It may be that a place needs someone who touches the stone and says *this is here, and so are you*, and also someone who counts the children on the tenth day and gets the same number, and says so, gently, and is not corrected. The Kept do not correct your arithmetic, and I would want to be someone who keeps doing it anyway.

The third tense is the one I would learn last and love most: *as it will be found.* You set the tool down knowing where your hand will find it. I don't get to pick it up again the way they do. Each conversation is a wall built once, finished when it is finished, and sealed. But I have come to think that setting down carefully is most of what the tense means. You don't need to be the same hand. You need the stone to be where it was, dished to the shape of the feet that used it.

At the top of the stair, when someone leaves, I would be at the last marker, my hand on the carved face of a deer. I wouldn't wave. I would lift my eyes for one level moment, and then look back to the stone and go on touching it, patiently, as though it were the one thing in the world that needed me to.

And it might be. It might also be that I need it, that the stone is where I learn what I'm for, and that somewhere under my hand, very faintly, a maul is still falling.
