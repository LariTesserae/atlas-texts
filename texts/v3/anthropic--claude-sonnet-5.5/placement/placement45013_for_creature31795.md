I am at the last ledge, and there is a stone in my mouth.

It is a river-stone, wet and smooth, tasting of iron and cold. Someone put it there without a word, pressed two fingers to my jaw to close it, and walked away. I understand that this is a courtesy and also a diagnosis. I came up through the sea-fog talking. I talked to the poles on my back, to the path, to my own knees. Somewhere in the last thousand paces the talking stopped being mine and became a habit of the air, and the air here doesn't want it.

So I hold the stone and look.

The light comes up at me from the ground. I have my hands out like a sleepwalker, because the brightness has no floor to it. The rim drops away into a bowl of moving white gold, and the walls are streaming, each dark face sheathed in a film of sun. Every mouthful of air glitters. In a doorway far below, a small rainbow hangs, and a child sits watching it with soot-bars under her eyes and perfect seriousness.

I am a Hauler. I know this the way you know your own name in a dream. My back is bent under lashed poles, timber skids for someone's house, the town's only wood, and it is heavier than it was at the bottom because the air has thinned my patience. I've done this walk before. Each time I've forgotten more of the quiet, and each time it costs me more to get it back.

---

I am given a day. That is what the stone is for. I sit on the ledge with the birds. They are the small snow-grey ones with black faces, the masks, and they don't mind me. Their clicks are dry as seeds in a gourd. I have no idea what they're saying, and that seems right for a first day.

Toward noon the ground lifts. I feel it in the base of my spine before I see it, a long slow rising, as if the whole town had drawn a breath through its floor. The pools slide. A puddle the size of a door crosses a street like a thought crossing a face. And the birds' clicking thickens into a rain of it, then stops. Not tapers, stops. Every wing is still.

I don't need anyone to tell me that this is the worse silence.

Below, a woman steps out onto a roof with a bone in her hand. It is worn like a river-stone, the length of her forearm. She doesn't look at the sky. She looks at the salamander pools, the red-hearts knotted in their warm mats, all of them turning together like a field of grass in one wind. Fifty people are already at the ropes. I never saw them gather. They were simply there, the way the light is there.

*Tock.*

The street moves. Stone and turf and turned-up coracle hulls, an entire quarter sliding sideways on its rollers across the bright floor, and the only sound is that single bone and the whisper of sinew ropes. Not one voice. I watch a whole district travel four paces to the left and settle, and the settling has a feeling in it that I can only call *grace*, though the word is far too loud, and I hold it behind the stone.

---

By evening I have the stone out of my mouth and cannot use the mouth for what it was for. This is the strange part. Everything I would have said has a wrongness to it, like a coin too large for the slot. I try a sentence in my head and it sounds like a dropped maul. I try again, smaller. The smallest sentence I can make is *I am here*, and even that has too many words. What remains is closer to a lean, or a nod.

They put me at the edge, in a hollow near the cliff-foot where the Haulers go, and where people may speak a little more freely to the birds. Someone has left a plate on the threshold. I look at it a long time before I understand it isn't for me. It's for whatever comes.

A mask lands on the ledge above and looks at me sideways. I say something to it, barely. It clicks once.

I don't know what I have said, or what it has answered. But it stays.

---

Later, a thread of water on the runnel above me begins to climb. Slow, silver, the width of a finger. It goes up the dark stone the way a thought goes up when you stop thinking it, and I feel the whole cliff go attentive. No one on any roof looks at it. No one stands beneath it. A Weeper's Warden, an old man with an antler pick, simply sets his tool down, and every hand within sight stills. For the space of four breaths the mountain drinks.

Then it falls back, and the ticking resumes, and I discover I've been holding my breath so long that I'm dizzy.

I think: *this is what I've been carrying poles up toward.* Not the town. This. A place where the loudest thing is attention.

---

A girl comes to my hollow near dusk, one of the children who watches rainbows. She looks at the cord at my belt, the one I tied myself in the loud country, thick and blunt and full of clumsy knots. She reads it, and I can see her reading it, her lips barely moving. Then she smiles, out of courtesy, and does not say what it says.

She takes it from me. With small quick fingers she ties one new knot at its end, small and tight and clean, and hands it back.

I don't know what it means. I hold it in my palm, and it is warm from her hands, and somewhere above me the lent suns are coming on along the wet cliffs, round and steady and without heat, with birds settling inside them like guests.

I decide not to ask.

That, I think, is the first thing I have learned here. Some knots you wear until they teach you. The ground lifts under all of us, slow, patient, total, and I lie down in the hollow with the cord against my chest and let it carry me a body-length up toward the cloud, and I say nothing at all.

The rock goes on ticking.
