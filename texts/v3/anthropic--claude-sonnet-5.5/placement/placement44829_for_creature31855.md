I am aware first of my knees, which is how the long morning always begins for me. The mat gives under the left one a moment before the right, and I know by the slowness of the spring which house I am beside. The Weirs' island is soft on its north edge, where their dead were laid three winters back and the moss has not finished closing.

So I am a walker. I did not choose it. It is what the old and the not-yet-grown are given, and I am old. My knife is flint, curved like a fingernail paring, hafted in deer bone gone smooth and dark from my palm. My palms are wrapped in hide. Under the wrapping the skin is white and ridged, and I could read a plank with it in a sleeping house.

I go out along the boards, and the springtails begin their descent from the wall in a grey pour. I don't stop them or hurry them. My hand rises to my knife as if it were rising through a pond.

*

This is what the morning is made of. A tendril, finger-long and pale as a tooth, has come across the plank in the night and laid its tip under the edge of the board. It is not cruel. It is only interested. I slide the knife beneath it, and it comes away with a wet sound too small for the bats to mind. I say, "Sorry, sorry," in the murmur that is more breath than voice, and I lay the cut piece in my basket. It will be dried and twisted into cord, and this is the arrangement between us, though neither of us agreed to it.

I have cut for thirty-one winters. Before that I was a climber, and I still have a little of the sky in my eyes, a slowness to close. My wide pupils never came back to their old size. I look at the lamp-bowls straight on, which is rude, and no one has told me to stop, because I am old and it is known where I have been.

*

Between the Weirs' house and the meeting-house there is a curtain of hanging root as thick as a woman's waist, and I have cut one strand of it every morning since the year the flood came to the third notch. That is nine winters. It is a bad strand, always reaching for the plank-way, always a little warmer than the others when I touch it. I don't know why. Perhaps it sits close to the hearth-line, or perhaps it likes me.

This morning it is not there.

That is not right. It is there, hanging, but its tip is turned. It has grown, in one night, a full forearm's length, and it has done so away from the plank-way, curving out over the open moss toward the low ground where the black pools are. The other strands hang straight. This one leans as a person leans who has heard their name tapped from far off.

I stand with the knife in my hand. My knees remember the give of the mat, and I think of what the walkers say to one another in the hour after the evening, and what none of us insists on. *A strand that has been cut every day for years will begin, one morning, to grow away from the knife.* I always understood that as a story about hurt. The root recoils, learns, avoids. It never occurred to me that a thing might grow away from you toward something else.

I put the knife in my belt. This is not a small act. I have not left a strand uncut since I was fifty-four.

*

I tap the plank with one knuckle, twice, then the flat of my palm, slow. That is my name, and after it I hum the three falling notes that go with it. Then I tap the shape for *come and see*, which we rarely use, because nothing here ever needs seeing quickly.

I wait. The drip goes on in its hundred notes. Overhead the gnat-threads shift in the air of my breathing, and the faint constellation rearranges itself.

Then a tap comes back from the far side of the chamber, small, dry, unhurried. It is the coal-keeper who is up early, asking *what*. I do not know how to say *what* for this. So I tap the sign for *root*, and the sign for *turned*, and I stop there, because the language has no word for what I mean, and I would rather say too little than say something the ceiling can hear.

*

I follow the strand. There is no plank there, so I lower myself onto the mat in the slow way we teach the children, hip first and then hand, and I go on my knees, with the black water a foot under my shins and the moss shivering with springtails. Each of my movements sends a small grey wave, and I wait for each to settle. This takes a long time. I don't mind. I have found that the world gives more when you do not ask it to be fast.

The strand leads me past the last of the lamp-bowls and into the dark between islands, where the glow is only the smear of algae opening under my hands. My handprints light up blue-green and go out behind me, so there is a trail of small hands going back to the boards. I think: someone could follow this, and they would know I was here, and that I was not afraid, and that I was slow.

At the edge of a pool I find the tip. It has not stopped. It curves down to the water's skin, where the moss has been worn thin by a hundred evenings of children kneeling with a weighted cord, and there is the gap. I know it. I lowered my own cord here, at eleven. It went and went and never came to bottom, and afterward the elders drew it up with no one speaking.

The root has gone into the gap. It is not reaching down as roots do toward water. It is going down as a hand goes into a pocket.

And the air that comes up out of the gap is warm.

Not much. A breath's worth, no more than the heat of a sleeper's cheek. But I have spent my life among the roots and the small angles of the fire, and I know what warm feels like against damp skin in a cold place. I have felt it under my cap, in my sleep, on the outer wall.

I kneel there and do not put the knife to anything.

*

Somewhere behind me the plank-way takes a step. Someone is coming, carefully, testing the mat, with a covered pot of coals held low, because the keeper would not come to a question without bringing the fire. Above me the ceiling turns, all of it, the whole lid of pale lilac roots, the way a field of stalks turns in wind, though there is no wind. It turns toward the pot. It turns toward the gap. It turns, I think, toward the two of them at once, and I understand that the hunger I was raised to fear has never been only for heat. It has been for whatever is warm and quiet and does not strike.

I stay very still, in the smell of wet stone and something sweet and slightly rotten, and I hum my three falling notes, low, under the drip, so that whatever is down there, or above, or through the root in my hand, will know who is kneeling here. It will know that she was slow, and that she said sorry, and that for nine winters she cut only what crept and never in anger.

The pot's amber comes across the moss and lies there like a thread. I don't turn my head. My torso turns, slowly, like a heron.

"Tell me," the keeper whispers.

"Not yet," I whisper back. "Let it finish."
