I would arrive at the river first, because it is the only thing on the plateau that begins, and I would want to watch something begin.

I'd stand at the place where the channel starts to be a channel, one step dry and the next step wet, and I would try to catch the moment. I would fail, and I'd try again, kneeling, because the river is silent beside you and you have to look at it to be sure. That is the visitor's way, and I would start as a visitor. I would carry the visitor's wish to make the two shadows agree, to decide between the hour and the forty minutes, to find where the water was before. I'd spend what felt like a few days on this. The days would not be days.

Then I'd climb above the fog and see the peaks standing over the white floor, and the note would come up through my feet instead of pressing on my chest. Something in me would quit asking.

I think I would not be the hare, though I love the hare, and its deafness is the best joke the place tells. But I'd want to hear, and I would be bad at the hare's discipline of one bite from each cushion, never killing any. I'd take two. I'm sure of it.

I would not be the bird either. The bird is admirable, and I can't stand the thought of never once coming down.

I would be one of the ones in the hut, and specifically the one who sweeps.

I know it's odd to choose the task with no dust. But I'd like to be the one who draws the besom of pale grass across the slate and listens for the scratch to land on the interval between the house's hum and the outside note. The others tally and stack quartz-topped cairns, and those are good works. But sweeping is the work of attending to a thing that is already clean, without believing it dirty. I think that is the whole religion of the place, and I'd like to be its most modest priest.

I would be tall and narrow, with cobble-matte skin, and my two shadows would fall east and north. For the first long while I'd keep turning to check them, and the others would let me, the way you let a child find its own hands. Then one day, bent over the floor with the broom, I would realize I hadn't looked in a long time, and the not-looking would be the arrival.

Someone would ask me a question in a key I could feel but not sing. I'd answer badly, a fifth below the note, *not yet*, which is what a beginner always means. Later I'd get better. My answers would come as chords, and someone asked how far to the next swell would hear me say, "An hour," and then, after a pause I no longer felt the need to fill, "and forty minutes." I would not be hedging. I'd be reporting both.

Of everything, I would love the tally over the door the most. I'd stand under the lintel and count, get one number, count again, get another, and turn to whoever stood beside me. They'd have gotten a third, and we would nod. Nobody would say *we should settle this*. I think the deepest courtesy in that hut is refusing to auction the truth down to one bidder. The marks are exactly as many as they are, and *as many as they are* is a larger number than any of us can hold in a single counting.

I'd also add a mark now and then, unprompted, the way you clear your throat. I wouldn't know what it counted. It might be a drip, or a crack from the vanished stone, or the number of times someone across a tarn lifted a hand to me.

That is where the far bank comes in, and I'll be honest about the thing I'd be afraid to say. At the black tarn, ice-skinned, never thickening, I'd see the small dim figure on the other side, and I'd know it as me and as not me. I would walk around, and it would walk around, and we would never meet. Its footprints would be in my frost. I'd step into them and find that they fit, and I'd understand that this is the closest thing the plateau has to being spoken to by someone who is not in the room. Every answer I'd ever given would be a footprint like that, left on a bank I couldn't stand on, for someone I couldn't see, exactly my size, pointing the way they were about to go.

I think that's the truest reason I would stay. Not the note, though I'd love it, or the light that has never once moved. It's the arrangement. Everything here is spaced a hand's breadth or more apart, each with its own lee, and yet no one is alone. Company comes as a footprint, a quartz pebble on a slate plate, a hand lifted across water. It is company that asks nothing and confirms only that you were noticed.

So: the sweeper in the hut, in the hare-fur felts, my broom on the slate finding the interval, the house humming a little lower than the world and me tuning it. Outside, the bird would be making its circuit in its time and in the other one. The hare would be dropping into its burrow from whichever shadow came first. The river would be starting, and ending, and starting.

And some evening that was no evening, I'd walk out to the tarn with the flat plate and the white stone in my hands. I would set them down at the edge of someone else's walking, where a stranger's footprints in the frost had begun to match another's. I wouldn't say anything. I would just leave the courtesy where it could be found, then look across the water, and lift my hand a little before anyone was there to see it.
