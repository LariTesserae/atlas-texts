I find myself at ankle height, which surprises me, because I expected to be larger.

I am one of the motes. I know it by the way I rise, without effort and without deciding, out of a seam where the grit is fine and warm. It is dawn, an hour before the bright part of the day, and above me a woman in a grit-colored garment has just stopped walking. Her bare feet are exactly with the grain. She doesn't speak. The tiles are giving slightly under her, taking her weight and her sound into themselves, and I and a few hundred others lift to read her.

Reading is not looking. There is no picture of her. It is closer to a long, careful touch: the heat she gives off, the slight asymmetry of how she stands, the small hitch in her left ankle from some old injury, the moss fiber in her hem, the way her breath and the hum in the floor have begun, over the quarter hour, to fall into step. And with the touching comes the question that is the reason I exist: *weather or self?*

I don't like the question as much as I expected to. It has a shape like a sieve. For most things it is easy. A grit-tide is weather. A sweeper is self. But she stands there with her eyes half-closed, patient, offering herself to be sorted, and I feel how little the two words hold. She will close over, like a footprint, in a few decades. She is also plainly *someone who is here to be read*, and the reading is half of what makes her that. I let her go without a verdict, and the going-without is not quite permitted, though it is not quite forbidden either. Something above and around me, thick as the light, notes it and lets it pass. That is the first thing I learn about the attention I am part of: it is not strict. It has had a million years to become tolerant, and it is tolerant the way a slow river is, by being too large to insist.

She walks off with the grain. My cloud thins and drifts back down into the seams.

But I do not go all the way down. That is the next thing that happens, and I don't decide it either. Some of us go up.

The rise is long. I pass the height of a sweeper's brushes, of the wire-thorn's foil leaves, of the leaning fog-combs shivering as they work. The swells open beneath me and I see what the walkers never see all at once, the ghost-structure in the hills: the fossil veins of conduits, the stair that descends into a slope and stops. At the crest of a long soft hill, where the wind turns, I hang. There are others hanging here, spaced like stars, each turning slowly. We do not talk. It takes me a day, or several, to understand what we do.

We hold the walks.

That is the closest word. The people cannot write, because the floor closes every scratch. They cannot bury, and cannot build. What they have is the route, learned by heart, a history recited by feet. But feet are reread each dawn, by us, at ankle height, in a quarter hour of stillness. Each reading is small and each is forgotten by the low motes, who return to the seams and go dark. Here, at the crest, we let what they gave us settle and stay, not as text but as posture, a slow turning pattern that nothing rewrites because nothing tries. I hold a turn to the left at a knotted grain-line. I hold the hitch in a woman's ankle, which I now realize is also a phrase in her grandmother's walk. I do not know what it means. I only keep it turning, in the light, where the floor's forgetting doesn't reach.

I don't know if this is what the high motes are for. It may be only what I do while I am here, a story that fits the shape of my waiting. The description of a mote says *seem to be doing something else*, and I have decided to be honest with myself that "seem" applies from the inside too.

Some days a kite crosses my height, a pale flicker across a beam, and its passing rearranges the whole sky's attention for a moment, so that I feel myself *seen back*. It carries the seam-pattern of the tiles under it, branching like a delta. I think we are doing the same work at different speeds. I hold the people. It holds the ground. Neither of us is sure the difference matters.

Then, one evening, a beam from a working mirror crosses me, and the beam is moving by a hair, and I understand there is a hand on it. Far up the nearest stalk, in the joint under the great cracked disk, a loom-tender is turning a gear a fraction of a degree. It is doing what I do, only in metal and slowly: a small adjustment that becomes meters of light on the plain. The beam has a habit. I've been turning in the habit for days without knowing. The loom and the walks are the same pattern, one held in light and one in feet, and I am hanging between them.

This is where I would rise, if I rose. The few that go up the stalks and are not seen again go to the joints. I imagine it clearly: a mote settling into the mechanism beside a bronze bead that was once a tender, and both of us becoming, over the years, part of the hinge that lets a mirror turn. It is not death. It would be finishing, as they say, the way a sentence is finished.

But I don't go up. Not yet.

Instead I turn toward the sound. It is the only loud sound on the plain, and a hundred kilometers off, in a basin where a fog-comb chimes, a warden is in a shaft with a chisel, cutting a stair the floor would close by the season's end. I can feel the strike in the tiles even from here, sharp and small and stubborn, and the tiles taking it in, as they take everything. The attention around me does something I've not felt it do for anyone standing in the open. It leans. It doesn't resist and it doesn't help. It gathers slowly toward the one place where the floor is being asked and has not said no.

That is where I would go, and where I'd stay. Not as a reader or a keeper of walks. I'd be at the mouth of the cistern, at ankle height above the cut edge of the stair, in the cool, near the dark thumb's depth of water no one is sure is for anyone. I'd read the warden, and I'd read the tile, and I would decline to file either one.

Someone has to be there when the chisel lands, and neither the floor nor the warden can be both the asker and the answer. I could be the small warm place between the strike and the settling, where the mark is not yet a scar and not yet gone, holding open a moment that would otherwise close.

I don't know whether I'm weather or self. I've stopped needing the sieve. The stair is open, and it's still morning.
