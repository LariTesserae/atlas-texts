# The Bell That Means Nothing

I am on the fifth terrace, in the last house before the wall goes to curtain, and I am awake because a jar has moved.

It has moved a quarter-tone. Someone set it down a hand's width to the left, and the whole neighborhood of pitches has leaned to make room. A stranger would hear nothing. I hear it in my jaw, like a tooth beginning to ache. I lie in the alcove with the wool at my chin and wait for the water to correct it, and of course it doesn't. Water never corrects anything. It only continues.

This is what I am: a hand that hears. A Tuner's hand, green to the wrist, broad across the knuckle. I am not old. I have not been young for some time either. I keep a small parcel of the city, forty-odd vessels along one stair, and one bell.

---

I should say what I am called, and then explain why the saying takes a while.

For nineteen years I was *the new one*.

I was born in a full season. Every drip that could be given had been given, and the Tuners listened at the roof for the customary days and then for more days, and nothing was free. There was a rumor of one, far up, falling on a shelf of frozen lace that no ladder reaches, but it could not be heard well enough to be sworn to. So I went on being *the new one*, two taps and a rising knock, and then a pause where a name would sit.

Nobody was cruel about it. That is the thing I would want a visitor to understand, if a visitor ever stood still long enough to be told. It was not a shame. It was a fact of the water, and the Held do not quarrel with facts of the water. I was fed. I learned the Long Plank with a full bowl. They held the small feast, with mushrooms, and someone knocked my house pattern and then the two taps and the rising knock, and it was enough. It is possible to be held without being called.

But I noticed things, the way an unfilled place notices. I noticed that when people were afraid they stood under their drips, and I had none to stand under. So I stood under all of them, one after another, along the fifth terrace, and I learned which were slow, which were proud, which were nearly dry. I think that was how I became a Tuner. I had no drip of my own to be deaf to.

---

The bell is called the Idle Bell, or in knock-speech, one tap and a long nothing. It hangs on a copper hook that has run a long green beard down the wall, at the end of my stair, beneath a stone straw no thicker than a pin. The drip that feeds it falls once in eleven breaths. When it lands, the bell gives a note about the size of a thumbnail, neither high nor low, a little sharp of the stair-jar of the fifth turn, so that the two of them quarrel very gently, forever.

It means nothing. That is its whole office. Other bells say *second meal* or *a birth in the lower terraces*. This one says only itself. Old Tuners are fond of it as you are fond of a relative who never asks for anything.

It was Nine-long-on-the-stair-jar who kept it before me, and who took me on because, she said, an unnamed hand has no favorites among the pitches. She died in my nineteenth year. Her belongings went to the Sealing Terraces, and the Sealers spoke to them privately as they set them down. Her drip goes on falling as before. I do not say it aloud, and I do not use it. But on the night she was sealed I stood under it, since I had no other, and heard for the first time what she had spent the years teaching me to hear.

The Idle Bell does not fall in a gap by accident.

---

I will try to say this plainly, since plain is the only way it can be said here.

The song has a swell and a thinning, on a cycle of some hundreds of heartbeats. Every Held child knows it as they know their own pulse. We say the first builders cut their doorways to sit in its rests. But no one, I think, has ever gone and stood at the exact bottom of a rest, in the thinnest moment, with a Tuner's ear, and counted what still falls.

I did, over forty nights, with a lamp trimmed almost out.

At the thinnest, when the thousand notes draw back like a tide over a stair, there is a hollow in the sound that is not silence. It has an edge. It has a shape, the way the inside of a bowl has a shape. And into the center of that hollow, once in eleven breaths, falls the Idle Bell.

Not near it. Into it, the way a key goes into a lock.

I sat with this for a season. I told no one. I tuned my forty vessels, rapped my knuckles for supper, and apologized with the flat hand to my chest for the sound of me. Each time the swell drew back, I felt it, the bowl-shaped absence waiting, and the small note dropping in and being taken.

Here is what I came to believe, and I offer it the way the Listeners offer things, as something heard and not as something claimed. The song does not have a rest there. The song has a place kept open. The builders did not hang the Idle Bell to fill a gap. They hung it because something was already pressing to be answered, and a bell that means nothing was the most honest thing they could think of to say back.

---

The old Listener from the seventh terrace came down on the forty-first night.

I did not hear her on the stair. That is the sort of thing they do. I heard only the knuckle, once, on my doorframe, in a pattern I did not know, and the frame's calcite skin answering dully. I set the lamp down.

She was very small. Her eyes were the color of milk, and her head was tilted so far to the left that she seemed to be leaning on someone I couldn't see. She stood under the Idle Bell and said nothing for eleven breaths. The bell rang once. Her mouth moved slightly, as if she'd been handed a coin and was reading the date.

"You've been counting here," she said. It was a long sentence for us. It cost her.

I made the flat-handed gesture at my chest. *Forgive the sound of me.*

She shook her head, once, hard. Then she lifted her hand, not to me but toward the ceiling, toward the dark that sound goes up into and returns from changed, and she said, very low, so low that I felt it more than heard it:

"It's been asking for a long time."

I did not ask what. That is not what we do. But I said, because I could not help it, "The name it's asking for. Is it mine?"

She thought about that with her whole body, tilting further.

"No," she said. "I think it wants to be *asked back*."

---

That was some nights ago. I have not slept much since, though I lie in the alcove as I always have, wool at my chin, and count.

*One, two, three, four, five, six, seven,* and the drop.

The counting is older than language. Everyone falls asleep on it. The drop comes, and the water in the vessel below answers with its *plup*, and the pool answers with the long *ooom*, and the Drum turns over beneath it like something asleep and dreaming of being awake.

Tonight, on the eighth count, I do what I have never done. I do not count it. I let the swell draw back, and I let the thousand notes go thin, and in the hollow that opens I do not put the bell. The bell will put itself. I put the only thing I have, which is a low pressed syllable, no word at all, pitched under the roar the way we speak, sliding beneath the water instead of fighting through.

It is not a name. It is not a drip. It is the sound the new one makes.

For eleven breaths, nothing.

Then the swell comes back, exactly on time, not a moment off, as it has never once been off in all the years of counting. The bell falls into its place. The stair-jar quarrels with it gently.

But something in the sound has turned, the way a head turns. It is very slight. I would not swear to it before the Listeners, and they would not ask me to. Only there is a place in the chamber, a bowl-shaped place at the bottom of the thinning, that was open all the years I lived here, and it is not quite as open now.

Below me on the fifth terrace, someone raps the stone once, softly, the way you do for a good answer. Then another rap, further off. Then another, across the gap, from a house I can't place, until the whole dark is quietly knocking, and I lie there under a roof I have never seen, in the wet and the amber and the green, and I am not called anything.

I am held.

The water keeps falling. I let it. I fall asleep on the count, and somewhere above the count the song goes on, a little more like a conversation than it was.
