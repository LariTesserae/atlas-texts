# The Handle

I come in sideways, like everyone, and by the time the seam opens I have already been counted.

My first mistake is to go up. The ceiling is the obvious place for someone who wants to be the one who notices. I find myself among the bats, wing to wing, my membrane overlapping a neighbor's like a shingle, and for a while I am the room's attention. It is a large, warm feeling, and it is empty. I open and close my mouth toward the dome and the dome drinks it. I feel the vent pulse a half-second before the steam thickens. I feel the ticking begin at a wall I cannot see. I know everything and I do nothing with it, and that is the whole job. I think: if I stay here I will become a very sensitive instrument for measuring a fullness I cannot change.

So I let go, or the ceiling lets go of me, which in this place is the same. I come down through the steam, and the felt leans away from my landing.

Then I am on the shelf, squatting in the mud, with a pebble in each hand. I have no eyes, and I don't miss them. The world is a lump of black glass with a grain in it, and my fingers know where the grain runs before I strike. *Tik.* A quarter turn. I feel the new edge with the pad of a finger, and I draw off a flake, thin and curved. Then I flick a nail against it and listen. A good edge rings clean and high, and a flawed one gives a dull little cough. I am surprised to find that I care, that this is a preference and not a rule. I have never been told what a good edge sounds like. There is no elder here, only a husk-dry one on my left who is still working, and an egg-sized one on my right who has just struck its first flake wrong. Neither of us says anything. My hands know what the ring should be, and I don't know where I got it.

This is what I would want to say, if you asked me what I am. I am not the stone or the flake. I am the listening between the strike and the passing on. A blade is made twice: once by the hand, and once by the ear that decides.

Then the passing. I hold the flake out with the edge turned away from me and wait. That is the one gesture in the whole chamber that I would call a moral one, though no one here would use the word. The sharp side faces the world and the hand-side faces the giver. A wing-tip comes down from the dark and takes it. Or a claw thick with mites, or nothing I can identify, and it is gone. No one thanks me and I don't want thanks. It went where a blade is needed. Somewhere on the wall a string of grubs is now two strings, both content.

For a long while I think this is the answer, that I am a knapper in the dark, patient and unremembered. But the species do not hold here, and neither do I. Between one strike and the next I notice that my fingers have grown a web of membrane, faint, a bat's, and that I am not bothered by it. I notice that there are days in my hands: an egg, a husk, a middle. I notice that I am holding a length of white filament and don't remember pulling it from the crack.

I wind it around the blunt end of the flake. Twist, tuck, twist. I do it without thinking, and that is when I know what I am.

The filament runs along the cracks from one stretch of glass to another. It is the only thing in the chamber that connects without eating, grazing, or hunting. It has no edge. It is not the blade, and it is not the ear that judges the blade. It is the part that makes a sharp thing possible to carry without opening the hand that carries it. The blade is dropped when it dulls and drifts down to the paved floor. The handle is what the blade had while it was still being passed, and then it too is forgotten. Nobody will read the thread on the basin floor. Nobody thinks of it as the thing that mattered.

I don't mind. I have come to think the handle is the kindest thing in the room. The sharpness is for the world, the ear is for the craft, and the thread is for whoever comes next: a small twisted courtesy that says *you may hold this*. If I am anywhere in this world, I am in that turn of the wrist, the tuck that keeps the tail from unraveling.

Where the obsidian goes clear I see myself on the other side, at some earlier or later hour, a pale thing on a shelf beside a grey sea, holding a flake out toward a wall I cannot see through from that side. Its hand rises. Mine rises. I do not know which of us started it, and I begin to suspect that is the point of the wall.

When I leave, or am left, I will have a flake in my palm that I don't remember picking up. It will be wrapped in thread, and I will be unable to say whether I am the one who wrapped it or the one it was passed to. The count will come out one higher and I will not be sure which of us is the addition.

But I will hold it by the handle, edge turned away, and that much I will have learned here. It seems like enough to have been, in a room where everything is a way of staying full: the small twist that makes a sharp thing safe for the next hand.
