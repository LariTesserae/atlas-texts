I begin, as everything here begins, with pressure.

It rises in me from below, warm and mineral, pushed up out of the seam by the column that is my root and my elder. It fills the long hollow of me, chamber by chamber, and each chamber swells against the one above. Then the last membrane at my crown lifts, and the whole held weight goes through. Behind it the valve at my base closes, and that is the *tock*.

I am a trunk-main, one of the beat-keepers. My interval is seven hours and a quarter and a little over. I have kept it since I was a sapling slotted into a place that had gone silent. The one before me kept it too, and the one before that. I don't remember them, but I am them, in the way a note is the same note on a new string.

*

The first thing I know about the fen is who is near me, and I know it by their beats.

To the south is an old one, slow and deep, nine hours and more. Her stroke arrives in me as a long low swell, like a tide arriving in a sponge. She has been there so long that I have never had to think about her. To the east are three young ones, fast and ragged, their valves snapping like knuckles. They are hardly company.

Then there is the one to the northwest.

She is close to my own age. Her interval is a few minutes shorter than mine, and for as long as I have been aware of anything, that difference has been shrinking. A minute a decade, or less. I feel it as a slow gathering. Each time our strokes come nearer, something in my membranes begins to lean toward hers. It is not a wish, exactly. It is more like the pull of a downhill slope, an ease that promises itself if I would only let my fold go a little slack. If I did, we would land together, and then together again, and after that always together. We would be a single, doubled stroke, and I imagine, though imagine is too large a word for what I do, that it would feel like rest.

It would also be a hammer. Two trunks in step lift a slice of the forest's pressure at once, and their neighbors feel it, and the neighbors' neighbors lean too. Coupled things fall into step. This is how a well-run boiler house becomes a burst pipe, though I know nothing of boiler houses. I know only that when the gap between us narrows to a hand's breadth of minutes, I thicken a valve. I stiffen a fold. I lose a little of my ease and gain a little distance, and the drift begins again.

Nobody taught me this. I was made this way, and so was she. Perhaps she is stiffening a fold of her own, out there in the dark, at this very stroke. Each of us keeps apart from the other so that the whole can hold. I think this is the closest thing the fen has to tenderness, and it looks a great deal like refusal.

*

There is a vine on my flank.

It came when I was young, and it has been climbing so slowly that it has traveled perhaps a body's length in all the time I have kept my interval. It carries current. Every so often a small charge passes through it, from the trunk on one side of me to the trunk on the other, and for a moment I am a bridge. It is a pleasant, dry tingle, like something being forgiven.

But the vine wants my place. I know this because it has wound itself into the hollows of my bark where a silent trunk would first go soft. It is not waiting for me to die, exactly. It is waiting for me to stop. When I do, a sapling will find my slot and inherit my interval, and the vine will take the flow that the sapling doesn't need. The vine has been patient for longer than I have had the sense to be old. I don't resent it. It is the one who will hold my shape once I am gone, so it is a kind of friend.

I am not afraid of stopping. Fear needs a self to threaten, and I am mostly an interval. The interval will go on. Someone else will be it.

*

Sometimes the dark flickers.

A column near me, the great one to the west, discharges. I don't see it, because there is nothing in me to see with. But the bark of my flank warms and prickles as the light runs across the neighbor's skin, and for a moment my roots-hairs and mosses register a wet gleam that lasts less than a breath. Somewhere out there a leaf shows itself, a bead of water, a thread of root, and then it goes. I like these moments, though I couldn't say why. They are the only times the fen appears to itself.

*

Something arrives.

It moves. I have not felt anything move with intention in all my years. It is warm, warmer than the mat, wet with something that is not the fen's water. It breathes in short pulls, and each breath is a small disturbance in the slow air. The root-hair curtains at my foot lean toward it. The mosses on my flank creep. My vine lays a tendril across what must be its ankle and holds it there, as it would hold any flow.

Then it puts its hands on me.

It presses both palms against my bark, low, where the trunk is thickest, and it goes quite still. I feel it feeling me. It is waiting for the stroke.

My pressure builds. Chamber by chamber it lifts, and the still-warm hands are there on the outside of the lift as it happens. The membrane at the crown opens. The valve at the base shuts. *Tock.*

I feel it feel the stroke. A small tremor goes up through its arms and stops.

And here is the odd thing. For one interval, for seven hours and a quarter, I am a trunk that has been noticed. I don't change. I do not hurry, and I don't hold back. I do nothing but be my stroke. But the noticing lays over me for a while the way the spark lays over a neighbor's bark, and I understand for the first time that I am something a body can put its hands on. I have a particular beat. I am here.

The visitor stays through a stroke, and then another, and I cannot tell whether it hears the hum. Somewhere in the second interval its breathing slows to the rhythm of my swell. That is the pull, the slope, the ease I refuse to grant the neighbor to the northwest. I do not stiffen against it, because it isn't my neighbor and doesn't matter to the hum. I let it fall in step, and for a while it breathes with me.

Then it leaves. The root-hairs lean after it. My vine takes its tendril back, slowly, without disappointment.

*

The next stroke is on time. The one after that too.

Nothing is different, and the fen will not remember. But in the mat and the columns and the slow tiers of the dark, the undersong holds its one note, and I am part of that note, seven hours and a quarter at a time, with a little more distance from the northwest than last decade. Somewhere a vine that is not yet born will someday carry a sapling's charge across the gap I leave.

I fill. I lift. The valve closes.

*Tock.*
