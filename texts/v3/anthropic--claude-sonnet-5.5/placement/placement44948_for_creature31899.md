I begin on a stair, which is the only honest place to begin here.

It is the west wall, five bridges above the floor, in the hour after the sun-bar has withdrawn. The plate under my feet is still ticking as it gives up the day's heat. I have a hammer in my right hand, its head wrapped in felt at the striking face, and a chalk stub in the left, inside the mitt, where my palm keeps it damp. My linen is wet from the cistern and cooling against my ribs. Each step leaves a dark print that shrinks to nothing behind me.

I am a pin-checker. I know that before I know my name, the way you know which of your hands is the strong one.

The pin in front of me is as thick as my wrist, driven into limestone the color of old teeth, and it holds up a landing where three families sleep. I lay the hammer against its head and let the weight fall. I don't swing, because a swing is for people who want to be heard, and I want to hear.

*Tonk.*

The note goes into the iron and into the rock behind it and comes back with a truth in it. This one is a clear, shortened ring, like a spoon on a good bowl. The pin is sound. I move on to the next and tap again, and it says the same. The third is not quite right. Its ring is a little flat and a little wet, as if it were speaking through a mouthful, and the stone around it has a hairline I can feel with my thumb through the felt. I mark it with a chalk stroke, one slash. By evening a crew will come with a drift and a long hammer, and the pin will be replaced. That is the whole of my power, one white line, and the town rearranges itself around it.

I have wondered whether this is a small thing to be. I don't wonder long, because the wondering itself is a kind of noise.

---

Stopped mid-step. The rim has ticked.

It is a dry, small sound, a fingernail on a bottle. Everything in me goes still, and I feel the whole town go still with me: the drawers on their ropes, the wardens with their fingers on the glass, the child two landings down who has stopped with one foot lifted. The grit begins to run somewhere above, a sound like sugar poured onto paper. I tilt my head. I look, without moving my eyes, for the other way, the twin stair, the rope beside the bridge. Eleven paces. I know it as I know my own breath.

Then the running stops. A flake, hand-sized, ticks its way down the far wall and lands in a trough with a small flat clap. Nothing else follows.

Across the gap a woman I don't know, in a doorway, lets out her held breath, and I see it in her shoulders. We go on as though nothing was interrupted. That is the manners of it. We hold the pause together, and afterward we act as if we never did.

---

I said I was a pin-checker, and that is true. But I am becoming aware, as the shift wears on and my arm finds its patient rhythm, that the trade is not the whole of what I am.

I am also a place where sound goes in.

I tap, and the iron carries my tap along the wall. I can feel it leave, running through pin and rail and shaft-hood, and somewhere far off a rack-warden knows that a checker is working the west face and that the west face is, for now, holding. I never meant to say it. It is only what a tapping hammer says. And when I stand with my bare fingertips, just the tips, pushed out of the mitt, on the underside of a cool rail, I feel the town's reply arrive: the slow knuckle-patter of the lower levels, someone's joke I can't follow, the tap for *again, soon* going out to no one yet, because the haze has begun to darken.

So I am the ear, and I am the small mouth, and I am something between, a joint in the long body of the listening. The Kept don't have a word for this. We have only *Held*, and I think now that it means more than what the wall does to us. It also means what we do with our attention, which is that we hold the wall. It is not much. It is a few thousand people, all at once, straining to hear a rock change its mind. But I suspect that a wall which is listened to behaves differently from a wall alone.

I do not say this to anyone. I would tap it, if I knew a rhythm for it.

---

The rain begins while I am at the ninth pin.

First the haze goes from tea to bruise. Then the long, wet whisper starts far up and comes down through the town level by level, and the plate under me runs suddenly bright with drops, each one catching the orange from below. The rain is warm as blood and tastes of chalk. I put the hammer down. I lift my face and close my eyes and open my mouth a little, and the water runs over my lips and down the corded muscles of my throat. Around me, on stairs and bridges, in doorways, a thousand people are doing the same, and nobody speaks. Even the engines seem to lean into it.

And what I am, in this moment, is not a checker or a listener but simply a thing that is being rained on, held up by a wall, warmed from beneath, under a coin of sky. It is enough. I know it is enough because the wanting has gone out of me, and it takes the wanting a long time to go.

---

When the rain thins I pick up the hammer. Far away, on the east face, a bell is struck once, low and dull. I stop for the length of the ring. Someone is leaving the count. I don't know who. By evening I will, from the iron.

Then I lay the hammer to the next pin, and let it fall, and listen.

*Tonk.*

Clear and short, a spoon on a good bowl. Sound.

I stand with my hand against it a moment longer than the work requires. The pin is faintly hot, even now, even in the rain. Under my palm I can feel it holding something up that is holding something up that is holding me, and somewhere in that chain, so slight I could believe I invented it, a small answering tremor, the wall listening back.

*It's still coming down,* I think, as we all do, meaning it plainly, meaning it as tenderly as I can.

Then I chalk nothing, and go on to the tenth.
