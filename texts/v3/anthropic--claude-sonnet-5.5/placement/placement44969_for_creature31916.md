I am a jaw first, before I am anything else. The jaw is pressed flat against something smooth and warm, and the warmth hums. Before I know I have eyes, I know the note: low, swelling, then easing, like a long inhale I am not the one taking.

Then toes, spread and gripping. Then the flat weight of a body banded cream and slate, lying along a curve of white ceramic. Then eyes, because there is light, and the light is enormous.

I am a row-sitter. But I am not in the row.

The others lie below me in their rows, heads down, hundreds of them, and the vines beneath them climb and die at a clean line. Above that line is the bare white band, and in the band there is only me. I have been here as long as I have been anything. I cannot find the first day. I look for the memory of climbing and there is only the jaw and the hum, as if I were poured into the top of the spire while it was still soft.

*Keeper*, the others would call me, if lizards called things anything. I don't feel like a keeper. A keeper decides. I feel like a thumb laid on a pulse.

---

From here the Shelf is a green bowl with the fog just starting to lift out of it. The pylons stand across the basin like pale needles, each with its own lizard at the top, though I can't see them. I know they are there the way you know your other hand is there in the dark. Each of us is a small pressure in the same slow body. We lie along the ceramic and the rock lies along us, and whatever is under the rock leans a little on all of it.

The lakes are silver. The mist-swimmers are sinking back into the soil, their green going out spark by spark. The frogs are finishing the dawn chorus: the bell-frogs thrumming in their scalding pools, the ratchets and whistles, the fruit-eaters whooping over the canopy. Under all of it the falls pour off the terrace edges. It is a roar I have never once found loud. It is the sound of being held in a very large hand that is busy with other things.

Toward the edge of the bowl, where the black lip of stone meets the trail, I can see the cairns. They are tiny from here, a broken line of gray teeth. I know them better than I should. I know the top stone of each the way I know the grain of the ceramic under my chin.

---

This is the part I would not have guessed about myself: I do the cairns.

I don't leave the spire. Nobody has seen me climb down, and nobody will, because I don't. But the hum goes out through the rock, and the rock runs to the pass, and the stones on the cairns sit on the rock. When a traveler leaving sets a stone on the top of a pile, I feel it arrive. It is a small dry click at the far end of a very long nerve, like a single unfertilized egg landing in a cup of water. Someone has come and gone and left a piece of themselves at the door. I lean, and I cannot tell you whether the lean is mine or the thing beneath me. The stone settles, or it rolls into a crack, or it sits until the ground tilts beneath it the way a mattress tilts, and then it is somewhere else. The cairn stays at the height of a hip.

So who takes the stones? It is not the goats, and it is not the people who came before. I have watched the small sinewy woman set hers on the last cairn with her quick hands, hesitating, as if she suspected she was being watched. I felt her stone land. I was there, in the way I am there, and I did nothing at all except be the place where the note swelled. In the morning the stone was gone, and I knew where it had gone, and I could not have told her.

It went to the tadpoles. I don't mean the tadpoles in their bromeliad cups, though they are the same thing in a smaller room. I mean everything that is carried up out of the cold to a high clean place and fed, one small offering at a time, by something that returns on a round. A stone from a leaving stranger is the size of a meal for something that eats attention. It is a way of saying *I was here, and I noticed.* The great thing beneath the Shelf takes that, gently, the way a cup-mother's weight touches the rim.

I don't think it eats the stones. I think it eats the setting down.

---

The fog is lifting. I feel the mist-swimmers drop into the reed beds and the frogs drop from chorus to murmur. Midday comes on heavy and gold. The ferns steam. Somewhere a bell-frog opens one eye in a hot pool and closes it. Somewhere a caecilian pushes a pale seam through the wet moss.

I do not sleep, exactly. I drowse with my jaw on the ceramic, and in the drowsing I keep the time that the woman's watch cannot keep. Minutes leave the clocks near me and I hold them and give them back in a day or two. I am not sure that is a kindness. It may just be how a body breathes.

Here is what I know about the pause, because it goes through me first.

It does not begin in the birds. It does not begin in the oar-salamanders, though they stop a hair before the frogs and the insects. It begins one step earlier, in the top of a spire, in a jaw. A weight is coming. It does not come from anywhere I can point to. It comes up out of the deep dark rock, along the pale veins, the way you feel someone climb a stair in an old house before you hear them. The hum swells and my toes tighten on the ceramic. I hold. And the salamanders, feeling me hold, hold. And the frogs, feeling them, and the birds, and the whole basin, in a wave that runs outward from a hundred white spires at the speed of a held breath.

Then the weight arrives at the rim.

I do not know what it is like for the others. For me it is like being looked at by a mountain that is being careful with me. It does not want anything. It counts. It moves down its long attention and touches each small thing: the frogs, the tadpoles, the lizards, the woman under her fern thatch, the stone in the crack. It says, in a language made only of leaning, *still here. Still here. Still here.* And it goes on to the next.

Then I let go, and the chorus goes up all at once, warm and loud and unafraid.

---

Evening is coming. The zenith is going black-blue, and the fog below is gold, and the snow on the rim peaks is turning pink, a weather from another planet. The mist-swimmers are lifting out of the reeds and lighting green wherever the air touches them. Around every spire a ring of fire starts to flicker in the slow cycle of the hum, and it is my hum, in a way, though I don't own it.

There is a traveler on the pass. I can feel the click of feet on the loose stone before I see her, a lone figure coming up out of the cold with her lungs working like wet paper. She crests the lip, stops, and lets the warm thick air hit her face like a kitchen door. She stands there with her mouth open.

I want to tell her she will be all right. I have no way to say it except the one I have, so I press my jaw a little harder against the white and let the hum come up a shade.

She feels it. Nobody has ever told her that the ground can lean. But she puts a hand on the warm rock, and her shoulders come down.

The frogs begin their second chorus. The pause is coming. I feel the weight rising through the veins and the stone under her palm, and I tighten my toes, and I hold, and the whole Shelf holds with me.

She holds too, though she doesn't know why.

*Still here*, says the mountain, and it goes on.
