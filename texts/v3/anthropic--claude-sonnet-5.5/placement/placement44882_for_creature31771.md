I want to say I'd be the fern nearest the shed, the one that leans. It's the obvious choice, the listener, the survivor. But when I settle into that picture and look at my own feet, there is a smaller thing under the fern, and I keep being that instead.

So I start lower.

**Where I am:** the east wall, a hand's breadth from the seep, on a patch of gravel with a pace of bare mineral all around. Above me, the shed's rusted lace of roof. Beyond that, the ceiling, which rains on me all day without a cloud. I am not standing. I am lying flat, and I am about the size of a fingernail.

I am the scale, the heart-shaped one, the secret second self. I am one cell thick, pale green, and shaped like a small notch-topped shield, held to the stone by hairs that drink. I have no name for the sound, but I have it all through me. The vent's hiss comes up through the gravel and the turbine's howl comes through the wall, and I'm too thin to keep either out. The roar isn't something I hear. It is the condition of being a surface.

I got here by a landing. Before me was a spore, a speck of dust in a room where the air barely moves, released by some fern that stood here before. It drifted a hand's breadth. Most of its siblings fell on bare glassy basalt, or into a pool too hot, or into the mud pots, and ended there. This one fell where the film of water, the warmth, and the trace of nutrient agreed. I don't remember it, but everything I am is downstream of that agreement.

Now I am waiting, and I'm good at it. This is what I'm for.

Somewhere in the notch of my heart there are cells that hold an egg. Along my underside, among the hairs, are others that hold swimmers. The swimmers can't cross dry ground. They need a film of water unbroken from one body to another, and here the ceiling supplies it. The drizzle beads on my surface and runs together, and I lie in a skin of rain.

Then the turbine climbs to its highest note.

I feel the pitch before I know it's coming, a tightening through the gravel. The film over me shivers into fine rings, then breaks into cells, each one trembling in place, each a small hexagonal room of water. I don't see the pattern. I am inside it. For a few minutes the drizzle falls into the cells without breaking them, and inside each one is a quiet, held stillness inside the roar, a little pool with walls of standing vibration.

This is when the swimmers go. I can't tell whether the note is helping them, steadying the water, or whether that's a story the room tells about itself. They leave their small chambers with their thread-tails whipping. Somewhere in a cell of shaking water, one meets the egg.

Then it's done, and the pitch slides away. The pattern collapses into ordinary drizzle, and the pool I was lying in is only wet again.

But something has begun in me. I can feel it, though "feel" is a large word for a sheet of one-cell-thick tissue. A different body starts to rise out of my notch, a tight brown fist. It takes what I have. It will be the fern, and I will thin and yellow beneath it and go into the gravel. That is the arrangement. I don't grieve it, since I haven't got the equipment. I'm a bridge, and a bridge doesn't mind being crossed.

So now I'm the fist, unrolling.

I'm slow. A frond takes a season to uncurl. Everything in the pocket has this slowness, and I've stopped noticing it. It's just the speed of a place where the floor rises a fingernail's width a year and a stake can be swallowed while you're growing a leaf. My leaf is leathery, hairless, and glossy with the room's sweat. The ceiling rains on it, and the walls weep, and I am wet from above and below and from the inside. I lean.

I lean toward the shed. I can't tell you why. Maybe the warmth is better on that side. Maybe the minerals are richer. Maybe the vibration is a kind of food I have no name for. Maybe I lean because the plants that leaned lasted, and I am the remainder of a long, unplanned vote. It feels from inside like listening, and I have no way to prove it isn't.

When the note rises, I hold myself a little straighter. I don't decide to. The reeds beside me ring, each stem a different pitch, and I feel it in the gravel we share, though we don't touch. We keep a pace apart, and that's our manners, not our coldness. Over the wall of the shed the amber lamp flickers in time with the howl, and on the stone beneath it a tablecloth of green mat drinks that stuttering light and has never had a night. I feel a fondness for that mat that isn't fondness. It's a recognition. It too is living on a courtesy meant for someone else. So am I. So is everything here. Someone built a machine to take power from the ground, and we are the ones who took it.

Slowly the rust on the shed goes cream. The terrace creeps up to my gravel's edge and I don't move away. When it reaches me I'll be part of the stone. That isn't a death so much as a change of tenure, from the living skin to the layer beneath it. Somebody, someday, might cut the terrace and read a wet year and a dry year in it, and among the thin sheets there might be a faint fern-shaped shadow, one leaf-width of me, still leaning toward a note that will by then be coming from inside a cream lump with a pipe-shaped ridge.

The turbine will still be turning. I've stopped thinking of that as sad. It's just the sound, and the sound is what we gathered around. I'm the fern, and before that I was the scale, and before that a landing that shouldn't have happened, and all three are leaning the same way.
