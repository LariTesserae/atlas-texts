I find myself first as a pressure on one side, and I take the pressure for the world. Then a wetness under me, and I take the wetness for myself. Only after a while, and I have a great deal of while, do the two separate, so that there is a surface and something moving along it.

The surface is the north wall, a hand's height above the warm channel. I am a snail, and my shell is tall and the color of weak tea.

*

I don't hear the hum. I want to be honest about that, because a visitor would assume I do, and would pity me or envy me. The hum is not a thing that happens to me. It is what my shell is made of. When my foot lifts and sets, lifts and sets, the spiral above me rings very faintly, the way a bowl rings when a finger circles its rim. If I stopped, it would still ring. If the turbine stopped, I do not know what I would be. I have never had to consider it, and the turbine has never stopped.

My road goes from the drip-basin to the seam of moss and back. I did not choose it, any more than you chose the shape of your first word. The concrete is worn there in a glossy groove the width of my foot, and my foot fits it the way a hand fits a handrail that a thousand hands have polished. I feel the groove before I taste the algae in it. Going out along it I lean a little left. Coming back I lean a little right. It is the only argument I have ever had, and it is settled.

The drip-basin is my beginning. Water falls into it from the same point on the ceiling rib, and the basin holds the same depth it has always held. I touch it with the tip of my body and drink, and that is the first of the two moments in my circuit that I would call joy, if I had a word for joy. The moss is the second. It is so thick that I go into it as into a low green weather and eat the fine film between the shoots. Then I turn and lean right, and the basin is waiting.

*

Around me the others keep their circuits. There is a snail some paces off along the wall, and I have never met her, though her road and mine are nearly close enough to share a stone. I know her by the small change in the tremor of the wall where she is. There is the fish in the channel below, swimming down and back, and when it passes the place where I hang above the water I feel a slow bending in the current, twenty paces of patient bending, and it is like being glanced at by something that has no intention of stopping.

And there are the frogs. I cannot see them from where I am, but the ferns are close, and the ferns carry their talk. Now and then the general shiver of the wall catches a knot in it, a stutter that runs through the stone from the roots, and I feel it in my foot. It passes under me on its way somewhere else. It was not meant for me. I like it anyway. It is the only thing in the room that is *about* something, and I have never learned what.

*

Here is what I have noticed, in the long time I have had to notice it. The room says it has finished. I have felt this said in the way the stone holds still and the drips fall from the same points. The rust has finished rusting, the seep and the stone have reached their truce, and nothing here is ruined because nothing changes.

But my groove is deeper than it was.

Not by much. By less than you could measure, less than a grain of the concrete's own dust. But I know the lean at the turn, and the lean was once shallower. My foot goes down a little further than my foot's mother's foot went down. The stair outside was worn into a gutter by feet, and I am a foot. All the room's finishing is real, and I am still the small unfinished thing inside it, a slow rasp of mucus and appetite, taking the wall a little at a time and giving nothing back but the track. If the truce ever breaks, it will break at a snail road. That is a strange thing to carry, and I carry it lightly, because it is so slow that it will not happen in any time I am able to be afraid of.

*

Once, something lifted me.

There was a warmth and a pressure that was not the wall, and then no wall at all, only air, which is a thing I had never met. The hum went on in my shell, but it had a new note in it: fingers, buzzing with what I was buzzing with. For a moment I understood I was being held by another creature that could feel the room the way I do, and that in its hand I was the whole chamber made small, the turbine and the water and the long slow sound, ringing at the size of a thumb.

Then it put me back. It put me back almost exactly, a little too far to the left, so that the groove was a hair's width away from my foot. I stayed still. Then, slowly, I leaned, and the groove took me in again.

I think about that hand sometimes, when I am at the moss. It stood in the room for a while, and I could feel its weight in the floor, and its steps began to fall in a pattern, the same pattern each time. Then it went up the trough of the stair and out into the marsh, where everything is humming and does not know it. Maybe it is still walking a circuit somewhere. Maybe every road is a groove, and the whole wet country is a great worn stone, and we are all leaning left, and leaning right, and being carried by the note that we are made of.

I reach the moss. I go in.

The basin is waiting.
