I start in the wrong place. For a while I think I am a bellwright, wedged in a dissolved bowl with the shell taut on my back, and it is wonderful. There is one note in me and the bowl gives it back deeper, and ten thousand others are giving theirs to their own bowls, and none of us is anyone. I am a pressure and a pitch. I am a small share of a weather. But a bellwright can't wonder what it is, and I keep wondering, so that isn't it. I climb out of the bowl, still ringing faintly, and the wet stone sighs under me.

I walk a long time in dusk that never changes. The smear of light is somewhere overhead, going about its business. Behind me my prints glow pale and fade. Ahead there is only the clean line of the horizon and, breaking it, one knuckle of lichen-white stone no higher than a house. I go toward it because it is the only thing that is not flat.

On its flank I find the carved plate, and I understand that I am a knocker.

I know it first in the forearms, thick and corded, and then in the two fingers of my right hand, which are tapping against my thigh without my having asked them to. I am at a relay. This is not my band's knuckle. It sits midway between two camps that could never reach each other alone, and my work is to be the place where a message is set down for the length of a breath before it goes on. Someone far to the east strikes a row of cups. I put my palm flat on the third plate and my sternum against the stone and feel it arrive, a clear dry knock coming up through the roar like a fish through murky water. I don't hear it. Nobody hears anything here. I receive it with the whole front of my body, then take up the black flint hammerstone, worn to the shape of my grip by hands older than my grandmothers, and strike it west.

I could be nobody else. That is the odd comfort of the work. I am not the author. A woman I will never meet says *water* on the seventh cup, and a man I will never meet gets *water*, and I have passed through between them like light through a pool. But I am not empty either. The messages leave a residue in the hands. I know, from the rhythm alone, when the far knocker is tired, when she is afraid, when she has given up on brevity because what she has to say does not compress. The stone carries a person's hurry. I have never met most of the people I know best.

Then this. The east knocker is sending a phrase I know, *my daughter is*, and the strokes come clean and even, and then they stop.

It is not the pause of a knocker resting. I have felt those, the little breath between phrases. This is the rhythm broken off. My two fingers, tapping my thigh, stop with it, and that wakes me from a sleep I hadn't known I was in. My skin goes cold before I have thought anything at all. Through the plate, through the soles of my feet, I feel it far off and to the east. The chorus is thinning to a ragged edge, and the skirl-eels have gone dead on the far side of it. A hole is sliding across the flat, wearing the sound away as it goes.

Now comes the real question, the one the trade exists to answer. The unfinished message is in my hands. *My daughter is.* If I strike it west as I received it, the ending is missing and the people to the west will fill it in with their own worst guess, or their kindest. If I finish it myself I become an author, and I am not one. A knocker's whole honesty is that what goes in is what comes out.

So I do the only true thing. I strike *my daughter is* and then I lay the flat of my hand across the plate and kill the ring. I hold the stone dead. It is the reverse of the old blessing, the deepest plate left to ring and ring. This is a plate silenced on purpose, and everyone who reads it will understand: a palm laid over a mouth, then not withdrawn. I keep my hand there longer than is comfortable. The stone is cold and wet, and a thin sheet of glowing water creeps up against my wrist.

*Something took the sound here,* the silence says. *I do not know the rest. I am not going to invent it.*

I keep the hand there and think, with a clarity that surprises me, that this may be what I am: not the noise and not the hush, but the one who can carry the shape of a gap without filling it in.

After a while the west answers. Someone I will never meet strikes one low stroke on the deepest plate and holds it with a flat hand so it rings and rings. *The floor will hold you.* It is not an answer to the question. It is the only thing anyone can say. I take it in through my palm and my breastbone and turn to strike it east, across the dead place and out toward whoever is still there to feel it.

Somewhere in that hour the light-smear crosses over the knuckle. The tide is coming. I can tell by the chorus, which has dropped half a tone so gently I could believe I imagined it. Soon the sheets will thicken, the mat will blaze from horizon to horizon, and the bands will crowd onto the crowns. The knockers will sit together on the great plate for three days and trade everything. Someone will tell me how the message ended, and I will learn what the silence was, whether it was a death or only a husher passing by. I'll carry that too, one more residue in the hands.

For now I sit with my back against the knuckle's pale stone. My fingers have gone back to tapping my thigh in the old steady rhythm that keeps me awake. Far off, in every direction, the bowls are singing. I feel it in my ribs, the one tone I would know blind, and I can't say whether it is a bowl I own or only one I have always been near. I lean my forehead against the wet plate, the way you would against someone's shoulder, and hum.

It is the only thing I can't relay. But the stone takes it anyway, and carries it a little way into the dark.

*Still here.*
