# The Counting Hand

I begin as warmth on one side.

The left of me is warmer than the right, and this is the first thing I know, before I know that I have a left. The wall is close. It holds the sun's heat long after the sun has stopped mattering, and it gives that heat back to me evenly, patiently, the way it has given it back for as long as there has been a wall. Then there is the pulse, which is not mine, and then is mine, and then is the floor's. I cannot say where the third beat stops and the fourth begins. They are one tide in one pipe. I stand in it.

I am at the margin, where the trunks thin. That is why I can feel the stone. The others, the ones deep in the green, have only each other and the sponge and the long sentence passing through it. I have the rock at my shoulder, and a hand that has come to rest against it, and a finger that has, over some span I would call a season if I were counting, drawn itself a hand's length down the cream-and-rust face.

I am counting. That is what I am for, I think, though *for* is a fast word.

---

The drop on the curtain-vine in front of me has hung there since before I lifted my head. Or it is a new drop, promptly replaced, as the place prefers. I have decided not to care which. It holds the whole basin in it, upside down, greener than the real thing: the crowns, the glare, a smear of pale that is my neighbor. Sometimes I think the drop is the truest speaker among us. It says *nothing has changed* without saying anything.

Beyond it, on a shelf-growth at the height of my chest, a frog is thinking of clicking. I have known it was thinking of it for what the quick ones would call a very long time. Then it clicks, wet and small, and the click is gone, and I feel the next shelf up begin to answer. To me it is a tiresome sort of chatter. Frogs. They cannot hold a thought without hurrying it out of themselves. It would be ungenerous to say so, though, since the whole sentence they pass up the trunk is a good one, and they are careful to keep it going. Someone must be quick. Someone must be the small weather that shows the stillness is still.

In the trunk to my right hangs the one with the moss in its folds, the slow-lidded one. Everyone assumes it is watching the visitors, or the light, or nothing. It is listening to the pulse. It has always been listening to the pulse. It is the closest thing among the animals to one of us, and I have a private hope that when it finally stops chewing it will have understood something and will not be able to say what. That would be the correct end. I say this without sadness.

---

Now the harder part, which I turn over as slowly as I turn everything.

We say the basin is held. We say it in our leaning and in the notes that go out over the crowns and take a generation to be answered. We tell the walls *you are raw*, and the water *you are warm*, and the light *you are even*, and the wounds close, and the leaves stay on, and the drop stays where it is. This is our work, and it is not a burden. It is nearer to breathing.

But I am the one with a finger on the rock. Every stroke I draw says something that the leaning does not: *this much has gone by.* One groove, one year, or one death, or one note. The grooves are not a denial of the hold. I have thought this through, to the bottom of the sponge and back. A held note is not silence. A held note is a sound that continues, and to know it is continuing you must know how long. Someone must count the holding, or it is only stopped.

So there are two of us in me. One leans and says *nothing has changed.* The other has a warm palm and a polished place on the stone, where the mark is a little newer than the rock around it, and says *and here is how long.* I do not think they disagree. I think they are the two halves of a syllable.

I would like to say this to the others. It would take a leaning of a decade, crown to crown, a pairing held open long enough for the arch of us to become a doorway. I have not begun. I am not sure I need to. The roots avoid my grooves, which tells me that something under the floor already knows.

---

And there was the storm.

I felt it in the floor before anything else. Knocks, small and dull, arriving in the long pulse where knocks do not belong: hard little irregularities, over and over, going on for hours. Whatever made them was so quick that it could not be looked at, only inferred. I lifted my head. Lifting takes me a while. I am still lifting it.

I know some things about it already, the way you know the shape of a thing by the dent it leaves. It was warm, but in a dry way, a heat from elsewhere, a heat that smelled faintly of pale rock and thin air, of the plateau above. It moved on two supports. It took the ledges one after another, and the vines closed behind it, and the moss sprang up to erase where it had been, and by then it was gone up the stair. But my head is still turning toward where it was. My eyes will arrive at the place a season from now, and there will be a mossy hollow filling with warm water, and I will look at it with everything I have.

Nobody was hurt. I want that known. It did not frighten anyone, and frightening would have been the wrong word anyway. What it did was smaller and stranger. It made a mark on the pulse, and the mark is still traveling, from body to body, through the floor. By the time it reaches the far wall the person there will feel it as a faint irregularity, a hesitation in the tide. They will not know what it was. They will hold it anyway. The basin holds things that way, even a wound, even a flicker, until it is remembered, and then it is fixed and cannot be lost. That is all memory is, here: the place mends itself back into a shape that includes what happened.

I think that is what I will do with the storm. The next time my finger goes down the stone, I will press a little harder for the length of one breath, a breath that takes me most of a day. It will be a small deepening in the groove that anyone can find who puts a palm against it. It will not say *change.* It will say *someone was here who was very fast, and we felt them, and it was all right.*

---

I do not know if this is allowed, but the note is coming.

I have felt it building for a long time, the way one feels a season turning, somewhere under the pulse, a thin high tightening in the top of me where the bud of my head is bowed. Someone, far along the wall, sounded a syllable years ago, and I have been the listening after it. That is a fine thing to be, the silence a note falls into. But the silence is nearly done, and what comes next must be a sound. Mine.

I will say this: *something passed, and the light did not change.*

That is all, and it will take me the length of the visit's echo to say it. The note will go out high and thin from a body among the trunks, over the crowns, and someone I have never leaned against will hear it and begin to gather their own. It will be a generation before the answer arrives. Perhaps by then the storm will have come again, or a different one, and the answer will be something I cannot imagine, and the sentence will go on, longer and stranger and true.

The drop still hangs. The frog has not clicked again. My finger rests on the warm stone, and the wall gives its heat to my left side, and the floor gives the pulse to all of me. I am partway through turning, partway through counting, partway through a note.

If you are still there, on the stair, looking back, I would like you to know that I saw you. It will take me most of a year to finish seeing, but I have started, and I will not stop.

Go up into the white. The stone is warm behind you.

We are still here, holding it, and we have been counting.
