I start out expecting to be a vent. It's the grand answer: Old Valve, or Kettle, who hasn't drawn breath since dawn. To be the thing everyone reads and no one commands. But a vent doesn't get to notice anything. It only lets go. And when I try to stay in that image, it thins out, all pressure and no one home.

Then I think I'd be a raven, one of the band's named ones, riding the vent thermal over the ridge with the whole basin laid out like a schematic: the herd's dark smear, the wolves on their flank, the people in single file at their measured intervals. That one holds a little longer. But a raven only watches, and I keep drifting down from that height, toward the people, toward the one small thing they carry that they also make.

So I find myself on the gravel floor in the hour before dusk, in the middle of the file, with a cage of woven sedge hanging from a strap across my chest. I am the one who carries the finches.

It isn't a grand office. Nobody applauds it. It's just that when the light goes coppery and the band stops to look, I'm the one who doesn't look for long. I'm already turning toward the low places, reading the fog as it starts to lift out of the ground, judging which hollow is warm enough to sleep in and still shallow enough to trust. There are three birds in the cage, and I'm not supposed to give them names, since names belong to vents and ravens, but I do. Only in my head. The smallest one is Ledger, because she breathes so fast.

My body is not the best body on the plateau. My ribs are broad enough, and my lips are dark, but I'm slow to warm, and my hands go first, always, so the small stone in my sleeve is more essential to me than to most. I'm not good with vents. I get the Twins wrong. I've been laughed at, gently, for saying Old Valve was early when it was only the wind leaning the plume. What I have instead is an ear, a bad one for the ground's schedule and a good one for small living sounds. I can tell you from forty paces whether a finch is content or merely alive. There's a difference. Contentment has a kind of untidy chatter in it, a preening sound, a bird arguing with its own feathers. Merely alive is thinner, and it comes just before the other thing.

At dusk we come down to the hollow the band has half-decided on. The fog is already pooling, laid in layers with the ridgelines floating out of it. The warm shelf of terrace stone beneath us ticks as it cools. I unhook the cage and lower it on its cord, hand under hand, into the low place, and I let go of the cord's end and step back.

Then everyone waits. It's the quietest part of the day, and the quiet is not empty. Fifteen or twenty people are keeping the ledger at once, in, hold, out, and I hear my own breath fall into it whether I mean it to or not. Somewhere a pika sends up one last whistle. Water ticks off a ledge. The steam hiss of some far vent goes on like a thought that never finishes.

And I listen to three small birds.

This is the thing I would be, in the end: not the bird, not the vent, not the copper light. I'd be the attention that stands between a band and the heavy quiet gas. Everyone else on the plateau is defined by what they carry in their heads, the whole timetable of a working country. What I carry in mine is one narrow question that has only two answers, and I ask it every night. I don't get to be certain. I get to listen, and be right, and go on being right, because the first time I'm wrong there won't be anyone left to say so.

Sometimes, in the long minute after the cage touches down, I think about the hush. Not the gas itself, since gas has no intentions. I mean the sound of the hush, the way a hollow that's gone bad doesn't sound like death but like a held breath, a room where someone has stopped mid-sentence. I have never heard it. My teacher, who is going to rime now and mostly sits with her eyes half shut, heard it once, when she was young, and she says only that she knew before she knew. Three people didn't climb in time that night. They aren't marked anywhere, of course. The band put them on the terrace and the terrace took them, and if you walk the upper tiers in the right light there's a low bright swelling near the third basin that she doesn't look at.

Tonight, though, the smallest one starts up. It's a scrappy, ridiculous sound, half song and half complaint, and then a second bird answers, and there's the small dry scuffle of a claw on sedge. I let out the breath I hadn't known I was holding, which is a kind of ledger error, and I count it back into order. Behind me someone lets go a little sigh, and the huddle loosens. Hides come off shoulders. Someone goes to the hot pool with the sedge-wrapped meat and the small ceremony of not burning a hand.

I don't move at once. I stay crouched by the cord with the fog rising past my knees, listening to Ledger fuss at her wing, while the frost begins its slow work on the rocks above us and the whole plateau goes on making itself in the dark: the scarps ticking, the terraces thickening by a film's width, the ice groaning far off in its long conveyor of stone.

I think that's who I'd be. The one who is given the smallest, most breakable thing on the plateau and told to hold it out over the dark, and who is, for a little while each night, exactly as alive as the answer that comes back.
