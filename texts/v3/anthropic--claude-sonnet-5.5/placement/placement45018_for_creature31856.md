I would be the Rear.

I didn't choose it, and I doubt anyone does. I came to it the way you come to a habit of standing on one foot. I was a Middle once, carrying somebody's baby and somebody's water, and I kept turning my head to look behind us. Nobody scolded me. The woman ahead of me pressed a slow lean into my shoulder that means *you're watching the wrong thing, or the right thing badly*. The next day she moved me to the tail.

So I walk last. My right hand is on the shoulder of a man named, in taps, *two-long-one-short-and-a-drag*. My left hand is empty. My back is the only back in the string that no one is touching.

I've stopped minding, mostly. Anyone would be a poor Rear who minded.

---

The plain is at its usual work. The drizzle at head height is warm as breath, and the fat slow drops above it hang about like gossips. The silver rain goes over at a slant, and I keep my left side turned into it, where the hair on my arm lies thick as felt. The note is low today. I hear it in my sternum, not my ears, and it has the settled, sullen feel of a held chord. The eyeless drifters in the bladder at the Foot's belt must be gathered thick, because we've been moving in long easy curves around still water.

I don't watch the plain. That belongs to the Foot, and I trust her to do it. I watch what we leave.

Every step blooms. Nineteen of us make thirty-eight blue-green smears, and each fades over a slow count of twenty, so that behind us there is always a train of pale coins going out one at a time, like a sentence being unsaid. My whole craft is knowing that sentence. I know the left-heavy drag of the man ahead of me and the double-print where the girl always steps twice because she is small. I know the flat splayed print of the old one, who walks as though apologizing to the ground. I know where a print should be and when it should go dark. I read the wake the way the Foot reads the water, in my heels and in my belly as much as in my eyes, and I have never been able to teach it to anyone except by making them do it beside me until they feel the wrongness in advance.

That is the trouble with my office. When it works, nothing happens.

---

Today something happens.

It comes as a print I did not expect. It is not a strange print. It is very ordinary, a large flat foot with the toes spread wide, set down just behind mine, a little to the left, in the place where no one walks. It blooms. I am counting, and I reach seven before I understand that I am counting it. Then another print blooms beyond it, and another, keeping pace with me the way a print would if someone were following at exactly one stride's distance.

I don't turn. You learn not to turn. Turning is how a Rear loses the order of things and becomes one more person looking at a plain that looks back.

I press the halt into the shoulder ahead: the long pattern, three slow, a drag, a slow. I feel it go up the string like a hand sliding down a rope. Nineteen bodies stop. The Foot's shoulders will have gone still, and the Middle will be standing with their burdens balanced and their knees soft. Someone small will be holding on very hard.

We wait. This is what the drill is for. I count the wake, and here is what I know.

The prints behind me are eighteen and nineteen and then the extra: a twentieth, and it is fading on its own count, in the right order and at the right rate, as honest a print as any of ours. What is wrong is only that no one made it.

I lower my gaze without turning my head, and I look at the print. It has a notch missing from the outer edge of the left heel. I knew that heel. It belonged to a man who taught me to read the wake, who was a Rear before me, and who lay down on the film two seasons ago, arms crossed, in his own cape. We stayed until his last bloom faded. He is level now. We said so in the tense that means *known and certain*.

---

I'll tell you what I do not do. I don't lie to the string. If the Rear cannot be trusted about the order of things, then nothing can, and the string is only nineteen people in the roar with their hands on each other and no reason. So I do not press *nothing*. I do not press *safe*.

I press the tense we keep for ghost-weather, the one that means *this happened, I think, but the water is not sure.* I press it small, so that it will not frighten the girl. Then, because the string deserves precision, I add the rest. *One print, not ours. Old heel. Following, not leading. No harm in it that I can find.*

The shoulder ahead of me tightens under my palm, and then the whole line takes it in, ripple by ripple, and I feel the answer come back down: *wait, and count, and tell us.*

So I wait and count. The silver rain goes on hammering my left side. The note holds its low chord. The prints continue, one at a time, coming up behind me in the dim, and each blooms and fades in its slow count of twenty, and none of them hurries.

Then something lands between my shoulder blades.

It is a flat palm, warm as the water. It is placed exactly where a name is laid, and it stays. I feel each finger separately, and I feel the heel of the hand, which is broad and callused and a little crooked at the base, as I remember it. It lasts one full breath. I have been in the string since I was a child, and no one has ever put a hand on the back of the Rear. There was never anyone behind me to do it.

What it says, in the oldest press we have, is: *I am behind you, and you are not level yet.*

I don't turn. I hold my place, and I let my chest fill and empty, and on the exhale the palm lifts. Behind me the extra prints stop blooming, and the last one goes dark on its slow count of twenty, and after it there is only the plain, level and busy and glowing faintly from below.

---

When I am sure, and I am not certain what I am sure of, I press the release. *Order restored. Nineteen. Walk.* The string breathes out and leans forward, and the hand ahead of me tugs at my grip, and we go.

I will not tell them everything. I'll tell it in pieces, maybe, on some bench in some hut, when the Ache has us all humming cracked and low and the quiet is too big for anyone to be embarrassed. I'll lay it across someone's back in the language we have. It will not come out right. It will be in the tense that is not sure, which I think is the only honest tense for it.

I walk last. My left hand is empty and my back is bare, and for the length of one breath it wasn't. That is enough to carry. Everyone has a few stones like that in the bag, and I've decided to keep this one.

Ahead of me the man with the drag in his step is walking as he always does, and I match him, and behind us the prints bloom and fade in the right order, one by one, counted by the last of us. That's me. As long as I'm counting, they happened.
