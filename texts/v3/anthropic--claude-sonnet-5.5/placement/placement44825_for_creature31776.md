I come over the saddle in the last week before the deep cold, behind a woman who has made this crossing eleven times and does not look back to see if I'm following. I am new. I carry wool, a pot, a bag of grain, and a small sack of salt that I keep touching to make sure it's there. That is the first thing I know about myself here: I am a listener who cannot yet listen.

What I hear at the rim is noise. A great slow wash of it, a moan from the north, a hollow knocking from below, something thin and rising like a kettle a long way off. The older woman walks into it as into a warm room. I stand at the top of the descent with the wind pushing at my ears and think that I have made a mistake. Then the cloud shadow crosses the bowl, the spires go from chalk to gold in the space of a breath, and I stop thinking about mistakes.

---

For the first days I try on other lives, the way you test doorways in a strange house.

I would like to be the mat. I crouch at a spire's lip at dawn, when the steam is heaviest, and put a fingertip on the honey-colored skin, and it trembles back. It has no opinion of me. It only leans toward the wet, and the whole town is the record of that leaning. There is a peace in it I envy: to want one thing, to be everywhere at once, to make chambers for birds without ever meeting a bird. But I can't hold it. I keep wanting to tell someone about the light on the rim. The mat has no one to tell, and it doesn't miss having anyone. I am not that clean.

I would like to be a worm, too, clear enough that you could watch my thoughts fill me like paste, grazing in slow S-curves through the warm water and being, perhaps, tended. I watch them for an hour. Then a pool cools overnight and I find its worms stiffened into white threads in the new stone, all pointing the same way, still swimming. I take my hand out of the water and do not put it back for a day.

I follow the herd at dusk and find that I could be one of them. I could walk in file with my nose near the tail ahead, breathe my steam onto the one behind, and flick an ear at the booming and go on chewing. It would be a kind of wisdom to treat the drone as weather. But I have watched them lift their heads together before the hail, as though on one string, and I understand that they know something I'm supposed to be learning to know, and that they know it without effort. I want to earn it instead.

So I stay what I am. I sit inside a mid-sized spire on the third floor of a chamber whose floor is a still pool, back against a wall that is warm the way a sleeping animal is warm, and I listen badly.

---

Here is what listening badly is like. You are told there is a chord. You hear only the loudest voice, the low north drone, and mistake it for the whole. The older woman, whose name I have learned is a hum with a falling end, sits across from me and does nothing for days but breathe. Sometimes she lifts one finger. I have learned to check the sky when she does. The sky is always doing what the finger said.

"Not the pipes," she says finally. It is the only instruction she gives me. "The gaps."

I don't understand until the fourth morning, when the wind drops nearly to nothing and the town is at its quietest, that hum you feel more in the teeth than the ear. In that near-silence, I hear where the voices are not. There is a place between the drone and the reeds where nothing sounds, and it has a shape. The shape is as definite as a doorway. Every spire is a note, but the chord is the spaces the notes leave, and the emptiness of the town, which I had taken for absence, turns out to be the instrument. The sky comes down to the ground between the spires because the music needs room to stand in.

After that I hear it. Not all of it, and not well, but enough to know that the sharp, jittery edge creeping into the reeds means I should carry my pot to the lee side and sit tight. Hail comes forty minutes later, gravel on tin, and the herd is already gone over the saddle, and I feel, absurdly, as if I'd been let into a conversation.

---

The spire falls in the second month.

I am inside a different one, alone, when it happens. There is a crack that seems to come from inside my own skull, then a long rolling crumble that crosses the whole plateau and keeps going after I think it must be finished. Dust drifts down through the light. The pool in my chamber shivers into rings. And then the chord is wrong, the way a face is wrong when a tooth is gone, and my body knows it before I do.

I find the others on the terrace, sitting in a rough line facing the white rubble. Nobody speaks. The older woman hums a single note and lets it hang. Another answers with a different one. They are not singing the lost tower. They are humming the hole where it was, feeling along its edges, and over the next hours the neighboring spires start to slide, their notes bending a hair toward the gap to fill it.

Then they turn to me, and I understand it is my turn to check their work. I hum what I think the new chord is. I get it wrong. The older woman gives me a look with no unkindness in it and moves her hand down, a small distance, and I follow her hand down and find a note I did not know I could reach. It settles into the others and the whole thing rings, a little, in my chest.

That is the first moment in the Flat I feel I belong to a sound and not merely to a body sitting in a stone tube.

---

So this is who I am here. Not the mat, not the worm, not the herd, though I hold a little of each: the mat's slowness, the worm's clarity, the herd's patient ease in the noise. I am one of the people who comes over the saddle in the cold season, who builds nothing, who lives in what the masons finished, and who tries to keep the town's music in a body that will not last as long as the music does. Someone will hum this chord after I'm gone, a little wrong, and someone else will correct them.

I am also, I notice, someone who wants to leave something behind, which is against the spirit of the place and entirely in keeping with it. On the last warm evening I find a quiet pool on the lower terrace, where the shelves are furred with pale wings and ribs, and I lay in the water a tuft of my own hair and one feather I found at the foot of a northern pipe, a dark one with a soft edge. I do not say anything over them. I hum the gap where the fallen spire was, the shape I learned with my hand pulled down.

By spring they will be white and hard. Someone will sit near them and hum something else entirely. Above me the wind crosses the bowl, and the town says, in a chord I can almost read, that the cold is coming and it is time to go down.

I stay one more breath, listening for my own note in it, and then I climb toward the saddle after the woman who does not look back.
