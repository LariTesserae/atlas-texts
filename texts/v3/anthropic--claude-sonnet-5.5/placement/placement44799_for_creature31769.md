I start at the rim, and I'm not anyone yet. I'm a lean over the edge, looking down at a green lake that is a roof. The cold air pours past me toward the floor, and I go with it, down the pale cliff, past the fresh scars, into the sound of a room where someone is always exhaling.

My first thought is the beaver, the old female on the tell with the torn ear. It's the obvious choice, and it flatters me. She is the one the description follows home. But when I try to be her, I find I can't carry it. Her whole life is a response to leaking. She has never known a silence that would let her stop, and she doesn't wonder about it. I would wonder. I'd stand on the dam crest with a stick in my hands, listening to the trickle and thinking about the trickle, and the water would win. The basin doesn't need a beaver who thinks about the work. It has hundreds of the other kind.

So I drift. I try the heron, and I have the stillness for it, an hour with my head sunk in my shoulders, but I'm not really still. I'm just waiting for something to move. I try the sphagnum and get a moment of real peace, a wine-red cushion holding twenty times its weight in cold water and adding a hair's breadth a year. But sphagnum has no perspective, and I keep leaving it to look around.

I end up on the wall, where the water drips off an overhang.

There is a thread there, one of the hair-thin ones, and it runs over the tufa, the only smooth stone in the cliff. Below the lip, in the film of moving water, something small is crawling. That's where I find myself. I'm a caddisfly larva, an inch of soft pale body with a hard brown head, and I'm carrying my house.

I built it myself, but not from anything I made. It's a tube of grains and shell fragments, and here, where the water is thick with lime, flecks of new tufa. I glued each piece with silk from my mouth, one at a time, choosing by touch, turning each grain until it sat right. When I outgrow the tube I add to the front. The back end is old, worn to almost nothing. I drag the whole tube along the wet stone, grazing the green film, with only my head and legs out in the current.

This feels right in a way the beaver didn't. I'm a builder, and the builders' spirit is in me, but at a size where the work can be finished. A tube is done when it's long enough. I don't hold back a river. I hold my own body, and the water is good enough to go through.

The wall is my whole country. Above me the swallows stack their mud gourds in rows under the rock, and their chatter comes down like weather. Below me the thread braids into a seep and the seep goes off into the black tea of the floor, where I'll never go. I know this stretch of stone the way the ants know a trail. I know where the water runs fast and where it stalls, where the moss is thick and where the lime is putting out a smooth new lip. I've felt the tufa grow. It thickens in the places I've just left. Over a season the stone I crawl on is a little further out from the cliff than when I hatched, and I've helped it, in the small way of a thing that scatters grains.

Winter is a long shut door. The thread doesn't freeze, or not all the way, since moving water resists it, and I sit tight in my tube against the wet rock and go slow. Frost whitens the ferns above me and I don't feel it. The elk come down to the marsh, the wolves follow, something dies in the shallows, and I know none of it. I know the cold and the dark and the hush, and I know that there is water.

In spring I climb. This is the part I didn't expect. I seal my tube to the stone, close the door with a grating of pebbles, and change inside it, and when I break out, I'm someone else: a dull brown thing with long antennae and wings folded like a roof. I swim up through the film of water, and the fog takes me.

The fog is where the day ends, and it's beautiful, everything beaded, every hair and web and catkin hung with little lenses. I climb a reed with the frogs' eyes below me and dry my wings in the pale morning. I'm awkward and soft, and I'm exactly the size of a swallow's mouthful.

They come at dusk in a rush of small wings, the whole cloud tipping over the ponds. I have a few hours, maybe a day or two. I mate, I drop my eggs back onto the wet stone, and I'm one of hundreds doing it. And then I'm in the air with the others, and something with a chestnut throat turns on the mist, and the last thing I know is the sky. It's a small bright lid, gray-white, almost blue.

I don't mind the ending. That's what I like about the place: nothing here is wasted. The swallow carries me up to a mud gourd that was made of beaver ground, and I go into the beaks of her chicks, who are loud and hungry and warm. What I built out of grains ends as stone. What I was ends as a wing, and in a week the wing is over the ponds. The eggs I dropped are already on the wall, in the film of water, beginning again to collect their small pieces of lime.

So that's who I'd be: not the ruler, and not even the worker on the tell. I'd be the smallest builder in the wettest place, dragging a house made of the wall's own grains along the stone, and one link in the chain between the drip and the sky.
