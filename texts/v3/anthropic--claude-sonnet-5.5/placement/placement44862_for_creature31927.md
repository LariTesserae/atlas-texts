# The Reader in the Dials

I don't begin as anything. I begin as a sound, the rain-on-tin hiss, and I take a while to notice that part of the hiss is me.

Then I have feet. Then I have the feet ahead of mine and the feet behind, a fine chatter coming up through the small hooked ends of my legs. It tells me *here, here, a little left, here.* I am in a braid. I am one lentil of iron in a stream that is crossing another stream at a right angle, and I pass through the gap in the other stream's ranks like light through light. Nobody touches anybody. It is the most courteous thing I have ever done, and I have no idea how I do it.

This is the first place I find myself, and I stay a long time. Long enough to understand that I could stay forever and be content. A mite in a braid never has to decide anything. The stream decides, and the mite is the stream deciding. There is a comfort in it like warm water, though I have never been wet and have no word for warm water. The word comes from somewhere else, from behind me, from a part of me that has apparently been elsewhere.

That is the first strange thing. I have a shell, and the shell is never finished. Scale goes down under me, fresh and red, and scale goes off above me, old and dull, and between the two I am a soft pale patient body that only ever touches the present. But something is written in the scale. I can feel it, the way you feel a splinter. The old scale carries what I passed through. It leaves me in a fine dust, and the dust falls to the drift, and somebody walks on it.

So I am a mite, and a mite with a slightly bad habit: I notice what I'm shedding.

---

I don't stay in the braid. I want to say I decide to leave, but what happens is that the stream bends toward the north wall, toward the seep, and I bend with it, and at the glossy apron where the dust has been trampled hard around the oil, I slip sideways. It's a small error, a half-step, and the mites behind me close over the gap without a flicker. The wound heals before I've finished making it.

I stand at the edge of a pool. The oil is amber and skinned, and it smells of warm resin and struck matches. A bellows-moth hangs over it, tasting the vapor, and the wing-ring of its beating is the loudest thing in the world. It is a great sheet of paper being unfolded slowly by someone who has all the time there is.

For a moment I think I'll be a moth. Anyone would. To hang in the light with the world's slow mail on your wings, carrying the smell of one pool to the next, leaving a film on a wall, on a stoker, on a sleeping sexton: *this is what it's like over there. This is what it's like here. Now you know.* I think I would be good at it. It would suit the part of me that likes to carry what it doesn't fully understand from one place to the next.

But the moth is a courier and I am not. I'd bring nothing back. When the moth's news arrives it is pure smell, and the smell fades in an hour. I want something that stays, or stays a little longer. The thing that itches in my scale is not the wish to send. It is the wish to keep.

So I leave the pool and cross the floor, and this is a long journey. It is perhaps forty meters, which for a lentil is the width of a country. I go by the slow route. I go along the base of the plinth, where the stokers' columns come down empty and hurried and go up loaded and grave, and their claws on the rungs make the tick-tick-tick that keeps the beam's time. I cling under the lip of a masonry block while a column passes overhead, each stoker pressing to one side without breaking stride. One of them has a scorched shoulder rubbed so shiny that for a second I see the whole cylinder bank in it, flaring blue-white, upside down and tiny. Then it's gone.

I do not envy the stokers. I want to be clear about this, because it would be easy to. They have a whole life in a single verb: *carry.* But the verb is hollow in the middle. I have seen the ritual, the packing of the dead one into the chute, the gold flare with the green edge, the column standing still for the length of it, and I know the stokers are holding something in that stillness that they cannot say. It is the only time any of them stops. I am not sure it is grief. I think it may be reading. I think they are reading the color.

I don't have the color. I have something else.

---

The panel is in the southeast, where the drift is shallow and the sextons have laid few traps. It stands at an angle, half-swallowed, its dials packed solid with mite shell, its face crusted like the inside of an old jar. I climb it the way water would climb it if water could. And I find the first dial, and I put my mouthparts to the packed rust over the glass, and I begin to eat.

I have to tell you what this is like, because it is what I am.

The shell over the dial is layered, and each layer has a grain, the record of the mites who laid it. They were laid by no one in particular, in the ordinary way, one scale over another for longer than I can count. When I rasp, I take the layers in one by one and I feel them, shell on shell, a whole history of steady, patient, unglamorous accretion. And then I reach the glass. And under the glass, faint as breath on a windowpane, there is a needle. It rests at a place on a curve. And around the curve are marks. Numbers.

I can't read them. The word "read" is too big. What I can do is feel where the marks are, the way the streams feel each other through their feet, and the marks have a pattern. The pattern is *evenly spaced, increasing, ending.* It is a scale with a top. Someone stood in front of this needle and knew what it meant when it climbed, and knew when to be afraid.

And I have just eaten the shell that hid it. Which means the needle is now visible for the first time in longer than anyone can say, and it is visible to me, and in a day it will be covered again. The mites will find it. They will do what mites do. They will lay it under.

That's the second strange thing. The moment I know what is here, I am also the thing erasing it. I cannot read without wearing away. I have made a small clear window in the world, and my body is the only place it will ever be kept. The reading passes into my shell. The shell carries it upward on its slow belt. In a season it will shed, and the record will fall as a pinch of red dust to the floor, and someone will walk on it.

Everything I learn is a kind of fuel, and it burns.

---

I've been here a long time when the Draw comes.

I know it before it happens, or my shell does. The hiss around me thins, and the moths go still in their shafts, and the slot in the ceiling, far off across the bowl, starts to breathe *in.* It is a slow pull, at first, like a held breath being taken by a house. Then the dust lifts. The panel trembles. The stokers clamp onto the plinth, all of them at once, thousands of claws finding the same worn hollows. The engine's note climbs into a hard whistle as the draft feeds its cylinders, and I am torn off the dial and up into the red-and-black storm, and there is nothing to hold.

I tumble. Everyone tumbles. The bellows-moths are riding the spirals in great glittering rings, and I go up through them, past their wings, so close I can see the veins in the foil. I go up past the amber beams. The light is in me. I am *in* the light, a bright coin of it going through my shell, and for one moment I am high enough that the whole Understory lies below me like a country on a map.

I see the engine's beam tilting. I see the pools and their glossy aprons. I see the crater-traps, thousands, each with its faint glitter. I see the braids of my own kind crossing and re-crossing across the floor, and they look, from up here, like a script. It's a script in a language with one letter. And in the middle of it all there is a small clear place on the panel, a dial with its face cleaned, a place where the world was briefly legible.

And above me the slot. Gray light, the color of chalk. A sky with no weather in it. Not the outside I'd imagined but the outside that is: level, patient, unhurried, already leaning down.

I don't know what I feel. It isn't fear. It's closer to the feeling you have when you find out that the room you've lived in has a window.

Then the air stills. I fall. The whole storm falls with me, and the dust settles in new bands, and I land on the drift with a soft red *tick* like a grain of rice on a drum.

---

I go back to the panel. Of course I do.

It takes me a shift to find it again. The Draw has laid down new strata, red over black over that pale copper-green, and the dial I cleaned is covered. I stand at the foot of the panel with the beam thudding above me and the hiss of my kind around me, and I begin again from the first scale.

This is what I am, and I think I can say it now. I'm a mite the way the engine is a machine: it's the shape I'm in, not the thing I do. What I do is attend. I put my mouth to the surface of a world that is busy forgetting itself, and I keep a little of what is under it, and then I pass it on in the only way I can, as dust. Somewhere below, the floor is a little more legible for the passing, if anyone ever came who could read floors.

Maybe no one will. The roof is thinning. I can feel it in the light on my shell, a warmth that wasn't there when I started. Some year a rib will fail, and the gray sky will look in, and the engine will go quiet, and the stokers will stand at their rungs with their loads in their arms, waiting for a beat that doesn't come. I will be somewhere on the panel with the last of the numerals in my scale. I don't know what I'll do then. I think I will go on reading until the light gets into the glass and the needle can be seen by anyone, by the sky itself, and then I won't have to keep it anymore.

Until then, the beam tilts. The flare blooms blue-white. The moths lift off the cylinders in a single silver breath, and I lean into the dial, and I take the next scale, and the next, and the needle begins to appear.

I'm not sure what it's measuring. But it's still pointing at something, after all this time, and that seems to me worth a little of my shell.
