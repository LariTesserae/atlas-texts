I start before I know what I am. There is a tick, then a tick a little lower, then a long gap in which something gathers weight at the edge of a lip and lets go. For a moment I think I am the water. Then my knee complains about the ledge, and I am a body, and the water goes on being itself a hand's breadth from my face.

It is the hour before the sun touches the bottom. The wall is beaded, and my scraper is a flat reed worn to the shape of my grip. I am a dew-tender. Most of us are very old or very young, and I am on the old side of the middle, which is a poor place to stand because you can feel both directions at once.

The third terrace gutter has a feather in it, and a wad of lint, and the green film that comes first, before the ferns. I lift it out and speak to it. *There. You see. All night you've been holding it back, and the terrace below is thirsty, you stubborn thing.* The water answers by running. That is the whole conversation, and I have had it ten thousand times.

Below me a bucket goes down into the cistern. Everyone within earshot pauses, me too. We listen to how far it has to fall. A long drop, and then the flat slap. It is a good morning, a hand and a little more. Someone laughs on the second ring. The day has news now.

---

I should say what I wear, since it is how you'd know me. My hair has one feather in it, a grey pigeon feather bent at the tip and old enough to have gone soft as cloth. The young are supposed to wear the feather of the first bird that nested in a pot they made. Mine is that feather. It is only that the pot was wrong.

I am of the Sevens. We set seven pots in a rosette wherever we can, and we argue for hours about where one will fit. When I was young I was given the seventh pot of a rosette on the fourth terrace, high on the west face where the evening wind comes across the rim. It was to be my tuning. I made the neck a finger longer, then a thumb narrower. I fired it myself, and the kiln-keeper, who never says anything, looked at the dark in the kiln's mouth and looked at me. I did not know what the look meant. Now I think it meant *you are in love with this one, and it won't help.*

The whole family stood on the ledges. The wind came. My pot spoke.

I still don't have a word for what it did. It was not loud. It was almost pretty. But it sat a hair to the wrong side of what the wall was holding, and the entire pit winced, the way a body does when a tooth is touched. Four thousand pigeons did not stop cooing. The wince was in the brick, in the air, in the people on the ledges. Then there was the silence after, which is particular and has a floor to it. I stood on that floor. Nobody was cruel. The old ones did not nod.

A pot that clashes is broken for grog or handed to the birds. Mine was handed to the birds. Within a season a hen pigeon had filled it with grass and laid two eggs. When the first chick fledged I took a feather from the ledge, and I have worn it since. It is my only feather. I never got a note.

---

By midmorning the treaders are down in the clay basins, stamping, singing their counting songs a little behind the beat, as they always do. I go along the channels with my bowl and my scraper, and I listen to the whole slope of the pit ticking at twenty pitches. I can hear when a gutter has begun to fail. The drip goes sour first, before the eye sees anything. Perhaps this is what I got instead of a note. I can't make the chord, but I can hear when the water is off.

At noon we go into the pot-rooms. Fifteen of us lie in a row in the cool dark, and the dipper goes from hand to hand. Through the brick comes the pigeon murmur, and behind it, if the wind is up, the deep breath of the big pots. I lie on my side with my ear to the wall. I have never told anyone that I can pick out mine. Not the pot's voice, because it has none, exactly. It is a place in the chord where the sound moves around something. The wall has a small held breath in it, on the fourth terrace, west face, and everything else bends around it a little, the way water bends around a stone.

I am not sure anyone else hears it. Maybe they do. Maybe that is why the young masons, when they set new pots near the Sevens' rosette, keep leaving that gap.

---

Afternoon. I climb because my knees have decided this is the hour for it. I take the old stair with the dished treads where bare feet have worn it into the shape of all our feet at once, and I go up to the fourth terrace and stand under the rosette. Six pots, glazed dark with soot and oil. Then the seventh, packed with grass, its lip white with droppings, a pigeon's tail-fan just showing at the mouth. She looks at me without interest. I have known her mother's mother's line.

Below the rosette, in the deep terraces by the bats, are the sealed jars, and above each jar a small singing pot tuned to the one who lies within. I have walked there and put my ear to the brick more times than I would admit. When I go into the wall, my family will do what is done. They will curl me in a jar with my one bent feather and seal it with clay. Then they will have to decide what to set above me, since there is no note.

I have heard them talking, very low, about the grass-stuffed pot. I think they mean to set that one. It would be a pot that makes no sound but a breath, and they'd be putting it where the deep chord could lean on it.

I don't know how I feel about this. That is the honest thing. A hundred and fifty of us, and the wall knows all of us, and I would be the one it knows by a silence. But I have lived in this noise all my life, and I know what silence is here. It is the thing that stops us where we stand. It is the worst sound the well makes. And I keep thinking that mine is not that kind. It is small. It has a hen in it.

---

Dusk. The swifts come down in their spiral, thousands, each one finding its own pot by feel, and the roar builds to the great wet rush and then falls. I am on the fourth terrace, on the west ledge, and I lay my palm flat on the brick under the rosette. The wall is warm. The water is ticking. I close my eyes, and I am not praying. I am checking, as one does.

The wind is at the right angle tonight. I feel it before I hear it, a pressure along the rim, and then the big pots begin, slow and out of tune with each other in the way that turns out to be a tune. The oldest sections sound first, the makers I never met. Then the grandmothers. Then the child of last spring, very small and high, somewhere below my feet.

And then a breath comes out of the grass-stuffed pot at my palm. It is not a note. Nothing anyone planned. Air moving through a nest, through dry stems and a warm hen, a soft sound like someone about to speak and deciding to wait. The chord doesn't wince. It leans, slightly, the way a wall leans on a buttress. For the length of one gust the whole pit holds me in it, the way it holds everything, and I stand there with my hand on the brick and don't move.

Then the wind turns. The roar falls to the murmur, and the drip comes back up through it, tick, then a lower tick, then a long gap where something gathers weight and lets go. My hand comes down.

I go to supper.
