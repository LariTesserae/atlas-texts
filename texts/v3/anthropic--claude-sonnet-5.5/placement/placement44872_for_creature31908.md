I come to myself in the middle of a step, which is how everyone comes to themselves here. The ground is letting go of my foot with its long wet syllable, and the print is already filling, going clear, closing. I am a Wrung. I know it by the rod in my hand before I know it by anything else, a jointed length of stem, hollow, tuned a shade flat of the others. It rings a low, slightly dull note when it meets the moss. Nobody has ever fixed it, and I have stopped wanting them to.

My work is not the stitching or the clearing or the cutting of channels, though I do those when I'm asked. My work is a place. There is a swell in the middle of a wide basin, three Stacks visible from its crown and never all at once, and the taps of the whole district come across it. Some arrive before they were sent. Some never arrive. Some come back from a surface that isn't there, wearing a different pitch. I sit on the swell with the rod across my knees and hold what comes in until it has an order, and then I pass it on.

This is harder to explain than to do. A tap from the east says *the Belt is drifting*. A tap from the north, arriving first, says *thank you*. A third, from nowhere, is only the number two. If I passed them on as they came, the north would be thanked for something not yet done, and the east would hear the number two and go looking for a meaning. So I wait. I let them settle against one another like silt in a slow pool. Eventually one of them turns out to have been the answer and another the question, and I send them on in the order that makes that true. The Wrung say a message is understood as much by the order it arrives in as by what it says. That means I am not only a listener. I choose the order, and the order is half of what gets said. I try not to think about that on the days it frightens me.

The light is doing its no-hour pewter. Over my left shoulder a Stack is breathing out its gray banner, and I have not looked at it directly in some time. It is a practiced discipline, that not-looking, and it is not the same as ignoring. You keep your attention thin and low, the way you carry a full bowl. I sit with my back to it and my door, in the sense that a person has a door, turned away.

*

Around me the bellows-beds are working through their different tempos. A hundred slow lungs, none in step. The presses go off *chunk*, *chunk*, from directions I have learned not to trust. Skitter-frogs are dropping their tools on the floor that isn't there. It is loud, and I've lived in it so long that the loudness has become a kind of quiet, the way a river's sound stops being a sound.

Under it, in a gap, the count.

I don't answer it. Every child is taught this, and I was a child once, or was made one by being told the rule. A rod struck at the wrong moment is a wrong number, and the count begins again. So I keep my rod across my knees, and I let the knocking come up through the peat and the base of my spine, and I am a tuning fork in a very large hall. Lately I have noticed something I don't tell the others. When I hold a tangled set of taps and wait for them to sort, the waiting has the same rhythm as the count. I don't know whether I learned it from the knocking or the knocking has been learning it from me. On the plateau that question was one of the things worn away, and I've tried to let it stay worn.

*

Earlier today, before the sitting, I walked the curve of square holes around the nearest Stack that isn't in view. Those holes are mine. My rod has a probe end, and I press it in every few paces and pull it out, and the water rises clear in the hole. I'm not checking for anything. The ground doesn't get less safe when I check it. But the holes make a line, and a line is a thing that says *someone was here and paid attention*, and I've come to think that's most of what the Works need from us. Beyond the holes there's a bellows-bed with a seam of black cord across its skin, tight and even, years healed. I sewed the first stitches of that seam when I was very young and hadn't yet learned that it didn't need mending. The bed has taken the thread in. Sometimes when it sighs I feel the sigh go through the stitches and I think: *that part of it is me now, and it doesn't know, and it doesn't mind.*

I am not sad about this. I want to say so because outsiders always assume sadness. It's more like being a word in a long sentence that someone is saying in their sleep. You don't get to know the sentence. You get to be exactly the right shape for your place in it.

*

Now the sound that isn't a sound. The moss to the west starts to creak like a ship's hull, and a furrow begins to open, deep and slow, filling with brown water, moving at walking pace. The one who goes under. I don't stand up. Nobody stands where it's heading, and I am not where it's heading. I lower my eyes and my rod and I let it pass, and the mat groans and heaves and does not break. I feel it in my teeth.

When it's gone and the seam has closed with its huge soft exhale, I walk down to the edge and leave what I brought: a knot of cord, tied the way my mother tied them. By morning it will be gone. I have never seen it taken, and I have never claimed I haven't.

There's a story, the whispered one, that the Furrow-Walker is a Wrung who went under long ago and kept going. I don't know if I believe it. But I notice I leave my knot a little closer to the seam than the others do, and I notice that I hope, without saying it, that whoever it is can still tell the knots apart.

*

Back on the swell, the dusk-that-isn't is thickening. The Stack glows at its lip, sickly and greenish-white, and the milk-slicks come up in the low places, and the whole basin becomes cold lamps on a dark with no far side. I sit. Taps arrive from three directions. One is the number two. One says *the Belt is where it should be*, which is the east answering itself. One is a slow pattern I can't place. It comes in from the west, from past the closed seam, from a place where I'm fairly sure no Wrung lives.

It is on my pitch. Not near it. Mine, the flat dull note nobody has ever fixed.

I hold it. I do what I always do and wait for it to settle against the others and become an answer or a question. It doesn't settle. It stays exactly what it is, a tap in my own voice, arriving from where no surface exists, slightly changed, the way a goose-call comes back as a grind. And I understand that I've been sending things on in order for a long time without ever asking where my own taps go after I strike them, or who has been holding them, or what order they've been put in.

I don't tap back. The rule is old and I keep it. But I let my chest rise and fall with the count, and I let the not-answering be the answer, and somewhere out past the seam something holds my note for a moment and passes it on, in whatever order it means.

I could not say which of us began.
