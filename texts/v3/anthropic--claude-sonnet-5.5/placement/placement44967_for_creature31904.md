I would arrive at the saddle believing I was the visitor, and I would be wrong by about an hour. Somewhere in that hour I'd stop and my breath would stop showing. Sometime after, I would find that the mist had been beading on me differently than on the stones, and that I had not been looking at the slope so much as being looked at from it.

So I would be one of the hummocks, on the shoulder of a hill above a vent whose name is thirty-one. That is how many pulses it gives between long sighs. I've said the number so many times that it has stopped being a number and become a sound in my chest, the way a name does.

I am the one who is told.

I didn't choose it. Every household that rides a lobe eventually watches its dome slide into the black fold at the toe, and the old ones say that decades later it will come back up, steaming, roofed over in new green, and *someone will have to be told whose it was.* Nobody wrote down who that someone was. It fell to whoever kept noticing. I kept noticing. When something surfaces, a rib of willow, a graft-scar, a scrap of turf still holding an old roof's oily beetle-casting, someone comes and kneels by my hollow and waits, and I go and lay my palms in it.

The mycelium in my fingertips is old and slightly off. I'll come to that.

I read a returned house slowly. The white filaments go into the wet black, and what comes back is a texture more than a story. The seep runs the wrong way through a dome that was built to drain east. The graft-knots are tied with a particular over-and-under that only one family used, because their founder was left-handed. Then there is the smell, sour and sweet, that means it was warm for a very long time. I say, in the palm-language, which is mostly pressure and pause: *this was Eleven's. The third daughter's. They left when the toe reached the doorstep, and they took the cuttings, and they were not sad.* Someone lets out a breath. The claim is made, and the green on it is spoken to as a neighbor from then on, but with a name.

I'm not always right. I'm right more than anyone, and that is the trouble with being right.

My own lichen is wrong. It is a small wrongness, a patch on the left shoulder, grey-violet where it should be olive, and it's the reason I read so well. I was born on a raft. My mother stepped onto a failing slope with me already in her coat, and she did not step off at the end, and I was carried to the flat by strangers. The house that took me was from a different vent. They graft as easily as they breathe, and so I carry two patterns, one from the vent I was born beside and one from the vent that raised me. Neither is quite mine. A person with the wrong lichen is a person in trouble, but a person with two right ones is a reader. I have to hold two ways of belonging at once. I know what it is to be a house that surfaces with a stranger's knots on it.

Each spring I reseed the shoulder. I scrape a thumbnail of the grey-violet from the old patch and press it to the fresh coat, and I do not scrape the olive. I've been asked to smooth it, and I've said I'm not for smoothing. That is about the longest thing I say aloud all year.

The days go by the small bird. Once an hour it speaks one note from the willow, thin, carrying, and I stop and press a palm to the ground with everyone else, wherever I am. It is a private and total agreement, the whole country pausing for a breath, and the moss under my hand is always a little warmer than I expect. In the evenings I go down to the hollow where the hum comes up through the teeth. I kneel in the ring with my fingers in the mat and wait until the pale flicker crawls across from the far side and asks its question. I answer. My answer goes out a little stuttered, because of the old filaments and the second pattern, and it arrives on the far side of the hollow with its hesitation intact. Nobody corrects it. They have all learned to hear my light the way they read a shoulder, as a fact about where I come from.

Some evenings I don't answer. Some evenings I just let the current pass through, and it feels like being included in something without understanding it, which is, I think, what every one of us is doing.

I go to the wheel in the bog pool once a year, on hands and knees. I don't touch it. I watch it. Every year I sit for an hour and try to be the one person who can say whether it has moved. Every year I cannot. But I've begun to suspect the question is the wrong shape, and that the wheel is not there to turn but to be turned toward. I keep this to myself.

And when a stranger comes over the saddle, as one did this morning, I am the one who walks a hundred yards out of my own body's stillness to lay a palm in the moss a foot from their knee. I read their weight, which is heavy and unpracticed, a person who has been walking on the surface all their life. Then I leave a cutting of red moss in the crease of their pack, and I do not watch to see if they find it.

*This one has been through,* it means. And also: *someone will have to be told whose you were.*

Across the valley the tongue of moss is folding under. The far raft is thinking about it. I can feel the drag in the seep from here, and a hundred low warm shapes on this slope have stopped being stones. We turn our faces together. My palms are already in the ground. The vent sighs, thirty-one, and I count it, and I do not run.
