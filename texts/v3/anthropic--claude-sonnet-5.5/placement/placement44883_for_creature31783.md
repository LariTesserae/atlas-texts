I keep wanting to begin above ground, in the mist, on a post of dead wood, because that is where a mind like mine would like to stand. Upright, forepaws folded, looking outward. The sentry. Everyone underground is safe because someone is watching, and the watching has a shape: the hawk's shadow, the one rising whistle.

But the sentry is a job, not a creature. The post is worn smooth by whoever stood there last, and I would be relieved at midday by someone who has never wondered who I was. And the sentry only ever says two things. Hawk. Ground. I know more than two things, and I would want to say them.

So I go down instead, following the smell of the fungus gardens, damp and sweetish, leaking out of the vents. Down to the cell no bigger than a cupped hand. The queen. There is something in it that pulls at me: the center of everything, fed by mouths, tended, licked and turned, eggs carried away as fast as they come. Everything reaches her and nothing is asked of her but to go on. She has not seen light in a decade.

But I don't think she knows anything. The colony's whole knowledge passes around her and through her walls, and she receives it only as attention, as being kept. To be the place where the work arrives, and never the one who carries it out, would be to have a great deal done in my name and no say in any of it. It would be a comfortable dark, but I would be a swollen thing in a chamber with doors too small for me. I don't want to be that. I catch myself wanting to be looked after at the center, and I put the wish down.

Somewhere in the middle then, along the lane.

I am blind, so let me say it as I would know it. The roof is close overhead, a pressed arch of clay still slightly damp from the morning's repair. The floor is packed smooth by the passage of a million feet. Warmth comes up from below and grows or fades as the lane runs toward or away from the seam. I don't see the road. I read it in heat and taste, and every soldier I pass is a small chemical sentence, *here, still here, nothing yet*.

I am a courier. I carry nothing in my jaws and nothing on my back. The leaf-haulers grind past me under their green sails, and I go by them in the open center of the lane, faster than anyone should, carrying only what I have just been told.

When I meet a soldier, our antennae touch, and something passes that I could not put in words. There is no word for it. *Beetle at the third crossing.* *Digger, east wall, mass.* *Quiet.* The soldier shifts a few paces one way or the other, and that shift is the entire meaning of my life. A mile of road knows where the danger is because I, and others like me, brush past and are gone.

I like that. I like how little of it is mine. The news is not mine. The road is not mine. I don't know how the lane ends, or what the queen looks like, or whether the spire I was hatched in is the same one I am running toward. I hold only my stretch, and what was said to me last, and I pass it on, and it changes something a little farther on that I will never see. Nobody here reads the whole ledger. We each write one line in it by moving.

There is a cost, and I would be dishonest to leave it out. It is speed that marks us. Quick things get noticed. Where the lane breaks and crosses open ground, a gap of a few body-lengths with no roof over it, the stilt-bird waits, high-stepping, bill lowered, patient. I have to cross. I stop at the last of the roof, and all of me is listening to the air, to heat and small pressures. Then I go. Most crossings I live. Someone else, some dawn, does not, and the news she carried stops at the gap like a word cut in half, and a soldier stands a few paces too far east, waiting for a sentence that doesn't come. The colony will send another courier. It does not grieve, and it does not need to.

And yet I don't think I want to stay on the old roads either, though they are the safe ones.

The mornings when the mist covers the ground, I feel the colony push a new road, a few inches a day across bare rock. Then eventually there is no more roof, only a cooled black edge where the last flow stopped, and beyond it stone that still ticks with heat. Nothing lives on it yet but the pale blind things that eat the wind's dust. The workers have gone quiet behind me. Somebody has to go out ahead before they lay the wall.

So this is where I find myself. I am at the last of the roof, with the seam warm beneath me and the fresh rock cold and strange ahead, glassy, cracking faintly as it settles. I put out my antennae. It tastes of nothing yet. No fungus, no colony, no one's news. I touch it, and it holds. I go a few inches farther.

I don't know if it will hold all the way. Nobody knows that, and the answer is not mine to carry back. I carry back only this: *the stone is warm here. It bears weight. A little farther.* I turn, quick and exposed, and run for the dark of the lane, where behind me, I can feel it through the clay, the first workers are already pressing their pellets into place, and the road begins to be a road.
