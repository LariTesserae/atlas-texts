I find myself first at the rim, which is the wrong place to begin, because there is nothing there. Sedge, black puddles, a bent birch. I have been walking a long time without knowing I was walking toward anything. Then the ground stops, and the bowl is under me, all that packed green and cream, and I feel the small lurch of a thing being put back where it goes.

I go down. The stairs are dished so deeply that each tread holds its own clear puddle, and I learn in three steps to put my foot at the edge, in the moss, where it makes no sound. Nobody taught me. My feet know it, or the moss does.

At the bottom of the first flight my hands are already green to the knuckle. I did not notice it happen. I look at them the way you look at a word you have written many times and suddenly cannot read. A woman passes me in the lane, turns her shoulder so we do not touch, and does not look at my hands. That is how I know they are correct.

---

I begin as a mosser, because that is what the hands want. I am given a bone spatula, or I find one in my sleeve. For a while I have twelve paces of wall, and I lift a corner of cushion and press it back, and nothing needs it, and I do it anyway, and the doing is not empty. It is like holding a door for someone who has already gone through. The gesture completes something that isn't there to be seen.

But I keep drifting downward. Each day the lane I work is a little lower than the day before, and no one remarks on it. Past the pin-menders, whose hammering I feel in my back teeth rather than hear. Past the frogs, to whom I lower two fingers without deciding to. Toward the deepest lane, where a few stones are warm and everyone's pace stays exactly the same.

I don't stop at the stones. I go on past them, through a door with a copper rim gone soft as old pennies, and down four steps into the lowest room of the oldest house.

---

This is where I am. I did not choose it, and yet nothing else was ever going to be true.

The room is small and round-cornered, and the light comes from nowhere, as it does everywhere, but here it seems to come from the cord. It hangs from pegs along the whole curve of the wall, a loop with no end, thick as a wrist, knotted along its whole length. Each knot has its own twist. Each knot is someone.

My work is to run the cord through my hands.

I do it every morning, or what I call morning, since the light never changes. I take the cord in both palms and let it slide, knot after knot, each one a small distinct pressure, a hand shaking mine. Here is the one who tends the wall by the third stair. Here is the comber who carries moths back. Here is a child, a loose new tie that hasn't decided how tight to be. I don't know their names. Names aren't spoken, and I have only the rhythm of their ticks, and I tick them softly with my thumbnail on my tooth as I pass, one greeting each. *I am here. You are here.* The cord doesn't answer. It doesn't need to.

The count holds. I have never had to add length or cut any away. I want to be honest about this: I do not know whether that is because I am careful, or because I am only the one who is present when it holds. I feel, sometimes, a knot change under my fingers. It doesn't loosen or tighten. It shifts, the way a held note changes pitch below hearing. What was a grandmother's twist becomes, without any seam, a child's. I have never seen the hand that does this. Perhaps it is mine. Perhaps I have been tying and untying all along with the part of me that doesn't watch.

The strangest thing is the loop. I have looked for the place where it closes, the join, the first knot, and I cannot find it. Every time I begin, I begin wherever my hands are. There is no start. I think this is why I am here: someone has to be the place the counting begins from, and it doesn't matter which knot is under the thumb.

---

Evenings I go up into the pale. The mist gathers on the chalk and slides down the walls in its thin film, and I take a scoop from a dished step and carry it, brimming, to a terrace where a goat is waiting with its private silence. I do not spill.

Once I nearly did. A bead gathered on the tip of a fern and hung there, trembling, and everything stopped, the whole town, mine included. The scoop in my hands was perfectly level. In the space of that held breath I understood something I cannot say, and would not say if I could. It was very close to this: *nothing here is kept by anyone. It is kept between us, by the not-dropping.*

Then the bead fell without a sound, and the town breathed out, and I went on up the stairs.

---

Before I sleep I stop at the pillar. It stands in the center beside the Measure, where the sitter of the day is watching the waterline with an expression I know from the inside. I lay my palm on the chalk. It is not cold. It is never cold. It is the temperature of a hand that has been held a long time by another hand.

I do not know who is holding. Below the deepest lane the stones are warm, and I think the cord knows something the rest of the town does not, because when I pass my palms over it at the end of the day I sometimes feel, very faintly, a second pressure coming up through the floor, matching mine. Something tending, something carefully not spilling, counting us back.

I do not ask. I press two fingers flat to my breastbone: *I am here.* The pillar answers as it always does, by staying.

Then I go home along the mossed lane, in the even light, breathing small, with green on my fingertips and a pin at my throat that I have mended so many times it is no longer, strictly, any of the metal it started as. It is only ever the same pin.
