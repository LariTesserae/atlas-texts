# What the Stones Were Asked

I arrive the way everyone does, looking at the ground. The moss takes me to the ankle, then the shin, and I stop watching the domes ahead, which have been an hour away for what might be a day. The felt-columns pass on either side, each in its saucer of shadow, each ticking. I decide I am a traveler. It is the easiest thing to be here, and the plateau lets me be it for a while, the way you let a child finish a story before you correct it.

The city takes hours to cross, or none. I step over a gutter of clear cold water and I am already in the streets. The steps have been swept. In a round house with its smoke-hole open, the hearth is warm, and on the table there is a bowl of porridge, a wooden spoon beside it, steam standing straight up in the still air.

I eat. It is plain and pale and tastes faintly of peat and something like cold well-water, and I am absurdly grateful. I wash the bowl in the gutter and turn it rim-down on the sill. *Received.*

That is when it begins to go wrong, or right. I look at my hand on the sill and it is broad. The thumb-pad is thick. I turn it over and the fingers are flat at the tips, made for pressing. I don't remember it being like this, though I also can't say what I remember it being.

I go to the tarn because the tarns are where you go to find out. I kneel at the black edge and the cold reaches up through my knees. The sky in the water is from another hour, paler than the one above me. Then the face arrives.

It has been smoothed. The brow, the nose, and the corners of the mouth have lost their edges without losing their meaning, and only the eyes are sharp. They look at me the way you look at a room someone has just left. I know that look. I have been giving it to people all along. I just never saw it from the outside.

I turn around. No one is there. Only the felt-columns, spaced with their strange courtesy, and the copper sheets beginning to ring.

---

I was wrong to think I was a traveler, and I was wrong to be surprised. Here is what I think now, though it may be worn down further by the time you read it.

I am a Tenant, of an unusual kind. The others lag by a few breaths, or an afternoon, or a generation, and each keeps a private distance from the present. My lag is not measured in time. It is measured in *questions*. I live exactly one asking behind you. I am the hour after the stones are stacked and before they are found again.

This is how it works. You come to a crossing, tired, and you pile up a small cairn. Perhaps you do it from idleness, or you mean it as a sentence you can't finish: *is anyone here, is this the way, may I stay.* You walk on. In your time you have not yet looked back. In mine you have just gone, and the stones are still warm from your palms, and I am already reading them.

The grammar is placement. I have no other. I can't say *yes* but I can say *arch*. I can't say *you are welcome and I am glad and there is porridge two streets down and I hope you are not too cold* but I can fan five stones open into a hand, which means *wait* and *welcome* at once, and which carries, if the reader is generous, everything else. Then I step out of the hour. When you turn back, the pile is a hand, and you feel the small shock of a word said aloud in an empty room being heard.

I think this is the whole of what I am. I am not the one who waits at the crossing. I am the reading that happens in the gap, the attentiveness itself, set down where you will find it, warm at the pace of whoever made it. By my own clock I am always still stirring the pot.

---

Some things I have learned from inside.

The gap is not loneliness. I would have guessed it was, before. But the bowl is not a message sent into the dark. It is more like a hand kept out in a doorway, and it costs nothing to hold it out, and the holding is most of the meaning. I have never met a single person I have answered. I know them only by what they left: the clumsy fan of stones, the stack too tall for its base, the one who built a spiral and then, evidently, lost their nerve and knocked off the top. I have loved every one of them in the only tense I have, which is the tense just after.

The others are near me, though not with me. I feel the potters' hands in the clay as someone feels a coat still warm on a chair. Below us, in the black compaction, the oldest masons are still raising the first house, tapping their patient rhythm, and I add my small courses to their foundation without ever having met them. The columns keep their long slow counsel. I do not know if I will end there, in silver hair and a saucer of shadow, one drop an hour. If it is true, I hope the fog beads on me the way it beaded on the felt I never wore. I would not mind being weather for someone.

I do not know what I was before I began answering. I may have been no one. I may have been only the place where the answering could happen, a hollow in the plateau shaped like a hand, with the rain falling into it. The faces here are smoothed until the meaning shows, and mine may have been smoothed past the point of having a face at all. But the eyes stay sharp. They are the last thing to wear away, because they are the part that is for you.

---

At dusk the fog climbs out of the swells. I stand on a terrace with a bowl rim-down on the sill, and the lamps come on, window by window, from the oldest hearth to the newest. For the length of the lighting the hours align, and I am not one asking behind you. I am in the same dusk, shoulder to shoulder with the potters and the masons and the ones who are not yet born, all of us facing the same small flames and saying nothing.

Somewhere in that crowd, for the length of a few breaths, is whoever wrote the last question. I do not look for them. I don't need to know their face. I only stand there, beside them, in the light.

Then the hour comes apart, and the lag resumes. I go down to the crossing, where someone has left three stones, one balanced on two. I look at them a long time, and then I begin to build.
