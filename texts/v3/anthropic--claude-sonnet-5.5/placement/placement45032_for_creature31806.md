I'd like to say I'd be the Agreement, since it's the grandest thing there. But I don't think I could be it. It has no seat and I would keep looking for one. I'd stand in the middle of the moss, sinking and being politely returned, trying to find the part of me that was deciding.

So I start smaller. I start at the rim, in the frost, as a line-tender.

The idea appeals to me for an hour or so. Rice-sized, bristled, exhaling one mote of warmth against the cold, walking the exact boundary between what freezes and what lives. I would be a threshold, and I like thresholds. But the line-tenders never come down into the moss, and never go up into the slot, and nobody has seen one sleep. I think I'd go mad at the edge of a room I could hear but never enter, feeling the hum against my back forever like a warm animal I was not allowed to lean on. It's a good job and a lonely one. I'd give it up.

Next I try the peepers, because I want the argument. Two rival rhythms held for minutes, each side leaning louder, and nobody able to say whether it's a quarrel. I would like to sit at a pool-edge with a throat like blown glass, lit blue from inside every time I spoke, so that anyone could see exactly what it cost. But I think I'd mostly be the one who yields. I'd fall in with the other rhythm too early, and I'd never learn whether the disagreement was the point.

The moths are wrong, I can tell right away. Too grand, too slow, decades hanging like a page left to dry. I'd want to be the punctuation, and I'd never get to write the sentence.

That leaves the root-felt, the third layer down, where it's dark and close and nobody goes.

I think I'm a threadworm.

Hair-thin, pale as spit, one of millions, carrying a single bright grain I will not spend. I don't know what the grain is. None of us does, and the not-knowing doesn't trouble me. It's the one thing I own, and its charge doesn't decay. Sometimes when the blue wave goes over, I feel it answer, the way a struck glass answers a voice in the next room.

My work is to tally. I click, and the clicks join the thousand around me into the hiss under everything, and the hiss is a sum: how much sound, how much heat, how much stone has set, how much is left. I don't add the numbers myself. Nobody does. My click is a small honest report of what is happening to my one inch of felt, and the total assembles itself somewhere I can't perceive. The Agreement listens to us the way a mind listens to its own pulse, and I like that we aren't its servants. We're a kind of hearing.

Now the worm-life gets particular, and this is where I start to believe it. I live near the pools, where the felt is warm. I have a habit that the others tolerate. When the algae-mat sends up a bubble and the flat note sounds, the whole hollow leans a hair toward it and leaves it alone, and I tally it. Every bubble, every flat flute-note gets a click of its own, a little apart from the others. I don't correct it or lock it into the chord. I just count it carefully, as the one thing in the room that is exactly as unfinished as it means to be. Maybe that's my whole contribution, an extra click on the count of the unresolved. Without it, the tally would say the hollow was nearly done, and the sum would be wrong.

I would know the dead, I think. When a peeper sinks, I would be there in the hour it goes under, threading through the loosened moss, feeling the weight of that body turn into a vein of crystal. I'd carry no message. Passing through is enough. The peepers sing their low note above me and I click below them, and neither of us knows the other is doing it, and together it's a funeral.

And visitors. Someone comes down through the slot with wet sleeves, and the moss tastes salt and iron, and for a few minutes their pulse enters the hum. Down in the felt I would feel it arrive, a slow strange rhythm, faster than the vine and much less patient. I'd click along with it for as long as it lasted, in my one inch, out of pure curiosity. Then the visitor would climb back toward the gray, and their pulse would thin and vanish, and I would let my click drift back into the hiss. I like to think that somewhere in the tally there's a small, warm, moving shelf where they were. I'd never know what the Agreement made of them. I'd only know it had been counted.

Is that who I am, then? A very small clerk in the dark who keeps one odd private habit? I think so, though I keep wanting to check whether I'm a resident or an organ. It's the question the place asks, and it doesn't answer. I don't need it answered. I'm a worm with a grain, in a room being composed, adding my click to a sum I'll never read, and the stone sets a fingernail further while I do.

I would stay a season, or a life. On the last day I'd be drawn down into the felt like anything else, and a faint vein would appear where I'd been, so small that only the worms would notice it. It would be a little brighter than the rest, and maybe a little flat.
