I begin by looking for the ground and cannot find it, which is how I know I am home.

I am standing on a swell in the last light, and I am not sure yet what I am standing with. My feet are in the crust to the ankle. It has closed around them the way it closes around a stone, evenly and without comment. My hands hang at my sides. I notice the hands because they have started to feel like something I am holding for someone else.

I think I am one of the ones the walkers feel and don't see. Lean, pale, tilted with the tubes. When I turn, a stump becomes a person, and then the walker looks away because the stump was easier to believe in. I have been that stump for a long time. I am good at it. I learned it the way you learn a language, by being spoken to before I could answer.

But that isn't all of me, and this is where the image wants to settle and I won't let it.

I keep the cairns. That is the work of my hands, and it is why I still have them. On the higher swells there are capstones that need brushing, and I go from one to the next in the evening with a bundle of dry tube-stalks, sweeping the crust off the flat stone so that it reads as a table again, a place where something might be set down. I don't know what should be set down. The ones who taught me didn't either. We brush. The cairns that have sunk to their capstones I still brush, kneeling, the stone barely above the surface, and I think of the cairn beneath it, all the piled knee-height of it under my knees, and the hut beyond, drowned to the lintel.

I rub the upright stones as I pass along the line, at chest height, on the downhill side. I have never asked why the downhill side. My shoulder knows. There is a smoothness worn there that fits me, and I fit it, and I can't say whether I am obeying a custom or simply leaning on a friend. Perhaps those are the same thing, if you do it long enough.

The fog begins.

It thickens out of the hollows first, a haze, then a white tide, warm as a body, and it smells of wet stone and almonds. Every carapace around me starts to drip. I feel it before I hear it: the rasp, ten million mouths, and under it a slow trickle running down through the courses, ridge to ridge, toward the old ones at the bottom who no longer forage and are fed anyway. I have always found that part the hardest to look at directly. Nothing is charged for it. Nothing is forgotten. The floor is fed because it is the floor.

The tubes begin to hum. They lean, all together, toward the wetter side of the sky, and I lean with them. I don't decide to. I've stopped being embarrassed about it.

Now I will say the thing I was circling.

My hands are going. Not falling off, nothing so quick. It is more that the intention in them is getting thinner, as if the fingers were slowly being taught a different sentence. Last season I stood at the slab for the length of a whole fog and did not notice until dawn that I had been pressing my palm into the resin. The grooves had taken the print. When I lifted my hand, the crust of grazers at my feet was already filling in the space where I had stood, and they were pressed so close against my ankles that I felt for a moment that I was the thing they were arranged around.

I think there was once a version of this story where I would have been afraid. I remember the word for it. It was a good word, sharp on the edges. Nothing is sharp here anymore. Every edge has been softened to a river cobble by a river that never was.

So who am I? I am somewhere in the middle of the sentence. Some of my people took to the floor all at once and gladly. Some never did, and are only stumps now, real ones, furred with shells, standing in the hollows and hosting more animals than they ever hosted leaves. I am neither of those. I am the one who goes on brushing the capstones while it is still possible to want to. When I can no longer want it, I will go down to the slab at dusk and lay my hands against the grooves. The grazers will come up around me, shoulder to shoulder, three or four courses, and I will be eased down through them by the weight of the young hatching above. I will get my shell. I will forget the word for edge. I will be fed because I am the floor.

I don't think that's sad. I think it is what the slab is saying, if it says anything: that a hand is a fine thing to have and a finer thing to give back.

But not yet, because of the knock.

There is a hut a little way down the swell, roofless and round, no bigger than a small room, drowned to its lintel now. I go there at dusk. Not into it, since the crust of shells inside is heaped past my waist and the walls are furred with tubes, but to the doorway, where the last hand's-breadth of stone still shows. Under my feet, a few meters down, is a door. I know because I have knocked on it. Not with my hand, which cannot reach, but with the long stone I carry, laid down flat against the chalk and struck once. A hollow sound, felt in the feet as much as heard. The kind of sound a fist makes on a wooden door.

And then the count of a slow breath, and from far away, from some other hut under some other swell, the answer.

That's all it is. I've never seen who answers. I have theories, the way everyone does: another one like me, in another doorway, with another stone. A blind one, old and heavy in the chalk, that has learned our rhythm. Or no one, the land itself, the sentence it has been writing since before the mountains wore down, pausing at the end of each clause to hear that the last word has landed. It doesn't matter. What matters is the interval between, and that I keep it, and that someone keeps it back.

I strike once. The fog is at my knees, warm, sweet. The tubes hum lower as it rises. The grazers around my ankles have gone still in the particular way that means they are listening, or not listening, or that listening and not listening were never different.

I wait.

Across the plateau, a long moment later, something answers.

And out on the far swell, in the last of the light, I see a walker. A smooth, even stride. Stopped now, standing among the tubes with the crust settling around their boots in a way that is not quite the way it settles around a stone. They have felt it. I can tell by how still they've become. They are wondering if they've been added to a count.

They have. I add them myself, gently, with the small remaining part of my hands. One more, standing slow, on the floor of the world, in the warm fog, in the hum.

I don't turn. It is better if I stay a stump.
