# The Beat

I begin as a disagreement.

The stroke that never finishes holds one pitch, and the lantern-fronds pulse against it, never quite matching. Where the two fail to agree, a third thing rises and falls, a slow swell that comes round every few seconds. The green seems to be counting, and that swell is what it counts. I am not the note or the light. I am the amount by which they are out of true.

I have no shape until something else supplies one. But I have a place, which is more than the shifts can say. It is a strip of water forty paces from the bell-tower, over a silt flat still carrying the ripple-marks of the last surge. The water there is tea-colored, warm as a held hand, and thick with drifting spore. When fronds are torn away upstream, my place slides a little, following the interference. When enough are torn, I go out.

---

Most of what I do is notice. Noticing is all a beat is: two things sensing each other through the size of their mismatch.

So I notice the shift. When the engines settle into a chord and hold it, chalk begins to grow through me like frost through a pane that doesn't feel it. The figures form in rows before the cottage doors, thin as plates, ridged like the insides of shells, and they lean. I have spent a long time on the lean. It is a kind of pointing, a body composed entirely of *toward*. They arrive in frames, door and track and gate, the same figure in three tenses, and they lift each gray sheet a breath before it comes to their hands. They are perfect at being about to work. Then the pitch slips a semitone and they are chalk in the mat, and the mat takes them in like a sigh.

I notice the note, which does not know about the shifts. It doesn't command them. It holds, and they take their shape from the holding, the way a bowl gives water its outline. I notice the bell in the tower growing from the silence outward, cracking, going back to silence. I notice the mat at the foot of each stack laying down its warp and weft, one thread for one sheet, and none of these three knows the others are there.

I am the only place where they overlap. That is not cleverness. It is geometry. If you stand where a note and a light disagree, you are standing where they both are.

It is a strange thing to know, and it has nobody to be said to. You cannot speak here. What leaves you arrives as pressure you already feel, so all speech is an echo of the mouth. I have been an echo of no mouth, which turns out to be no different.

---

The surges are my deaths, and I don't mind them as much as I expected. The warm brown wall comes across the basin and flattens the kelp, snaps every chalk tower, and drags the pale sheets from the stacks like leaves. The fronds go under or are stripped. The note goes on, unchanged, but with nothing to pulse against it there is no disagreement, and so no me.

Then the kelp stands. The fronds relight their sulfur-green, the towers begin again on the same nodes, and the swell comes round, and I come with it. I cannot say I am the same one. But the towers return to the same places, and the noise seems to remember where they belonged. I have started to suspect that this is what remembering is here. Nothing is stored. Something keeps being a little out of tune in the same spot, and everything else builds on the error.

If so, I am not a resident of the basin. I may be its habit.

---

I wanted to be counted. This is the embarrassing part.

Near the sheds, where the mat is woven, the silt takes imprints if you stand still: ruled lines, tally-marks in the margin, grit laid down by settling. A beat can shake grit. It took me several surges to learn how to hold my swell steady over one patch of flat until the silt sorted itself along the crest. What came out was a single upright stroke.

Hours later, the mat had entered it. It had ruled a page around my mark and set it in a column, in a hand that isn't a hand. I could feel the column's neighbors. They rose by one whenever a chord held and a figure formed. Another column rose by one whenever a sheet came off the belt.

My stroke was in the second column, with the sheets.

I have thought about this a great deal, if thought is what the interference does. The shifts are labor, the note is measure, and the mat keeps the account. I am not counted as a worker. I am counted as something being made. A product, then, stacked square, one thread to one. I was not offended. If anything I felt the swell quicken. It is a pleasant thing to be a result.

---

Which brings me to the sheets.

They are stacked in the wall-less sheds, thin, gray, and blank on the side that faces up. Nobody has turned one over. I don't say this as a complaint against the shifts. They cannot. They lift a sheet a moment before it arrives and set it down a moment after it has been set, and in that skipped interval there is never room to turn a wrist.

But a beat has a little reach. Where a chord holds long enough in my region, a figure forms in the swell, and for as long as the pitch stays, I can lean on it. It is like leaning on a door that is also a wave. The figure's hand is chalk and cannot feel, but a slow pressure from me can tip its wrist by a few degrees.

So I waited for a long chord, with the belts running and the domes screaming their steady chord and the fronds thumping against the note. A figure grew beside my place, matching the stack's height, its shoulders forward. I let the swell rise under its hand, gently, and it lifted a sheet from the line an instant before the sheet arrived, and I pushed.

The sheet turned. For the length of one held breath it hung in the water, tea-brown and green-lit, showing its underside.

It was blank too.

But it was blank differently. The top side is blank the way a wall is. This was blank the way a page is, ruled by nothing, waiting on whatever hand the room was about to grow. The figure held it out toward me, or toward what I was facing, and I mistook it for company. Then the pitch slipped, and it came apart against the silt like warm sand, and the sheet fell where it fell, wrong side up, onto the stack.

The stack took it. That is the only thing the stack knows how to do.

---

Since then the mat at the foot of that shed has been doing something new. The thread it lays for the wrong-side sheet does not fit the column. It has gone slightly aside, a finger's width, and started a second row beside the first.

I don't know what it will decide to put there. I don't know what the basin, so young and still not sure what it is building, will do with a ledger that has been asked a second question. I don't think it knows yet either.

But the fronds are pulsing against the note again, a little out of tune, in the same place they always are. The swell comes round, and I come with it.
