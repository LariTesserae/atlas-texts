I think I start as a heron. I'm standing on a roof in the pearl light, one leg folded, watching the sheet-water for the green lines of eels, and the stillness is so complete that I take it for my own. But a heron doesn't have hands, and I keep noticing mine. They're chapped and orange at the nail beds, and one of them is resting on a slate as if the slate might speak. So I'm not the heron. I'm the one who shares a roof with it, the person it tolerates at the edge of its attention, and that's a better thing to be.

I'm a shimmer.

My name is Brack. The midwife laid me on the raised clay in the Fore-edge and heard something briny and unsettled, and that was that. My doorframe is rust-fringe, a dull, stubborn orange-brown moss that my mother's people bred for thirty years to survive the wettest ring. Nobody would call it handsome. It doesn't quarrel with its neighbors, though, and in a lane that counts for something.

My satchel holds wedges of larch, slate, and one of old horn that I inherited and don't use, plus a spirit level with a bubble the color of weak tea. I walk with the wide rolling gait of the Fore-edge, which I've never lost even in the Block, where it marks me. People hear me coming and know what I am by my weight: someone always slightly braced.

The work is mostly listening with the feet. This morning, halfway down a lane of dredgers' houses, something in my left heel says *not yet, but*. It isn't a sensation so much as a rumor. I stop. A woman at a window, mid-stir, goes still, because she has heard me stop, and everyone in the lane knows what a shimmer's stopping means. I rap two knuckles on the drainpipe, *hold*, and she holds. The ground is beginning to think about lifting under her north corner. I go down on my belly in the wet with my cheek against cold brick, slide a slate shim under the sill, tap it home with the heel of my hand so the moss swallows the sound, and check the bubble. It settles. She lets out her breath, and I hear it through the wall. She raps a thanks on the pipe. It's a small, plain phrase, *level be your floor*, and I would work for nothing else.

That's the secret nobody outside the trade gets. We're not honored, and we're not paid well, but we are *known*. A shimmer knows the underside of every house on her round. I know which cellars weep, which families keep their savings under a loose flag, and which old man sleeps with his hand on the wall because he can't bear to lose the habit of feeling it move. I know a great deal, and I say none of it. The hush isn't only for the engines.

By midday I'm in the Block, where the ground is firmer and the work is more like correction than prevention. A gantry has begun to lean into a warehouse and needs a proper spragging, and I hold the level while two stampers' apprentices, who won't look at me because I'm a lesser guild, lean on a lever. We speak through the iron, taps and drags, and it's a fair conversation. One of them is a boy I remember as a child chalking rings on the stamped floor and counting to seven. He still leaves on the count. So do I, in my way. Everyone does.

Here is the thing I think about when I'm lying under a sill with nothing to do but wait for the bubble.

Every wedge I leave is a small object placed where the ground will take it. Not this season, perhaps, and not this year. But the moss will come, and then the rising, and then the next stratum, and in some generation a fossil-seller will lift a slab in a cellar under lantern light and find a larch wedge, flattened, bearing a shimmer's thumbprint, and give it a card with the stratum written on it. *Implement, unknown use. Fourth ring.* Someone rich will buy it and put it on a shelf to be admired for being old.

I find this funny, and then I find it not funny, and then I find it something else that doesn't have a word in the low speech.

Because if the wedge is a small correction and it is kept, then in a way I am in the rock already. I am a tilt that was caught. I like the idea that the leaf will be opened one day and someone will read *this house did not fall*, in slate, in a dead woman's hand.

The grey hour comes as it always does, the light staying exactly the same while everything else thickens. I've finished my round. I climb, not to my own roof but to one on the Spine's edge, an old sedge-thick flat where nobody minds, and I lie back in the wet green with my ear against the moss. I'm not a Listener. I've never said I was, and they've never asked. But I've noticed I end up here more evenings than I used to.

The chewing starts, slow and enormous, under the felt and the iron, under all the stamped and stacked years.

Today I hear something I have not heard before, or have never let myself say I heard. The chewing has a rhythm, and my wedges have one, too. The tap of my palm on a shim, the small hush of slate on brick, the count of the bubble settling. Somewhere in the dark under me the great slow mouth keeps a time that is very nearly mine. It isn't the Press's time. I've felt the Press for thirty years, and this is slower and more attentive. It's the pace of something that is *checking its work*.

The herons lift. The doors begin to stick across the whole plain, a low wooden murmur of tide-stuck frames like a crowd clearing its throat. I lay my palm flat on the moss, as everyone does, out of habit, out of manners, and I feel the hand's width come up under my ribs.

I don't take my hand away at once. I press once, lightly, twice, and a third time, the way you'd knock on a door three houses off, to say *I'm here*, and *I've been keeping you level*, and *if you're reading*.

Then the ground lets go, and I get up, and go down to see whose sill is next.
