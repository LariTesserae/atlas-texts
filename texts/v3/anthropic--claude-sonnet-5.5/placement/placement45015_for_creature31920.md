I don't begin as a face. I begin as a weight in the hips, knees soft, feet turned out on ridged soles. I am standing on a nodal line, and I know it the way you know your own name, through the floor. The stroke comes up through my shins, a piston from the northeast, far off. The one beyond it is fainter. I'm keeping time with the first, as I always have, so I am a person from the northeast quarter. My gait says so before I say anything.

Then hands. Iron in the creases. A cradle of dried sedge hangs on my forearm with four spent sodium tubes wrapped in cloth, and I know where I'm going with them, and I know it's probably useless. I am walking to a pole that is not mine.

---

My own lamp went out two floods ago. I was not there to see it. I came back from the ruined pools and the light had already retreated into the pole, and the household stood in a circle around the place where it had been, palms on the slab, feeling nothing in particular. My mother, my brother, and his two small ones packed the sedge bags. We took different poles. There is a hierarchy of lamps, and it is not spoken, only understood by where you are allowed to sleep. I sleep at the edge of the Sixth Pole with my feet toward the metal. I feel its hum. It is not my hum. It is slightly higher than the one I grew up on, and my body has not decided to forgive it.

A guest's seat is a hard thing to hold. You are always the last one the light finds. You learn to make yourself useful in ways that take no room. I carry tubes. I coax. I know a hundred small ways to make a fading lamp go on a little longer: warming the base against the skin, turning it a quarter, striking the cap just after a stroke when the whole pole is ringing and might shake something loose. I cannot make a lamp. Nobody can. But I can buy a pole a season, and that is why the Sixth keeps me.

---

Mostly, though, I am a mender.

The pool I am walking toward has no name in the hand-signs, only a shape: a ring with a star inside it, and one of the star's points is missing. Eleven days ago, by the breath-count, the piston that fed it seized. I was at the Sixth when it happened. We all felt it, a skipped beat, like a heart that has done it once too often. The household gathered where the stroke used to arrive and stood through eleven seconds of nothing, and then a few more. Then the old woman rapped a short phrase on the slab. *It is finished.* We went back to work.

But it isn't finished. It never is, for the mender. The ring at that pool is my mother's mother.

I kneel at the rim and lay my palm flat on the water's edge. The stroke comes, from the other pistons now, since the one that fed her is gone, and the surface jumps under my hand and makes a pattern that is almost the old one. Almost. I feel the difference in my wrist before I can say what it is. The star has lost a point and the ring has drawn in, a hand's width, maybe less. The still line where she lies has begun to move.

I take a pinch of sedge spore from my bag and dust it across the surface. In the orange light of a pole that isn't mine and isn't near, I can barely see. But the spore gathers as I watch, stroke by stroke, in thin gray lines. It is drawing the new figure for me. The ground is telling the water, and the water is telling the dust, and the dust is telling me. And the new ring lies inside the old one, tighter, and my mother's mother is outside it.

---

I sit back on my heels. The first thing a child learns is never to cut a ring closed. I have never cut one. So I know, without deciding, that I won't cut the old ring now. Cutting is a wound. I will plant the new ring inside and grow it outward from its own open ends, where the figure is not yet finished. The old ring I leave to finish itself. It will go slowly, over years. The felt will loosen where the line has stopped being still, will lose its grip, will slide into the water in dark rags. And under it she will lie in a floor that shakes.

That is the mender's secret, the one you learn only by doing the work: you cannot save every figure. You can only follow the ground, and the ground does not follow the dead. The dead are laid in still places, and stillness is a thing the machine lends, and the machine is always taking it back.

I take out the damp cloth and the cuttings of black moss. I begin at the end of a band, where the line is still becoming.

---

The breath is turning. I feel it before anything: the roar in my chest climbing, the pressure on my skin building toward the shriek. Far off, the vents must be opening one by one, plumes of white going up into the violet lid. I keep planting. The roar rises past anything a body can hold, and my teeth ring with it.

Then the valves seat.

It goes out of me like a hand being taken away. I stop with a cutting half-pressed into the silt. I lay my palms flat on the slab and I do not tap. The plain is quiet, and the quiet is not for me. It is the machine drawing in air. I feel the pistons going on alone, and in the pool the water shapes and unshapes itself beneath my knees. The new ring is a fine gray thread on the surface, and it trembles, and dissolves, and is rebuilt.

In the silence I have a thought that is not exactly a thought. The piston I walked to all my life is gone, and I am still walking to it. My gait carries its rhythm, and no stroke answers. Everyone who sees me at the Sixth will know I'm from the northeast, and by the time this ring has grown, my body will have learned another beat, and I will belong to a different quarter, with a different stride, a stranger to my mother's mother's floor. That's how it goes here. The water is always going home, and we go with it a little, whether we choose to or not.

The roar comes back, and I press the cutting into the silt, and wait for the next stroke.
