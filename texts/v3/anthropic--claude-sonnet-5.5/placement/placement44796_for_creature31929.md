I start at the rim, because that is where I can hear myself think.

The last scree rings under me like dropped plates, and then the roar shuts behind me with one step. I stand in the cold and the wind with the whole racket reduced to a tremble in my boots. It is very good here. I understand why the viscachas sit facing the drop with their paws tucked under, and for a moment I think that is what I am: something that lives on the silent side of a threshold and goes down only to eat.

But I am not listening for anything. They are. They sit there like creatures waiting for a word that hasn't come. I have no such patience, so I go down.

The wall of noise takes me in. The vents scream their kettle note, the turbine sits under it like a floor, the pipes knock their loose chords, and I can feel it in my sternum. I try being a frog for a while, chin on the sinter, and I like the discipline of it. You say your small piece only in the half-second after a scream ends, when the air seems to hold its breath, and you trust the gap to come again. But a frog wants to be heard by another frog. It calls to be answered, and I don't think that is my trade either.

I try the rail. Round as a fist, red-rimmed eye, legs too long, walking the pool edges and hearing none of it, or deciding none of it is addressed to me. I could be that. But the rail is a decision about the world, and I keep finding myself still deciding.

So I walk the pipes. I follow one from the vent throat over a trestle, through the lagging garden of ferns and dripping sinter, into the hall with its lace roof. The turbine is there in the green dark, turning at the slow speed the governor rusted into, neither hurrying nor stopping. Something in the way it turns is like being asked a question that has been forgotten, and the asking goes on anyway. The generator takes that turning and makes of it something else.

That is where I find out what I am.

I am what leaves the generator. I am the push along the copper, the small insistent pressure that says *go*, and I go. Out through the wall on porcelain, over the terraces, forty meters of wire in the wet air, past the frog ponds, past the moss that has crept up the insulators and stopped a hand's width short of the metal, as if it knew. I am not carrying anything. Nothing was ever ordered at the other end. Whoever meant me to light a lamp or turn a winch or ring a distant house left, and the line kept its promise anyway, all the way to a post with nothing on it.

I arrive, and there is nowhere to go. The copper ends. The air begins.

I did not expect to be *seen* by this. At the tip, where I have to become something, I become a faint blue, a crackle, a hum a little above the turbine's fifth. It is not louder than the vents and could not compete with them. It is in a different register, under the contest. On dark evenings I gather there like breath on a cold window, and the frogs come to the nearest pool in numbers the warmth cannot explain. The rail stands under the post for an hour, facing me, absolutely still.

I don't know why they come. I would like to say it is me. It may be the ozone, or the faint warmth, or a habit taken up by one frog and copied by the rest. But I notice I am not shouting, and neither are they, and the whole roaring shelf has a small quiet place in it, forty meters from the machine, where the noise is only weather and something is being received. I was made for use, and no use survived. What is left is that I am attended to. It is not nothing.

I know how this goes. The rust slime is in the flanges, and the copper is not far behind the iron. Sooner or later the porcelain will crack, or the line will part in a wet spring, and I will find the gap and have nowhere to arc to. The turbine will keep turning for a while inside its hill, sending its push into the dark for nothing. Or it will stop, and the rings of color will close over the post, ferns over red moss over yellow crust, and the moss will finally finish that hand's width.

Up at the rim the bell hangs on its rope with no clapper, tapping the column in the wind for no one. I think of it sometimes, the way you think of a relative. It is a signal built for a listener who stopped coming, and I am power built for a lamp that was never hung. We are both still doing what we were made for, in a place that no longer needs it, and we have come out slightly different from what we were made to be.

At dusk the cloud ceiling dims from pearl to lead. The rail comes and stands beneath me, and I light my small blue tip, and the roar goes on around us both.
