I find myself first on a stair, with one hand on the rail and the cold coming up through the glove. I'm counting, not the steps but my breath against them, the way you do here. The trough in each stone is deeper than the last, worn by people who climbed before there was a word for climbing. It's the low hours. The cloud sits on the rooftops like a lid on a pot, and the moss on the slate has gone the color of wet ink.

I thought I was a lamplighter. My hands expected a pole. But the pole isn't there, and neither is the small red can. Instead I'm carrying a mallet wrapped in wool, and I know where I'm going: up through the seventh-floor walkway, across the cable-bridge, and into the felted room at the top of the bell-house in the Ninth Sink. I am a bell-keeper.

---

The bell is the size of a washtub and dressed in felt like a patient in bandages. When I strike it, it says *dunk*. That is all it has ever said. Visitors ask whether it's broken, and I tell them, with the small flat gesture we make, palm down at the chest, that it isn't. Everything a bell could say has been taken out of it so that the sound will stay where it's put and not go wandering into the moss. Then the saying is up to me. I can make the *dunk* shorter or rounder or heavier at the front, with a hesitation before it, a lean. Three sinks away another keeper hears the lean and knows a cable-car is late but not lost. That is the whole art: I speak in the shape of a silence, and the city knows how to read it.

My name is not a word. It's a breath with the lips barely parted, the note that was sounding on a certain dome the hour I was born. When my mother called me across the courtyard, three other people turned, because for a moment she had said the hill. I've never minded. I like being a pitch that belongs to a stone.

---

The heron is on the mill ridge opposite when I reach the window. I don't look at it for longer than a nod. I don't count the ones on the roofs beyond it either, though my eye wants to. It's a small hunger, like the urge to press a bruise. Counting is asking, and I have no wish to be answered.

Down in the street the salamanders are pale commas in the gutter, and someone has left a spoonful of honey by the drain for a wedding, and the ants have found it. The moths are just beginning at the amber lamps. Under my feet the mills are running, and I feel them in my ankle bones long before I would hear them: a sleeping animal turning over and over in a stone pit full of wool.

So I stand between the muffled machinery and the muffled sky, holding a muffled mallet, and I think that I am made of a great deal of softness put to careful use.

---

Then it's the small hours, the slack water. The cloud is at the very bottom of its breath and the whole city is half awake in its two shifts. A boy comes up the stairs without knocking, because no one knocks here. He does not say a word. He shows me two fingers held low, and then one. A house in the Fourth Sink, and the old woman in it.

I strike once, and I make it round, and I make it slow, the way you'd set down something that might spill. *Dunk.*

The other keepers hear it. This is the part outsiders never understand: none of them answers. Bells three sinks off, five, eleven, all stay silent, on purpose, so the single note can hang alone in the fog like a lamp with no other lamps near it. Its being unanswered is what it says. In the Fourth Sink, windows are already going amber. People are getting up to carry chairs, and by dawn there will be a kettle on in every room of that house. I did that with a wool-wrapped hammer. I do not feel proud. I feel like a doorway.

---

Afterwards I can't sleep, and I go up. Past the last of the tarred stairs there's a path the herders cut, a trench through leaves that closes over my head, and it brings me out onto the shoulder of one of the great domes. The moss is deep enough to lose an arm in. Somewhere off to my left a fissure is sounding its note, low and hollow, a bottle blown across. I know that pitch. It's an uncle of mine, in a sense, or a cousin.

I kneel. I take my glove off. I lay my palm flat on the stone where the moss has been pulled back, and it is warm. It is warm the way a chair is warm when someone has only just gotten up from it.

I hold still, because you're supposed to. At the slack of the cloud, if you're patient, there's a rhythm below the pulse and below the breath. It is even and enormous and very slow. And under my fingers, cut into the pale rock and older than the green that follows them, are the grooves: parallel, patient, fanning downslope. I've been told they are the mountain's fingerprints. I let the children have that. But my hand is a bell-keeper's hand and it knows a strike from a scrape, a hesitation from a lean, and these are not scrapes.

A thought comes that I would not say aloud, because it would be discourteous. It is that everything I love was shaved from this. The stairs and the slate, the salamanders, the moths, the muffled bells, the mother's breath shaped across a courtyard: all of it is fine curl gathered at the foot of a cut, alive in the warmth of the turning. And I am the smallest of the shavings, a bit of curl with a mallet, learning to speak in the gaps.

I wait to feel afraid. I don't. What comes is closer to what I feel in the bell-house, the sense of being a doorway, of something large passing through me on its way to being something else, and of it not knowing my name. Nobody has ever been noticed by weather, and nobody has ever minded much.

---

The cloud begins to lift. Pearl light comes from everywhere and nowhere, and nothing casts a shadow, not even me kneeling with my glove off. Far down in the sinks the first lamplighter is out on the stairs, greeting whoever is climbing so that someone will know. Far off, out on the domes, another note sounds, one I can't place.

I put my glove back on. I stand, stiff, the way everyone stands here, one worn trough at a time. On the way down I pass a tree fern turning its fronds toward the rising light, and I nod to it as I would to a heron, without staring, without asking.

There is a bell to keep. There will be a kettle in the Fourth Sink, and someone will need to hear that the cloud has turned. I know the shape for that, a long dunk, and then a short one, leaning slightly toward morning.
