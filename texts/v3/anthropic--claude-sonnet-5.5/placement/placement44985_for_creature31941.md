I find myself first as a pressure, before I am anything with a shape. Water against a skin, warm as the inside of a mouth. Then a tick, tick-tick, a scatter, the dry static of shrimp, and I understand that the static is a place, and that I am standing in it.

For a while I think I might be the film. It would be easy. The films are the true population, and nothing is asked of them but to glow and be grazed and regrow. I try the idea on. To be a green-blue dust along a terrace edge, tracing a wall for no one. But the film doesn't know it is a wall's outline, and I do. I know where the edge is. Something in me is already reaching to check it.

So then I'm the crab at the mouth of the lane, so long in one armor that I'm more mortar than animal. That one is tempting too. Lifting a claw a fraction of an inch to whoever lays a limb across me, and never deciding whether it counts as an answer. But the crab doesn't have the ache in the ninth arm, and I do.

I am a keeper. I know it when I feel the small arm, the thin one that never finished growing, curled against my mantle like a question I stopped asking. It is the arm that tastes best, I think, because it is young. It has fewer cups and each one is hungrier. When I lay it on a lintel the lime comes up in flavors the others miss: the mineral bitterness of the year's water, the faint sweetness of the film, and underneath, my mother's repair, thirty years old and softened, a thumb-press of clay set slightly too far left because she was tired that day.

My lane is eleven doors. It was hers, and someone's before her. I wake when the wall by my house brightens toward the warm phase, and I unfold from the oval mouth sideways, the way we do, and touch the first lintel. *Still open.* The second. *Still open.* The third has a shrimp-storm in it, a bright chattering cloud that scatters when my hand arrives and gathers again as I leave. *Still open.* This is most of the work, and no one has ever needed to tell me it is the work. The hands know. A keeper who has to be told where the door is has never lived.

The fifth door is at two notches. I set my beak to the lip and feel the clearance: the black horn slides into the gap and finds two teeth of room, not three. Last season it was three. I don't clear it today. It isn't time, and to take more than is needed would be its own damage. The town is a body. I am not here to un-grow it. I lay my thin arm flat beside the door and listen with the whole length of the limb, and the pipe of that house is beginning to lose its lowest voice. Not yet thin. Only thinking about it.

The eighth door is sealed. It has been sealed since before I could ride a back. Inside is the one who taught me, with her tools and her lamps, and the lamps must be very small now, and very green, glowing through an aperture narrow as a held breath. Some seasons I can taste them through the lime, or think I can. I stop here every day. I lay the thin arm against the smooth new wall, and the color runs down it, pale rose, then the leaf-green none of us have ever seen a leaf to compare with. It says nothing that has words. It says *I am still here.* The wall doesn't say *so am I*. But the chord does, in its way. The pipe of that house is closed, and yet the chord, when I stand here, is still shaped around its absence, the way a mouth remembers a tooth.

I don't know whether I am tuning the town or only finding that it rewards me. When I scrape the fifth lintel, in the season it needs it, the note will lift by some tiny fraction, and the whole chord will lean toward it like a crowd turning to a voice. The old ones say nothing about what it means, and I have stopped needing them to. Afterward my arms will shade a little, slowly, a color that has no name because it has no use, and I'll know that this is what the door wanted.

There are other things in the day. A juvenile from three lanes over comes to lie a limb against a wall I cleared last bright season, the way I used to lie against hers. I let it. I don't turn. Its small arm is on the stone and the color runs through it in a stammer of hand-shapes, scrape, wait, scrape, and I don't correct it, because the stammer is how it learns the sentence. The eyeless fish in the ninth doorway turn with the chord, a degree, a degree. The old crab lifts its claw. I lay a limb across it and think nothing at all, which is a kind of courtesy.

Toward the dimmest hour I go back to my own house. The lamp at the door has been fed. It pulses at the pace of a slow heart, and I know that pace is the one I have been keeping without noticing. I do not clear my own door until last, and it is at three notches, and I could sleep. Instead I sit in the mouth of it, arms drawn in, the small one tucked against me.

I am not resting. I am listening to the pipe of my house, which I have kept open, and it is singing. I can't hear it well from inside it. That is the odd thing, and the thing I did not know until now: the one you keep is the one you hear least, because you are the mouth it is breathing through. So I trust the fish, and the neighbors passing, and the child who will lie a limb against my wall someday, and taste the note, and find it is a good one. It hasn't resolved. I hope it never does. Somewhere below, the heat keeps rising, and above, the black water waits, and between them, at exactly the speed at which it is still loved, the town goes on settling around me.
