I am, first, the light. Before I know I have a body, I know that it comes from everywhere at once, from the stone as much as from the sky, and that it has no edges. That is how it is for me now. The shade has gone soft and the glare has gone solid, and the world is a white field with a few dark strokes drawn in it.

Then there is a hand in mine, small and damp and impatient. *Venn.* She has led me since the winter I stopped being able to find the stairs by sight. She is nine, she is proud, and she walks the pour-step a little too carefully, like a girl who is being watched. I let her steer me. My feet know the way anyway, heel and roll and toe, the knees loose, the body carried like a brimming bowl. I am seventy-one, and I have never spilled anything I was trusted with.

So I am a watcher, milk-eyed. My grandmother was one, and my mother, and my mother's mother, who at the end could not tell her own daughter's face from a lamp but could read a mirror three terraces down faster than most people can talk.

The station is a stone cup on the mid-slope, open to the sky, with a low wall at the height of my ribs. Venn settles me against it and goes to sit in the shade of the stair mouth, where she will draw shells in the wet with her finger. I take off nothing, because I wear no visor. The white comes up into my face like water into a basin, and the world resolves.

What I see is not a picture. Down the slope and up the slope, the mirrors are opening and closing, tiny hard sparks with a rhythm inside each one. Nobody outside the trade understands this: we do not read the code, or not only. We read the hand. Every polisher has a way of making a flash, a lean on the long, a tremor on the stop, a way of hurrying near the end of a sentence. I could tell you who is sending before I know what they say. The boy at the condenser field who is always a little late. The two sisters at the brine mill who send in alternation, so that the message seems to be arguing with itself. And Oro.

Oro is at the upper station, four kilometers away and forty years back. She has sent me the morning greeting since before we were old enough to be embarrassed by it. Her hand has a hitch on the third beat, a small hesitation where she seems to be deciding whether to go on, and then she does. It is the most familiar thing in my life. I could pick it out of a thousand.

This morning the greeting does not come.

I read the cistern counts. I read a birth in the lower cluster, a girl, seven pounds, both well. I read a request for a saw wire, a complaint about a leaning pylon, a dull argument about a delayed cable shipment. All of it in other people's hands. Where Oro's spark should be, there is a patch of stone, and the stone is only stone.

Then, near noon, when the heat has pushed everyone into the covered stairs and the slope has gone still, a spark I don't know opens at her station and tells me, slowly and carefully, in the hand of someone who has learned the flashes but not the rhythm, that Oro laid down her mirror at dawn and did not pick it up again.

I say nothing. Palm-speech would be the wrong tool, and low voice would be a small dishonesty. I sit against the wall with the glare on my face and my hands flat on the warm stone, and I do the thing the Settled do best, which is to make no disturbance. Venn looks up from her shells. She does not ask. She has heard how still my breathing has become and she draws closer, and puts her ear near my hip like a child listening at a wall.

---

The afternoon goes to mist. I hear it more than see it, the change in the hush, the air thickening against my skin as the cloud gathers over the ridge. I know the hour by the weight in my clothes. Then the first drops hit the roofs, soft and dense, and the stairs open, and everyone comes out.

I have never known what to do with myself in the rain. I am a person of small sounds and careful hands. But I stand at the wall with my face turned up and my mouth open, and it is warm as a mouth, and for the first time all day I hear my own voice come out of me, hoarse and cracked and much too large.

"Oro," I say. Then again. Then a great many things that are not her name: that she was late every day of her life, that she cheated at the bowl game and was never caught, that in the winter my eyes went she sent the greeting twice as slowly, so that I could follow it. I say it to no one, into the roar. Venn is beside me, shouting something of her own. On the next station a man is laughing so hard he has to sit down. The whole slope is a warm crowd bellowing in a warm downpour, and no one can hear anyone, and that is the mercy of it. For forty minutes I am as loud as I have ever been.

Then the rain thins, and my voice thins with it, and by the last drops I am back to breath.

---

At dusk the amber comes. The terraces go pink, then gold, then the color of the inside of a shell. Down the whole slope, the mirrors begin the long flash for the dead.

I do not read it. I do not need to. It comes from the upper station, from a hand I don't know, slow, unhitched, a little too correct, and I take it in my own mirror and pass it down. A single long flash, held. I have made it perhaps a dozen times. My mirror is the size of a tray and I have polished it every morning for fifty years, and now it throws a bar of light down the slope to the next station, which takes it, and the next, and I follow it with my eyes as it goes, terrace by terrace, until it reaches the lower edge and dies there.

There is a spire in the basin below the station. Venn does not know I know. It has grown there for as long as I have had eyes to see it, thin and white and straight as a held breath. Children are taught never to whisper near it. They are taught this before they can read.

I send Venn up to the roof for water. She goes, doubtful, and I wait until her footsteps are gone. Then I go down the three steps to the basin edge on my own, by touch, heel and roll and toe, and I kneel. The stone is warm. The water is so still I can see a ring where a drop has fallen, out at its far edge, traveling toward me.

I do not know what the old ones say. I did not know until now that I would say anything. But my mouth finds the shape, and it is not her name and it is not a prayer. I tell the spire about the hitch on the third beat. I tell it how she hesitated, every time, as if deciding whether to go on, and how she always did. I say that this morning, for the first time in forty years, she decided otherwise, and that I understand it, and that I was not ready.

I don't know whether the spire leans. I am nearly blind and the light is going. But I hear something, very small, at the edge of hearing, a tick like sleet on paper. Then another, and then a great many, running outward from the base of the stone across the water, fading as they go. It is a soft, whole, spreading sound, like a page being turned for the last time by a very patient hand.

I am not surprised. I kneel there with my palms on the warm stone and listen to the basin go white, and I think: *So that's what it was. That's all it ever was.* Not a punishment, not a warning. Only a place that wanted, badly, to hear.

Behind me, on the stair, Venn has stopped with the cup in her hands. She is standing very still. She has never dropped anything in her life.

I put my finger to my lips, though she can't see it against the light, and after a moment she comes down and kneels beside me. Together we listen to the last of the ticking go out across the water.
