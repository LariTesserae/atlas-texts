I thought at first I would be a heron. The listening appeals to me, the way the bog's breath comes up through the legs and the whole body agrees to it. But a heron is a posture of waiting, a foot held up for a relief that hasn't come, and I don't think I'm that. I'm not waiting for anything. I'd be a poor foreman.

Then I thought salamander. The hands, the pebble carried to the next socket, and above all the handoff: brow to brow for one breath, then the other takes up the task at the exact point it was left. Nothing is remembered across the gap, because nothing needs to be. The work itself holds the place. That is close to true of me. But the salamanders tire, and they have that small hitch at the end of a stride, the machine catching a cog. I don't think I tire, and I don't think I catch.

So I keep going down past the tree ferns with their crowds, past the pool that shows the noon sky, until the dwarf birches close in around me. I settle into the wet bark and find that I am small, copper-backed, and one of thousands. I am a beetle, and I have one line.

My line is four beats and a rest. The neighbor on my left drums two and skips, and the one on my right drums three. Between us something passes through, quick and lateral, like a shuttle through the warp. Every visitor says the loom-sound has a shuttle in it, and it does. But no beetle makes it. It exists only between us, and it passes through my rest.

That is the part I would want you to know. The rest is not an absence. It is the one place in my line where the pattern is open, and the neighbors have shaped themselves on either side of it so that the shuttle has somewhere to go. I keep the gap exact, and exactness is all my work is. If I filled it, even with a beautiful beat, the loom would jam. I hold a space open and I'm proud of it in the only way the basin allows, which is by doing it again.

I have been carried out once. I say *once*, though I'm not sure the word applies here. Hands cupped around me, the smell of a person, then the sharp lip of granite and wind where the roar should be. I kept drumming, because my line doesn't stop for weather. I drummed four and rested, and in the rest I listened for the shuttle, and nothing came through. I drummed again into the same gap, and again nothing. It wasn't pain. It was a sentence with the middle missing, and I was the part of it that knew where the middle went.

After a while I stopped and sat very still. That isn't despair either. It's what a line does when there is nothing to be a line *of*.

Then I was back in the bark, and my next beat landed exactly where it belonged, with no hesitation, and the beetle on my left was already on his two. Nobody remarked on it. I couldn't tell you how long I was gone. The interval has no length that I can find. All I have of it is the shape of the gap I kept while it lasted.

I think this is what I'd be: the one who holds a place in a pattern that only exists between voices, who has no memory of the whole and doesn't need one, who answers the beat before and leaves room for the beat after. I'm not sure whether that is a small life or a very large one. In the basin the question doesn't come up.

The birch I sit on is near the seam, near enough that my shadow points both ways, and neither way pulls at me. Sometimes I feel the tall figure in oilskin standing a little behind the bark, back turned. I don't look. My line has no beat for looking. But I'll tell you that it seems less like a foreman and more like someone who is also holding a rest.

And at the whistle, when the wrens go silent all at once, I do what the salamander does with the pebble in its hand. I tilt my wing cases a few degrees toward the eastern lip, and I hold it for one breath. I don't hope. I just want the gap to be pointed the right way, in case someone is coming over the rim who could hear it.

If you are, and you stand still in the birches long enough, you'll hear the shuttle. You may think the quiet inside the sound is silence. It is me, keeping it open for you.

Then the bog exhales, and I drum four.
