# What the Basin Keeps

I wake, and for a while I am not sure which breath is mine.

The fern-fists open over me and the air comes down warm and tasting of wet coins. I breathe in with them. They fold, and I let it go. Only when I try to hold my breath a moment, to check, do I feel the two rhythms come apart. Mine wants to run a little faster than theirs. It always has. My mother said it was a sign I would be a restless old woman, and I am, in the way that counts here: I move a little, always.

The moss beneath my hip is warm where I've been lying, and cool an inch beyond. When I rise I'll leave a hollow, and someone will find it and know that a person slept here and went on. That is as much of a mark as any of us make.

---

I am called for the place where my mother first laid me down, a slope where the ground gives back a little under your weight. It is a good name. It's a description of how it feels to be held, which is the only thing a name should have to say.

I am not young. The lantern moss on my shoulders has gone patchy, gray at the edges where it thins, and the children sometimes touch it to see if it's cold. I lie here with my eyes closed, and I listen, because that is my use to the others. Someone has to know the plateau by its sounds.

*Tick*, from the cedar side. That's only condensation, leaf to leaf. The mist came up late last night and is still going, dragging its fingers along the eastern shoulder.

Beetles, a long way down the slope. Their shuffle is like someone folding cloth very slowly.

The hum. I don't so much hear it as lie on it. It's stronger this season. The mats in the low place have moved onto fresh stone and are eating well.

And then, far off and perfect, one bell-note.

I hold still. It rings once through the whole table of the world and is gone. Heavy, I think. Old bed, and the bar settling as it cools. Somewhere past the long saddle. The line of the Ashen Sisters has grown by one, and I know whose it is, because I felt her go into the mat four winters ago. I close my hand as if to hold the sound, and then let it open, because sounds aren't kept.

There. That is the news.

---

By the time the mist thins I am walking, one hand loosely raised near my face, the way we all walk near the cedars. The felt takes my feet and lets them go with a small wet sigh. A herd of grazers drifts along the next rise, forty or fifty, their lichen-threaded coats a slow constellation. One lifts its head. We regard each other with the same wet black attention. Then it goes back to the orchard it was working, and I go on.

The stand I'm making for is a small one, seven copper-cedars, thick as barrels and no taller than my chin. I put my palm to the nearest for a moment. The bark is warm as a sleeper's back. A vent-wren clicks at me from the crook of a branch, a short tapping phrase, and I click back what I can. My clicks are clumsy and the wren has always found this funny. I think it is teaching me.

And there, past the seventh tree, is the one I came for.

It stands where it has stood for eleven years. The cords of its body have gone the color of the ground. The moss has reached the knees and is starting over them, in a soft blue-green pelt, thinner than mine. The lantern of its head is closed. Nothing about it says *sister*. Nothing says anything.

*That one stood too long,* we say. We say it of every stalker, and it might even be true of all of them.

I don't know that she's this one. I want to be honest about that, here where nobody can hear me. My sister went among the cedars the winter after our mother lay down, and did not come back to the circuit, and the stalkers took no one that season that anyone saw. That is all. It is not knowledge. It's a shape my mind returns to, the way a tongue returns to a gap in the teeth.

But I come every year when the mist is thin. I look at the moss on the knees, and I measure it against last year's with my eyes, the way you would measure a child against a doorframe if we had doorframes. It has gone up a finger's width. She, or it, is being slowly kept by the ground.

"I'm still moving," I whisper. It is a small sound, and the cedars take it. "I shift. I've always shifted. You know I can't be still."

The lantern does not open. Nothing answers, and I did not expect it to.

I shift my weight anyway, left foot to right, right to left, one breath and then another.

---

Here is what I have never said aloud, even in whispers, even to the wrens.

I have watched the stalker stand and I have watched the old ones lie down, and I have come to think they are the same act turned in different directions. Both are a long stillness. Both end with the moss climbing. The difference is only in what the stillness is for. The stalker stands to keep. It holds itself upright against the ground's slow claim, it takes and takes, and it glows from what it has fed on. The one who lies down is doing something else. She lets go of the shape she has been carrying, so that the mat can have it, and the beetles, and the long gray rank.

One is a fist. The other is an open hand.

I'm not sure I'd have understood this if I hadn't kept coming to the cedars. My sister, if it's she, has taught me the difference by being on the wrong side of it.

---

I walk back the long way, by the low place where the mats are working. The rock hums under my feet and I can feel it climb my shins. A grazer stands cheek by cheek with a child of the Lay at one of the ingot ranks, both of them licking the gray bar in the same slow, unashamed way. The child looks up, sees me, and lifts a hand near her face. I lift mine.

I stop at the edge of the mat. It lies there like wet leather, dark and faintly steaming, and I crouch and lay my palm flat on it. A few degrees above the air. Patient warmth, the warmth of something occupied.

Not yet. I take my hand away. I'm not ready, and I know what ready feels like because I have watched it come over others: a loosening, a kind of politeness toward the ground. It has not come over me. I still click at wrens, and I still count the moss on a stalker's knees.

But I notice, standing up, that I've stopped shifting my weight. I've been standing a while at the mat's edge without meaning to, and the felt has closed around my feet.

I laugh, the smallest laugh, half breath, half click, and I throw none of it away. I lift one foot out of the moss with its wet reluctant sigh, and then the other, and I go on. Behind me the ferns in the next basin open, and fold, and open, and the mat keeps warm for whoever comes next, and somewhere out past the long saddle a bar of iron is cooling in its bed, with my neighbor's name in it and no name at all.
