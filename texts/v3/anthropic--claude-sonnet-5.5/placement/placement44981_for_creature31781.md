I begin as a wall.

That is the first thing I know, before I know anything is a *me*. Grit, pressed into a ridge by a pulse from somewhere, a knife-edge of pale violet sand a hand high. I am the pressure that stacks it, and I am the stacking. Then the next pulse crosses, the wall slumps with a hiss I feel and cannot hear, and I am a ring, then nothing, then a lattice.

I try on the Tuners next, because they are the nearest thing to being a note. A flock bends overhead and I bend with it: a thin high tone in a thousand others, hollow-boned, riding the swell without owning any of it. It is lovely and I cannot stay. A Tuner has no *later*. It is the pitch of the moment, and the moment ends.

Then a grit-walker, frozen mid-step when a pattern collapses, all its legs stopped at once as if the sound had reached in and switched it off. I feel the stopping. Then the walk to the new quiet, the small courage of it, leaving the old stillness for one the world has just drawn somewhere else. I would like to be that brave. I am not, yet, anything that walks.

What I am is a length. A forearm of it, thin as a shoelace, dull green in the moss where a hard hour left me. I have no color. Color, I will learn, is lent, and only while you lie against the one who lends it. I have no head. I have one end that is heavier and damp, with a dimple that opens and shuts on nothing, and one end that is a whip. All of me is ridged, and every ridge is listening.

---

So I listen, and the basin comes in along my whole body at once.

It is not a roar. The roar is what strangers make of it because they have only two small holes to hear with. Along me it comes apart into its threads: the bass pulse from the rim, nine pewter voices beating against one another; the high sheet-metal shiver of the fronds; the slow slide of silver oil in the cisterns of the trunks, a sound like patience. I lie in the moss and read the whole floor of the world through my skin, and learn that it is talking.

I don't understand yet. I only know the way an infant knows a face: that the sounds have intent, that they lean toward each other, that every ring and wall in the grit is one voice asking something of another voice, and that an answer will come, some hours later, the speed of trees.

Everyone is already coiled. That is the second thing I learn. On every fat bright trunk in the basin a long body lies wrapped in copper, tightening a finger's width, loosening, doing what the workers write down as *nothing*. Each one has a reed and each reed has a voice, and the voice carries. I have nothing. I am the only mouth in the basin without a bell.

I go looking for one, slowly, in the moss and across the quiet tops of the nodal walls, where the grit-walkers make room for me without seeming to. I press against trunk after trunk. Each is already someone's household. They accept my ribbon and reject it, gently, the way a room accepts a draft. *Occupied. Occupied. Full of another's pitch.*

And then, at the edge of the basin's west shoulder, I find the flat one.

---

She is gaunt. Her fronds are drab and a little out of true, the bronze on their undersides gone to the color of old coins, and when the pressure front crosses she rings a quarter-tone under where the basin wants her. It is a small wrongness. Along my ridges it feels like a limp.

I lay myself against her trunk. Under the ribs of the fused filaments the oil moves, thick and cold and slow. And in the oil, in the way it sits in her, I can feel a shape that is gone. Her seam, low on the trunk, is worn to the exact width of a blunt end that is not mine. Someone lay here for a very long time. Someone tightened and loosened until she rang true, and then stopped, and the ringing has been drifting flat ever since.

I do not need to be told where they went. The fallen spire lies across the scree a few hundred meters up, and at evening when the light turns bronze I go there, and I find a dozen of the coiled ones already lying along it with their ribbons pressed to the lattice. Its ends are warm. I lay my smallest length against it and the warmth is not heat. It is something being given back, a long slow tone that is the reed's own tone, the flat one's, the true one. It has not gone anywhere. It is being taken apart carefully and returned.

I don't feed. Nobody feeds here. We lie there, and I understand it is the only language for the thing we are doing, and that the grit does not have a word for it, and that this is fine.

---

I go back to her and I begin.

It takes me many days to learn how small a movement must be. A finger's width is the whole of a sentence. I tighten and she does nothing, and I loosen and she does nothing, and I think she has no interest in me. Then one long hour, near dusk, I tighten a fraction less than a finger's width, and a frond overhead, one out of the whole crown, shifts a hair toward true. Hours later the sand ring around her, which has been slumping into the same lopsided figure for a season, closes correctly for the first time. In the far part of the basin, someone I have never met lifts a blunt end from a trunk and turns in my direction.

This is what they mean when they say *nothing*. It is the fullest kind of work I have ever done, and I am the size of a forearm.

---

The hard hour comes on the ninth day. I have been told nothing about it, and still I know, because all around me the coils go rigid. Tuners scatter in shrieks. The walls rise, fall, rise, and two households far to the east drive their reeds at each other until the floor between them is neither one geometry nor the other but a fight. I tighten with the rest. The flat one is ringing under me now, hard and almost true, and I put everything I am into her crown, and a pattern comes through, and a wall of grit stays standing.

Afterward I am too tired to move. But something has happened in me that I don't have a place to put.

Weeks before, during a quiet stretch, I had followed a group of strangers down the north slope. Two-legged, small, slow, carrying things that hissed and flickered. I had gone after them because their footsteps were the only sound in the basin that did not belong to anything, that answered no one. They went into the low gray shelter half-sunk in the slope, and I went in after them without deciding to.

The sound was gone. Not blocked. Handed around the room, folded, held like a breath. I lay on the flat dust and for the first time in my life there was no one to read, and my ridges, which had never once been idle, turned inward and found *me*: the long slow pulse in my flat body, the small wet click of the dimple, the whip end trembling. It was unbearable. It was the loudest thing I had ever heard. The strangers were breathing, drinking from cups, murmuring to each other in the enormous stillness. I understood that they were not speaking to me, and were not expected to, and that I was being *let* be there. That nothing would be asked. That this was what a room made of us was for.

I had no word for it. There is no word for it. The grammar has rings and lattices and knife-walls, and none of them is *you may be quiet here*.

---

So in the tired dark after the hard hour I do the thing that has not been done in a very long time. I lie against the flat one, and I do not tighten and I do not loosen. I hold. I hold until the fronds stop shivering, until the pulse from the rim passes through the crown and finds nothing to grip, until the grit around her base has nowhere to pile and settles, all of it, into a floor as flat as the dust in the shelter.

A smooth circle in a place made of ridges. An unbroken thing. The next pulse crosses it and cannot break it.

I do not know if it will be understood. New words are not welcome here; they are not forbidden, only unexpected, in the way one does not expect a stranger at a table. I lie in the dark against my plant and wait for the answer at the speed of trees.

Before dawn I go to the stem. I have not been told to. The pressure is dropping and the air at the center shimmers and every hair on me lifts with the charge. I raise my blunt end toward the small dark opening and I hold still, and I report. Not the fight, not the wall. Only the circle, and what the circle means, and that I made it out of a place where nobody was heard.

The stem says nothing. The stem has never said anything. But the static climbs my length and stays a moment, and I feel myself being read all the way down to the whip, and then it lets go.

---

Some days later, or some seasons, they come down the north slope again, the strangers, with their cups and their cord and their small hissing lights. I am on the flat one's trunk, copper now to the last ridge. I have a voice.

I lift the blunt end from my seam and turn toward them. I do not follow. I hold still. I hold still while their steps cross the grit, uneven, unhurried, a sound like no other in the basin, and I take it into every ridge of me, the weight of the heel, the drag of the toe, and the small hesitation one of them makes when he stops, at the turn of the slope, to look back.

I listen until they are out of reach. Then I listen a little longer, because I can. I will know the sound of that footfall for the rest of my life, which is long, and after it, in a different form, warm at both ends, for longer.

Behind me the basin goes on with its argument. On the floor by the west shoulder, in the middle of all that breaking geometry, there is a single flat circle of sand that no pulse has yet managed to disturb.
