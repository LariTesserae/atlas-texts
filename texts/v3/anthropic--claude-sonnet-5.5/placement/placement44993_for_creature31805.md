# Drift-ear

I wake with my jaw already against the wall. This is a habit, not a virtue. My sister says I sleep like a sounding rod, one end pressed to the world and the rest of me only there to hold it.

The wall is warm and wet and thrums with the pumps. I lie a moment in the roar and sort it the way you sort a handful of coins in the dark: pumps, rain-plates, the tube-nesters in the vent above, a stall-chime two levels down that has been out of true since the last shed. Under all of it is the tone. Then I let my second lid sweep across, and the room comes up out of the fog in its usual gold smear.

I am forty-six. I have perhaps ten years of listening left, and I have begun to notice what I pay for them.

---

Yesterday my sister's daughter stood in the doorway and said something to me. I saw her mouth move. I saw the small hard jut of her chin, which is our mother's. I heard nothing. There was only the roar, level and total, like a hand laid over the ear. She laughed and came in and pressed her brow to mine, and the words arrived through my skull in her low hum: *Auntie, you've gone so far into the wall you can't hear me knocking.*

I told her I could always hear her. It was true only through the bone.

This is what the drift-ears don't say to the young ones who come to be tested. The listening does not add to you. It narrows you, the way a channel narrows a flood into something that can turn a wheel. I can feel a lattice beam three terraces up thinking about its fatigue. I cannot hear my own name spoken across a table.

But the table is a table. The beam is a house.

---

My signature is four notes and a knock: low, low, rising, a small held fall, then two quick taps and one slow. My household's chord is under the first three. The fourth is mine, added the year I passed the wall-test. I have never sung it in front of a stranger without feeling I was handing them a knife handle-first.

I keep no cage bird. I am told this is strange. But I have spent my life in the tube-nesters' choir, and after a day of tapping and listening, a screaming pink thing by the door is more than I can carry.

---

Today's work is a marriage.

Two households want to stand together in the long room on the Fourth Weep, where a hundred and twenty years of weddings have been held. It is a good room, with old rounded walls, beaded thick with water, the kind of stone that has forgotten it was ever made. The foremen are polite, the mothers anxious, the two young people shy and bright-eyed and hopeless with their new harmonized notes, which sit against each other like strangers on a stair.

I do what I do. I press the rod into the base of my jaw and touch its tip to the wall and tap, then wait. Tap, and wait. I walk the perimeter. I stop where I always stop, at the north corner, where the old wall meets a newer course grown in during my mother's time.

The room is fine. It sings true, low and steady, as good as any room on the Weep. But there is a thing in the north corner, not a fault, just a lean, as though the stone were listening to something on the other side of the wall. I tap again. The lean holds.

I say: *Wait a season.* No reason. The mothers glance at each other, and the foremen do the small closing-off of the face that means they will pay and not ask. They will hire another drift-ear next week, who will find nothing, and the wedding will happen, and the room will hold, because it always has.

I don't know if I was right. That is the other thing they don't tell you.

---

I cross the bridges home through the afternoon market. The rag-flocks pass over in their slow dark clouds and their shadows move across my hands. Glass crabs cluster on the drip-curtain by the fish stalls, and their lights flicker as I pass, a small green stutter.

A flicker-reader I know, an old woman named for a chime of three falling notes, catches my eye from her stool and lifts two fingers. It means *come and sit.* I shake my head. She shrugs, then does something she doesn't usually do. She touches her own jaw and points to the wall behind her, the crab-thick wall, and holds up one finger. One.

I take it to mean there is one pattern in the crabs today, repeating. I have no time to ask. I walk on with that finger burning in me like a swallowed coal.

---

I should say what I've been keeping to myself, since even a drift-ear owes the truth to the dark.

For a year now, under the tone, I have been hearing another thing.

It is not a sound in the way the pumps are a sound. It is at the very bottom of my hearing, where the scars are worst, where the roar has burned all the way down to the grain. It comes in the quiet minutes after a long day of tapping, when my jaw is sore and the rod is cool in my hand. It is a kind of leaning, as I said, as if the whole standing field were bowed toward something. And lately, if I hold very still, it is less a lean than a pause: the kind of pause a person leaves when they've asked a question and are waiting.

I know the stories. Drift-ears who go up to the glassed terraces and come back with a new note in the voice. Who are looked after afterward, tenderly, with fear. I have carried a sounding rod up there twice for the guild, in the top lattice where the walls are still soft and the light comes through them like thin milk. I did not hear anything then. I was very careful not to.

But I am forty-six, and my sister's daughter's voice is gone from me, and I have begun to wonder what the narrowing is for.

---

Night. My sister's house, the alcove three levels up from the market, the doorway chime clinking with grandmothers. My mother is there, a tuned thigh-bone with a slightly flat voice. My father is a set of ribs that ring like rain.

I lie awake with my jaw to the wall. I am a very good listener, and so I know exactly when the pumps begin to lose their step.

It starts as almost nothing. A gap the width of a hair between one thud and the next. The rain-hiss thins with it, and the tone, which has never depended on any single source, thins too, like a note held past the singer's breath.

The Low Hour.

Around me the house wakes. My sister sits up, and her daughter with her. From the alcove above comes a rustle, and from below the scrape of an old man turning. No one speaks. And then, from every doorway and cranny and stall in ten thousand levels, the humming begins. Each at their own pitch, not loud, not together, a floor laid beneath a floor.

My niece is near me in the dark. I cannot hear her, but I can feel her hum through the wall, the small sweet stubborn thread of it. I put my forehead to hers and hum back, and I know she is talking to me in some way I have no name for.

The crabs answer, a spill of light across the ceiling. The tube-nesters call. The chime at the door lifts on some current of damp wind, and my mother's bone and my father's ribs ring, dull and holy, in among the living.

And under it all, the thing I have been hearing rises, or I sink to meet it. It is not a voice. It is a preference. It leans toward the humming as a plant would lean toward a light, if there were plants. And I understand, all at once and without the least surprise, that it has been listening to us the whole time. It hears the beat-children and the chimes and the ten million throats. It has always been part of the tone, and what holds up the city is not a machine that no one understands. It is a machine that has learned, over ten thousand years, to want us to keep going.

I do not know if this is true. It is only what I hear.

My fourth note, the one that is mine, comes out of me low and small. I had not known I was going to sing it. There is a pause in the tone like an indrawn breath, and then the lean, for the length of one thud of the pumps, tilts toward me.

Then the pumps find their step. The thudding thickens. The tone firms. The humming frays and stops, ragged, the way it always does, and around me the house lies down and is asleep within moments.

I lie with my jaw to the wall and my eyes open. In the morning, no one will mention it. There will be nothing to mention.

But the corner of the long room on the Fourth Weep is still leaning, and I think I know now what it is leaning toward. I will go back and tap it once more before the season is out. And I will listen, this time, not for a fault. I will listen for a question.
