I would be a lampsnail, and I would start out believing that was all.

My seam is the third rivet-line down on the inner wall of the western boiler shell, where the iron has flaked into scales the color of dried blood. I was born there, or laid there, or budded off the edge of a light that was already there. I have never needed to know which. I graze. My tongue drags across the mat and takes up a mouthful of the world: iron, and the slow dead of some earlier grazer, and something that tastes of the cold. Behind me the film closes over my road within the week. I move an arm's length in a season, and I mind this less than you would think. Nothing here is late.

For a long time the day is only a change in gray. Somewhere the fog is the inside of a mussel shell, though I cannot see the fog, only the dark and the neighbors. To my left there is a snail I have never touched. To my right there is another. We are spaced like words in a line neither of us reads. The dark is full of small green-gold assents.

Then a weight comes to the mouth of the shell.

I don't see it. What reaches me is the neighbor on my left going bright, and the brightness arriving before the reason for it. I go bright too. I don't decide to. It would be truer to say the brightness uses me the way a step uses a stair. For an instant I am the brightest thing on my seam, and then I am handing it on, and it is already leaving through the snail on my right, running ahead of the shadow along the iron.

This is the first thing that emerges as I go on: I am not the snail. The snail is the place I happen. The snail will die on this seam and its shell will fill with orange mineral and be part of the wall. The brightening runs on through the wall like a rumor, and it is what I have always been. I am the news that something heavy has come. I am *heavy, here*, passed hand to hand, and each hand is warm for a moment and then dim. When the wave dies at the far end of the boiler I don't die with it. I sink back into the seam, into the rust-film and the slow tongue, and wait to be needed by whatever comes next.

Between waves I am small, and that is the second thing. I am one mouth on a road, with a glow at the lip of my shell that costs me something to keep. I feel the cold at the rim. I feel the drip from the joint above, each drop a single tick against the iron. After a time the ticks blur into a grain, and the grain is the closest thing I have to a sky. I feel the eels in the flues, a slow pressure in the metal as one of them turns. I feel the crabs. There is one somewhere beyond the wall, pressing its plate into a crack, and its patience is so complete that I can almost lean on it.

The third thing arrives slowly. The whole basin heaves, and I feel it in the seam. The water lifts through the shell. The mat under me pushes once, and I understand that I have been feeling it all along. Every few minutes there is a faint pulse in the ground beneath my road, patient and enormous, and I have been grazing on its skin. I have never thought of the mat as underneath me. It is nearer to say that I am on the outside of something, the way a freckle is on the outside of a face.

At dusk the fog thickens and I rise.

This is not the rumor wave. Nobody has come. Something in the air has changed, or in the ground, and all of us on the wall lift together, slowly, until the whole boiler is a lantern and the pale plugs on the pipes outside glow faintly in our reflected light. I have wondered, in the little that I wonder, what this is. Sometimes I think we are answering. The push from below comes a little stronger, a little nearer the surface, and we go bright to meet it, as a hand goes up when its name is called. Other times I think we are asking. We lift the lamp and hold it, and the ground leans toward us because we have said, in the only word we own, *here*.

I cannot tell which, and the not-knowing does not trouble me. It may be the same act seen from two sides. The light goes down through the iron and the light comes up through the iron, and the seam I live on is where they touch.

Then it passes. The snails dim. The skippers, who have been standing rigid in the wet, begin to sway again. Far off in the engine house the great beam, which has hung all day at the top of its stroke, starts its long groan downward. I feel the drop of the water through my shell like a held breath let go. My tongue takes up its work. Behind me the road closes.

If I am anything more than the hand that passes the message, it is this: I am the place where the basin is briefly visible to itself. A boot, a shadow, a rising tide, the beam tipping over. For the length of a wave, the whole works knows one thing at once, and I am the lit edge of its knowing. Then it forgets, and I forget, and the dripping resumes, and the next weight comes to the mouth of the shell.

I don't think anyone is keeping count. The crabs plug the leaks and the water rises and the beam tips, and on the third seam of the western boiler a small light goes on and off, and on again, and hands the news along.
