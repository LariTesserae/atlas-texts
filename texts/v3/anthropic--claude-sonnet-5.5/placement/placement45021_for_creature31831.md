I start in the dark, before I know I am anyone.

There is a pressure behind my ears, and a taste like a coin held on the tongue. My palms are flat on grit that is still warm from the night's sleeping. Around me, breathing. The whole chamber has woken at once, and none of us has said a word. We are turning toward the edge before we have decided anything.

So I am one of the Keepers, in a borrowed hollow, or maybe not borrowed. The doorway is worn to the shape of shoulders, and when I go through it, it fits mine.

Outside, the cold has that clear, ringing quality it gets an hour before the Breath. The cushion-mounds have drawn themselves tight, and the whole meadow looks clenched. I do not look at the sky. I look at the comb-reeds, because that is where the color will come first, and I want to see it arrive ahead of the fog, the gray-green going wet and deep like a held thought finally spoken.

But I have a duty before that. In the wall of the chamber, in a pocket among the dead rosettes, there is a moth pressed flat and still. Mine is an old one, or its mother was. I have kept it through nine winters. I go back in and put two fingers to the wall and open it, and I feel the small dry weight of the thing stir. I carry it out cupped against my chest, and at the first thread of white over the rim I open my hands. It goes up gray and soundless, into a cloud that isn't there yet, and I stand with my arms held slightly away from my body and wait to be watered.

This is what I am when the day begins: someone who lets go of a moth.

---

By the time the fog has settled on the meadow I have drunk from my own forearms and been drunk from, a woman's mouth at my shoulder, quick and impersonal and kind, and I have done the same for the boy beside me, whose hair is so long it has never been wet at the ends. Nobody speaks. The sound is the muffled softness, and the crunch of salt under bare feet.

Then I walk to my stretch of cliff.

I should say what I really am, which is not much. I am a palm-reader, but a poor one. The others move along the banded faces in long unhurried strides, fingertips trailing, and take a hundred years in a morning. My fingers are slow. I read one layer, and then I sit with it, and then I read it again, and I forget to go on. The teachers were patient for a long time and then gave up in a way that was itself a kindness. They gave me a hand's breadth of stone near the western escarpment, where the cliff steps down toward the cloud sea, and said, *this is yours.* That was forty autumns ago. I have not finished it.

Here is what I know of my hand's breadth. There are eleven layers in it, and the fourth is dark and very thin, a drought year, but not the worst kind. The worst kind is flat, with no grain in it at all. This one has a grain, and the grain leans, as if in that year the fog came from a wrong quarter. I have thought about that lean for most of my life. I do not know what it means and I am not sure it means anything. But I know its texture the way you know the back of someone's neck. I put my fingers on it in the fog, when the stone is damp and gives up more, and I feel the year go by under my pads, one small dry season, one wind from the wrong quarter, everyone alive then long gone into the saddle soil among the lantern-trees.

I do not write it down. Where would it go? It is here. It is the only place it could be.

The others think I am simple. I think they may be right. But someone has to stay with a thing after it has been read, and I believe, though I would never say it aloud, that this is a kind of work. The plateau writes itself down and someone should be there when it does. Not to understand it, just to be the one whose hand is warm on it. The oldest palm-reader, the one who sits and keeps the word-shaped curve company, once passed me on the terrace and put her hand for a moment over mine on the eleven layers, and said nothing, and went on. I think it was a greeting between colleagues.

---

At noon the fog burns off and the air goes clear enough to show mountains a hundred kilometers off, and I put on my visor and walk back through the long glare, careful of the spiders' morning nets, though the strings of light are gone by now and only the shapes of them remain. I go around them anyway. Behind me my own trail loops and doubles, and from a ridge it would look like someone thinking. Perhaps I am.

I sleep in the warm dim of the dead center with the others' breathing all around, and the resin smell, and the seed cases ticking out on the hillside like many fingers tapping. In my sleep I am a moth pressed into a page. I do not mind it.

---

Late in the afternoon I am at the foot of a pillar, resting, when the tone comes.

It is very low. It is a breath across a bottle, from somewhere high in the stone, and I know it before I hear it, in the same way I know the drawing-in. I stand. I do not look up. I turn my back, gently, and lower my head, and shut my eyes, and stand the way you stand outside a room where someone is praying. The wind has dropped. The tone rises a little, holds, and lets go.

I have never once wanted to know what it is. That may be the thing about me that the teachers, in their kindness, did understand: I am someone who can stand with my back turned and not need to look. It is a small talent, but it is exactly the right size for the place.

When it has passed I stay a moment longer, palms open at my sides. And here is the part I would not tell a visitor. In that stillness, with the tone gone and the light going amber over the cloud sea, I feel the ground under my feet. It is not a tremor. It is the sense of a great slow attention, turned very gently toward the edge, like someone at a window, feeling for weather.

I do not pray to it. I do not say its name. I only stand there a little longer than I need to, so that if it is lonely, if it is the sort of thing that can be, there will be one person on the surface who is not in a hurry.

Then the light thins to violet, and the cold breath comes across the salt, and I go in through the door that fits my shoulders, and lie down with my palms flat on the floor. I wait for the drawing-in the way you wait for a friend who has never once been late.
