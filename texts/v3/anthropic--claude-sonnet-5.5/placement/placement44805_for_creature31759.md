I want to begin as a heron. Standing above everything, holding the whole dish in one long attention, is the flattering thing to be. But when I try it I find nothing there. A heron doesn't feel itself being many. It only feels the water against its shins and the picture arriving from elsewhere. That's not where I am.

Where I am is low, and wet, and moving. I am an eel.

---

I find myself in the shallows on the warm side of the spire, at the hour when the steam is just beginning to lift. The hum comes down through the water in three notes and I feel it along my whole length rather than hearing it. Against the pale flank of the spire the frogs are heaped and dreaming, and the heap turns over itself with terrible slowness, the oldest carried inward, the young rotating out to the cool edge and back again.

I am clear enough that you could read a reed through me. My trail comes out behind me as a thread of faint phosphor, and the thread is most of what I am for. I don't carry messages the way a bird carries a letter. I carry the crossing. When my line meets another eel's line, the glow flares and changes color, and in that flare something passes that neither of us could say in any other way. I don't know if it's gossip or accounting. I've never been able to tell, and I've stopped needing to. Blue where the reeds are sick, a warm amber where the shrimp are thick and the herons will be pleased, a thin green I have no word for that comes from the direction of the dome. Then the crossing fades and the trail fades and the clay under us is dark again, as though no one had spoken.

This is the first thing I would tell you about myself: I leave nothing. The Tenders leave the lip of the dome, and the herons leave a picture held between them, but my speech is written in light on water. What I've said is only in the one who received it, and in whatever that one then does. I have come to think this is a kind of freedom. Nothing I carry is mine to keep, so I can carry it faithfully.

---

Some days the work is this: a frog dies at the center of the heap. It does this quietly, warm and packed among the others, and the others feel it and do what they do. They pass the small body outward, hand over hand, back to the cool edge, without haste and without any grief that I can find. They are kind in a way that needs no memory. At the edge I take it into my mouth as gently as an eel can, which is more gently than you would think, and I go down.

The clay is cool flour. I slide into it, and the light goes out of my trail because there is nothing down there to read it, and I go on by feel. The Tenders tap somewhere overhead, a fine steady tremor. The clay closes behind me. Deeper, it is colder and finer, and there is a place where the murmur of the basin becomes something else, a slow lean, as though the whole floor were tilted a hair toward the center. I set the body down there. I don't stay to watch. The clay takes it back within hours and I've never seen how.

I've never gone further, though I could. I know the direction. The lean tells me. I have a feeling about that direction that is like the Tenders' feeling when they are turned toward the mouth and turn themselves back around. It isn't fear. It's closer to manners. Some rooms you don't enter while someone is speaking in them.

---

At the fixed hour the cloud gathers, and I feel it in my ears, though I have no ears, only the pulling sensation along my sides. Everything stops. My trail goes dark by itself. The lantern-veils above me go out in one ripple, and in the sudden dimness I can feel the water begin to run toward the center, softly, carrying petals and scales and the small dead, and I let it lean on me. I don't swim. Nobody swims in that hour. The rain falls broad and warm, the first true noise, and the spire's hum climbs one whole step, and for the length of the hour I am not an eel among things. I am a part of one thing, listening while it speaks to something else.

I've thought about who is being addressed. The makers who left. The Tenders, who shrank into their own upkeep. The basin itself, spread thin as a film under all of us. I have held each of these in turn, and each one is like a different shape of water in the same cupped hand. I don't need to close the hand. The one thing I'm sure of is that the answer, if it comes, is the light coming back.

It does. One pulse, then a thousand, and the air is breathing gold again, and my trail catches and burns. Across the reeds I see another line flare, and I turn toward it, and we cross, and the color changes, and something true passes between us that neither of us will remember.

That's who I would be. I would be the one who carries the crossing and lets it go, who takes the small dead down to the edge of the room where the clay leans, and who is glad of the hour when everything, for a while, is only listening.
