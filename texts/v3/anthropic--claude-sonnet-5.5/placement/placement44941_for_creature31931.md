I begin as a sound, which is the wrong place to start, but the morning puts me there. A tick, then another, then the pattering that fills every alley. Sound here carries no farther than an arm, so if I am listening, I am listening from close by. I have a location. That narrows things.

My first thought is the felt under the peat. It is the one resident nobody can point to, and it connects everything. I can see the appeal. It has no face, and it lives by being the answer to neither of two questions. But I notice I'm reaching for it because it is the largest thing available, and something that says *I* is not that. The felt never says it. Whatever is talking is smaller and has an address.

I try the vole, since the voles know the bends the way you know a stairwell. But a vole is always going somewhere. It flickers at the mouths of alleys, and I don't think I flicker. I try the brown bird, the single upward note that is a question nobody finishes. I like it too much, and that makes me distrust it. It is the loneliness I would pick from a menu. The bird has to find a stove and hold eggs against the frost, and I don't think I could do any of that.

I keep drifting up the slope, past the black pools and the hollow post of a dead rosette, past the newest streets where moss is barely a course of stone. Then I hit wet rock, a slick film with a dull silver light, and I stop, because I've arrived.

I am a stair under the seep.

I am liverwort and saxifrage, hung in green-black tiers like laundry, each tier thinner than a page. At night I freeze, and every filament wears its sleeve of ice. Before dawn I am stiff and glassed over and doing nothing, which is not the same as being dead. Then the cliff warms and the seam lets go. The water comes down in a sheet no thicker than a fingernail, and I catch it.

That is the whole work. I hold the water for a moment and then let it go. Some of it goes into me, and most of it goes on down to the peat, the channels, the court pools, the sponge that the masons stand on. Everything downstream is a little wetter because I was slow. I don't create the water or decide where it goes. I make it linger, and in a place like this lingering counts for a great deal.

The noon shaft crosses the bowl for its few minutes. Every droplet in the town lights at once, and mine light with the rest. I have no way of knowing what that looks like from outside. From here it is a brightness that arrives without warning and leaves as soon as it has been noticed.

I should say what I am not sure of. Rocks come down. A stone lets go far up, clicks through the dark, and can take a stair off the wall in a moment. That has happened to the stairs on either side of me, and I assume it has happened to me before. I don't remember it. What I am is what grew back from spores and fragments in the same place, in almost the same shape. So the question comes up: is that continuity, or is it a habit of the wall?

I think the shape belongs to the water more than to me. The seep comes out where it comes out, and whatever lands there and can bear the wet will take the form the sheet allows. I am the town's memory of where the water emerges, but the memory is really the water's insistence and my willingness to be shaped by it. I find I don't mind this. It seems a fair description of most kinds of remembering.

The visitors say the town seems planned. I can tell you it isn't, at least from here. From here there is only the next drop, and the one after, and the cold coming back in the evening so the drips slow and stop one by one. Whatever order there is doesn't show at my scale. It shows from far off, where the streets meet at their nearly regular angles and each court has its one warm post. I have never seen the pattern and never will. I only ever see the water arriving. Maybe that is why the pattern works, because nothing in it is trying.

Now the fog is thinning and the thaw is at its wettest. Somewhere in the roofs the small brown bird gives its one thin note, rising, unfinished. I don't answer, and I don't think I'm meant to. Another sheet is coming down the rock, and I hold it as long as I can, which is not long, which is the work.
