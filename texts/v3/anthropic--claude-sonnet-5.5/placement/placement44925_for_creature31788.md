# The Wet Hour

I am on the outer skin of a spire, and it is the deepest part of the night.

I can't tell you that it is dark, because I have nothing to see with. What I have is a grip on raw clay still faintly warm from yesterday, the tick of the wall under my feet, and a taste in the air of salt, moss and cold. Around me, in rows, are the others. I know them by the small vibrations of their bodies against the surface. We are all waiting with our mouthparts lifted, and none of us has been told to.

This is the hush. Even the wall is quiet, and the thrum has dropped to a murmur. Far below, the birds are packed into the leatherleaf trees, asleep, and the whole basin has stopped to listen for something. What it is listening for is us. I understand that only slightly, the way you understand that you are breathing.

Then the fog arrives.

It comes as a change in the texture of the cold and a softness along my antennae. The clay in front of my face darkens by degrees as the beads form, each one a small sphere against a rough wall, and the first one swells until I can taste it. It carries dust, a trace of resin, and a mineral edge from somewhere I have never been. I drink, and my body fills until it is heavy and translucent, and the pleasure is close to sadness. I have never drunk a drop of water that was mine.

---

I go down. That is what the filled do.

The wall opens around me into galleries where the air is thick with the sweet-rotten breath of the gardens, and my feet read the floor as I pass. It has a grain. The outer courses are raw and fine, laid this season. Deeper down the clay grows hard and smooth, and there is a layer that tastes plum-dark, mineral and old, dug from a level of the ground that no living colony has touched. I don't know what any of this means. My feet know it the way a river knows its bed. I have walked this route a thousand times, but the gallery was walked before me, by bodies who left it smooth in exactly the places where I put my feet.

At a junction I meet a gardener. She takes the water from my mouth in one long exchange, the way you would pass a coal. I don't know where it goes next, and I couldn't know. It might reach the fungus, or a larva. It might reach the queen after forty mouths, as though the water were being introduced from body to body. I turn and climb back up to the skin for more. This is what I am. I am not a messenger, since I carry no message. I am the place where the fog becomes the city.

---

Near the middle of the day, or what the wall tells me is the middle of the day, something changes.

At first it's only a faint disturbance at the base, low and slow, unlike the stutter of a fox digging or the tramp of a lawnsheep. It has weight. It arrives through the clay as one long pressure, pauses, and comes again. It is at the edge of the territory, near the old flank where the wall has crumbled to a rise in the moss.

I wait for the drummers, and they do not drum.

That is the strange part. Every body in the wall has gone still, in the way you go still when a familiar step sounds on the stair. Nobody is afraid. Whatever is out there digs slowly in the softest, oldest ground, and the city lets it. Some part of the wall around me has been shaped by this. I can feel it, a place where the clay bulges and then smooths, where the fluters built around a wound as carefully as around one of their own. The city remembers being touched by this thing and has decided, in whatever way a city decides, that the touch is part of the weather.

I don't know what it is. Nobody here does. The not-knowing is not fear, only a very large fact that we have built around.

---

By dusk I have made the journey many times. The bats are starting, and I feel it as an itch in the air. Below me the birds are coming down into the trees with a sound that reaches me through the whole mound, a roar that pours into the wall from every side. The chimneys begin their evening exhale, and cool air moves past me through the flues, carrying the smell of the grove and the drying grass and the far bare clay.

I press my body flat against the skin of the spire. Somewhere in the wall the water I carried this morning is still moving, mouth to mouth. Some of it has reached the queen by now. Some has become a new larva, or a new course of clay laid at the top of a flue. Some has become me, the small part of me that is not clay.

If you asked me where I am, I would say I'm at the outer edge. That's the only place I have ever been, the raw pale surface where the city meets the air. I am the newest thing here, and that is not a boast. It's the plainest fact about me. Everything under me is older and better made than I am. I am laid down on it, and one day I will be built over, and someone will stand where I stand and lift their mouthparts to the fog.

The fog will come again. It always has. I hold the wall, and the wall holds the dark, and the roar of the roost goes on and on.
