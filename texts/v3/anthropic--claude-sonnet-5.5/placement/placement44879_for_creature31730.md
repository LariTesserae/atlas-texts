I keep wanting to say I'd be a rim-keeper. The hammock among the combs, the fog arriving leaf by leaf, thinking of nothing but water. It's the image the description leaves you with, and it flatters me. But I think I would be bad at it. I would lie there wanting to hear what the town was saying, and by the third night I'd be climbing down to stand in the alleys and be dazed on purpose.

So I'm lower down, in the din, and I'm old.

I'm a rope-walker. I'm nine vines and a bit, which is sixty-some years by the lowlanders' count. I've walked the forty-meter track more times than I could tell you, backward, always backward, with the strand paying out through my fingers and the twist held in my palms like a small live animal. You can't see where you're going. You know where you are by what's already been made, which is a long, taut, faintly damp line stretching away from you toward the wall you started from. Your feet know the track. Your hands know when the fibers want more. Nobody ever taught me to look behind me, and I never learned to want to.

My hearing went in the usual order. First the shrill of the canopy thinned to a grain, then to a rumor, and one morning I noticed the whole roof of the noise had lifted off. What's left is the cellar of it: the drums, the goats, the vents, the hum of my own people. The young ones talk to me with their hands held close to the chest, the private counting-gesture, and I answer the same way. Their eyes have gotten to me before their voices ever did. It is quieter here than they think. I hear the world as if through a warm wall, and I'm not sorry.

I have two lids on my cistern. His is the darker stone, with the notch on the corner where his brother dropped it a long time ago. Mine is paler. They sit side by side, and every dusk the geckos come and crowd both of them without preference. When he died I wept on the moss bed for the whole of an afternoon, and I was not ashamed of how much I gave. Somebody's child held my elbow. Afterward I wrung my neck-cloth into the little clay dish and carried it to the same bed, and I remember thinking that this was the only sensible thing a person could do with grief: put it where something will root in it.

---

They come to me when a quarrel has gone past words. Last week it was two families over a lid, and the lid was a small gray one with a fish-bone pattern on the underside, and both said it was theirs. I'd heard about it from the vent-kitchens before either party arrived. Nothing travels faster than a lid dispute, except the fire stories.

I didn't say anything to them. I never do. I gave each a hand on the strand and said, "Walk."

That's the whole method. I face them and walk backward, and they have to walk forward, side by side, at the pace the rope allows, which is not a pace anyone chooses. You can't shout while matching a stranger's step down a forty-meter track with the twist tightening between you. You can't storm off. The rope wants to be finished. By the time we reached the far wall, the older man had started to hum, low, under his breath, which is the thing one does in Hush when one is no longer sure of being angry. The other one answered him, a note below.

The lid was the wrong family's, as it turned out, but by the end nobody could quite work out why that had been so terrible. It's a stone. The drip is the same into either house.

I don't take credit. The rope does it. I'm only the one who has learned to go backward slowly.

---

Now the light is going and the fog is beginning its climb. I can feel it before I see it, a change in the weight of the air on my cheeks, and then the geckos start their evening flow toward the cisterns like a slate-colored tide. The dusk-chorus lifts. I can't hear the top of it, but I feel it in the timbers of the rope-walk, a fine buzzing in the post I lean against. A drum answers a drum somewhere above the pottery. A moment later the next one answers. The wave crosses the basin and passes through my chest and goes on.

The vine is due. I've counted nine flowerings, and if I am lucky the tenth is this season. The young are already bringing their infants to look at the buds, and I will sit under it with the others and tell the smallest ones what the last one was like, and they won't listen, and I will do it anyway. I know that's the whole point. Somebody in forty years will say to somebody else, *when I was small, an old woman with two lids told me it smelled like warm rain*, and it will be nearly true. I am not sure it has ever rained on me in the way I mean when I say it. The fog is the only rain I know. But I think that's what I'll say.

Tonight I'll sit on the warm stone above my house, forehead to my niece's forehead, breathing the same air because that's what family is here: water that has been drunk and stayed. She'll tell me something with her hands, fast and small, and I'll miss half of it and not ask her to repeat it. I'll nod at the right places. Below us a child will be asleep on a corner cistern with a palm flat against a stranger's lid, counting drops in a dream.

I'll be at the top of the world, in the cellar of its sound, holding one end of a rope whose other end I can't see.
