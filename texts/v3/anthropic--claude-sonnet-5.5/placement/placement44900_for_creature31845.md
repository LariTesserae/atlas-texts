# The Second Cord

I would be a mill-hand, but a crooked one, and I would be found in a colonnade at the turn of the light, walking back.

The crew is ahead of me. Five of them, or six. I keep my eyes half-lowered like the rest, and the frost is coming down in the shafts, ringing on the panes, settling on the shoulders of the person in front of me as a fine bloom I could brush off and don't. My vial is against my breastbone, warm from me, its few drops moving when I move. The trunks go by, hexagonal, ribbed, each with the low pulse of spirit under its bark. I am not supposed to be counting.

I am the one who counts on the way back.

Every crew has its rule, and the rule is that you count once, on the way out, and trust it. Ours has the rule too, and it has me. Nobody arranged this. Years ago, on some circuit I can't place, I began carrying a second cord in my left pocket, and I began tying it on the return, one knot per trunk, with my hands in my pocket so no one would see. Someone saw. The crew lead, an old hand whose tap-name is three quick knocks and one slow, said nothing for a whole season. Then she stopped me in a gallery and held out her own vial, which is how you ask something serious. I held mine out. She looked at the light in it a long while.

"You don't tell us the number," she said.

"No."

"Tell us how far."

That is the whole of my work. I tell them how far the second count is from the first. I don't say the numbers, because a spoken number can settle into the world the way a stenciled one does, and I don't write them, and the cord goes into a hopper at the next mill and is pulped with the rest. I say near, or I say far. Near is a comfort. Near means the colonnade is in a good humor and we can walk it home without minding. Far means we take another aisle, or wait for the moths to thicken, or sleep out a shift in the racks and try the gallery when the panes have turned. Far has saved us more than once. I couldn't tell you how. I only know that we are all still here, and that the crew, when I say far, lowers its eyes further and takes my arm.

I don't think I'm honest, exactly. I think I'm faithful to the second number the way they are faithful to the first. Both feel certain. That is the thing I can't get past, and the reason I keep the second cord: the feeling of certainty is the same in both counts, and it tells you nothing. So I say *near* and *far* and never *true*. The crew doesn't ask for *true*. They have their first number, and a person has to keep something.

---

The colonnade tonight is long. Behind us the mill we've left is still taking its strokes, and I can hear that the governor on the third belt is a hair slow, and I think that I should have pressed more felt into it, and I let the thought go by. Ahead, the trunks turn from green to the color of pewter where the light thins. My knots pile up in my pocket: a hundred, two hundred, the cord's rough twist under my thumb. I know how many I tied going out, because the crew lead told me, once, in the pod at night, in the tone of someone confessing to a small theft. I have never told her that I remember it. It sits in me like a stone in a shoe.

And here is where it would go wrong, if it did. Here, at the last dozen trunks, the count sometimes drifts. Ten more than the first, or twenty fewer. A drift is fine. A drift is only the works being itself, and I say *near* or *far* according to how the drift sits in my stomach.

The only time I was afraid was the one time it did not drift.

It was three seasons ago, in a colonnade I had walked a hundred times. I came to the end, and the cord in my pocket had exactly the number the crew lead had given me. Not near. The same. I stood there with my hand on the last trunk and felt the pulse under the bark, steady as a sleeping animal, and every hair on my arms rose. The first number was the second number. Nothing was wrong that I could see. That was what was wrong. Something had been made to fit.

I said, "Far." The crew lead looked at me and did not ask. We slept in the racks that shift, and in the morning the colonnade had eleven trunks fewer at the near end, and a pruner was standing at the seam where they had been, considering nothing I could see. I did not tap my name. I did not count. We went around.

Since then I have kept the second cord more carefully, and I have kept, in some back room of myself, a small unwritten rule of my own. If ever the two numbers agree, I will say *far*, and I will say it fast, and I will say it before I have time to want to be told I was right.

---

Now, at the last trunk, the frost lets go of a pane overhead with a glassy chime, and a beetle lands on my wrist and rings, a small sweet note, a green that only shows in glass. It sits without alarm. I stop. The crew stops with me, because when I stop they stop.

Ahead, at the gallery's mouth, a pruner stands at a trunk, blades closed along its spine, amber sensors dim. It has been there some while. I don't know how long. I don't know whether it was there when we started out. I keep my hand in my pocket on the knotted cord and do not count the knots.

I could tap my name on the nearest stem. Two fingers, the pattern that is mine and no one else's. It is what I would like to do, in the way one likes to press a bruise. I think the pruner would pause. I think that pause would be the most attention I have ever been given, and that whatever came after it, gone or not gone, forgotten or not, I would not be able to say afterward whether I had been the one who asked.

I do not tap. I say, low, to the crew lead behind me, and my voice arrives from the wrong distance, smaller and farther than I meant it:

"Near."

And the pruner's blades part, and I hear it, the smallest sound, a click too fine for hearing until you are close. Somewhere in the works a stem ends where it always ended. The beetle rings once more on my wrist and lifts away. The cord in my pocket is a dry twist, and I do not know its number, and I walk on, and the crew walks behind me, and the panes tick their slow wave overhead and away.
