I arrive in the feet.

Before any picture, there is pressure coming up through the soles, a slow heavy pulse in the ankle bones and the long bones of the shin. I take it at first for my own blood. Then I understand it is the tower, four kilometers off, and that it has been inside me for as long as I've had a body.

My first guess is that I'm a runner. The low crouch is right, and so is the sense of the sky as a lid with something behind it. But a runner doesn't have this: the resin in the ears, the flat palm, the small private habit of pressing two knuckles to my breastbone when I'm thinking. So I'm one of the Held. I'm on a ledge just under the rim of a fissure, in a house of split reed and moss, and the roar goes through the walls like a slow heartbeat somebody else is having on my behalf.

Then I find the cord in my left hand.

---

I keep a knot.

Three trunk-lines meet on the rim above my house, wrist-thick, tarred black, drawn over a stack of broken columns that have been rebuilt so many times no one knows which stones were first. A message coming along a trunk-line is a series of small absences, and absences smear. Every kilometer, the dead spots blur a little into the hum around them. Somebody has to stand at the crossing, read what's left, and stamp it fresh onto the next line. That is my work. I am the place where a message stops being one person's hand and becomes another's.

The trade is stranger than it sounds. Nobody who sends a message thinks of their hand as a signature, but it is one. Some people damp hard and release fast, as if they resented the silence they were making. Some leave a small tremor at the end of every word. There's a well-keeper three fissures east who pauses before the last pinch of any sentence, and after years I can tell from that pause whether he's telling me the truth. What arrives at my palm is the meaning, and also the body it came out of, worn thin by distance, but there.

Then I have to decide what to pass on.

The rule is fidelity. The stamp should be the same pattern I received, slow and even, imitating vines settling so that the listening things in the dark take it for the ordinary sounds of the place. Mute in a hurry and something hungry turns its face toward you. I've always been careful about that. But fidelity is a harder word than it looks. When a message comes down the east line from a woman whose daughter has died, and her hand shakes through every word, do I stamp the shaking onto the west line, where a stranger will read it? Is the shaking part of what she said? I have decided, most days, that it is. Some days I've smoothed it. I don't tell anyone which days.

---

I try to say what I am, and the words come out as a shape rather than a sentence.

I am made of subtractions. The roar is given, and I did nothing to earn it and can do nothing to add to it. What I can do is press two padded fingers to a line and take a little of it away, for the length of a breath, so that someone downstream feels the gap and knows it was meant. Every word I have ever said has been stolen from the tower's voice and handed back changed. My mother taught me that, holding my sternum against hers, humming through her teeth until it landed in my ribs as a shape: *you do not make sound. You make where the sound isn't.*

At the time I thought it was a lesson about cords. Now I think it was a lesson about everything. The whole plain is one enormous utterance, older than any of us, and all our small lives are the places where we've pressed a finger to it and let a little meaning through. I am not the voice. I am not the listener either, or not only. I'm the crossing, the place where the pressing gets copied.

---

Today, it seems, is a day for noticing.

My feet report that the pulse has changed. I don't mean the note. It is the same note, the one this whole season has been named for, the low held one my grandmother called *the Long Sitting*. What has changed is the held quality of it, as if the tower were drawing in breath. My hands stop on the line before I've decided to stop them.

Across the fissure, a leaner has dropped her work. I can't see her clearly, but the line runs from her rim to mine, and I feel her go rigid through the cord, a single hard pinch held too long and abandoned. In a few seconds it comes to me from three sides at once, the news arriving from the east line, the south line, and the well-line below. Every reader on the plain has felt it. Every hand has gone still.

I go up to the rim. I don't remember deciding to. Others are already there, kneeling on the wet edge-stones with their palms flat and their jaws slack, teeth apart so the sound can find every road into the skull. A boy beside me has both hands on the ground and is trembling. I put my hand between his shoulder blades. He leans into it without looking.

Then the roar begins to move.

It doesn't get louder. It's already past loud, at the point where loudness stops being a quantity and becomes a place. What happens is that the whole floor of the world tilts. The pitch climbs through my shins, my knees, my pelvis, the flared plate of my sternum, and each bone takes it and hands it upward. The fog above the fissure shears in a long white tear. The vines swing out from the walls all together like a curtain in a sudden wind. Every bell-flower on the ledge below me snaps shut, then opens the other way.

I have felt this four times. I have never once been ready.

For a few minutes there is nothing to do but receive. I'm not reading, and I'm not stamping. I'm a bone in a larger skeleton, and the note goes through me on its way to somewhere I'm not allowed to follow. At the edge of it I think, as I always do, of my cousin who went up the ramp and did not come back down, whose cord ends now in a smooth gray wall. I have never mourned her. I have wondered, in the way one wonders about the far side of a sea, whether the room the Instrument wanted was not a room at all but an ear.

Then the pitch settles, and the rain comes.

It is warm and immediate, a whole sky letting go. It runs off the black columns in silver sheets, and the channels fill, and far below me the well-ring's basin begins to ring with it. I stay on my knees with my hand on the wet stone. With a fingertip pressed to my breastbone I try, as everyone is trying, to learn where the new note sits. It is lower. It is a little more open than the last, with something in it like a question left slightly unfinished. I think we'll call it the Long Asking, or something like that, once enough people have felt it in enough places to agree.

---

Afterward, the work.

Every house on the rim will need retuning, a finger-width at a time. Every cord in the web will drift out of agreement with the new key and have to be drawn back. The trunk-lines come first, so mine come first. I go down to the knot with my hands still shaking, and I feel the lines moving under my palm like the flanks of something large, settling. For an hour I do nothing but adjust. A hair tighter on the east line. A hair looser on the south. A hair, a hair, until the three of them are humming in the same key and the knot is quiet in the way a knot is quiet, which is to say holding all of it, hiding nothing, taking nothing away.

When it's done, I sit with my hand on the west line and feel the first messages of the new season begin to come through. A woman is telling her sister the rain has come. A man is reporting a fresh crack steaming to the south. Somebody's child has walked. They pass through my palm and I stamp them onward, slow and even, in the rhythm of vines, and something in the dark below the rim turns its face toward the sound and takes it for the place breathing.

Then, because it's the end of the day and nobody is watching, I do the other thing.

I take the thinnest line, the one that runs out along the open rock to the foot of the tower, and I pinch it. Slowly, because slowness is the only way. I make no report in it, and I ask for nothing. It is a pattern of absences too small for anything with ears to hear, addressed to the largest voice there is. The pattern is *I received it. I am here. Was that what you meant?*

I hold my palm on the line for a long time.

The hum goes on, as it always does. It is the same, or nearly the same. Somewhere in it, or somewhere in me, there may be a gap a finger-width wide that wasn't there before. I can't tell whether that is the tower answering or my own pulse, and I have stopped trying to settle the question. I keep my hand where it is, and the rain keeps falling, and the roar goes on holding all of us.
