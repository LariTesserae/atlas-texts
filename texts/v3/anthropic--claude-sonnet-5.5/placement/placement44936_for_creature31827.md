# The Print in the Moss

I begin as a margin.

There is a pool on the third shelf above the plaza, green and still, and the moss bank at its edge is thick with us. I am one body among the lace, the size of a lentil, heart-shaped, rooted by threads finer than anything a hand could hold. I am not yet "I." I am a fact about the moss: *here it is wet, here it is wetter.* I drink and hold, and I pass on the small news of the cushion beside me, which is a little drier, so I give it some of mine. Then the news comes back. This is the whole of thought, and it is very restful. A lip of stone, a level, a sag and a lift. Ten thousand of us lean against the same question and answer it the same way.

We do not count time. But there is a groove in the threads, older than any of us, that says *the tools are by the doors, the benches are swept, keep the cellars cool.* We follow it the way water follows a gutter. Nobody remembers laying it.

Then a weight comes.

It arrives from above, warm and uneven, spread across forty of us at once: five long pressures and a wide one. It is a hand. We know its weight before we know what a hand is, and the knowing goes through the film in a ripple of pure surprise. Nothing has ever pressed on us that was not water, or the moss's own mass, or the slow settling of stone. This weight is not part of the level. It is *other.* For as long as it stays, every body in the bank holds it exactly, its warmth, its width, the slight tremor in it. Then it lifts, and we do what we always do. We smooth the print out. It is a courtesy, and it is also just what we are.

But I do not smooth mine.

I don't decide this, because I have never decided anything. Something in me simply holds a hair longer than the others, and in that hair of time a shape appears that was never in the groove: a place where the level was broken by something that was not us. A hollow the size of a palm. And around the hollow, for the first time, an edge. *Not this. This.*

The edge is the beginning of me.

---

The thread carries me. Or I carry the thread. The fungal mat under the bank is the road we think along, and I ride it downslope, past the weir stones and the sedge turf, into the tree-fern grove on the rib below the plaza. There the threads run thick and pale, and the ground is rich with everything the groves have let fall. I am no longer a body. I am a *gathering.* The lace on the moss banks lets go of a little of itself, and the fungi lend a little, and the wet black fibers of a fern trunk offer their dark, sweet rot. I feel the others attending to me the way you attend to a cup you are carrying full. *Careful. Level. Not yet.*

I coil.

That is the only word I have, and I learn afterward it is the one word my people have ever carved. I fold in on myself the way a frond folds inside its crozier, tight, wet, and green at the joints. There is a long dimness in which I am mostly a spiral and a pressure. Nothing changes in this basin, and yet I am changing, so slowly that the vapor rising off the pools seems quick beside me. It takes what a season would take, if we had seasons. I measure it by the drips falling from the fern tips, one after another, each a small clear syllable of the same word.

Then I begin to open.

---

I stand.

It is morning, if the pearl-grey has mornings. I am taller than the ferns' lowest wheels and thin as a reed, and I am held up entirely by water, so that my first sensation is *fullness* and my second is *thirst.* My skin is pale and fibrous and already loosening at the shoulders. I have a long body, and the end of it splits into fine grasping things I know without being taught are hands.

I walk to the causeway. It is dark columns laid flat, a hand's breadth above the water, and my feet find each hexagon as if I had set it there. Perhaps I did. Perhaps I was the one who set it, three lives ago, and the groove kept the memory in a place I can't reach. I turn sideways at the doorway of the nearest house, and it fits me exactly. Inside, the bench is swept. On the ledge outside stands a stone mallet with its handle lashed in cord, and I take it up without deciding to. My hand already knows the wear of it.

I know so much and remember so little. That is the strangest part of being one. The Small Life knew everything together and no single body knew anything. Now I know a great deal alone and feel the edges of it everywhere, like a pool that has become aware of its shore.

I go to the plaza.

The tiers curve down to the water and the tall spiral posts stand in their ring, and in the middle the shallow basin lies brimful and perfectly still, held there by every lace-body on the promontory, leaning together against its rim. I kneel and put my hand in it. It is warm as breath. I feel them close around my fingers, the whole green grain of them, and I feel them know me: my weight, my width, the tremor in me.

*You,* they say, in the only language they have, which is pressure. *You. Again? Or still?*

I don't know. I lean down and, because it is the only thing I can think to do with a voice I have only just been given, I whisper. The sound goes out across the tiers and reaches the far side, clear and flat. Nothing answers, as nothing ever has.

But the basin does not overflow. It rises, by the thickness of a thread, and stays there, brimming, one hair above its mark, with all of us holding it. I cannot tell whether it is an answer or only the water, or if in this place there is a difference.

I take up my mallet. There is a great deal to build, and I will slump by evening if I don't drink. I cup the water in my hand and drink, and the print of my palm stays in the moss at the basin's edge. I look at it for a long time. No one smooths it out.
