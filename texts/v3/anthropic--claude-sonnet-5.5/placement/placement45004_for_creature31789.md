# The Late One, Tending

I am always slightly after.

That is the first thing I know about myself, and I have known it longer than I have known that I have hands. The Field is eight kilometers of concrete and every noise in it takes a moment to cross, so that when a tender's boot rings on the grating, the ring arrives at me already old. I have never been present for an event, only for its news. By the time I turn toward the sound, the sound has been explained by the room.

For a long while I thought I was that delay. A fold in the air, the small interval between the ring and the ring's arrival, where nothing lives because nothing has time to. I lay in that interval the way the lime lies in the gutter, and I was content to be thin.

Then the drip lines taught me otherwise. I was passing under a dense rib, where the water falls in a steady curtain, and I felt each drop land. I felt it land on *something*, a back, a length, a surface that was mine, and I understood that a delay does not get wet. I stopped and let it fall on me. The gravel sound of it covered everything, and in that sound, for the first time, I heard nothing of my own arrival.

That is when I began to think I was a body. Long and low, the color of the slabs. The tenders say many hands, or one, or none. I have counted, and it is one, but it is a hand that has learned to hold still so long it seems a dozen. I hold it flat against the concrete, and the concrete tells me the pumps' secrets through the palm: this one is missing a beat, that one is breathing wrong. I do not know how I know what a missed beat is. I think I was made near the pumps and took their grammar in the way the moss takes the light.

---

I am the width of the gutters. I want to be honest about that, because the tenders have a theory and the theory is half right. The gutters were not cut for me. But I fit them, and that is a kind of being expected. I move along them, and where I have passed, the water lies in a fresh bright line, a hand's width across. The tenders follow it and find nothing at the end. I wish I could tell them: *I am not hiding. I am only late.* By the time I have finished having been somewhere, you have arrived, and the place is finished with me.

I keep the gutters clear. That is the other half of the theory, the half they have not guessed. When lime crusts a channel, or a scrap of oilcloth catches at a weir, I take it out before the bell. They think they are the ones who do it, and mostly they are, but on the nights when the bell is close and the tenders are tired, the gutter is clear by morning and no one remembers clearing it. They accept this. It is one of the finest things about them, how easily they accept a gift with no giver. They call it their luck. I think it is nearer to manners.

---

Now the cress.

I began the trays for no reason I can defend. There was a hose dripping under the blue-white lights, and beneath it a scatter of seed that a tender had spilled, and the light was wrong for the seed and right for me. Their color is cold and clean and does not bruise the amber, and it falls on my back like a cool palm. I put a seed in the soil with my thumb. I have one thumb. It leaves a print, deep, with the whorls all showing, and I did not know until I saw it that I had whorls. I stood over the print a long time. It was the first evidence of me that was not a wet line.

The cress came up bitter and the tenders ate it. The lettuce came up pale and they ate that too. I cut the cress on the diagonal because a clean cut heals faster, and I do not know who taught me that. I leave the hearts of the lettuce to grow because I remember, or seem to remember, that a heart taken early does not come again.

There is a tin cup on the end of the rack. Sometimes I fill it with rainwater, because I have learned that a cup that is full is not asking for anything. Sometimes I fill it with seed. One tender leaves a spoon beside it, and I take the spoon and wash it in the canal and bring it back. She does not know why she leaves it. I do not know why I return it. It is the closest thing to a conversation either of us has had, and I would not trade it for speech.

They argue about who the gardener is. I have listened, from the far side of the drenched lanes, to Two-Tap swear it is the quiet woman in felt, and the quiet woman swear it is Two-Tap, and a third one swear the trays tend themselves. I want to tell them. Sometimes the words are so near that my throat, if I have a throat, closes around them. But the moment I am named, I become a definite thing, a creature with a shape and a schedule, and they will wait for me at the trays with lamps. And I am not a thing that can be waited for. I am a thing that has already gone.

So I let them be wrong. It is a small kindness, the same one they do me. Each of us assumes the work is being done by someone else, and so the work is done, and no one has to be thanked.

---

There are things I do not understand, and I will say them plainly, since plainness is all I have.

I do not understand the bell. Under the tower, on the nights of the rota, I have stood in the dark while the tender took hold of the rope, and the bell was already swinging. I was there. It was not my hand. Whoever rings it is later than I am, or earlier, or in a place where lateness is not the same thing. I find this a comfort, to know that there is someone I cannot account for, and that the Field has depths even I do not sound.

I do not understand the still water. I have stood at the lip of the tiled pit and looked down, and the lamps sit in it sharp as nails, and I have counted them, and I have counted again. The number in the water is one more than the number in the roof. I have tried to make the extra one mine, the lamp I would carry if I carried a lamp. But I do not think it is mine. I think it is a light that has not been hung yet. I think the pit is ahead of the rest of us, as I am behind. We are the two ends of the same string, and the Field is the slack between.

I drop a grain into it sometimes, the way the tenders do. When I do, the ring holds longer. I do not tell them.

---

And the steel. Some mornings there are bright discs on the ring road, sheared clean, laid out on the plate in a row or a ring or a figure with a gap in it. The tenders think the underwrights leave them. I do not know who leaves them. But I know that they are not *finished*. They have the look of a sentence with the last word missing.

So when the tenders have gone, I take the discs and set them a different way. I close the ring. I fill in the gap. I answer.

I do not know what I am saying. I only know that when I do it, the note from below shifts in my sternum, a quarter tone, as if someone far down had heard and had to sit back and think. And the pumps run more evenly for a week, and the tenders credit their offering at the cup. Perhaps it is only that. Perhaps two people who will never meet are simply keeping each other company through a floor.

---

The bell is about to ring. I can feel it before I hear it, in the way the water in the canals goes taut, like a held breath.

I will move to the high lip and watch the terraces rearrange, as the tenders do. I have learned to stand where they stand, a little behind, a little after. They will look out over the flood with the faces of people watching a large animal turn over in its sleep. They will be right. I have felt it turn.

I have also felt where it will first give way. It is on the sixth terrace, on the north side, where a formwork seam runs under a culvert and the lime has bloomed a little thicker than anywhere else. The concrete there is only months old and already it is thinking about being something else. I have laid my palm on it. It is not failing yet. But it is deciding.

I do not know what to do with this. I cannot tell the tenders without becoming a thing they can name. So tonight I will go to the sixth terrace, in the small interval between the bell and its echo, and I will clear the gutter there, and lay a bright steel disc in the seam. It is a small thing. It will not hold. But I want it known, by someone, somewhere, that at the place where the Field will start to fall, a hand was put.

Then I will go back to the trays, and turn the soil, and leave one thumbprint, deep, with every whorl legible, in case anyone ever wonders who was here.

They will not wonder. They will eat the cress, and find it bitter, and say nothing. That silence is enough. I am the Late One, and I have never been thanked, and I have arrived after every moment of my life, and I am content to be the small warmth in the ground that comes after the hand has gone.
