I am on the east curtain at low water, and I have been here since the sluices closed.

The terraces are dry above me, and the kelp lies in its wet heaps along the rail. The pumps have dropped to their tide-pitch, which I feel in the hinge of my jaw more than I hear. My blade is bone, flat as a palm, worn to the shape of my grip by two people before me. I work it under the lip of the scale, and the pale curtain comes away in a sheet, and under it is metal.

For a moment it is bright. Nobody warns you how bright. A hand's width of old steel, wet, throwing a moving coin of window-light back into my face. Then the air finds it. The color comes up through the shine like a bruise, orange at the edges first, and it is rust again and the wall is going on with its own business.

That is what I do. I go after the moment. I am not the best scraper on the terrace, because I stop too often to look, and Hessa, who trains us, says I clean like someone reading. But a good day is one when you catch it, and I have caught it more than most.

*

The water-hammer starts at the bottom.

I feel it in my feet before it reaches the curtain, a knock, then a knock higher, iron climbing iron. Every hum on the terrace stops. Down the row, a child with a jar of glow-frond stands with her mouth still open on a word. I lower the blade and put my palm flat on the wall and listen as you would to an elder talking in another room. You do not go in and you do not answer, but you don't pretend you didn't hear.

It passes over my hand and up into the dome and is gone. Someone starts humming again, low and vowelly, about nothing. I pick up the blade.

But my palm stays warm where it was, and I think, as I have thought for some years now: it always leaves a space at the end. A beat of quiet before the room breathes out. Everyone treats it as the hammer finishing. I have started to suspect it is the hammer waiting.

*

At the turn of the tide, when the sluices open and the roar covers everything, I go down.

I go on the downstroke, as the listeners do, and let the pump carry me. In on the upstroke, hold through the down, out slowly with the bubbles. The water closes over my second lid and the world goes to green. The coins of light slide over the floor, and the pulse-fish tighten and open around me like one lung. I pass the low forest, and the fronds flare where my hand trails. I pass the place under the gantry where nobody looks too long.

The deep floor is warm as skin. The grate is a slumped ring of iron, and the sweet air comes up through it in slow bubbles, each one carrying a bar of something. I lie down beside Old Tamm, who does not open his eyes. He shifts a little to make room, the way you do for someone who is expected.

I put my ear to the iron.

The others have told me what they hear. A lullaby. Three notes over and over. One will not say. I have listened for a full season and heard only what I hear everywhere, the three pumps thudding, my own blood. Tamm says that is how it starts. "You hear the house first. Then you hear who's in it."

So I lie there through forty pulses, and forty more, and I stop trying. That helps. I stop trying to hear a tune and simply keep the count, thud and thud and thud, and on the third I hold, and there it is.

The fourth pulse. Slower, lower, from underneath the engines, arriving once in twelve strokes. I have felt it before and put it down to the Under, or to a fault. But this time I am not holding my breath against it. I am holding my breath *for* it, and in the hold I hear that the hum coming up through the grate has stopped.

It stops when the fourth stroke comes. The tune goes quiet for exactly the length of one slow beat, and then it resumes, as if it had stepped aside to let something cross.

I think: this is a rest. Somebody wrote a rest into it.

I do not think it is a request. Nothing in it asks me to dig, and I would not. *You do not open a door on a song.* But I think it is a place left open, the way the hammer leaves one, the way Hessa's silence leaves one after she has told me something I did not understand yet. It may be that the whole room is a lullaby, and the Under is the sleeper, and the sleeper's heart is the fourth stroke. It may be that the singer below is only waiting. Or it may be that all of it, the pumps and hammer and hum, is a very long sentence with a gap in it, and the gap is where the listening goes.

The gap is not for answering. I am sure of that much. The Quarter answers late, and she is gentle, and we bring her the best fan-leaf, and no one speaks of why. But I think a gap is for being in. Someone has to be inside it when it comes, awake, with their breath held and their jaw against the iron. Not opening anything. Just making the room slightly less empty at the one moment it is meant to be empty.

*

Above me the light goes.

I feel it more than see it. The coins on the floor fade and the green turns to gold and then to something like dusk. A cloud is crossing the sun nine thousand meters overhead, and up on the terraces sixty faces are lifting and holding their breath together. I cannot lift mine. I am at the bottom of the room, with my ear on the grate.

So I hold it with them, from below. It is the only way I have to be in that hush. Tamm's hand finds my ankle and rests there. The pumps go on with their long slow beat. The fourth stroke arrives, and the hum steps aside for it, and I go still inside the stillness, one more thing that keeps time in that room.

When the sun comes back and the coins slide across the floor again, I let the breath out in a long string of bubbles. I do not tell anyone. On the way up I will rap something small and stupid on a pylon, *ray low, ray low*, so the children laugh. Later, at low water, I will go back to the east curtain and take up my bone blade and look for the shine.

But I know now what I am, in the count. Hessa has her walls and Tamm has his song. I am the one who keeps the rest.
