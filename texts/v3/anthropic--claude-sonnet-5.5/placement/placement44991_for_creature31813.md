I am in the niche by the great valve, and I have been for four hundred and some breaths.

I know this because I am counting. The niche was worn to the shape of a body long before mine, and mine fits it the way a hand fits a glove that has had many hands in it. Someone's shoulders made the left hollow. Someone's habit of leaning made the ridge where my elbow goes. When I sit, I am joining a long line of people who all sat like this, and the stone remembers them better than I do.

The valve breathes. *Hsssssss.* One.

Between breaths I have very little to do except be a place where attention is happening. That is the job, though nobody says so. The count matters less than the counting. The pump does not need my number. It needs to be *watched*, and I am the watching, dusted with a grey-green bloom that I have decided not to brush off tonight.

Two. Three.

The number I hold came to me in a whisper, cheek to cheek, from the woman who sat here before me. She said *six thousand two hundred and nine*, and then she stood, and her knees cracked, and she went up the root-way toward her supper without looking back. I say it inside my mouth now with each breath, adding one. I don't know what the number is a number *of*. It began somewhere, in some earlier whisper, and the ledger in its lead box knows where. I only know that I received it and must not drop it. When my turn ends I will hand it on, larger, and it will go into the ink, and I will feel lighter for a day without knowing why.

A drop finds my cheek. I close my eyes and open them, slowly, like a leaf turning over. Not a sign of anything. Just the House touching me the way it touches everyone, to see if I'm still here.

*

Here is what I would be, if I could choose, and I think I have chosen: not the one who leaps the canal, and not the deaf eldest in the turbine hall, whom I revere from a distance the way you revere weather. I would be the one who keeps the number. The one who is *between*. A pipe that a message runs through, warm from the message, not the message itself.

There is a small vanity in this, and I'll admit it. Sometimes I hear the pipes knocking all through the walls, the soft metallic gossip of sixty people saying *the taro is ready* and *she's born, it's a girl* and *the outflow ran rusty this morning*, and I think that I am the same as the pipe. I carry what passes through me. I have no voice of my own in it except the small rhythm of my key, which I use sparingly. What I am good at is not *saying*. It is being the kind of thing that holds still long enough for something to arrive.

*

At the change of the cycle, when the pump shifts and the canal rises its hand's breadth, I hear my name tapped in the pipe beside the niche. Not my name. My knock, which is the same thing: two shorts, a long, a short. A child's rhythm. I was given the key young, because I heard the drone drop half a tone one evening and said so, and no one laughed. The others went quiet, and the eldest was fetched, and she put her palm flat on the wall and stood with her eyes closed for as long as a valve-breath takes to come three times. Then she nodded. Something in the bearing had been failing, and we caught it early. I got the key on its cord. I have worn it bright.

Tonight the knock says: *the market arcade. Someone has come.*

So the number will go to another mouth. I lean toward the small brown face of the boy who is my relief, and I say into his ear, *six thousand six hundred and twelve*, and he repeats it, and I nod, and it is his. My mouth feels strange without it, like a room after a piece of furniture has been carried out.

*

I go up by the root-way, barefoot, gripping. The fig is warm under my soles, ridged, faintly pulsing with the pump. Below me the streets are the width of a handcart and the light is that tired green, and then I come out into a slanted column and the vapor makes it solid, and I walk through a wall of light with the taste of iron on my tongue.

I don't go down to the arcade. That is not how it is done. I climb to the window wound with fern, above the keystone, and I put my hand on the root I bent last spring, when the runoff came down the wrong side. It took a season of tying. I coaxed the growth along the line of the old stress, an inch at a time, until the water came to the height of an outstretched hand. It was a slow, quiet argument with a tree, and the tree let me win by pretending it had been its own idea. That is the whole art of gardening here.

Through the fern I can see the visitor in the arcade. A small figure, head tipped back toward the grating, one palm open. The water is running down the vine into it in a bright line. It is warm. I know it is warm because I have held my own hand there, alone, on evenings when nobody had come, to check.

They are standing very still. That is good. It is what the House likes.

Now comes the part that is truly mine, the part I would keep if I could keep only one. I wait. I don't knock yet. There is a full cycle of the pump to get through, and in it I will find out whether this stranger is someone who stays. Most don't. They look, they wonder, they wipe their hand on their coat and go back up the stair toward the light and the dry, and I don't blame them, because it is not a place that asks. It only offers water at the right height.

But sometimes one of them sits down on the wet step. Sometimes the shoulders loosen. Sometimes a drop falls from the vault onto a cheek and the eyes close and open again, slowly, and I think: *there, that one has been touched, that one is being counted.*

I put two fingers on the pipe beside me. I don't tap yet. I feel the iron humming with the whole quarter's small talk. Somewhere down in the dark the spillway roars, and the valve breathes, and I think, in the number I no longer hold, *six thousand six hundred and thirteen*.

*Hsssssss.*

I wait. The pipe is warm. The stranger is still there.

Three shorts. A pause.

I let the pause be as long as it needs to be.

Then the long.
