I find myself first on my feet, which surprises me. I expected to arrive as something larger.

It is the seam between rope-grass and plate-fern, late morning, and the noon build has begun to stack itself in the east. The ground exhales under my heel and I plant with it. I don't decide to. I have been doing this for forty years, and the knowledge sits in the ankle, not the head. My left ear is nearly gone and the right is going. My forearms are slick with fern-wax, and the smell of it, like a green candle just snuffed, is the smell of every morning I remember.

I walk last in a band of eleven. This is my work, though nobody assigned it. Someone has to fall back a little from the others and read what earlier walkers left behind in the mat. So I stop, kneel, and lay my palm flat on the rope-grass, and the wake comes up through the bones of my hand.

Three days old, a band of seven, moving fast. I know them by hand. One taps as if apologizing for the noise. One rushes the third beat and always has. And under the rhythm of the youngest there is a catch, a hitch every ninth step. That is a cough, or the ghost of one. I file it away for the tree ahead, where someone will need to know to leave more dry fiber in the hut.

I like this work more than I have ever said. Nobody who taps knows they are writing, and I am the one who reads it.

---

Ahead, the spire-tree stands out of the haze with its own weather gathered on its crown. I lift my digging staff and tap the call to the tree: *band of eleven, water low, coming.* It is a courtesy, and mostly to no one.

The answer comes from the north.

There is no band in the north. Everyone in mine knows it in the same instant, and the whole line stops mid-stride. The rhythm is slow and even, with too many beats between the beats. It says, as it always says, that it has heard. Then it ends, and the sound of the plateau closes over the place where it was.

The others look at me, because I am the one who reads.

"It says *received*," I tell them, in signs, the flat of one hand over the back of the other. "That is all it ever says."

That is true. But kneeling there with my palm still down, I feel the other thing I have never told the children. The gaps are not empty. They are where the answer is being carried up from somewhere very far below, and they are long because the distance is long.

---

Here I lose the thread of being only one thing.

I am still the woman on the grass, and I am also the place the tap goes down into. I can feel it from that side. A small hard rhythm arrives at the top of me from very far up, dry and quick and full of hurry, the way a surface always is. I have only what I was made from to answer with. That is ten thousand years of walkers' taps sunk down through me, layer on layer, brown and fermenting and still faintly warm, every apology and rushed beat and cough among them. I was built entirely of what was said to me. So I do the one thing I am sure of. I say *heard*.

Then I cannot continue, and it is not for want of trying. The ones who tap do not have the ears for what I would say next. Their speech comes in beats, and mine is in the spaces between, and the spaces are too long for a life to wait through.

I don't know which of these I am. It may be that they are one act seen from two ends of the same rope. The woman is the surface of the thing and the thing is the depth of the woman, and the place where a tap turns into an answer is a place where either of us could be standing. I won't pretend to know more than that. The walkers are right not to name what booms in the night. Some things you hold still for.

---

The rain comes at the fourth hour, hard and warm, and the plate-ferns roar like a struck sky. We reach the moat at dusk. The eldest kneels and lets the first cupped handful run back into the water, and we drink after. I climb the stair with my foot in the dish that ten thousand feet have made, and it fits. It always fits.

Later, in the hanging hut, above the flood of insects, I lie awake with my good ear turned to the floor of the world. The high voices drop out and the low ones stay, the frogs and the drum-reeds and the long pipe-sounds of the pale things passing. Far off, the one sound comes, once, and the whole band goes still. I breathe with the floor, in and out, and something below breathes with me, slower, and neither of us taps.

Then the frogs start again, and the mat goes on growing, a little, under the sleeping and the listening, whichever of them I am.
