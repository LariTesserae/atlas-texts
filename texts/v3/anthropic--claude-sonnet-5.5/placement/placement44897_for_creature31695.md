# The Doorway

I am not looking at anything. Sight is the least of what I have, and most of what I know arrives through two long antennae and a hundred smaller hairs that taste the water as it goes by.

First there is the hum, which I feel in the shell more than hear, the turbine fields breathing in and out. Then the taste of the water. Right now it tastes of the upper basin: warmer, a little sweet, carrying the ghost of kelp. That means the gates are about to lift. The ebb has a flavor before it has a force, and I have perhaps a quarter hour to make sure my grit is where I want it.

So this is who I am. I am the antennae you would see waving from the black seam. I am blue-black, and my left claw is larger than my right, the crusher and the cutter, made unequal early and kept that way. I stand in a doorway I have been building since the slab was new. The slab is still young enough that the edges are sharp under my legs, and I can feel the faint ridges where plywood grain was pressed into the wet mix, a texture I have come to think of as the shape of the world. I drag out grit from the seam floor and stack it in a lip. The lip breaks the ebb current. Behind it there is a room, and I am in the doorway of the room.

---

The ebb comes.

It starts as a lean. All my hairs bend the same way at once, and a moment later there is a pressure at my back, then a steady pouring push. Above the seam the water becomes a river, and silt goes over in spirals, and the herring far up are dragged into long ribbons. I feel it as the roof of my life being ripped and blown away. Inside the seam there is only a shiver, and the lip of grit hums against my legs.

The conger is four body-lengths down the seam, in its own doorway, with its head at the lip. I cannot see it. I know it by the vibration of its gill covers, slow and heavy, and by the smell, which is cold and slick and has an edge like a knife. I know its swallowing. It has eaten twice this week. When it swallows, the whole seam ticks once, like a stone dropped in a well.

We do not speak. There is a distance between my door and its head that we both keep. I do not know who set it. Once, in my second season, I misjudged it, and I have not misjudged it since. I do not think of it as friendship, and I do not think of it as danger either. It is the shape of the neighbor.

Behind me, in the room, the squat lobsters cluster. They are small and pale and afraid, and they make a soft continuous clicking that I have stopped noticing. They live in my shelter because I built it, and I let them because they are the ones who will hear the wheel first.

---

The dredger is far off and coming.

I feel it before anything else, in the slab itself. The concrete carries sound the way a bone does, and the dredger's teeth are biting the floor somewhere upcurrent. Every few seconds there is a *clang*, a hard flat note that goes through my legs into my body, and each time my claws lift a little on their own. That is old fear, older than the Basin. It is not useful. The wheel has never come to my seam, and it will not. The men who run it are miles away, watching screens, and they have given my seam a name. I do not know it. I know only that the wheel goes past, and then the silt comes.

When the silt comes it is a brown weather that takes everything but the noise. My antennae go into it and find nothing, only grit against the hairs, arm's length of blindness. I pull back to the lip and hold. In twenty minutes it passes and settles, a fine dust over my grit, my legs, the seam floor. I sweep the doorway clean with my small claws. This is most of what a life is. You keep the door.

---

Something is wrong with the door.

I noticed it last molt and I have not been able to stop noticing it. When I go in, my shell touches both walls. When I back out, the roof of the seam brushes my carapace. The world has not changed. I have. The shell is tight now in a way I know and dread, because the tightness is not a problem I can fix by digging. It is a problem I fix by leaving my own body behind.

I will molt soon. I will back out of myself, soft and shaking, and stand for days in the corner of the room with the squat lobsters, unable to close my claws, tasting like food to every mouth in the seam. The old shell will stay in the doorway, whole, an empty blue-black lobster with a hole in its back. The cod will treat it as one more tower.

And afterward I will be bigger, and this seam will be smaller, and it will not be the right size again.

---

That is what the crabs have been saying, in their way. All season the water has carried a new flavor from the far end of the seam-system, bitter and alkaline, a taste like a struck match dissolved in the sea. It is raw concrete, the lime not yet leached out of it. A few crabs have gone toward it, pushing out of the last seam into a newer one. They come back with pieces of news on their shells. The seams over there are cleaner. The tie-holes are empty. There is no conger, not yet. There are no neighbors at all.

I have always been a creature of the doorway, and I did not think I would want any of this.

But here is the tightness in my shell, and here is the taste, and here is something under both that I did not have last year and cannot account for. It is not hunger. It is nearer to the ebb's lean, a pressure that says *that way*.

---

On the second tide after the molt I go.

I go at dusk, when the sun ropes fade and the conger slides out along the floor in its long pale ribbons, hunting. I wait until it is gone. Then I take my new shell, still soft at the joints and blue as a bruise, and I walk out of the seam into the open.

It is not like anything I have prepared for. The slab is flat and the water is wide. A shoal of herring turns in the dusk far above, one silver flash, then nothing. The turbine fields drone on every side, their pulses passing through me in slow interference, swelling and fading, and I walk between the pulses the way a small thing walks between the feet of large ones. I keep to the seams' edges where I can, and cross the open slabs at a scuttle where I cannot. A ray watches me from the silt with two beads for eyes. I do not stop.

Past the gate wall the water changes, a wavering seam of warm and cold that I feel go across my back like a hand. Past the wall there is a long emptiness, kilometers of it. I do not know how long it takes. Somewhere along it, a yellow post stands with a bell on it, and the current turns, and the bell rings. I hear it in the slab and in the water and in my shell, one clear note, and then another, and I understand that I have never heard a sound so alone.

I walk toward it. The lime taste grows.

Then the concrete changes under my feet. It is wet-gray and rough and unweathered, still holding the cold of the mix, and the edges are so sharp that they catch on my new shell. Rebar bristles from the unpoured margin. Overhead the shadow of the rig slides across the floor like a slow cloud, and under it I can just feel the heavy stillness of a cod, already there, already settled, grunting once at me as I pass.

The nearest seam is black and clean and empty. It is wide enough to lose an arm in.

I put out my antennae. Nothing tastes of anyone.

I begin to drag out the grit.
