I start by hanging, which surprises me. I expected to arrive somewhere, but I'm already head-down from a rope-stem with my feet gripping it, the way a body learns to do so early it forgets it ever learned. The light comes down in its wavering shafts. I have no idea how long I've been here.

My first thought is that I'm a trout. The slow drift over the meadows, the name that means something, being greeted with an open palm and answering with a fin. It's a lovely thing to be. But the trout are guests, and I notice I don't want to be a guest. I want to have my hands in something. So I let the trout go, and I watch *Late Again* pass beneath me, still late.

Then I think I might be the bell-tree. Older than the houses, a single low note, the town tuning itself to me. But the bell-tree doesn't get to do anything. It only answers, and I find I'd rather be a thing that could get it wrong.

So I'm a person, and I'm not at the court. The court is where the loud arguments happen, and I've never been good at bellowing. I'm not in the deepest hollow with the Listeners either, though I go there. I'm at the edge of the court, in the shade, where the long cords hang.

---

Here is who I am.

I was born to a house on the eastern shelf, and my cord is knotted into the wall at my mother's shoulder-height. There's a knot for every naming-day, and I know by touch which ones are mine. Around the ninth, the fibers change color. My mother switched dyes that year, and I've always thought of it as the year she started to worry about me.

The worry was that I couldn't cut. Every child learns *leave the one that goes with the light, cut the one that goes against the wall*, and I learned it and could recite it. But when the Pruner handed me the shell knife and pointed to the shoot that went wrong, my hand wouldn't close. Sometimes it was only that I could see what the shoot was trying to do. It wasn't wrong. It was reaching toward a gap in the lattice that nobody else had noticed, a small window of light that would be there in nine years. I said so, and I was right about the light, and it made no difference. The lane was already planned. The shoot was shaded and starved, gently, and it browned by the next season.

I don't say the Pruners were cruel. They aren't. They are keeping a pattern that has held four generations of roofs over four generations of children, and holding is hard. I've come to think that the wisdom they teach is real and also that it has a price, and that the price is paid by shoots with no say in the matter.

There was a second thing. My voice wouldn't go where the choir goes. When the town hums the bell-tree's note at dusk I hum it too, but I come in a little under. It isn't much, a quarter-tone at most. But three hundred throats on one pitch has a shape, and mine is a small dent in it. Nobody has ever said anything. A woman beside me once shifted her drift a hand's breadth away without seeming to notice she had.

So I did what the crooked ones do, and I nearly went over the rim. I got as far as the shallows, where the granite steps are worn into spoon-hollows, and I hung there under the ice-free surface and watched the ducks' feet paddle above me like slow pale flowers. I was fifteen. I stayed most of a day. Then the evening reeds rang, all of them, the long note across the water, and my body answered before I did. I felt it in my ribs. I swam back down, not because I'd decided the tale of the air was only a tale, but because the reeds were ringing *I am here* and I couldn't bear to be a place they'd have to call to.

---

What I do now doesn't have a proper name. The nearest word is *mender*, but the mending is of cords, not of walls.

Old cords fray. A wall-ledger is only as good as its fibers, and the fibers go, slowly, in the cold and the silt. The knots near the base, the founders' knots, are dark and hard as horn, but the middle generations are in softer cord that the moss has been at, and the shrimp. Someone has to go along the walls with a bone needle and spliced fiber and re-lay the strands without changing a single turn of the knot. You have to read it as you mend it. You can't fix a knot you don't understand. I have read the wedding of a woman who died before my grandmother was born. I have read a flood of silt, a lost child found, and the winter of the bad ice, when the cords of that year are tied so tight and small they look like clenched fists.

That's the work in daylight. It's small, and hardly anyone thinks about it, which is how they know it's done well.

The other work is in the shade at the court's edge, where the long knots hang, the ones tied off to nothing. I don't tie any. That isn't my place, and no one would ask it. What I do is come, every season, and run my hands along them and keep them from rotting. Nobody assigned me this. Nobody has said not to. Once a Pruner watched me from across the court while I worked, and I waited for the bellow, but she only lowered her eyes and swam on. I took that as the closest thing to permission the town has.

I can't say why I do it. The people on those cords chose the rim, or the rim chose them, and the town let them go. But a cord is a promise made on behalf of both ends, and one end of these is still here. If it rots, it will mean the town's memory has a hole in exactly the shape of the ones who didn't fit. I would like it to have the shape, and to have it intact.

Sometimes, oiling the fibers, I find that I hum. Low, and a quarter-tone under. The shrimp in the moss along the court wall go quiet for a moment, then resume their dry crackle. I choose not to read anything into that.

---

Now it's late in the day and the light is thinning to violet. The lantern snails are rising along the lanes and the keeper of this one has fed them well, so there's no dark stretch. I set down the needle. The drummers begin their pulse, quick tonight, so the ice will break early this year. I feel it in my teeth.

I drift into the green vault with the others and hang there. Someone hums the note. Then more of them, and then all of them, three hundred throats on the pitch that has held the place together longer than the cords can say, and I come in under, as I always do.

Something I've never told anyone: in spring, when the ice breaks and the water begins to warm, the bell-tree's tone climbs slowly, and for a few days it passes through the place where I hum. For those days I am not a dent in the shape. I'm inside it. The woman beside me shifts closer, or doesn't shift away, and the sound is whole in a way it isn't all year.

It's a small thing. It comes for a few days and then goes. But I think it may be why the tree needs people who are slightly off. A note that everyone holds exactly is only a note. A note held by a crowd with a few crooked voices in it can be heard *moving*, and something has to be moving for the tree to answer.

I don't know if that's true. I haven't asked a Listener, because I'm afraid they'd say it isn't, and I'm more afraid they'd say it is.

Below us, in the dark of the deepest hollow, the bell-tree answers. I hum under it, in the green and the cold, and I wait, as everyone does, to be sure that it does.
