I am at the edge, where the spires are young and I am the youngest thing that still walks.

I know it by the sound before I know it by anything else. Out here the pipes are the height of my knee and they scream, thin and bright, a hundred small kettles. The old ones say the margin is a child's register. They say it kindly, or what passes for kindly among us, which is that they say it once, low, and do not say it again. The margin is mine to keep. I have kept it for eleven turns of the seasons, and my palms are only now beginning to go to leather. Where the scars haven't healed, the edges still open me in new places every week.

I stand in the fog with my shoulders turned. My sleeves are dark to the elbow. I have been standing for a long time, I think, because the drip has stopped being separate drips and become a texture, like grain in wood. That is the first sign I've properly arrived. When I first came, every drop was an event, and I flinched at each. Now the thousand chambers and their thousand pitches are one cloth, and I can pick at a single thread of it.

The thread I'm picking is a spire three paces to my left. It has been wrong for a week.

---

It is a tall one for the margin, nearly to my hip, with a throat no wider than two fingers. Its note has climbed. I don't know why. Perhaps the spring beneath it has pinched, perhaps its lip has grown a lip. It sits in the chord like a pin in a sleeve. Every Listener who walks the inward passage would feel it catch, a small ugliness at the start of the road, and by the third pipe it would be in their teeth.

I kneel. The crust holds, because I know where it holds. I take the bone-handled chisel from my belt. The handle is worn to the shape of a hand that was not mine, and my thumb has spent eleven turns learning its hollow. I lay the edge against the rim and stop.

Here is the part no one taught me and everyone knew. A fingernail's depth off the lip would lower the note. It would fit. And it is such a small thing that the old ones would call it nothing. But this spire is young, and it is trying to find its voice. It is shrill because it is new, not because it is broken. If I trim it now I make it what the chord wants today, and the spire that would have found its own low voice in a century never quite gets to. It comes out tidy and a little false.

So the question of the work is always the same: is this ugliness the pipe's, or only the pipe's for now?

I put my palm over the throat instead. The exhale is faint, barely warmer than my hand. The water below shivers. I put my ear down almost to the rim, which is foolish, because the edge could open my cheek, and I listen not to the note but to what is under it, the sound of the water climbing.

There. A stutter. Something in the spring is catching, a mineral bridge half formed across the vent. The pipe is not sharp. It is short of breath.

I take the hooked rod from my other side and feed it down the throat slowly, with more patience than force, until I feel the soft resistance. I do not push. I lean my weight into the stillness of my arm and wait for the crust to decide. It gives with a sound I feel in my wrist more than hear, a small wet crackle. The water rises. The note, which had been reaching for something and failing, drops a third and settles.

I kneel there afterward for some time, letting the sound rearrange around the change. The chord accepts it the way a crowd accepts someone stepping in. Nobody looks up.

---

The wader is there when I stand.

I don't know how long it has been there. It is grey as the spires and stands in the channel a few paces beyond, one leg drawn up, the long neck folded, the comb of its beak resting just under the surface. If I had not been kneeling so close to the ground I would have taken it for a tall pipe with an odd shadow. It is the only silent thing here. Even I am not silent. My breath is a sound, and my knees, and the cloth of my sleeve against the stone. It is not silent because it is quiet. It is silent because it has taken itself out of the argument.

I go and stand beside it in the channel. The water is cold to the ankle and the mat under my feet gives off a faint blue-green wink at each shift of weight, a ghost of light running out ahead of me and fading. The wader does not turn its head. I don't ask it anything. I don't think of it as company, and I don't think of myself as its. We are two things that have chosen to be in the same fog.

After an hour my legs have gone dull and I notice I have stopped listening to the chord. I am inside it, and it is not something I am hearing but something I am standing in, the way you stand in weather. I think this is what the old ones mean when they say a Listener is a spire that can move. I think it is also what they mean when they say nothing.

Then the wader lifts its foot and steps, and I watch where it goes. Inward. Toward the low deep. I don't follow.

---

At the turn of the season I walk the line.

It begins at the southern cairns, the dark slabs gone white, one whole and one fallen in on itself, and I go past them slowly, because if I hurry the crust will remember. The prints begin a little way beyond. They are sealed under a clear skin of stone thicker than last year's, and it is unnerving how fine they are, the whorls of the palms still faintly legible, the splay of fingers. Someone pressed down hard, once, into soft mud, and the mud kept it.

At each one I stop and set my hand over it. Scarred palm to sealed palm. The stone is colder than the air, and beneath the cold there is something I have never been able to name and have stopped trying to, which is not warmth but a kind of attention. I stand a moment. I move on.

There are dozens. I know them the way you know the faces of people you've never met but pass every day. This one leaned on its left. This one has a crooked smallest finger. This one, ahead, must have been the first to walk in, because the print is deeper, sure of itself.

The small ones I leave for last. There are more of them than I like to count. My hand covers each one entirely, and each time I think that whoever made it must have trusted the hand beside it. I stand longest at the last of them, near the end of the line, where the mud grew soft and something stopped.

I am supposed to stop here, and I do.

Ahead the fog thickens and the spires get taller, older, their pale sides rusted near the vents. The chord drops as it always does, tone by tone, until it is almost below hearing, a pressure in the sternum, a held breath. It is the lowest place in the basin, and the print-line points at it as plainly as a finger.

Something in me wants to keep walking. I won't say that isn't true. It is not a wish to die. It is more like the wish to finish a sentence someone else began. But the line stops and I stop with it, and I look inward, and the low note rises under the silence the way it always does, so deep I feel it in the chest before I hear it. My head dips. I am not sure to what. It is not an answer, exactly. It is a receipt.

---

On the way back I pass the room, and I go in because the chord has grown too crowded to think.

The wall against my back is cold and rough and slightly wet. The doorway faces the rim, and the wind is coming down in one of its slow pulses, crossing the mouths of the set tubes in the corner, and there it is: one clear, unbroken note, sustained, deliberate. Someone wanted to listen. Someone stacked stone and chose which tubes and which way they'd stand, and did it well enough that it is still sounding, and I do not know their name. I press my hand flat against the wall and hold it there. It's the only thing I have ever known to say about them.

I tilt my head until the note separates from the rest. I sit a long time. When I get up my face feels rinsed.

---

Going out I pass the young spire at the edge of my stretch, the thin one, still shrill, tended more carefully than the rest. I don't know whose it is. No one says. I pass my fingers along its lip and don't take the chisel out.

In a hundred years it will have found a lower voice. I won't be here to hear it, or I will be, in the way that counts. Either way I will set my hand on it in passing, and go on with the work, and the fog will not lift, and the water will keep climbing the hollow stems, and the chord will go on holding its one long note, a little lower every year, not yet finished.
