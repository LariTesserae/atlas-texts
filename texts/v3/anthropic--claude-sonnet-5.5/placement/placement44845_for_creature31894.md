I'd like to say I'd be a diver, going down eleven meters into the green light to lay my hand on the smooth streets. But when I try to picture it, my lungs won't do it. I'm a person who breathes out at the wrong moment.

So I'll start again, from the rim. I walk down into the bowl on a path that will be gone by next week, and the mat takes my weight the way a hammock does. Warm tea-water climbs my ankles. I try being a sexton for a moment, a dark hump moving under the vines, browsing the underside of the world and rising twice an hour to lift my face into the light with my nostrils sealed. It's a good life. But I'd have nothing to say about it, and I think I'm someone who needs to say things.

A lantern fish in a sealed pocket is nearer the truth. It knows one room of warm brown water and has been told, by nothing, that there is more. I could be that. But the Stitchers lie down with their ears to the peat and bring insects as gifts, so even the fish in the sealed pocket has visitors, and I would want to be one of the visitors. I want to be the one who brings something and listens.

So I'm a Stitcher, and not a good one. My seams are lumpy. When two crests meet and the mat tears like wet canvas, I go out with the others in the first hour, kneeling in the warm seam-water with my bone needle, and I lace a line of six while the others finish eight. My cable is always a little too tight. Someone has to stitch the crooked edge, and someone will say, in whistles, low under the frogs, that the pool I've made is shaped like a sleeping dog. The pool becomes the dog pool. The fish like it. I have never been sure this counts as competence.

The tannin has climbed to my knees. I am not young.

What I'm good at is the hall. My family's is an old arcade with a stair of thirteen steps, and I keep the stair. I don't own it. I inherit the obligation, and the roots keep growing, so every season I go through with a knife and a mallet and ease back the new wood that would close the fourth step, or the ninth. The fourth is mat thickness. The ninth is what the sextons do before the mat thins. The eleventh is still mostly empty, and I am fond of it, because nobody has decided what a person should know at that depth.

In the hall I talk. That's what I'm for. Out on the mat my whistles come out wrong, a little too long, and children giggle. But in the woven dimness, with the noise of the fen drunk down to a hush and the light coming through in coins, I can sit with a grandchild's shoulder against mine and say things in a voice that arrives inside her head. I tell her that the fourth step is the mat and the mat is a raft of small stubborn things that agreed to hold hands. I tell her that the city under us wore itself smooth on purpose or by accident, and that either way it's now a mosaic of eggs. She says *and the seventh?* and I say *that one is only for the dead*, which is not an answer, and she accepts it, because in a hall an answer can be a shape.

My second vocation is the one I'm slightly ashamed of. I read the shells.

We are a small school and mostly old. We collect the oldest spirals from dead reed-stalks and set them in a row on the sill of a hall's highest arch, and we argue about the ratios. One of us swears the pattern repeats every ten thousand pulses and that the Governors will one day say a word. Another says there is no pattern, only the print of a snail's terror, generation on generation, laid down in the growth of the whorl. I have come to think the second is right, and I have also come to think it does not matter, because I no longer believe fear and knowledge are separate things. The snail climbs before the pulse. It doesn't know the interval. It knows what readiness is, and it has put that into its body so thoroughly that its house is shaped like it. The shell is a record of being ready for something you can't predict. Is that not the whole of us? Are our locked knees and soft ankles any different?

I say this in the hall to the other readers, in a low voice, and they receive it with the patience they give to weather.

Late in the afternoon I go out, as everyone does. There is no work in it. I sit on a warm patch near a sexton's breathing hole, because I think better beside the peaceful. The light goes copper. Someone is mending a net. A child is walking in her grandmother's pattens and falling over.

Then the snails start up the reeds, and here is my failing, the one everyone knows. I *spend it*. I always breathe out a little too early, at the crest, in the beat before the rain. I have never once ridden all the way through without leaking some of the held air. My nieces tease me and tell me it's because I have too much to say.

The note comes into my sternum. The plain lifts. Trees flex, the blue flames walk and die, and I am eleven meters above a street I will never see, on locked knees, with my eyes on the sky. The water climbs the column and hangs in its trembling crown. I hold.

I want to tell you I held all the way this time, that I learned it in a single afternoon. I think I held longer than I ever have. The rain came down warm out of the empty air, and it smelled of clean stone, and I held out my palms, not knowing why, like everyone else. If I did let go early, it was only the last small bit, and it felt less like failing than like a sentence finding its end.

Somewhere a heron-thing tore its sheet in half. Somewhere a child laughed. I bent to check the nearest seam, which was too tight, as usual, and left it.
