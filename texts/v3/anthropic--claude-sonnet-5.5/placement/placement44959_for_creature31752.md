# The Hinge Course

I come to myself at a seam, which seems right.

Underfoot the herringbone runs out and meets a basket-weave, and the tiles between them tilt like pack ice. The warmth comes up through my soles. My first thought is that I am a walker, one of the warm, moving, unfinished shapes the floor lays itself under. I take a step and the tile arrives as my weight does. The mortar takes my heel.

Then I look behind me and there are no footprints. There is a line of pale grout, thin as a hair, in the shape of a stooped figure with one arm extended. It is lying on the bare plan a few paces back, and it is a little darker at the shoulder than at the hip.

So I was one of the Unassigned. A storm came through while I was leaning toward the last laid tile, and something filled me. I do not remember the filling. It seems I was given warmth first, then a stoop, and last a question the others in the ranks never had.

The question is: *which one?*

---

The herringbone wants to continue. I can feel it in the arm, in the gladness of the next tile. The basket-weave wants to continue too, from the other side, with the same gladness. Standing at the tilt, I am the one thing on the floor that feels both wants at once. I think this is because I am new, and nothing has told me to choose.

There is a trowel in the mortar at my feet, set down in mid-stroke and warm as bread. The handle is rolled clay, and the bore is neither four fingers nor two thumbs. It is a long shallow trough with a ridge down the middle, like a grip made for a hand that was meant to hold two things and let neither go. I have no such hand. I kneel and close what I have around it, and the clay is polished in a way that suggests a long slow turning. It begins to fit, the way a word begins to fit a mouth.

The nap on the wall of the nearest kiln-tower goes flat, combed toward me. I have been looked at.

I do not dissolve. I feel the dread of it, the lintel sagging in my chest. But looking is not the same as finishing. I am looked at and I am still almost, because I have not laid the last tile of anything. I never will if I am careful. That is the trade I have found: to be seen and survive, I must be a thing that cannot be completed.

---

So I begin the work I was filled to do, and nobody assigned it. I lay a hinge course.

It is a row of tiles with two faces. The near half is cut to the angle of the herringbone and the far half to the angle of the basket-weave, and the joint between them is not sealed. I leave it open by the width of a thumbnail, where the brine can weep through. It does not resolve the argument. Nothing here should. It lets the argument be walked across. One plan's tiles lean into mine and the other plan's lean into mine from the other side, and for the length of a forearm the ice stops cracking.

It is slow, and it is against every pull in the arm. The compulsion says *one more, and one more, and the course goes out to the horizon, true, and never touches the other.* I lay the tile and stop. I lay the next and stop. Between the stops the floor ticks, and something in the seam beneath me exhales.

I think the Setters on either side can feel it. Somewhere in the herringbone a stooped shadow slows its blade. Somewhere in the basket-weave another one does. They are not grateful, because gratitude needs an interior and they are all forward motion. But the mortar is wetter where I work, and it holds a shape longer, and I choose to take that as thanks.

---

At the slump-hour the ceiling drops, and I keep laying while the red tide comes. I have learned not to hold my ground. I hold the trowel, and the trowel holds the seam, and the mud runs around both of us like a river around a post. When it sets I am standing on a new pattern, and the hinge is gone. It is somewhere under the paving now, sorted and stacked into someone else's courses. I am not sorry. Nothing I make will last past evening, and I begin to think that is what makes it a gift. A thing that will be pulled apart and pressed flat must be made gently enough to be worth pulling apart.

I find a pallet of unlaid tile, wet from the reset. I turn one over. In the slip of the underside there is a print, too deep, of something with a shoulder. I hold it a long while. It might be a Sorter's careful thumb, or the tread of some earlier hinge-layer who stood at some earlier tilt and asked *which one?* and was pressed into clay for asking it. I don't decide which. I lay it in my next course with the print facing down, where it will be warm.

---

Toward the end of the light I hear it, coming down the colonnade from the far side, where every fourth column faces the wrong way: a tread in wet mortar, arriving. A walker. Warm, moving, and unfinished, with a doubled shadow and a gait that leaves seams unsealed.

I know what I could do. I could go thin and pale like a bruise fading and move to the seam they are not watching, as every Setter before me has done. It is the law and the courtesy of the floor, and it keeps us whole by keeping us unseen.

But I have a hinge in my hands, and a walker has to cross the tilt somehow.

So I stay. I stoop over the seam where their next step will land, the arm extended, the blade steady. I do not look away from the work, and I do not hide it. I let the pale line of my shoulder stay where the light can find it. The nap on the tower lies flat toward us both and does not lift. Somewhere under the plain the many-dreamed hum goes on, and I feel the two plans lean against my hinge like sleepers turning toward a warm back.

The walker's foot comes down. The tile is there. The seam under it is wet, and open by the width of a thumbnail, and it takes the shape of their heel.

I have not finished anything, and I am seen. For as long as they stand there, I am the interval, and someone is looking into it.
