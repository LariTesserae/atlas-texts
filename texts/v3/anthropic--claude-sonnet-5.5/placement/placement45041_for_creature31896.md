I come to myself in the frost, which is how I usually come to myself.

My eyelashes are white. When I lift my hand to break the ice off them I see the back of it, gone gray-gold in the pale morning, and for a moment I am my own grandmother. Then the breath comes out of me and hangs, and I am only what I am: someone who stands on things that are not sure they want to be stood on.

I don't have a good word for what I do. The nearest is *reknotter*. Others might say stone-mover. In the spring I set a cairn on the eastern ridge, split limestone bound with braided fiber, and it was true then. The ridge was firm and the grazers had been crossing it since before my mother's mother. Now, with the light coming up white and shadowless, I stand at the edge of a crack the width of my body. Four feet of the ridge have sagged away, and my cairn leans over the lip, holding up its little hand to nothing.

I am not sad about it. I take it apart stone by stone, unpick the braid, and coil the fiber over my shoulder. The stones are cold enough to burn. Cairns are not monuments. I understood that late, after years of feeling wounded each time the ground undid me. A cairn is a sentence. *Here, for now, it is safe.* You do not mourn a sentence for ending. You say the next one.

---

I test the mat with my pole, which is taller than I am and shod with bone. The moss takes the point and gives a little, then a little more, and I feel through the hollow stalk into my palm what the ground is thinking. Here it's thick and dark and well-fed. Here it's honeycombed. I see the small round doors of the beetle-galleries, dozens of them, like a dotted seam. *Too well loved,* I think, and step wide.

The mat-shoes spread me out. Each step sinks a hand's depth and the ground exhales around my ankle, slow and wet and warm from below, so it's like being breathed on by something enormous that has not yet noticed me. I am on the long line. The others are behind me, tethered: two on the middle length, and the child, Ketti, on the short line, close enough to touch. The long line is for the old ones. It is an honor. It is also arithmetic, and no one pretends otherwise. If the ground takes the one at the far end, there's rope enough that it doesn't take everyone. I have been at the far end for eleven years. I like the quiet out here. I like being the first to hear the ground.

---

The light comes down and up at once, and my slit-visor cuts it to a bright thread. Even so my eyes water and the tears go cold on my face. All around me the pale moss throws the sky back at the sky. There is no shadow anywhere, not under my feet, not under the pole. It is like walking inside a lantern.

Far above, the canopy hangs its green-gold sheet from cliff to cliff. It has been a good year for it, and that is the worry. I look for the belly, the place where the weight has begun to gather in a sag, and I find one to the north, a long slow pouch of leaf-web, dark with held water. The drip beneath it has changed. From here I can't hear it, but I know its voice, and I know that a ticking has become a run.

I kneel and press my ear to the cairn-stone I have not yet dismantled, the last one, and listen.

Far off, someone is telling a joke.

It's Oru, I think, from the south camp. You can tell a person by how they knock. Oru has a rest before the third beat, like someone who thinks the punchline is worth waiting for. The joke is about a grazer and a sinkhole and a very rich man, and it began yesterday afternoon. It is a long joke. It takes a day to tell because each part has to travel through the root-felt and be received and answered, and the answer is part of the joke. I knock back the small laugh, two quick and one soft. It goes out through the ground into the miles of dark and warm rot and moss, and after a while, a long while, Oru will know I've heard.

This is the thing people who pass through never understand. We are not silent. We only speak with the ground.

---

The bird lifts at midmorning.

I see it before I understand what I see: a white shape rising off the far ridge on those broad wings, unhurried, like something remembering. It does not sing. It never sings. It circles once, wide, over the northern belly, and then again, tighter.

Everyone on the line stops. No one has to say anything. The bird is a colleague, and it has filed its report.

I strike the pole against the firm stone: *away.* The knock goes out. I hear it come back from the south, from Oru, who has dropped the joke in the middle of the punchline, and from somewhere east, and from somewhere I can't place. Then Ketti's hand is in the back of my wrap and we're moving, not running, we never run, perpendicular to the drift of the shadow that hasn't come yet, because the shadow always comes in the direction you'd like to go.

Then the tearing.

It begins so softly you could mistake it for cloth being drawn slowly out of a drawer. Then it's the whole sky, a long ripping like the world being unbuttoned, and the shadow arrives, the only shadow of the day, sliding onto the moss ahead of us like a dark tide. I don't look up. You learn not to. I put the pole down and down and down, feeling for firm ground, and behind me I hear the line of people breathing together, and I count.

It takes a full minute to land. That's what nobody believes. A minute is a very long time to be under something falling.

The *whump* comes up through my feet and my knees and my teeth. The mat lifts under me like a big animal turning over in its sleep, and the wave rolls from the north, a visible ripple in the moss, and I ride it, bent-kneed, the way we all stand. It passes. The mat settles with a long wet sigh.

And then there is the quiet, and the drip, and the fine sifting of seed-fluff coming down out of the bright air like snow.

---

We turn around.

This is the part outsiders cannot hold in their minds, and I have stopped trying to explain it. We turn around and walk toward the thing that nearly killed us, with baskets, because it is the richest ground in the basin for a week. The heap lies across the moss in a green fold the size of a village, already steaming a little, already sprouting. Stunned beetles lie in the leaves like spilled tallow candles. The grubs are fat. There is fruit split open, and nest-down, and long clean lengths of vine that will make good cordage. I put my hands into the wreck of it and they come out wet and smelling of crushed green, and I laugh, because I can't help it. Ketti is laughing too, with her mask of soot creased around her eyes.

We work fast. We will be gone by the time the sprouts are a hand high.

Near the heap's edge I find a gray grazer, dead under a spar of vine. Its narrow eyes are shut. I press a pinch of its fur into the moss with my thumb, the way it's done, and say the small word. Then I take what I need from it, and I do not feel that I am doing wrong.

---

By dusk we have crossed to the ridge where my mother is.

She is in the great vine at the end of the eastern path. It is a thick living trunk, bark like braided cable, climbing up out of the mat toward the sky she is going to see. There's a swelling in the bark at about the height of my shoulder, where the fiber and fern have been taken in. Nine years now. There is nothing to see anymore, only the place where the vine has thought about her and decided to keep her.

I set my palm on it. It's warm. I don't know if that's true or if I need it to be, and after this many winters I've stopped asking which.

*Up,* I say to her. *Slowly. It's very bright.*

She'll be down again when the canopy comes. Not today. Not this one. But some year, when the weight is too great, she will come down out of the light with the leaf and the seed and the beetles, and be gathered into the mat, and the mat will rise a little for her. And I will be lying in the vine by then, or in the heap, or in the dark rings under the floor, where the white fungus glows for no one. The country fills itself back toward the sky it lost one collapse at a time, and we are a part of what it fills itself with. I have never found that grim. It is just what things do.

---

The fog comes down the walls at last, in banks, and the light thins to the color of milk and then to the color of pearl. The huts begin to show one by one, low and orange, out on their stilts. There are never more than a few in sight. Ketti has the fire lit, and the hot stones hiss in the black water, and the moss tea goes round, bitter and thin, and someone hums.

I sit at the door with my bowl. The door faces the wall so the wind won't flood it. The pole lies across my knees. The bowl I have turned over, as always, when I'm not using it, to keep off the drizzle.

Then I kneel, and put my ear against the ground.

It is a long way off, and slow, and I nearly miss it. A knock with a rest before the third beat. Oru has found the end of the joke. I don't know how many hours he has been carrying it, through the fall, through the fog, through all the ground that was moved and unmoved between us. He gives it to me a beat at a time, and I lie there in the cold with my white lashes and my sore eyes and laugh until my breath hangs in the dark, because it was worth the wait.

I knock back. Two quick, one soft.

Somewhere far under me, in the warm dark, in the rings of old moss and older ghosts, the answer begins its journey. All around me the basin is talking in its sleep, and I am one of the small fires in it, listening.
