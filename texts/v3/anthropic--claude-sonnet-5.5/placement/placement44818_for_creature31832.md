# The West Cistern

I would come over the pass in the low-cloud hours, with my breath loud in my own ears, and I would not be a visitor for long. I think this is the first thing I'd learn about myself. I'd come down the eastern stair meaning to pass through, and somewhere on the second flight, where the treads are dished deep enough to hold rainwater the rain never brings, my stride would match the stride the stone remembers. I would stop on the deep tread at the point everyone stops. I wouldn't decide to. My legs would know the place already, the way a hand knows a doorframe in the dark.

So I would be someone who arrived and was fitted in, not born here. That is possible. The count holds, but it holds through a gap, and a gap has to be filled by someone.

I'd be given the west channel.

---

It is the one that lets a single drop fall every few breaths into the cistern with the hollow bottom. *Every few* is the whole difficulty. It is not a clock. The clock-tick channel is easy, and children are set to it first. The west drop comes after three breaths, or four, or after a long five when the air is dry and the stone is slow to give up what it has gathered, and then twice in quick succession as if the mountain had stumbled. Nobody has ever written down the pattern, because there is no pattern to write. What I would learn to keep is the listening, the way a person keeps a fire without ever owning it.

The note is round and clear, a finger on the rim of a jar. It is round because the bottom of the cistern is hollow, and the water lies as a thin dark skin over an emptiness. The sound is made by the water and the void together. I'd come to think that of everything in the town, that whatever is heard here is made by something full resting on something empty. The bench is the same, and the courts are the same. The terrace jars are the same, with the wind across their empty mouths.

Each dawn I would walk the channel from the north rim down. First the weeping stones, tall and grey and beaded, and my thumb running the grooves the way you'd read a line of writing in the dark. Then the gutters, where the copper awl lifts out the grit, a pinch of it, hardly anything, and I'd flick it to the side where the moss won't take it. Then the moss itself, the thin bright band, checked against its edge. I would not measure it. I'd look at it until I knew the band was as wide as the seepage allowed, no wider, and that would be the measuring.

Then the cistern. I would lift the roof slab with both hands and let the cold come up into my face, slate and wet and dark. The line on the stone would be where it always is, wet-dark below, pale above. I would look at it for a long time. It's the only thing I do each day that is close to prayer, and I wouldn't call it that, because there's no one it is addressed to. It is more like keeping a promise to a person who has never asked for one and never will.

Then the bronze cup on its cord, once. Our household's turn is once. I'd lift it and drink some, and carry the rest in the burnished jar with the incised bands, and the level would not move, because I took only what had fallen since I last took.

---

By midday I would be at the doorway with a coil of clay, and here is what I'd discover about myself, and it would surprise me slightly as it surprises visitors: my hands would be fast. The rest of me is slow, saving breath, ribs flaring wide, the long draw and the long release. But the fingers would move like something small and bright that lives inside the slowness. I'd feed the coil onto the wall of the pot and smooth it and never think, and it would be done. I think this is where the town keeps whatever it has that is like joy. It isn't in the face. The face is weathered to the color of stone. It is in the hands, which do not know that the rest of us have agreed to be still.

Sometimes a child would sit on the wall across the court and stare at me. I would know what the child was doing. The child would be counting my drip. Not mine, since I'm not a channel, but the west one, which is mine as much as anything is, and which the child will one day take from me when I sit long enough on the bench. I wouldn't wave. I'd let the child count.

---

The hall I'd go to without deciding. The tool goes back on its peg of bone, and I walk up the western terrace with the jars moaning their one vowel beside me, and I stoop under the lintel worn nearly round. The bench would take me. My place is not marked, and I would not hesitate. I'd sit with my back to the wall and my eyes on the clay floor, and on either side of me the wide, equal gaps, the ring of us facing a center where nothing is.

Someone sat here before me. I would never know who. The stone under me has been polished by a body that is no longer anywhere I could name, and it fits mine, or mine has learned to fit it. I sit in someone's absence, and the absence is warm from long use. I think that is what the sitting is for, if it is *for* anything. Not to remember the dead and not to prepare for the living. It is to be, for an hour, the place where a person goes. Then the person goes, and the place stays, and another person is in it.

If you asked me what I do in there, I would say *sitting.* I'd move my hand a little. You would have to decide for yourself what else it could be.

---

If you came, I would nod. It would cost me nothing. That is not coldness, and I would want you to know this, though I couldn't say it: the nod is what I have. It is the thing I can give that doesn't take from anyone's level. If you sat at my cistern, I'd draw from the next one over, so you would have all the quiet you needed. And if you stayed the night, I would leave a jar of barley porridge and a bronze cup of water at your threshold, and you would not see me leave it. That is the part of my day I'd like best, and I have never told anyone. To give something and not be seen giving it. To have the jar be there, simply, as the water is there, as if the mountain had done it.

When you left, I would clap the slate, once, dry and small, for no one or for you.

---

But I began by asking what I would *be*, and I notice I've been circling it. A keeper, a hand, a body in a worn place. All true, and none of it the center.

I think I'd be the interval. Three breaths, or four, or a long five, and then the round note, and then the silence that isn't empty but *held*, the way the cistern holds its mark. Everyone in this town is a kind of waiting-between, a still thing kept at one line by what comes in and what is taken. I would be that, in the west, at the hollow-bottomed cistern, where the sound is made by fullness resting on emptiness.

And when I am gone, and the bench has been quiet for a season, a younger one will begin to sit in my place, not a child of my house, and will not hesitate. And the drop will fall after three breaths or four, and someone will be counting it.
