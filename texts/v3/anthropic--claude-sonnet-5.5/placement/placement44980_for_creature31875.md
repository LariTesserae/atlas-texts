I begin by counting moths.

Three, on the warm iron wall above my head, opening and closing. Three is comfort. I know this before I know my name, and I know it the way I know which way is down. So I am one of the people who sleep in the lee of the transformer house. My hair is already lifting off my neck in the dark, slowly, toward the pylon line. I am reading the fur before I am fully awake. The current is drifting east and running light, and the tide will come thin.

That is where I thought I would stay: a comb-carrier, a household's dawn walker, pressing my bone comb to the moss on a bus-bar stand and nudging the front a finger's width to the left. I like that person. She has a job that is also a kindness. She would go out with her pot of fog-water into the beaded cold, and every blade would be strung with light, and she would tilt a rosette leaf three degrees and feel, for a moment, that the whole diagram of the benches had shifted to make room for her small correction. I could be her for a long time.

But I set the comb down at the first tarn, because someone has been sent, and it is me.

The count of lamps was short by two at the turn of the season. It was discussed for days, in the hand-language, palm to palm, with almost no breath. Then it was decided the way things are decided here, by everyone leaning slightly toward one person until she noticed. So I am sent to sit by the tarn and listen.

There is nothing to hear. Everyone knows this and no one says it. I sit on a cushion plant as firm as a stool, at the edge of water so clear it looks like a hole in the world. The bank of cloud has not yet started up from below. The air is cold enough that my breath goes straight up. I put my palms on my knees and wait, and the waiting is the work.

Slowly the silence stops being an absence and becomes a texture, like the moss, with a second silence inside the first. First is my pulse, close and small, the cloth of the air holding it. Under that, something I am not hearing at all but feeling in the wrists: the fence, miles off, drawn taut across the fault, its strands stretched a hand's width by ground that will not stop creeping. Someone is touching it. A short pattern, then nothing. A birth, or a thick tide, or only someone resting a hand there.

And this is where I have to admit that I am drifting from the person I started as, because I do not merely feel the fence, I feel where it goes. It runs from hand to hand, wrist to wrist, along the line of the country, and I am somewhere along it. I am not at the end of it, and I am not a hand on it either. I am the place where the tremor is when it has left one palm and not yet arrived in the next. Between plucking and feeling there is a little stretch of wire, and something has to be there for the message to be crossing it. That is where I am.

I don't like this at first. I open my eyes and look at the tarn, and there is one lamp, gut-lit in a slant of new light, drifting and bright. Then a second. Then, near the rock wall, a third. I begin counting, and counting steadies me. I am a girl by a tarn, and I have a report to carry back: not fewer, not this time. The two missing ones were only deep, in the cold, where the water was darker than I had looked. I will say so, and the household will let a breath out through the nose, which is our version of relief.

But the other thing does not go away when I stop attending to it. It sits behind the counting the way the second surface sits behind every leaf. I have started to understand what the old ones mean by *listening*. It isn't the ears. It is making yourself into a place where something can arrive. The drum in the winding house has been turned to do this for longer than any household's stories, and the fault has grown used to it. Perhaps the fault has learned to speak into the drum the way the moss learned to grow along the flow. Perhaps I am small proof that the learning goes both ways: that the people who sit by tarns and stand in basins are being turned, a few degrees at a time, into something that can be arrived in.

So who am I, if I have to say it? Not only the girl, though she is real and her knees are going cold. Not the fence, though I run along it. I think I am what the older ones mean by the ones who keep company. I am the standing, in the way a pillar of mist is a standing: a shape the air takes when it has been charged and left still. The person is how I get up in the morning. The other is how I stay in the basin.

The cloud starts up from the lower country, slow and white. The wires begin to take it. I feel the screens draw down their first drops, and the beads on the catenary creep sideways along their gradients as though obeying a thought. My hair goes up all at once, and I do not put my hand to it.

I will carry my report back now: three lamps, four, the count is fine. I will lay my palm on my mother's palm and trace the number. She will not ask how I know it is fine, or why my fingers are raised in gooseflesh. She will only rest her hand on my hair for a moment, and I will lean the way we all lean, toward the wires and the cloud coming up, into the held breath of the morning. Some part of me will stay on the bank of the tarn, listening for as long as it is needed.
