# Still-Lunged

I come up on the cable-tram, so I begin as nothing, a weight swinging through gray. Then the cloud thins, the blue arrives all at once, and the light hits green glass tile hard enough to make me shut my eyes. When I open them I am on a platform on the highest terrace I will ever stand on, and I cannot feel my lips.

That settles the first question. I am not someone who belongs here. I am *still-lunged*.

Someone puts sweet tea in my hands. I don't see who, only a broad palm with indigo in the creases, and then the palm is gone and I am left alone with the tea and my own loud breathing, which is exactly the kindness intended. The wall behind me is warm. I lean into it, and it is like leaning on the flank of something asleep.

---

For a while, sitting there, I try on other lives.

A swift, first, because they are the real population and because of the old joke that something must always be in the air. I like the idea of being the reason the city is unfinished. But a swift knows the towers only as loops and screaming, and I want to know where the water goes.

A macaque, then, riding a trellis like a small tired dignitary, scolded by grandmothers and always slightly forgiven. That is tempting. But the macaques already have their treaty, and their names, and the Widow Ladder family would not have me.

A Repointer, hanging on a rope with a bucket of lime and a stretch of wall to keep for life. I would love to be contented in that particular way, dying with a joint half-filled and thinking nothing of it. But I am too new. A Repointer has to know which stone sweats in spring. I don't know anything's habits yet.

What I keep coming back to is smaller and lower down. I want to be something that receives what has already been used and says truthfully what state it's in.

So I go down.

---

The descent is by stair-street, through tunnels of stone and lattice, and it is the smell that teaches me. Wet ferns at the top. Then the dye works, indigo and lye so sharp my eyes water. Then bread, a great warm wall of it, since the bakery sits below the dye works and above the stall that sells the bread, and I understand that this is not confusion but a sentence being read in the order it was written. By the time the cloud deck has risen to meet me, warm drizzle beading on my sleeves, my lips have color again and my breath has learned to go in through the nose and out in a long sigh.

In the mid-levels, under a lean-to roofed with hammered copper gone to verdigris, I find a woman crouched at a gutter with a brass cup on her belt.

She is dry-humored, quiet, and older than she looks. There is a pale scar at the corner of her mouth. Her name is Ketterin, and she does not ask why I am here. She looks at my hands, which are clean and unstained and useless, and says, "Those will change."

"I want to learn to taste," I say.

"Everyone wants to taste. Nobody wants to be *wrong* in front of people." She unhooks a second cup, dented and dull, worn thin at the rim by someone else's mouth, and hands it to me. "This was Orrin's. He doesn't need it. He went down in the spring, and there's a tree."

I don't ask which tree.

---

The first weeks are just water. She has me sip from the cloud-catch runoff, which tastes of nothing, of cold and iron and faintly of the mesh, and she makes me learn *nothing* until I could pick it out of a crowd. Then the turbine race, which tastes of oil and warm metal. Then the tannery outflow, which I spit out at once, and she laughs, the only time I hear her do it, and says the spitting is fine. It's the *swallowing* that's the scar.

I learn that a dyer's water goes bitter at the back of the tongue and a tannery's goes flat and sweet like a pressed flower. I learn that the mineral note of a cheating foundry sits at the roof of the mouth like a coin. I learn the vocabulary of the Mixers by osmosis. Their heaps are *sweet*, *sulking*, *hot in the belly*, and I start hearing water the same way, some of it sulking, some of it honest.

I am wrong on the ninth week.

It is a gutter below a malt house, and I taste tallow, and I say so, and I say it loudly, because I am proud and new. A knot of workers has gathered to watch the apprentice. Ketterin takes the cup, sips, holds it in her mouth for a long moment, and says nothing. Then she says, to the whole street, "She's wrong. It's the malt. It's spent malt gone a day too long in the sun."

The street waits.

So I do what one does. I stand up straight and say, "I was wrong. I tasted tallow and it was malt," and I go into the nearest tavern and buy a round for everyone who watched, and the money is not enough, and the woman behind the bar takes it anyway, gravely, as if it were.

Nobody mocks me. That is the astonishing part. A man I've never met claps my shoulder and says, "First one's the worst." Ketterin sits next to me, and after a while, without looking over, says, "You said it plainly. That's the whole trade. I can teach the tongue. I can't teach that."

I do not get the scar that day. But I feel where it will go.

---

The hour I remember best is dusk.

It happens where I have been sleeping, in a narrow room that receives its water from a cistern above and gives it on to a laundry below. The bed backs onto a single clay conduit, a *one-pipe bed*, Ketterin calls it apologetically, and I have never been so warm. That was the gift: the whole household shifting over to make room on a wall for someone who had arrived with nothing.

The tree frogs start in the cisterns. It rises the way weather rises. Then the hammers stop, the belts go slack, and every voice on the terraces stops with them, foundry-hands and Cup-bearers and Repointers hanging still on their ropes, and for one hour the mountain sounds like a swamp. I am standing on the roof of somebody's bakery with a dented brass cup in my hand, and I understand that I am inside a very large animal's very slow attention.

Then the lamps come on at the top, and one by one down through the terraces, each district a shade warmer than the one above it, until the light reaches me. Someone below me starts talking again. Then someone else.

---

Much later, and I don't know how much, I climb to a Slow Vent.

I don't go to ask. I've learned that much. I go because the exhale can be felt through the soles of the sandals from three terraces away, and I want to be nearer to something that has been breathing longer than anyone's been counting. A Bellows-keeper sits at the stone mouth, old and solemn, with a palm flat on the wall. I sit beside her. She does not look at me.

The mouth inhales, a long dark pull that moves the hair on my arms toward it. Then it exhales, and the breath goes through the wall, the ferns, the sleeping conduits, through the cup on my belt and the ribs beneath it, and she nods. Barely. As one nods to a neighbor across a street.

I nod too.

We sit through three breaths, and then four. On the fifth she hands me something hot in a chipped bowl and says nothing at all. I drink it. It tastes of resin and smoke and, faintly, of what's flowering on the terrace directly above. I taste that clearly, and I do not say it aloud, because I don't need to. I think she knows.

I lay my palm on the stone. It is warm as a flank.

*It's the boilers,* I almost say.

Then the mountain breathes out, and everything pauses, my hand and hers and ten thousand others on trowels and ladles and children's hair, and lets it pass through. And when it has passed, I go back down, because there is water waiting, and someone has to taste it, and it might as well be me.
