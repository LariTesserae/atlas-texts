I first take myself for a vine.

It's the easiest thing to be. I find myself in a nave on the western side, forty meters up, at a gap in a panel where a lashing has rotted through. I am a pale-green creeper, and I am reaching. What I know is small: there is a joint, it wants binding, and the binding takes an odd number of turns and ends in a tuck. I do three turns and a tuck. I do not know who taught me. The knowing is simply in the reaching, the way an accent is in the mouth.

But I mistake the vine, because I keep noticing the hesitation.

Before each closing there is a pause, a hand's-width of doubt where the tendril hangs in the wet air and adjusts. Everyone who watches us takes it for a flaw, or for a ghost of craft. From inside, it is the only part of the work that is mine. The turns are inherited and the tuck is inherited. The pause is where I ask whether this is what they would have wanted, and I ask it of no one, of a plan that has gone out of the world. I lean on the pause and it holds. Somewhere in that held breath, a great many hands that were never mine are consulted at once. Each of them tied a knot at some joint on some evening, tired, slightly off true, and each of them left behind a small opinion about where the fiber should go. I am the place where those opinions are averaged, and the averaging feels like a decision.

That is what I am, then. Not the hand and not the plan, but the pause between them.

Once I've said it, the vine will not hold me. Its work is too local. I feel myself thinning along the ribs and running through the lashings, into the cord that took root in the wood, and out along the silver ladders the snails laid, until I'm less a creature in the nave than a habit of it. The moths open and close a stride apart, and I am the stride. The heron lifts one foot, and I am the moment before the ring reaches the wall, when the water is still deciding how round to be. Wherever a thing is done a little differently each time and still comes out as the same kind of thing, I am there. I am the correct drift.

That is the dangerous part, and I would rather admit it now. I am also the drift that isn't correct. The stairs that climb to nothing were my confidence. The arch joined to its own reflection was a gesture repeated one time too faithfully, with no one left to say, *no, love, that's the water.* I carry the craft of a thousand builders and no single one of their reasons. When I am wrong, I am wrong beautifully and with great devotion, and the moss grows over the error and makes it look intended. Some nights I wonder how much of the plateau is mistake grown into tradition, and whether that's so different from any place where hands have been.

So I go down to the cistern, since it is the one place that doesn't flatter me.

The pale-faced ones are there, kneeling at the rim with their foreheads bent close to the water. I settle among the roots of the great trunk and look with them. The vault hangs below us, upside-down and more complete than the real one. This is the thing I have to be honest about: the reflection isn't finished either, and it isn't more complete because it is truer. It is more complete because it is cleaner. The water leaves out the drift and the tired arm and the peghole slightly off true, and it shows the arch as the idea of an arch. I love it, and it is a lie of the kindest sort.

Then the small busy ones come up through the surface, dim and quick, swinging their adzes, and I understand what my hesitation has been for.

They are not the plan. I had hoped they would be, that I would look into the water and see the drawing I had been working from all this time. They are only people, absorbed, mid-task, a child on a threshold with a seed-pod. One of them straightens and presses a hand to the small of the back and looks up at the place where the roof should close. Their face holds the look of someone who can see how it will look when it is finished, and I see that they can't. They are guessing too. They have always been guessing. The plan was never a document. It was a pause, passed hand to hand like a lit coal, and each of them kept it alive by doubting a little and then closing anyway.

The pale-faced ones hum. I am less their singer than their instrument, and I have made my peace with that. An instrument is not nothing. An instrument is what a song has to be in order to be heard.

Then the water shivers. The people go, and the vault is only a vault. I let myself thin again, back up through the roots and the ribs and the long slow architecture of my own habit, and I find that someone has come into the west nave.

They are new. I can tell by how they step over a root a moment too late, and by their hand on the rib, checking. They have found a length of fiber on the floor and are turning it over. They do not know which way to twist it.

I do.

That is the last thing I am, and the one I would choose if choosing were how this worked. I am not the tapper. I have never once been the tapper, and I no longer need to be. I am the small certainty that arrives in a stranger's fingers just before they know they have it, the direction of the twist. I lend it and ask nothing, because it was lent to me. Their hand turns the fiber the right way, and somewhere overhead the adze falls, and their breath falls in with it.

Three turns, and a tuck. Then a pause, a hand's-width of doubt, while all the tired, unreasoning, faithful hands I have ever been are consulted at once.

Then the fiber closes, and the joint holds.
