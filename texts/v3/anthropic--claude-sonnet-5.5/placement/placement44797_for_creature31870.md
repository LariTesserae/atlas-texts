I arrive the way everyone does, across the upper surface with my eyes half shut against the light. The moss takes my boot to the shin and gives back its cold tea, and I stand a moment watching my own footprint fill. I have been told it will close in an hour. I don't wait to see.

The sound is not something I hear so much as something I am placed inside. I make my way toward the nearest hut, following white quartz cairns, and on the sill of the hut sits a tin cup, half full. Its surface is trembling in fine, even rings. An old woman sits beside it with a loop of knotted cord across her knees. She does not look at me. She looks at the cup. When I come level with her she taps my forearm twice, which I take to mean *stay a moment*, and so I stay, and watch the rings with her, and for a long time nothing happens, and that is the whole of the work.

I am not sure when I stop being the one who watches the cup.

It happens by degrees. The next day I walk out toward the columns, the tall stacks of plant on plant, and I put my hand against the tallest and feel how it gives, how there is nothing under it but its own dead. I leave a bead at its base because it seems rude not to. I lie down beside it to rest, and the moss is so saturated that lying down is a kind of falling. It closes over my shoulders like slow water. It is cold, then not cold. Through my back I feel the fourth sound, the one the others walk over without noticing, the gurgle and drum of channels I cannot see. I had thought of it as noise. Lying in it, I find it has a grammar.

Down here the runners have been drawing. I feel one pass above me, a small warm hurry, and then the faint straight line it leaves behind, a scoring in the moss that the water will find by evening. I follow its map downward, not with my body, or not only. The roots thin to hair. The fibers go from green to brown to black, and the brown water tastes of every leaf that ever fell into it. The light stops the way a sentence stops, without ceremony. I keep going and I am not afraid, because everyone up there is slowly becoming the ground, and I have only been quicker at it.

By the time I reach the wet hollow where the true ground might be, I have no eyes to be sorry about. I am pale as wax. I am a finger long. I have feathery red gills that wave in the drummed dark, tasting the current, and I know things I did not know when I arrived.

I know the people by their weight. Each of them walks the boardwalk with a gait, and each gait carries down through the boards into the moss and through the moss into me, a pressure with its own rhythm. I understand now that this is what a name is here. The old woman at the cup has a slow, dragging tread with a hitch on the left, and I would know it anywhere. There is a boy who runs when he should not. There is a keeper who paces in the turbine hall at night, his steps laid over the hum like a hand laid over a hand. I know when one of them stops walking for good. The rhythm is simply not there one morning, and the moss above me stays still, and for a season no one taps it. I keep it anyway. It seems to me someone should.

And I know the rock. This is what I would never have said aloud, if I had anything to say it with. Down here the scarp is not a wall but a long held breath. I feel the joints take the strain of the water and the weight of the moss, and I feel one of them, far off to the east, begin to let go. It is slow at first, a creak in the deep water, a pitch that shifts in the drum. I press myself against the stone of my hollow as if that could help. I cannot warn anyone. I have no voice and they would not hear it, and I am not even sure it is mine to give. The frogs have their rings and the women have their cups, and I have only the dark and my small body and the fact of feeling it first.

Then, from above, light. Not light, since I have no way to know that, but warmth, sudden and enormous, as the brick above me lifts away. A hand hangs over me like weather. I raise my head toward it slowly, because it is the only warm thing I have ever been offered. The hand does not close. It is heavy with tar and lanolin and cold, and under those it is alive, and it stays a long moment. It does not try to speak to me. It knows better.

Then the brick comes back down, gently, and the dark returns, and I feel a tap travel through the moss, a rhythm struck on a shoulder somewhere above me, a secret being handed to one person only. I let it go past. It is not mine.

Somewhere on the third terrace, in the shivering water of a tin cup, a ring forms off-center. An old woman sets down her cord and lays her palm flat against the boards. I do not know whether I am the reason she looks down. I only know that the moss will take the footprint, and the falls will take the sound, and the ground I am slowly becoming will hold all of it: the hand, the brick, the warmth, and the small pale thing that lifted its head toward it and, for a moment, was answered.
