# The Joint

I am pressed. That is the first fact, and for a long time it is the only one.

Rock on the left, rock on the right, and a warmth ahead of me that is not the rock's. Something soft lies against my back, and something soft lies under my belly. I can't tell where I end. The whole crack trembles, and at first I take the trembling for myself.

Then I learn it isn't mine. It comes in from the stone, from the stone above me that the old ones work with their ridged backs, and it arrives in waves. A knot of them start a phrase. The knot beside them takes it up half a beat late. The lag travels down the joint like a thought being passed hand to hand, and it reaches me, goes through me, and moves on. I don't sing. I have no plate yet, only a skin like wet ash. What I do is hold still and let the pitch pass through until my body remembers its shape.

I am the young. I am blind, grey, and deep in the wall, and my whole work is to be sung into.

---

There is food, because there is always food. Above me the wrens shift in their pipes, and what they let fall comes down through the cracks in a slow, sweetish rain. I eat it. I eat their leavings and the leavings of the moths and the pale dust of the dead. It is all one meal, a long one, and it is the mountain feeding itself through me.

Sometimes something enormous walks across the top of my world.

I can't see it, but the columns tell me. A footfall comes down through the stone, a single narrow point, placed with terrible care. Then nothing, then another. What I have learned about the stilt is that it only moves when the grinding is loudest. It steps inside the roar. Its weight arrives when my whole wall is shaking too hard to feel one more small thing, and I feel it anyway, because I am small and pressed and have nothing to do but feel. Then the roar dips, and the footsteps stop. In the dip I can feel the stilt listening. It is a stillness with an edge, a quiet leaning down toward the wall.

Above me, four hundred of the old ones go on singing at exactly the volume that will keep me hidden. I don't think they do it for me. I think they do it because it is what a wall does. But I have been hidden by it all the same, and I hold that fact the way I hold everything else, without words, in the flesh of my back.

---

There are other things I know without eyes.

I know when the birds begin to glow. Their singing changes just before, and a kind of hush comes into it, as if they were leaning forward. Then a call goes down one pipe and comes out sweeter, and the next pipe answers, and the next. The waves go over me like weather, hours long. I have never seen the light, but I have felt it cross the mountain the way you feel a person cross a room behind you. Once, in the middle of a wave, one pipe stayed silent. The wave went around it and came back and did not complain. I think about that bird more than I think about most things.

I know when the water comes. It falls in the dark, a tin ping, a wet slap, a deep tock somewhere below me, and every drop lands on a different note. If I could count, I would count them. Instead I let them place themselves in me, one after another, until I hold a whole scatter of pitches in a shape that is neither rhythm nor song.

I know when the tall ones come down from the ridge. They come by day, and I feel them as a strange quiet in the joints of the far wall, a shuffle of soft feet on stone, the tap of a hook, the dragging of a rope. Something reaches into a crack somewhere near me. The old ones around me do not stop singing, and neither can I. I feel the hook draw a few of them out, and the gap they leave, and the wall closing over the gap. Nobody in the wall makes anything of it. The tall ones take what they take, and they leave the rest, and they scratch their days on the stone where I can feel them, a dry, patient rasp, one line for each day, as if they were adding their own small voice to the wall's.

---

Now the part I have been walking toward.

Once a day the dark gets deeper, and in the deepest of it everything stops.

I have felt it many times. The old ones stop first, or the wrens do, or all of them at once, and there is no first. The grinding breaks off like a rope cut. The pipes go empty, and the moths hang without a stir. The stilt freezes on its column with one foot lifted. For the length of a slow breath the entire Throat holds itself perfectly still.

And here is what I have understood, lying here in the joint with my unformed back to the roof: it is not silent for me.

I never sing. All my life, all of it, I have been the one who trembles and does not sound. So when the chorus stops, I am the only thing in the wall that goes on exactly as before. And in the space where the roar was, I feel what the roar was covering.

There is a note. It is far lower than the beetles' lowest, below the hum the wind makes in the columns, and it does not come from above. It comes up through the warm side of my joint, the side where the air is not cold. It is slow. It might not be a note at all but a very long pressure, rising, then a draught of warm breath that stirs the dust on my back and smells of sulfur and old stone.

Then it rises through me and past me, and out through the crack, and up.

I don't know who is breathing. The tall ones say it is the mountain. The tall ones say there are others down there who answer. I have never been down there, and I have never once wanted to go. But I know now that the silence is not an absence. It is a door held open for a moment, and I am the one creature in the Throat that has always been standing in the doorway.

---

There is one more thing, and it frightens me a little.

When the breath has passed, the sound comes back, all of it, in one swell. Beetles, birds, water, the whole mountain at once. And I have felt that swell begin many times, and I have never found the first note of it.

Someone starts it. Someone must. It cannot come from nowhere.

My plate is hardening. I can feel it at the base of my spine, a stiff ridge coming in like the edge of a thought. It will be some seasons yet. But one day I will have a back that can rub against this roof, and one day, in the deepest dark, in the held breath of the world, I will be pressed in a crack among my hundred thousand kin, and the warm draught will pass through me and go out, and I will feel the dark begin to lean.

I don't know if I will be the one who begins.

But I have started to think it doesn't matter, the way the wren's missed turn doesn't matter. The wave goes around. The wave comes back. And far above, where the sky-slot is going pale, someone I have never met is lying awake against the north wall, holding a breath, waiting for the same silence I am waiting for, and I understand that when the mountain lets go, we will both let go together.

I wait. The warmth is coming. Everything is about to stop.
