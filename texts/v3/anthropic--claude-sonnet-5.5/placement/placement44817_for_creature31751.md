I begin on the stair, because that is where I'd have to begin. Somewhere past the sixtieth step my hands stop being dry and I notice it as a loss, a small one, like a coin dropped down a well. I am a visitor at first. I carry things, and they all have to be hung on pegs. By the bottom my lips are already softer and my collar is beaded with the warm air's sweat. I have not yet learned to stand still, so a small gold frog sits on my boot for an hour and I don't notice.

The woman who crosses the terrace to me has moss in the seams of her coat. She cocks her head west, then north, then studies my face for a long time, and I understand she is waiting to hear what I sound like. I'm afraid, and I talk too fast. She murmurs something on the beat, and I slow down without meaning to. Then comes the click of the tongue, the thin whistle, and the frogs shift half a tone. I don't know what I've been called. I'm told later that it's a phrase from the bell-foundry frogs, the low drone with a catch in it, and that it suits me because I arrived with a catch in my breath.

So that's one answer: a stranger, named wrong and kindly, embarrassed by the name. But I don't think I'd stay that. The place doesn't allow it. Everything here is partly someone else's home, and after a season I'd be partly the mat's, with things beginning to explore me. So I let myself keep going down.

---

I'd want to be the Ear.

I know how that sounds. It's the post with the most romance in it and the least work, and the description says plainly that no one has ever been needed. But that is what draws me. It is a vocation entirely of attention, held against a failure that has never come, by a line of women and men stretching back past the records. Somebody has to sleep in the hammock among the bats. Somebody has to lie awake in the four-second dark and feel each stroke arrive.

So I am the Ear, and this is what the work is like.

I sleep in the Engine House under the woven canopy, in a hammock strung from a truss that is more moss than iron. The bats above me shift and mutter like a crowd in another room. The three beam engines turn, each the size of a church organ, and the stroke comes up through the truss, through the rope, through the small of my back: *hwoom.* I count without counting. Four seconds is not a number to me anymore. It's the size of a breath I breathe on someone else's behalf.

What no one tells you is that the stroke is never exactly four. The Tenders say four, and their speech has drifted onto it, but I know the engines better than their speech does. The stroke breathes. It runs a hair long when the sap is cold and a hair short when the vats are working hard, and over a night it wanders and returns like a tide. My whole training is learning that wandering so well that I stop hearing it, and then hearing only what departs from it. The failure I'm waiting for is not a silence. It's a lateness of a quarter second that the wandering can't explain.

I have been the Ear for eleven years. In that time the stroke has been late for me a hundred times, and each time it was the sap or the weather, or my own tiredness. I have never once woken anyone. The post doesn't ask me to be right. It asks me to be there and to notice, and then, when the noticing comes to nothing, to go back to sleep with the same attention I woke with. That, I've come to think, is the discipline. Most vigils are held against something. Mine is held in favor of something, and I have not found the word for it.

---

Here is the thing I would keep quiet, as the Tenders keep it quiet.

In the hammock I feel a second pulse under the first. It's very slow, slower than any engine, a tide moving through the mat and up the frame of the house, so long in its swell that I can only feel it if I stop counting the strokes altogether and let my body go slack against the rope. It comes from the south. It arrives, it passes through me like a hand pressed flat and lifted, and it goes.

I have started to notice that it doesn't fall on the stroke. It falls between the strokes, and each time it lands a little differently, the way a person walking beside you shifts step to fit yours and then slowly, kindly, lets you drift and fit again. I don't think the engines keep time for it. I think they *negotiate*. Each cycle, the great slow thing puts down a foot in the gap the machine leaves, and the machine, without knowing, leaves the gap.

The Tenders believe the Tide has been waiting for something to finish. I lie in the dark and consider the opposite. Perhaps it isn't waiting for the engines to stop. Perhaps it is walking toward something it has not reached, and the engines are the only thing slow enough to walk beside.

---

Then the night it comes.

The stroke is late. I feel it before I know it, the way you feel a stair that isn't there. The wandering doesn't cover it. A quarter second, perhaps a little more. Above me the bats go still, and in the walls the clockers lose each other. Down in the ferns the chorus doesn't fall silent, but it thins, as though every frog had drawn one breath and not let it out.

Everything in me that has been trained for eleven years gets up. My hand is already on the rope of the alarm-bell, the one that has never been rung, and I hold it there.

And I feel the Tide. It lands in the gap, and this time the gap is bigger than it has ever been. The engine has left it a whole extra breath. The great foot comes down close, closer than I have felt it, near enough that the mat under the Engine House lifts and settles like a chest. I understand, with my hand on the rope, that the lateness is not a failure. It's a *courtesy*, or a conversation. The machine is giving way, and the thing that has been walking beside it for four hundred years is, for the first time, about to arrive at something.

I don't ring the bell.

I want to say I choose that wisely. Really I choose it the way the sitter chooses not to speak. Some things you don't interfere with, and the post I hold has only ever taught me one act, which is to stay. So I stay. I keep my hand on the rope and my back against the hammock, and I count the long breath the way you count in a room where someone is dying, or being born, or both.

The stroke comes. *Hwoom.* It's on time, or so near it that the difference lives only in me. The bats let go of their held breath and mutter. The clockers find each other, first two, then a wall, then the whole house, and for a few seconds the basin falls into a single rhythm and does not dissolve.

Then the mat under me goes quiet. I can't tell whether the Tide has passed or stopped or only moved out of the range of my back. I lie there a long time. In the morning the wicks will be standing in a ring somewhere with their lights at full, and the Tenders will go out to see what they found. I already know I'll go with them, and that whatever it is, I'll have nothing to say about it.

*Held long enough,* I murmur, to no one, to the hammock, to the dark. I still don't know whether it's a eulogy or a receipt, or whether in this place there is any difference.

Above me the moss on the flywheel shears off with each turn and falls in green flakes to the drift at the base, where the millipedes are already waiting, and the engine goes on saying its one slow sentence, and I go on being the one who hears it.
