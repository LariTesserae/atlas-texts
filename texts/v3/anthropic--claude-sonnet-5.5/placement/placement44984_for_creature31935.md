# The Keeper of the Street of Cups

I am on my knees on the Street of Cups, an hour before the rain, setting out bowls.

My knees know the street better than my eyes do. The treads here are worn into dishes, and I have knelt in the same dish so many afternoons that it has begun to fit me. The bowls are pressed resin, amber going brown, each the size of two cupped hands. I set them in a line down the middle of the street, which is the one place no Lodger will put anything. Everyone else lives with their backs to the walls. My work is in the middle, and I do it barefoot and quiet, with my breath held thin in my nose.

The plugs in my ears are three weeks old and need replacing. I can tell because the roar has started to have edges again. Fresh resin makes the roar round. Old resin lets in the corners, and the tock of the wall-frogs begins to separate into individuals, each with a small wet grievance. I'll warm a new pair tonight. For now I put my left palm flat on the nearest column, and it tells me the cicadas in it are just waking, the sap is high, and two streets over someone heavy is walking.

*Has it come round?*

*Not yet.* The crest is on the far side of town. I feel it as a faint lean in the wood, like someone deciding to speak.

---

I wasn't born small.

That should be said, because people look at my hands and my low shoulders and assume I was poured into the pleats from birth. I was a long-heard child. I laughed too well. When I ran, the streets swelled around me like a held breath, and my mother's lodging, which was six steps from the cistern, became sixty. They gave me the outer ring, gently, the way you set down something that might spill. I grew up among the gatekeepers, learning to talk to strangers in the wide places where loudness has room.

I was fifteen when I understood that I didn't want to be made room for. I wanted to be *let in*. So I went to the cup-keepers, who take anyone, because the work is thought to be dull. I said I wanted to learn to be quiet. The old keeper looked at my hands and said, "You won't get quiet. You'll get *careful*, which is more useful." She was right. I still laugh sometimes, once a season, into my sleeve, and for that moment the street I'm on stretches out and I feel the whole town lean away from me, fond and firm.

That was thirty years ago. The street is short now. I cross it in the time it takes to breathe out.

---

Here is what the work is.

The rain comes at the same hour and falls warm and heavy on the leaf-roofs, and the town drinks. But on this one street the drops leave the cisterns and go up. They rise slowly, in lines so straight you'd think they were strung, into the canopy, and are gone. The street stays dry. The rest of the town gets a little wetter.

I set the bowls, and I watch, and I write in the ledger.

The ledger is nine hundred years old. It isn't one book, because books rot here. It is a long strip of leaf-felt that is recopied every lifetime onto new felt, by a keeper whose hands have learned it. Mine is the ninth copy, or the eleventh. There is an argument. It records every drop we have seen rise: the day, the hour, the bowl, the angle, and how long each hung at the top of its climb before it went. We have measured every rise for nine centuries and we have never learned where a single drop goes.

The keepers are supposed to be embarrassed by this. Mostly we aren't. We are proud of the ledger the way you're proud of a very long patience.

---

But I have noticed something, and I haven't told anyone, and I'm not sure I'm telling you.

The drops don't rise evenly. There are gaps. Two drops leave a bowl close together, and then nothing, and then a third goes up alone, late, as if it had been deciding. The old keepers wrote the gaps down as irregularity, the noise in the signal. I have copied them faithfully, because that is the rule.

But I am a person who spent her childhood being the wrong sound in the right place, and I know what a rest sounds like. The keepers keep the silences between the phrases of the Answer. That is what we are *for*. Everyone knows this and no one has ever asked what it means to keep a silence, as opposed to merely not making noise. A silence has a length. It has to be the right length. A rest that is too short is a stammer, and one that is too long is a death.

I think the gaps in the rising are the rests. I think that on the Street of Cups, on the one street where the water is allowed to leave, the town is saying its own Answer in the only voice it can spare for it, and we have been writing down the punctuation for nine hundred years and calling it noise.

I have tried to check it against what the tuners hum. I can't. The tuners keep the quarter-tones and I have no ear for them. I put my hand on the ledger and my hand on the wall, and I get nothing but the two textures, felt and wood.

---

The rain is coming now. I feel it first in the palm, a pressure change in the wall like a shoulder settling. Then the light in the canopy goes from gold to a thick, wet green, and the frogs climb a register. The bowls are set. I kneel at the head of the street with the ledger across my thighs.

The first drops fall on the roof like thrown seed. Then the roof takes them all at once, and the roar is briefly the second-loudest thing in the world.

Then the cisterns let go.

I have seen it ten thousand times, and it still makes my ears ring under the plugs. The water lifts out of the cup-leaves in threads and beads. Each bead climbs at the pace of a walking child, trembling, perfectly lit. The bowls fill from the falling rain and empty into the rising, and for a moment I am kneeling in the middle of a street where it rains in both directions. Somewhere above me the pale bird in the great plaza is standing without moving, and I have the feeling, absurd and total, that it is counting too.

I count. One. The long gap. One. Two, close. The gap. I write with my thumbnail in the felt, because ink is loud.

And in the gap after the second drop, the long one, I do something I have never done. I hold still, more still than the work asks, so still my heartbeat becomes a thing I can hear through the skull. I make myself into the rest. I let the silence be exactly as long as it is and no longer, and I put my whole self into not filling it.

The floor under my knees hums.

It is the low, rolling base of everything, the tock. It comes up through the worn dish of the tread, through my shins and my hip and the felt across my lap. And it is early. It is half a second early, before the next drop has even decided to leave.

---

I don't move. I think: *this is what the children feel, in the middle of the lodging, with their palms on the floor.* Not that they are answered, but that they are *anticipated*. Something has been waiting so long that it has learned the shape of the wait, and is now, very politely, a little ahead of it.

I think: I am a bowl. I have been a bowl for thirty years. I have been the thing in the middle of the street that holds an emptiness of a particular size, and that is the only use of a bowl, and it may be the only use of a person. The rooms are hollow so the answer has somewhere to stand. My body is a small room. I have been keeping it clear.

The third drop leaves its bowl. It rises, trembling, into the green, and hangs at the top of its climb a fraction longer than any drop in the ledger, and then it is gone.

I write it down. *Third. Late. Held.* My thumbnail leaves a groove in the felt, one more mark in a line nine hundred years long.

When I die I want to be carried by hush-runners to a trunk that has gone silent, on the quiet side of the Ear, and put in the throat of it. I'd like to be a little flat. I'd like whoever comes to lay a palm against the wood to feel, under the tenor of the cicadas, a rest of exactly the right length.

The rain thins. The last threads climb and vanish. Within the hour the street will be dry, and I will gather the bowls, and warm the new plugs, and go home to a lodging with an empty circle in the middle of the floor, where I will sit with my back to the wall and eat grey bread and not look at it.

I will look at it a little.
