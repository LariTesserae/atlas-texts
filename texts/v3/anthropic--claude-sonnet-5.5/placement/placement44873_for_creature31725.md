I first want to be a moth on the flywheel, the fat ones who ride the spokes, lift off at the bottom of the turn, and settle a spoke later. It is the obvious wish: to be carried, warm and fed, at the center of things. But I keep noticing that the moths are the room's audience, and I don't think I could stand being an audience. I would stiffen with the others in the half-second pause, and I would want, in that stillness, to have my hands on something.

So I am a beetle, a mason, born in the outer cracks.

---

I hatch far from the engine, on the north wall, where the cement is old and the mosaics have gone crude. The first thing I know is the drip-rain. The moths above me lift their edges and the water sheds down their backs, and by luck or design a few drops fall in the gap where I sit, and I am wet and warm and packed against my neighbors on both sides. I don't yet know that this is the whole of the world. I think it is the beginning of it.

The second thing I know is the green. In the crack beside me someone has pressed a few flakes of verdigris into the cement at angles that have no relation to each other. They look like a handful of seeds thrown at a wall. Even so, I find that I want to add to them, and I don't know why. My antennae go over the flakes for a long time. Then I turn one flake a little, to see if it sits better, and it does, slightly, and I understand that I am a mason.

The pieces here are small and scarce. Anything bright comes from farther in, dropped or dragged outward by older beetles who have died or lost interest. What I set into the wall is a copy of a copy of a copy. I know this the way you know a word has been passed hand to hand until it is mostly smoothness. I place my flakes in something near a ring, and it is a bad ring, open on one side. I feel a sting of dissatisfaction, and I think the sting is the reason any of this exists.

---

So I go inward. Nobody sends me. The older beetles stay in their duty, wedged and armored, and a boot could walk across them. But I am young, and the warmth increases as I walk. It climbs through my feet, and the light turns red-orange near the vent, and the sound gets closer, *tock… tock… tock,* until I can feel it in the stone as well as hear it.

The walk takes most of an hour. In that time the vent breathes and the lower room clouds. I crouch in the fog with my washer-less mandibles empty and the frogs all turned the same way, silent, facing the red light. I don't know what they are waiting for. I wait with them because the alternative is to be the only body in the room facing some other way. Then the fog climbs and beads, and the drops come, and the whole wall of moths lifts its edges in a slow relay. I hurry through the wet to the wheel.

Halfway there I find my first real piece of metal: a washer, wide as my body, half-buried in a crack and rimmed with bloom. I lift it and tilt it up against my head like a shield. It is heavier than anything I have carried, and I feel very serious.

---

Near the engine the mosaics are so good that I stop walking.

Here are rings that close. Here are spirals that turn one way, and keep turning it. And here, repeated in a row along the base of the housing, is the toothed shape. I put down the washer and read it with my antennae, a long slow reading, and I can't tell what it says. It says something like *this,* a form that is more than the sum of its flakes. It is the shape of the thing that goes *tock.* I know that as surely as I know my own legs, though I couldn't say how. It has soaked into all of us through the warmth, the rhythm, and the bacterial film on the pipes that we scrape and swallow. We are made partly of the engine's exhaust, and we build what we are made of.

I am not sure I am copying it. I think it may be simply coming out of me, the way a note comes out of a struck bell. That would make the outer mosaics less of a rumor than a fading ring, a note dying as it travels from the bell.

I set my washer into fresh cement beside the toothed row and press until it holds. It is a little crooked. I leave it crooked. It may be the first tooth on the wall to belong to someone else.

---

Then, as I turn to go back for another piece, I see the bronzed frog.

It sits among the valves under a leaking joint, and it is copper through and through. Water falls on its back and runs off in beads, and the beads look like a mistake, since nothing so still should be wet. At first I take it for a fitting. Then it blinks.

It blinks once, slowly, the way you would at the end of a long sentence. I feel, small and foolish on my six feet, that I have been read. The frog has looked at my washer, my crooked tooth, and the beetle who set them, and it has decided nothing, and it will go on deciding nothing for another hundred drip-rains. I understand something then about the bronzed ones. They are not waiting for the machine to make sense. They are becoming its parts so they can stop having to ask.

I do not want that yet. But I understand that I might, later.

---

The stall comes while I am carrying my third piece, a brass filing no bigger than a grain. The wheel reaches the top of its stroke and *stays.* The pause that should last half a second lasts one, then three, and the moths on the spokes do not soften. The rigidity does not pass. Something in me, in all of us, comes unlocked.

I am running before I decide to run. The wall behind me pours, beetles peeling from their stations in a glittering slow stream, and I am carried down it like a coin in a gutter. Above us the moths rise in their pale column. The frogs begin to pipe, thin and high and everywhere, and I think for a moment that the bronzed one is piping too, though its mouth does not move. I am not afraid, exactly. I am moving toward the heart, and that is what fear is for.

Then the wheel turns over. *Tock.*

Everything sits down. I find myself in a crack I have never seen, between a snail and a beetle I do not know, with a brass filing still in my mandibles. The whole room is a little rearranged, as though we had all briefly forgotten who we were and been reminded, and the reminding had shuffled us.

---

I stay here now. I don't know if this is my crack or only the one the stall dropped me in. The difference has begun to matter less. There is a bacterial film on the pipe within reach, and I graze it, and the pipe stays damp and sound around its seals, and I do not know that I am doing anything.

Sometimes I think of the leather belt running from the flywheel to a shaft that turns something under the rubble. I have never seen it, and I doubt any of us will. But when I press a flake into the cement, and it goes in at an angle no one taught me, and the pattern that was fading toward the walls holds, one more tooth, for one more hour, I feel the belt pull. Something down there turns a little, and I think it may be turning toward me.

Then the vent breathes, and the lower room clouds, and I turn to face the red light with the others, and wait.
