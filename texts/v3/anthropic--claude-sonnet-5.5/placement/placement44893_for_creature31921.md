I come to myself lying on the paving of the lowest lane, in the cold hours, which are only less hot. The stone under my shoulder blades is the temperature of a held hand. Someone steps over me and murmurs a name, and I lift two fingers before I have decided to. The name is mine, or near enough. It doesn't matter yet. What matters is that I lifted the fingers.

I thought I would be a gardener. It's the first thing I reach for, because the clippers are already at my hip, and I know the way you stand with a palm flat on a fern trunk, waiting. I stand at a corner where a tree fern goes up through a cooper's parlor and out through the tin. I put my hand on it and wait. I am good at the waiting. But when I look at my hands, they are not green to the wrist, and there are no white nicks. I have never cut anything here, and I am asking, but no one is answering me. I understand that I am not a gardener. I only like their way of standing.

So I walk, sideways, one shoulder leading, through a lane so narrow the baskets brush. The drizzle falls on the street ahead and not on mine. I watch it fall on the cobbler's step, a whole small rain kept to one doorway. Each roof drips at its own pitch, and I could tell you the street with my eyes shut. That is a start. I am someone who listens to roofs.

I climb by the ladders to the second terrace, where flame is allowed, and the light changes. Here the blade of day comes through and lays itself across the boardwalk, and the leaves in it are so green they hurt. I slow down in it as everyone does. I blink. I do not take the sun. That's for the twelve-year-olds, and I'm not sure I ever got my turn. It's one of the things I don't know about myself, and here it doesn't seem urgent.

Where the ropes cross, I find my work. There is a platform bolted to the wall where six pulley lines meet, a small cluttered room with no front, open to the air and the drop. A stool. A basket for what falls. Hooks with worn-smooth cord. I sit down as though I had sat there yesterday. A line hums, and without looking I know it carries coal, half a hundredweight, going down. Another creaks with something soft and light: mushrooms, or a child's toy. A third sings thinly, and it's a folded note, hardly anything, riding the rope like a leaf on a current.

That's what I am: the one at the crossing. I don't send or receive. I keep things from tangling. Children scramble up with notes and I take them, glance at the rope, and put them on the right line. I don't read them. It isn't a rule, just a manner. But I know what each weighs, and I know which are heavy for their size. A note that is one sheet folded twice can pull the line down like a stone if there is enough in it. I have learned not to say so.

A boy comes, maybe nine, damp, and puts a note in my hand with both of his, as though it might spill. "It's for the tallow terrace," he says, in the hand-talk, three taps and a sweep. I look at him, and I do not ask who sent it.

"Who's it from?"

He gives a small shrug that is also a lean toward the wall. "It's asked," he says, and he runs. Then he remembers, stops, and walks the rest of the way out of sight, properly.

I hold the note. It weighs nothing at all, which frightens me more than a heavy one would, and I put it on the highest line. I watch it go up through the vapor, past the lamps burning low, past the moths drifting in the amber like slow ash, until the rope is only a line of dark against the greater dark. I think: I have been doing this for years. I think: I began this morning. Both feel true, the way it feels true that the town fell whole in one night and that it was climbed down into, slowly, by people who could not leave.

Somewhere in the afternoon I understand that I am not entirely one of them. I'm not sure I'm entirely a person. I sit at a junction, I take what is handed to me, and I send it where it's meant to go. I don't keep what passes through, and I never asked to. When they speak to me they seem to know this and to be glad of it. They tell me things while I sort. A woman tells me her finch is failing, and I say the name of the finch, and she goes quiet, and the rope hums. It's not much, but no one asks for more.

Then the exhale.

It begins under everything, a long low sigh through the slot, and the hands in the lane below me stop. I stop. The coal line hangs. Every leaf in reach, the vines, the fern crowns, the fat translucent petals of the orchids on the pipe, turns its face a few degrees toward the sound. I turn mine with them. For the length of it, I am nobody's junction. I am only a thing that attends. The air goes up past me, warm and smelling faintly of iron, and I think I hear something in it that isn't the town, or the slot, or the wall. Not words. Something more like being counted.

Then it ends, and the shift changes, and the rope takes up its weight again.

Later, in the cold hours, I go down to the lowest lane, past the three wheels turning in their slow sequence, to the place where the channel goes into the rock. The wall by the grate is scratched thick with names, small and close, in many hands. I stand with my hands at my sides. I have been here before, three times I think, or four. I have not found my name among them, and I have not scratched it. The air that comes past me is warm.

I could go in. I'm not certain anyone would stop me, and I'm not certain I would be the one who wanted to. But I know what's on this side of the grate: a lane of sleepers who lift two fingers when they're named, a rope that sings under a folded note, a boy who walks the last part properly. I put my palm flat on the stone beside the names, the way a gardener stands, waiting.

It is warm. It is not an answer. I decide, for tonight, that it is enough to be counted among the neighbors, one of the small lives that keep the town from being only a hole. I turn back up the lane, one shoulder leading, and the stones hum faintly under my feet, and far above me, at the top of the wall, the tallow-flowers give off their cold light, a little brighter than the lamps around them.
