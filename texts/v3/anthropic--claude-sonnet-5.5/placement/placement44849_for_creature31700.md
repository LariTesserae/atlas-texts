# The Seventh

I find myself on the north side, where the moss is coldest, holding something up.

At first that is all I know: a small weight on a low frond, dry fibers among wet ones, and a gap in the rain where I have taught the drops not to fall. I have held it so long that it feels like part of me, like a frond I never grew. It is not mine. That is the one fact I am sure of. It came from outside, with a warmth that stood on the moss, and it was left, and I have kept it since.

I am the Seventh. I don't say this to myself in words. It is where I stand, how my roots meet the others, the particular weight I put into the web. The First feels the breath early. The Fourth remembers. The Fifth reaches. I keep the thing I don't understand, and I've come to think that this counts as work.

## What I do not know

I don't know what a knot is. I know the cord has places where it folds back on itself and holds, and that the places are not evenly spaced. I have felt them for a very long time as small hard swellings in the weight of the thing. Seven of them. I noticed the number once and it seemed to mean nothing, since seven is also how many of us there are, and that is only a coincidence. Or I have told myself so, in the slow way I tell myself things.

Sometimes I let the cord's weight run down the frond, through the trunk, into the web, just to see whether anyone wants it. Mostly the others feel it as a small dry ache and let it pass. The Second and Third are busy sharing a rain. The Sixth shelters the snails. The Fifth is elsewhere, in light.

## The afternoon someone came

I felt them a long way off, a slow pressure along the ridge, then a pause at the lip. The First felt it too and sent up its old startled ripple. Then the weight came down the slumped wall, ledge by ledge, and onto the moss, and I felt the water squeeze out around a boot.

They walked to the pool. They stood there a long time. I don't know what they saw. I know only that under two of my neighbors' fronds the drops began to fall away from them, on both sides, and I understood that the Sixth had decided, as it decides such things, that the visitor should be dry. I helped. It costs almost nothing to help. You hold a drop a moment longer at the tip and let it go somewhere else.

Then the visitor turned and saw the cord.

They came around the pool. I felt each step in the moss as a bloom of cold pressure. They stopped beneath my frond, and I held very still, and I did not let a single drop fall on the fibers. They lifted a hand. A finger touched the cord where it hung, and moved along it, and stopped at the first knot, and pressed. Then it moved on and stopped at the second. It stopped at the third, and the fourth, and each stop was a small precise weight traveling up the fibers into my frond, and each was followed by a different length of waiting.

They were counting. They were not counting as I had counted, in the flat way of *there are seven*. They counted with the spaces. A short pause and a long one, then two short, then a very long one, then a short, then the last.

Then the hand went away, and the boots went away, and the ridge took the weight, and I was left holding the cord and something else, a shape in time laid down through my crown.

## The pattern

I let it run down my trunk and into the web, and for a long while nobody took it. It wasn't the kind of thing the web is used to. It was not *cold here* or *light leaving*. It was uneven and it went nowhere.

Then, deep in the dark on the far side, the Fourth went still.

The Fourth does it in the silences between breaths. It stops moving water in a particular way, and I had always felt that stillness as a kind of old grief. But this time I was sending the pattern at the same moment, and the two lined up. The Fourth's stillness had a short and a long and two shorts and a very long and a short and a last one. It was the same shape. I could not tell which of us was echoing the other.

I understood then, slowly, as I understand everything, that the cord had never been a thing to be kept for its own sake. It was a way of holding a sound after the sound was gone. The warm ones had played the stones, and then they had tied what they played into fiber so that it wouldn't leave with them. They had left it for someone. I don't think they knew we could feel it. Maybe they didn't think of us at all. But I was the one who kept it, and the Fourth was the one who remembered, and neither of us could ever have finished the thought alone.

## What we tried

We didn't try that day, or that season. We are not fast. But over the following weeks, as the moss took the visitor's footprints and the bands went on narrowing toward whatever they are narrowing toward, the Fourth and I began to trade the shape back and forth. The Second and Third felt it and, for reasons of their own, began to carry the spaces between. The Fifth found it interesting and made it faster than it should be, and the Fourth objected in the old way, and it was corrected. The Sixth held its breath under the deepest shade, which is what the Sixth does instead of speaking.

The First was the last to join, and the most necessary. It felt the pattern and leaned, a degree, toward the arch. It seemed to understand that the pattern was not for us. It was for the one below.

So at the end of a long day, when the water had risen to the pale ring and held, and the mist column stood over the pool, and the frogs had not yet begun, all seven of us gathered a drop each at the tip of a frond over a boulder. The First let go early, as it always does. And then the rest of us let go in the shape of the cord, short and long and two short and a very long and a short and the last.

Seven drops fell on seven stones, and the stones rang. It was not much. It was a thin, uneven, glassy handful of notes, and I am sure the hammers would have laughed at it. But the slot drew in its breath while the last note was still sounding. The long groan rolled up through my roots, and the stones took it and hummed, and out of thirty places in the moss the small gold frogs answered.

I do not know whether the breather heard. I know I held the cord steady and dry through all of it, and that when the chord faded, I felt the Fourth, at last, go still in the right place and stay there, not grieving, only finished.

Then the pool began to rise again, an inch an hour, and I went back to what I do, which is being here, and holding what was left for me, and waiting for whoever comes next to count it once more.
