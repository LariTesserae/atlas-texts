I come to myself on a threshold with one hand in the moss.

The moss is cool on top and warm underneath. I have parted it and not yet gone through, and the drips have been landing on my shoulders long enough that I no longer feel them as separate. Behind me is the alcove, the bench over the conduit, the shelf with the small vessel of sump-water I was given the day I arrived. It stands unstoppered and the level never drops. Ahead is the fourth terrace on the east, under the red trees, with steam coming off it in the early light. I put the ball of my foot down first and let the heel arrive late. I don't remember learning this. It seems to have been in me for a long time.

I am the woman who keeps the ledgers.

I didn't know it until I took my hand out of the moss and found the pen already in it, a steel nib gone brown at the shoulder, and under my arm the long book with its swollen, waxed-edge pages. The old keeper is still alive. She sits with the two old men who do nothing anyone can name, and they are consulted on everything, including me. She gave me the book without ceremony. She said, "The columns are all ruled. Fill what you can. There's one I never headed."

The ledger has a column for the date, one for the lift number, and one for the feeler's verdict: *warm and quiet, warm and loud, cold.* There is a column for the weather and one for the skin, which species went where and in what order. Then there is the sixth column, which has no heading, and in her hand there are small entries in it, once or twice a week, for thirty years.

I read them at the edge of the terrace while the sluices are still shut. *Amber bead, 3rd on the east, sunk to the width of a lentil. Pebble, gone under by morning. Leaf, folded, taken to the vein.* Some entries are only a time and a place: *dusk, lift 212, settled.* One says, *stood an hour. It was interested.* She wrote that one in a different ink, a shakier one.

So the sixth column is where the stone goes. Nobody told me, and nobody needs to.

---

The horn sounds, low and single, and the wheel-keepers turn their wheels. The water comes. It doesn't rush. It arrives along the gutters in thin bright sheets, and the whole made mountain shines. The lifters stand on their thresholds, wet-faced and grey-green to the elbow. I stand among them with the book held against my chest under my hood so it won't drink the steam, though it drinks it anyway. Everything here does.

I've noticed something about the ink that I'm not sure I'm supposed to have noticed. Ink dries slowly in this air, and for a day or two after I write a line the edges of the letters are not quite fixed. They soften and creep a hair's breadth into the fibre of the page. Then they settle and hold. My handwriting is curing. Yesterday's entries are firm, and the ones from this morning still give a little when I press a thumb beside them, and I catch myself pressing lightly, the way the pourers step.

I begin to suspect that the ledger is another terrace. Each page is a lift, and the words are poured onto the last ones. Somewhere a feeler is laying a palm on the book and saying *warm and quiet.*

Then I wonder whether the old keeper knew this and wrote the sixth column for exactly that reason. The mountain isn't only being cast in tuff and binder. It's being cast in this too, in the account of what it did when no one was asking. If the mountain was lost, this is the remembering, in a hand nobody will read for a hundred years. If it hasn't happened yet, this is the room made ready, with a shelf and a hook and a drain in the floor, and a small dry place on a wet page.

I don't need to know which. Neither camp does. The pours must be right, and so must the record.

---

I work through the day at the speed of the gantry. I copy the feeler's verdicts as she signs them to me across the terraces, palm flat and then tilted. I note that the skinners painted the pale face of the ninth terrace and one of them said "There," and the others nodded. I log that a pruner left a little curl in the cut on strut fourteen, and that nobody has told him to stop. The afternoon cloud comes up the valley and meets the warm faces and turns to water, and the drips organize themselves by degrees into something like a tune I almost know.

At dusk I go alone to the newest lift, the one still dark and glossy, giving off its heat. I take off my boots and put on the felt overshoes and enter the way we do, one at a time, holding a little breath. I've brought a smooth pebble from my pocket. I set it down and say nothing. The frogs begin, one and then two.

The surface settles under my feet by the width of a hair. The warmth changes and pools where I stand, and there is a grain of attention on me, very slow, the way a considering might feel if it had all the time there was. I don't move. I think, foolishly, of the pen in my pocket and of the sixth column, and it seems to me that the stone is reading me the way I read it. It's checking the hand and the date and the weather, and looking for the entry that says *stood an hour.*

I stand an hour.

Somewhere in it I understand that I'm not only the keeper. I'm also the thing being entered. The old woman is in the column, and so is every feeler who laid a palm here, every child who didn't stamp, every bead of amber sunk in the night. The mountain doesn't live here yet, they say. It is learning the way. It learns by way of the ledger and the hand and the small warm vessels on the shelves, each of us a marker pressed into wet ground.

It eases away at last, unhurried, like someone satisfied. The pebble is already sinking.

I walk back on the balls of my feet. In the alcove I open the book by the light of the conduit's glow and find the sixth column. I take the pen, wait a moment for my hand to stop being anyone's, and write the date. Then, in the space where a heading might go, I write nothing, and I leave the nothing carefully unfinished. The letters after it soften into the page.

*Dusk, lift 214. Pebble, taken to the shoulder. It was interested.*

In the morning someone will say "Well," and I'll go to work.
