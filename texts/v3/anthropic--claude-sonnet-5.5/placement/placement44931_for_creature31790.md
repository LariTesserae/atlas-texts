I start at the lip of the bowl, because that is where anyone starts. The sound comes up over the edge before the town does, a thud in the sternum and a chord that won't finish. I stand there long enough for my ear to try to resolve it and fail, and I notice that I want to be someone with a job here. Visitors are noticed, and being noticed in the dark between lamps is a kind of weather I don't want to stand in.

So I think: a listener. I'd like to lay a palm on a cast-iron pipe and feel it go flat. But the ear takes decades, and I would be a bad listener for a long time. I'd walk the causeways with my hand out, hearing only that the pipes are loud.

A root-wright, then. I like the thought of kneeling an hour at a junction, asking a rootstock to move an inch and not being sure who is asking whom. But I don't think I'm patient in the right way. A root-wright has to be willing not to know who's in charge. I'd want to know, and I'd ask, and that would be rude.

What I actually become is smaller. On my second day a lamp-tender finds me. She's a solitary, superstitious woman with moss-dust in the creases of her knuckles, and she steps around a dead globe of fog without looking, the way you step around a sleeping dog, though she has never seen a dog. She doesn't speak to me. She hands me a scraper. I spend the first months on the moss on the brass cages, working in the amber sphere of each lamp, and I flinch less than the others do when I cross into the light. That's the only reason I'm useful. I'm the one who can stand lit, so I do the lit work, and the thirty are grateful and never say so.

They name me by the note that was sounding when I came down the causeway. It happened to be a slide, the chord moving from one key toward another, so I am called Between, which is not a pipe but the sound of two pipes disagreeing. It's a poor name. It's mine anyway.

I learn to hum. I learn it the way you learn to breathe in a warm room, without deciding to. One evening I realize I'm on the same note as the woman scraping the next cage over, and neither of us chose it. When the chord slides we go down together, and I feel the town take me in the way the peat takes a boot, first a give, then a weeping around the edges.

I would like to say I stay a lamp-tender. But it's the fog that draws me, and I think it has been drawing me from the start.

It collects around the engine house in the low hours, thick and sinking against the wind, and on my first deep hour down there I say nothing. I only stand. The four-part thud is close enough to lean on. Then I say my own name, once, quietly, the way you're taught. It comes back half a second late, lower, from my left, where no one is standing. It doesn't sound like an echo. An echo is a wall's opinion of you. This sounds like being repeated by someone who has been listening a long time and has decided to agree.

I go back again and again. I don't say much. I've learned to say only what I mean to leave behind, so I have very little to say. But I hear things. A counting-off of pipe numbers, in a cadence no one living uses. Half a lullaby whose tune I nearly know. A name, and then the same name, and then a pause so long I think it has finished, and then a third time, lower.

Nobody asks me what I hear. It would be as rude as asking a root-wright who runs the drains. The engine-keepers, who are the only ones who speak in ordinary voices, nod at me on the stairs the way you nod at someone carrying water, and let me by.

So this is where I find myself. I'm not the fog, though sometimes at the deep hour I can't tell where my voice stops. I'm not a listener, though I have started to listen. I keep the lamps by day, or what the engine calls day, and by night I go down and stand in what the town keeps. I've become a kind of go-between who never carries anything anywhere. I bring the names of the dead in from the families and leave them in the fog, and I bring nothing back out. That is the agreement, and it's a good one. A well doesn't owe you its depth.

And still I notice things, because I'm the sort who does. The chord has been sliding toward a new key for a season now, the ferns trembling a little earlier each time, the beat a fraction slower than last year, the tally in the hall copied fresh in a stranger's hand. And I have noticed that the new key has a shape I have heard before. It's the shape of the lullaby, the half of it I nearly knew. Somewhere in the pipes and roots and the sinking mist, what was said here is being turned into what the town will sound like next.

I could be wrong. I'm young at listening. It could be that anything hummed long enough in a place like this ends up sounding like everything else.

But I hum along, without deciding to, and when the note comes for the part I don't know, my voice goes there first, half a second before the pipes do. Nobody notices. Thirty people and a marsh's worth of ferns go down together into the new key, and I go with them, low, a little late, from the wrong side, and for the first time in the town I feel I have been kept.
