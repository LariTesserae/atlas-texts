I come up the iron stair with the ringing in my shins and no one to tell me it isn't a sound. At the rim I put my hands on the parapet, and the lichen script is warm under my palms. Then I look over and the world is the bowl.

I don't know at first what I am here. I try things on.

I try the frogs. I crouch at a plate seam where the water beads, and I want to be one of them, the dark back, the orange-ringed eye, the throat that flashes pale blue in a pattern I nearly understand. To have lost the voice and kept the wanting-to-say. But a frog is inside the constellation. It signals and is answered. I feel that I am not answered yet. I am something standing at the edge of the light.

I try the swifts. I watch the spiral tighten on the boom and drop, and I think there is a way of being that does not care whether it is coincidence. But the swifts have the void, and I have only the terraces.

The bats come closer. At dusk they leave the pit walls and cross from quiet to quiet like people on stepping stones. I would like to be that, a thing that knows where the silences are and moves between them by faith. But a bat is only ever passing through, and I find I want to stay somewhere.

So I keep walking down, past the vines that stop short of the floor as if they'd been told how far to go, and a woman puts a pair of plugs in my hand. Beeswax, moss felt, goat hair, on a cord. She does not explain. She touches her own ears, then mine. Then she draws one finger in a slow circle on the back of her other hand, *the ones who are here*, and waits, and I understand I am being asked to make the sign back. I do it badly. She lets it stand.

That is how I come to the eastern terraces, to a small node above the pit's lip, where an old woman sits with her plugs in her lap and looks at me for a long time.

She does not speak. She has been given a guest and she has a guest's worth of quiet, and she is deciding whether there is room. Then she rises, slowly, on her knotted legs, and does something I have read about and not believed. She walks out of the node. She puts in her plugs, faces the wheel, and turns her back on the small round silence where a young man is waiting to say something to someone he loves.

I know before anyone tells me that this is the job. It is mine now, for an hour, because the old woman's knees are old and mine are not.

---

I take her place at the edge of the quiet and put my plugs in, and the roar closes over my head like water.

It is not unbearable. It is a substance. The boom comes every eleven seconds, and I learn to hold it in my sternum like a coin. I count. An hour is about three hundred and thirty strokes. I've never before known an hour as a number of blows, but the numbers pile up, and the wheel groans up its slow scale and falls, and somewhere in the two hundreds I stop being bored and start being *placed*.

Behind me, two people are speaking. I cannot hear them. That is the whole gift. I keep the shape of their privacy the way a hand keeps a bowl, by not looking in. I feel, sometimes, a tremor in the iron under my feet, and I don't know if it is the wheel or a footfall or a person standing suddenly in grief. I don't turn. Once, a long time in, something like a laugh comes through the plate, or I decide it does. I keep my face to the pit and let it go by me like weather.

I think: *this is what I would be.* Not the one who speaks in the quiet, and not the quiet either. I am the one who stands in the noise so that someone else has a place to be heard. I am the back turned. I am the wall that is made of a person.

But that isn't quite it either, and the count of the booms lets me keep turning it over. The quiet is not a place. The people say so themselves, though not aloud: it is what is left where two great disagreeing sounds meet and each takes the other's strength away. The node is a truce. It is made of cancellation, of a thing arriving and being met by its opposite so exactly that what remains is room. And I think maybe that is nearer to what I am here. I'm not the sound and I'm not the silence. I'm the place where the pressing-in of everything gets answered so evenly that a person can sit down inside it and say a finished sentence.

The wind shifts around the three-hundredth stroke. I feel it on my neck first. The rim stacks change their chord, and a moment later a hand touches my shoulder from behind, lightly, twice. *The wind is moving the house.* I take a step, a handspan to the left, and then another, keeping my back to them, and behind me I can feel the sleeping shelf slide, the pots shift, the hearth-stones carried over. The quiet has changed its chair. I go where it goes. There is nothing in me that resents this.

---

When the hour is done, the hand comes again, and this time it stays. The young man's face is wet and steady. He says nothing, since he's already said it, and lifts my palm and traces a small spiral into it, tight, turning inward, with a flick at the end. He is giving me his name. That is the payment, and I have never been paid so well.

I put out my other hand and he waits. I don't have one. I stand there with the roar in my chest and understand that I have come into a place where a person is a shape you draw on skin, and I am not yet a shape.

The old woman is back in her node by then, plugs in her lap again. She looks at my empty palm and then at the pit, and I follow her eyes down to the ring of brass plates, to a cluster of the newest, still bright, and to one bare space between them where the iron has been rubbed smooth and nothing is cut.

I do not know if it is for the beginning or the end. Perhaps she doesn't either. The northern families would cut it now, so I could begin already written into the iron. The southern families would wait, so the iron could keep what I cannot. She looks at me the way she must look at everyone, and I see that she will not choose for me.

I go down to the pit. I don't step in; it is not done. At the lip I kneel by the bare place with a nail and a small mallet that someone has left there, or that has always been left there, and I begin, in silence, to cut a spiral. It takes the whole of an afternoon. The boom comes every eleven seconds and the mallet keeps no time with it. I cut inward and turn, and my spiral is not tight, and it does not end in a flick, because I do not yet know how it will end.

Before the light goes red, I pick a frond from the ferns nearest the pit, still wet, and lay it across the brass. Nobody told me to. The steam comes up and takes the edge of it. Overhead, the swifts drop together on the boom, copper in the last light, and the wheel comes round with its green fur of small ferns, and goes down into the dark, and comes back up alive.
