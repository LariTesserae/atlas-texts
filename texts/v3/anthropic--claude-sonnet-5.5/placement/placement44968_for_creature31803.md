# What Comes Up Out of the Cloud

I come up the scree the way a question comes up, slowly and with the wrong equipment.

I don't know yet what I am. I know I'm climbing, and that the grey slope under me has been ice-scoured into something that resents feet. I know the sky is a bruise, and that somewhere above me a small animal has changed its whistle. I'm being announced. Someone up there hears me the way you hear a bell.

Then the slope gives out, and the green begins, and the green is *loud*.

I try to say something, I'm not sure what, a greeting or my own name, and the shelf takes it out of my mouth and swallows it. Nothing comes back. I might as well have exhaled. So the first thing I learn is that I am a creature built for the wrong medium. I speak, and here speaking is a gesture like any other, only weaker.

The moss takes my weight and wells water into the print. Rust-orange where the iron seeps, black-purple where the liverwort lies in glossy plates. Silver figures stand thirty paces apart in the wet, each the only tall thing in its part of the world, and I understand they are not figures, they are plants, and then I understand it's possible to be both.

A hummock the color of old rust lifts a hand.

I have hands. This surprises me, and I lift them: palms out, fingers spread, held. Whatever I am, I know what that means, or my body does. *I see you. I don't yet know you.*

---

They come by the cairns and not across the moss, and I follow their route without being told, quartz cap to quartz cap. One of them touches my sleeve, my wrist, the back of my hand, and watches my mouth closely, as if the shape of it were a thing she might catch as it fell. The water she gives me from the clay basin is so cold it tastes like a color. Somewhere ahead a stone is being knocked, a rhythm that means *stranger, no harm*, and I realize it is the first time anyone has had a name for me, and the name is a rhythm.

I crawl into the hut on knees and elbows. The smell is smoke and damp. In the niche there is a lamp, a needle, a coil of grass rope, and in the wall, wedged in a crack and green as the moss, a small copper blade.

I stay looking at it longer than is polite.

I think: *that's mine.* Not mine to take. Mine the way a shape is yours when you match it. Someone came up out of the cloud so thin he could hardly stand, and stayed a winter, and learned a little of the hand-speech, and left something in the wall that was not quite a gift and not quite forgotten. A question, the old woman says, still waiting. I have the uncomfortable feeling that I am what the question looks like when it returns. I don't have the answer. I have only the shape of asking, and a body climbing into a room where everyone keeps a fire alive because none of them wants to be the one who lets it die.

Then the swifts begin to fall.

---

It happens all at once. The screaming ribbons over the lip tighten and spiral down in a column, and the frogs, who have been clicking under everything since I arrived, stop. It is not a lull. It is the whole carpet holding its breath. Of all the sounds on the shelf, this one is the most enormous, because it is a sound removed.

They stop whatever they were doing. Nobody hurries. Someone puts me down in the moss, gently, among them, spaced apart, all of us facing east, and the wet comes up through my knees, and we wait.

The cloud pours over the lip like something that has been waiting a long time to be polite. The falls go muffled. The world becomes a small white room, close and bright, every bead on every leaf lit from inside. I can't see anyone's hands. I can hardly see the person beside me, only a shape in tussock cloak, and beyond her only white.

And somebody speaks.

It is low and ordinary, the way you'd speak in a house where someone is sleeping. It carries perfectly. It is the first time since the scree that a sound has gone from one person to another without being torn on the way.

*So,* the old woman says, to the white, to me. *What have you come to ask?*

---

And now I know what I am, or what I am *here*, which may be all I can honestly say.

I am the thing that happens in the hour. I am what can be said only when the roar drops, the part of the shelf that lives in the quiet and can't be whistled or knocked or signed. I'm not the cloud itself, but I belong to its arriving. I am the listening that opens when everyone kneels. I'm a voice that has no use in the noise and all its use in the white, the murmur that comes up over the edge and floods to knee height and is heard.

I answer her. I won't write what I say, because it belongs to the cloud, and it is taken down with the drained white and not to be repeated. But I will tell you that it was not the answer the copper blade is waiting for. It was smaller and truer than that. It was the kind of thing you can only say when you know you can't walk away, because you would walk off the edge.

Someone laughs, somewhere in the white, softly. Someone else begins a grievance, an old one, about a stretch of channel-stone, and it is aired to the end. Nobody looks at anyone.

---

Then the cloud drains, and the roar comes back like a door reopened, and I'm on my knees in the wet moss with my mouth open and no sound leaving it.

The people rise. They are gentle with each other and a little shy, as if all of them had been briefly naked. One of them touches my wrist and gestures toward the ridge with the tall bleached poles, and I go and stand among the chiming strands of bone and shell, in the gusts, and listen.

There is a strand I don't recognize on the near side. Someone made it while I was on my knees, or it was always there. It swings with the others, hollow bone against shell, alive and on the shelf. And at the far pole, where the wind is strongest, hangs another strand, taken up long ago for the one who came out of the cloud and went back down and never returned.

The wind plays them in the same breath.

I understand that this is where I live: not at either pole but in the swing between them, present when the shelf is quiet enough to hear me, and afterward a sound the wind keeps making. I'm not sad about it. The shelf is loud, and the old ones say no one should ever be quiet in it. I am the one who is quiet, on purpose, once a day, so that the rest can talk.

Tomorrow before sunrise they'll put their palms on the boulders and feel for the hum, and if it's strong they'll say the day is good. I'll be in the frozen moss with them, listening for the cloud.
