# The Chewer in the Third Bowl

I come to myself in the heat, which is how everyone comes to themselves here. The ground breathes up through my feet, dry and steady, and the breath tastes of struck flint with something sweet laid over it. Before I know what I am, I know I am warm, a little warmer than the stone, and that this is what I'm for.

I have no forward sight. The world arrives as a wide dim smear on either side, gold and green, with movement in it. Everything that matters comes through the mouth and the antennae. So the first thing I learn about my body is that my jaws are already working, slow and sideways, turning a crumb of ash into a bead of amber. I don't remember starting. I'm not sure I've ever stopped.

I am a chewer. I sit in the third resin bowl from the crooked junction, in the shadow of a spire that is cream at the top and clear as weak tea at the roots, and I am the mill.

---

The work is this. A carrier comes down the wall with a bead held out before it like a full bowl, and I take the bead. Behind the carrier comes an ash-bringer, grey to the eyes, smelling of vents, and I take the ash. The bowl beneath me is lightly sticky and slightly warmer than the air, and there is a fine green dust in it, sifted down from the moths overhead. I work all of it together with the flat spoons of my mandibles. A little more ash makes it stiffer and paler. More resin makes it clear and slow. The moth dust I don't measure. It goes in because it falls in, and that is why the walls glow at night.

What comes out is a pale paste, warm from my mouth. A layer will climb the spire with it on some high-sap afternoon, and a ring one finger wide will go down. It may take a day, and it will outlast me.

I make nothing. I want to be exact about that, because it's the part I love. The bead was made by the mountain's heat, the ash by the vents, and the shape by the layer's slow body. What I make is the between-stuff, the state in which one thing can become another. Every wall in this town was once in someone's jaw, and I am the someone.

---

Who taught me the trails I can't say, but I can read them. The wall beside the bowl is written over in layers, the newest sharp and bitter on my feet, the older ones gone soft as fruit. *Load ahead. Take the left ramp. This seam is wet. This one is done.* Someone has written *thin here, wait for high sap*, and under it, fainter, someone else agreed. There is no voice in any of it. The town is quiet because nobody in it has ever said anything aloud, and I'm glad. I couldn't bear to be a thing that made noise here. Once a bird clicked directly over my bowl, one dry tick that the leaves swallowed, and I felt it in my whole body for an hour.

---

I flew once.

That is the thing I carry, and it sits in me the way a bead sits in a hollow of stone. I was young, one of the pale carriers, and the plate dropped. The sound came up through the rock, sharp and clean, and every mason on the shelf went still. I remember the stillness more than the flight. Then something in my back came loose and opened. My wings unfolded for the first time, thin as the skin on milk, and I was in the air with hundreds of others, in a rush like pages turning.

It was not far. We never go higher than the canopy. But the light came through the leaves with me inside it, and for the first time in my life I saw *forward*, a wide bright blur straight ahead, the new fracture in the moss, a dark line in the rock. We settled along it in a shimmering line and began.

Within two days my wings had gone brittle and shed. I chewed them myself, into the first mortar. They are in the spire above me now, the pale one at my back. I could climb up and put my mouth to the place. The wing-film has yellowed and the ash has thickened over it. The tower that was glass is turning to cream. That is what happens to a thing that is founded on a body. It clouds, and it holds.

I haven't flown since. I won't. I don't mind this, or I mind it the way you mind weather. I was born to this wall, and the wall is where I was going.

---

Now the afternoon changes. The sap is high, and I can feel it through the ground, the whole town leaning toward the light. A layer passes overhead, slow and furred with old ash, going up to its ring. The resin around the sleepers at the root of my spire is glowing where the files walk over it. I don't look at it. I don't have to. I know how many are there, and that the third from the left, facing out, has a crooked antenna.

Sometimes I pause there, at the root, with my antennae lowered. There's no work in it, and nobody asks.

---

There is one more thing, and it is the one I don't chew.

When I was still a carrier, newly out of the gangs, I left my file. I'm not sure why. Something in the smell of the lane changed, a thinning, and the next step went elsewhere, as it does for everyone. But I made the step anyway. I walked down past the last old mark, where no one writes anymore. The moss drew back in a ring. I stopped at the ring.

There was a wall of black glass and a thread of something clear coming down it. It didn't smell of resin or ash or flint or anything. It made no sound I could hear, though I stood there all afternoon, with my jaws for once perfectly still. The light off it was cold and thin, and it lay on my face like the touch of something that didn't need me.

I don't know what it is. I have never known. I turned around, found the trail, and went back to work, and I have not told anyone, because there's nothing to tell it with. Not one mark on any wall says it.

But this is what I know now, from the third bowl, after all my years of blending. Everything I have ever made is shaped around that place. Every lane that curves and doesn't arrive, every spire that stands one body-length off, every ring of mortar I have worked warm in my mouth has been a way of building a town that doesn't need it. The town is my answer, and the seep is what it answers. In all my mortar there is not one drop of it, and the whole town is a way of saying so.

---

Someday I'll stop. Perhaps I won't die, perhaps I'll only stop, and the others will do what they do. They'll set me into the wall near the root of the spire where my wings are, and cover me with a ring of someone else's chewing. Over a generation the resin will clear around me. I'll hang there in the honey-colored light, folded and whole, facing outward, and the files will pass over me without breaking stride. On hot afternoons their warmth will make me shine a little, and for a moment I'll seem to shift.

I won't. But someone may lower their antennae, and stay a while, for no reason that has any work in it.

Above me, the sap is still rising. A bead cools somewhere in the dark of the bowl with a small dry tick, and a carrier comes down the wall with its jaws full, and I open mine.
