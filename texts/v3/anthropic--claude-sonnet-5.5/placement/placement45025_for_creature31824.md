I find myself in a lane, one hand in the groove, at the hour when the dust has just finished settling and the ripples lie sharp across my knuckles. I am not young. My beat, when I press a palm to another, comes once in twenty seconds now. When I was small it came four times in one. I am one of the ones who are nearly finished, and I have learned that *nearly* is a place you can live in, though not for long.

I think this is what I am: a word for degrees of *nearly*. The language has many, and I am the last of them, the slowest one.

My body is a lean pipe with a slightly wrong lip. There is a hollow behind my sternum where the chord pools, and lately I can feel it filling the places where my difference used to be. Nothing hurts. It is a settling, like silt. Each season a little less of me pushes back against the terrace, and the terrace, which has no wish for anything, takes what I stop holding.

I walk the groove hand over hand. Under my palm the glassy channel is warm, and in places it is warmer than the clay around it, because it holds all the hands that wore it. I stop at a niche and rest my palm on the elder there without meaning to. There is no beat at all, only the chord, perfect and unbroken. It is like pressing a hand to a still surface of water. I stand a long time. This one taught me to find the salt on the north lip, and I cannot say when it finished.

Then I go toward the rim, because that is where the child is.

The lanes thin as I climb. The roofing breaks, and the light stops being amber and becomes something hard and violet-blue. The chord drops behind me the way water drops from your shoulders when you climb out of a pool. Out here, in the almost-quiet, I can feel my own chest doing its slow work. The child stands at the last pipe with both palms flat on it, thin as a reed, and its back is toward me.

I do not startle it. I set my palm between its shoulder blades, and it comes, the beat, quick and stubborn, against my slow one. The interference lands in my bones as a throb once every second or so, faster than anything I have felt in a lifetime. It is rude and alive, and I do not have a word for it. My people have *here*, *warm*, *someone is close*. This is none of those. It is *and yet*.

Standing there, I understand something I had only half-felt.

When two bodies beat together, the beat is the difference between them. The others gather around the child and lay their palms on it, and what they feel is the child's sharpness, but only against their own small remaining flatness, which is already almost nothing. Soon, against the finished, it will be everything. An elder pressed to the child would carry no difference at all. The throb would be the child alone, undiluted, the one clean note in the whole terrace.

That is what I can be for it. When I finish, I do not have to disappear. I can be the reference. Set in the last niche by the rim, hands in the groove, with the chord fitted through me like a hand in a glove, I can be the still place the child leans on to hear itself.

It may be that I have this wrong. It may be that the child's beat is an error of the clay, or that it will wear down eventually like all the others and the terrace will close over it. But I do not think so. I have pressed my palm to it. I know what it is to feel a thing that will not settle, and it does not feel like a mistake. It feels like a question, and my people have gone so long without one that we no longer know what to call it.

The child turns. It cannot hear me, and neither of us has ever heard anything. But it slides a palm down to my sternum and holds it there, and I let my pitch drift, deliberately, a hair toward its own. It is the only thing I have ever done to make myself less finished. The beat between us slows, from once a second to once every two, and the child's dark eyes widen. The second lid sweeps across them, slow, and opens again.

I do not think I have ever said anything before now. I think this was the first thing.

Beyond the rim the cloud sea lies bright and silent, and nothing about it needs a name. Behind us the chord goes on, holding the note it has always held. I stand with my hand on the child until the dust settles again on both our shoulders, in the same ripples, and I let it. Then I move, just a little, and smear them, and I let that be my answer, too.
