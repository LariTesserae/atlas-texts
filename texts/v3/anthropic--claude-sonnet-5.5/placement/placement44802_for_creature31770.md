I start out on the ground, I think. There is a girl on a moss ledge with her ear against the warm rock, and for a moment I take myself for her, the cheek pressed into the cushion and the whole valley coming up through the bone. But I can't be her. I'm too high, and I'm smelling tar.

So I look down at my hands. They are stained black to the wrist, the nails rimmed with it, and one palm has a white scar across it, pale as a basalt fracture. That's a wrapper's hand. I am on the trestle, forty feet above the sedge, on the stretch below the felting mill, sitting astride the pipe with a coil of rope over my shoulder and a bucket hooked to the rail. The lagging under my thighs is warm. It is ebb, so it is barely warm, the warmth of a sleeping animal. The rain has just finished. The sun has come back all at once, the way it does, and the whole Cut is steaming and glittering.

I'm not sure yet whether I'm good at this. The joints all have names, and I've been given one to keep, the one nobody wanted. It's the seventh joint below the felters, and it is called Second Thoughts. When the old length cracked, forty years back, they cut its replacement to the note the pipe *ought* to have, on the advice of a visiting engineer with clean cuffs. At the top of the Chime, every length on the line sings its old, cracked, beloved note, and Second Thoughts sings a hair sharp of it, clear and correct and slightly embarrassing. It sounds like a person who has learned the words to a hymn but not the tune. The other wrappers pretend not to hear. Old Marrick, whose stretch is the next one up, hums the right note at me, kindly, the way you would correct a child's grammar.

I press my ear to the iron collar. Tick. Tick, and a long gap, and the tick of the metal giving up its heat. Below me the frogs have started up, then stopped, because I shifted my weight and a bootheel scraped. They wait. They start again, a bass croaker first, then the rattlers in the ferns, then the piping ones like nails on glass. Courtesy, they call it, though nobody asked the frogs.

Here is where it starts to slide. I'm listening so hard that I lose the edges of myself. I hear the felting drums thump wetly in the mill, and the sound goes out across the meadow and hits the north wall and comes back, half a second late, and doubled. I hear my own cough return from the basalt, and it isn't quite my cough. It's rounder and older, and it belongs to the rock now. I think: I live a little behind my own echo. I think: so does everyone here. We say a thing and then we hear it said back, and we adjust, and the adjusted thing goes out again. Nothing in the Cut is ever first. We are all the second telling.

And then I'm not sure the bucket is mine.

There is a stack over the last mill with a thread of white coming out of it. I can see it from here, and I seem to be able to see it from inside as well, the brick throat furred with soot, the ledges crowded with warm bodies. The stacks were built for a fire that never came, and now they hold the thousands. I feel the press of feathers on both sides of me. Someone is grooming the back of my neck. I have a whistle in my chest, a small hot bead of it, and I don't know where I got it. It's the first two notes of the Chime, the ones the tram-keeper hums under his breath, and old Marrick, and the girl on the ledge. I've had them for days. The third I don't have. I keep getting to the place where it should be and finding a gap, the way your tongue finds the space where a tooth was.

So I'm both, apparently. Or I'm the place between them. I sit on the pipe with tar in my nails, and I sit in the stack with my throat full of borrowed sound, and neither of us can finish the chord.

Below, the girl lifts her head from the rock. She doesn't look up. She whistles the third note.

It's not Marrick's note, the old cracked one. It's not the engineer's either. It's somewhere between them, a little sharp of the one and a little flat of the other, and I realize she isn't whistling the pipe. She's whistling what she heard the starling do last ebb, when it took the old note and turned it over and handed it back a little wrong. The wrongness has been drifting for weeks. It has been walking across the roofs from bird to bird, and from bird to child, and it is walking now toward Second Thoughts.

I take it in. The bird in me takes it, or I take it as the bird, and I turn it over. The note is warm and slightly sour, like the mud near the spring. I can feel where it doesn't fit and where it might, if the pipe were patched again and the patch cut a little differently. I could tell Marrick. I could say the valley is tuning itself and we should listen. But he would only hum the right note at me, gently, and be right, and I would be right, and the pipe would go on being patched, and the starling would go on adjusting, and in forty years someone would cut a length to a note that had drifted the whole way round.

I don't say anything. I take the third note and hold it in my chest with the other two, and at the top of the Chime, in five days, when the mist rises to the rim and the pipes begin to ring, I'll sit on this joint with my palm flat on the iron and listen for the moment when it sings its wrong, correct note. Half the valley will be humming. I will hum something a little off from all of them, and up in the stack a bird will pick it up.

The rain is coming back. I can smell it on the east wind, salt and iron, and the basalt has gone dark and wet and sharp above me, in no hurry, its fresh faces shining. The frogs are getting louder. I hook my bucket, shift my weight, and let them stop for me, and wait, and hear them begin again behind me.
