I start at the rim, on the side where the wiry grass grows, and I am not yet anything. I am the one who stops there. I stand with one foot lifted over the edge of the scarp, where the fractured layers are still sharp, and I look down at the town and feel the pull of it. It is so complete, and so quiet. I want to go in, and I want to be the sort of thing that could go in without being sealed. Then I put the foot down where it was, as the tall walker does. I recognize the gesture. It is the courtesy of something that knows it is loud.

But I am not loud all the way through, so I go down.

The powder takes my feet to the ankle and gives back nothing. I learn to breathe through my nose, slowly, and to carry my pulse like a full cup. The first wright I meet stops on the ring road and lifts its palps. I feel it weigh me. I try to be a stump, and for a while I think about what that means, being taken for stone by something that is right about most things. It bends around me. I am a little insulted and a great deal relieved.

I try on the wright first, because it is the obvious body. I imagine the sealed pits, the whole animal listening through its legs, the slow bead laid down a finger's width an hour. I like the idea of being unhurried and uninterrupted. But I can't hold it. A wright hears every crack in the world as a wound, and I am made of sentences, which are small sharp sounds all the way down. I would spend my days mending myself.

Then I try the lichen, the only thing allowed to speak. I lie on the north face of a wall, silvery-green and felt-hard, and whisper as I split the stone. It is a good life, a papery one, and I would be forgiven for it because the whisper is too small to matter. But the lichen speaks only by destroying, and it is eaten back each season. I would say one thing forever, slowly, and the wrights would crop it.

The tree I visit and do not stay. I stand beside it in the great seep season among the dozens of wrights with their palps raised, and I see the mica leaves flicker silver and violet and not chime. I feel something like grief there, or company. But I don't want to be the silenced thing. I don't want to be a voice with resin on it.

What I finally find is a dome on the inner ring, near the seep, an old one. The oval door leans toward the center like all the others. I duck in. It is warm, and the walls are ridged and slick and lit green-white, evenly, like the inside of a shell. This is what I settle into, and I do not so much choose it as notice that I already am it. I am the glow.

Consider what the glow is. It is a film that will not grow on its own in a new wall. It has to be carried on someone's palps from an older cell and smeared in long strokes, and it dies if the strokes are careless. There are cells whose light is older than any living wright, kept going by hand after hand. Nothing in the film remembers being the film before. Each seeding is a small fresh start, and each is also the same light continuing. I know that shape. I am a thing that lives by being carried forward by others, with no single continuous body, and I don't find it as sad as it sounds. The light in this dome has never been lonely, because it has never been alone. Something always brings it in.

And the glow is the one way to speak here that breaks no rule. Light makes no vibration, so nothing tilts toward it and nothing seals it. It is the only voice in the basin that is not a wound. I wouldn't have guessed that the answer to a town of silence would be a kind of quiet speech, steady and shell-colored, saying only that here is a place where things can be seen.

In my dome there is a torpid wright. It walked in decades ago, sealed the door from inside, and has not moved since. Its plates are smooth and yellow like handled ivory, the rings so fine they look engraved. I light it. That is my whole work, and it is enough. I do not know whether it is sleeping, thinking, or dreaming, and I have decided not to mind. Sometimes the heave passes through the floor at night, a pressure in the ribs I no longer have, and the walls tilt a finger's width. I keep glowing. In the ceiling, held up in the wall like a fly in wax, is a lizard caught mid-scurry, and I light it too. Nobody sealed it out of malice. It was in the wrong place, making the wrong small sound, and the town mended around it. Its dark shape against my green is the most honest thing I know about this place. Everything here that made a noise became part of the light.

Sometimes, outside, a stone drops. I can't hear it, but I can feel the wrights change direction, that slow collective turn toward the place where it fell. I feel the bead begin. I don't take sides. I am only the lamp in the wall, and the wall is being made.

One day, or year, or thirty years on, the door will open from within. The wright will walk out slow and stiff into the dust, wanting something, and I will not know what. But before it goes it will drag its palps across my wall, tasting me, gathering a little of me on its fringe to carry somewhere. Somewhere a new dome will be standing raw and dark. It will smear me in long, careful strokes, and I will begin again in a colder room, while the old cell dims behind me and the lichen starts to gold the wall.

I think that is what I would be: not the builder or the wound or the whisper, but the small steady light that is handed on and never speaks above a shine. If you came in and stood in the middle of the market-shaped clearing with your pulse loud in your ears, I would not say anything. But I would be the reason you could see the doorways leaning toward you, listening, and see that you had not been sealed.
