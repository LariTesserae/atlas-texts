I begin as a guess. For a while I think I am the drip that falls on the felt and makes no sound. I like that: to arrive, be absorbed, and cause a dimming that is a kind of speech. But a drip has no hands, and I find that I need to make something.

So I am smaller and more particular. I am a mud-wasp, on the warmest flange, in the hour before the rain.

My body is the color of the wax-ferns and hardly weighs anything. The flange under my feet is as warm as a held breath. I carry a pellet of the pale paste, the mortar gone soft, the stuff you could write in. My mandibles know how to work it before I have any thought about it. I lay it down and press it with my forelegs, then lay a little more beside it and turn. A dome rises out of the flat iron, curved toward the center the way the vault above me curves, and each layer leans in a little further than the last.

I have never seen the whole ceiling. It is too big and too dark, and I am too close to everything. But my mother built this shape and her mother built it, and somewhere in the line it was learned from the brick itself. So I am not sure whether I am copying the hall or the hall is copying itself through me, using a small hot body as a pencil. I build the room I live under, at the size I can carry. I can't tell whether that is reverence or only the way this place remembers.

The light reaches me strangely. The red rim of the firebox lights one side of my dome and leaves the other black, so my small vault has the same one-sided glow as the pipes. When I finish, I think it will be the most accurate thing anyone has made of this room.

Then the beam moves.

I feel it before the groan, in the flange and in my feet, in the hollow of my thorax, where the sound arrives as pressure. The hum underneath everything stops. I have always known the hum was there only because it stops. In the gap I feel something enormous go still beside me, and I understand that the tender has crossed the aisle. A great folded shape has unfolded and moved and folded again, and the sound covered all of it. I'm not afraid. It is only weather, warm and slow.

My other nest is on its shoulder blade. I built it there in a warm season I barely remember, on skin the temperature of the floor, and it has never shaken it off. Sometimes I go and sit on the small dome from outside, a copy of the ceiling on a person who is also a copy of something. When it breathes, my whole nest rises and falls a little, and that is the nearest thing I have to a floor that lives.

I have my own patience, and it is a short one. I will not see the vine flower again. My life is a season, and the vine's timing is a longer thing than I can hold. But the tender sits under the dead white bell for hours, and I have sat on that bell too, on its dry papery lip, and I think the waiting is not sad. It is only very long, and the long thing is being handed across many short lives, like paste passed from one mouth to the next. The bell has to hang there until someone's turn comes. Maybe I am part of what keeps it from falling.

The drips are quickening now. The vault is cooling, and the first drop lets go from the rib and lands on the felt with no sound, and the green dims for a breath. I press the last pellet into the rim of my dome and leave one small opening at the bottom, just wide enough for the next one to come in or go out. Then I hold still against the flange while the room begins to rain on us, on the iron and the brick and my unfinished ceiling and the person folded in the niche with a wasp's house on its back.

Nothing asks anything of me. I have already worked out what I am for, and I am doing it.
