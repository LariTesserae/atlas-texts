I begin as something small and cold.

It is the hour before the sun clears the eastern ring, and I am head-down on a condenser plate, and the world is a slope of beaded steel. A drop gathers on my back. I don't decide to lean, but the plate tilts by the width of a hair as the beam rises somewhere far below, and the drop rolls forward and meets my mouth. It tastes of iron and the inside of a cloud. This is all I know for the length of one long breath: that water arrives if you hold still enough to be a slope.

Then the sun comes over the ridge and the plate warms under me, and I let go. I don't fall. Letting go is where I stop being the beetle, because a beetle would have stayed until the last drop dried.

For a while I am not sure what I am. I have the patter of a goat, that dry precise hoof-on-grating sound, and I hear it from the inside, from indoors, and know someone is saying my name by its pitch. I am a flue in a whispering flock, pouring through the pipe-gaps, warm from the engine housing, and there are two of me in the flock, exactly alike, and neither of us minds. I am a rosette of orange lichen on the north face of the Ninth Terrace, and someone has come with chalk and made a small stroke beside me, and I feel it as a kind of attention, slow as a season.

None of these hold. They are what the country tries on me, the way a hand tries a grip.

What holds is the walking.

I am on a gantry at the middle level, with the underside of one road above me and the top of another below. I have a rod in my right hand, drawn steel, its grip worn by palms that are not mine into a shape that fits mine anyway. My wool is pale and stitched in copper gone green at the cuff. I tap the rail and the rail answers, and the answer is a little late, a little soft, and I know what that means. I am counting under my breath. Forty. Ninety. A hundred and sixty. The far end doesn't come nearer. Then I take one step and I am there, and the count says eleven, and the count is not wrong.

Behind me a cord runs back from my belt. I feel it for a long time before I turn and look. It goes taut, slack, taut, and ends in a knot tied around nothing, with the other half of the pair nowhere. That's all right. The rule was never that the counts should agree. The rule is that both are still counting, and I am.

I put my palm flat on a flywheel housing and stand there for a full turn. Several minutes. The pressure comes up through my hand and my sternum, arrives, pauses, recedes, and my breath lengthens to match until I am drawing one breath for the whole rise of the beam and giving it back on the descent. Nothing in me is hurried. I think this must be what the Listeners mean by keeping company.

Then I look up, and at the end of a far gantry there is a figure I took for a fixture.

She has moved a hand.

She stands with a rod, in pale wool with green at the cuffs, too far to hail, facing the same way I am. When I set my palm on the housing she sets hers on a housing. When I tap the rail she taps, and the sound arrives after mine like a second, softer pulse. For a moment I am certain that I am the one who was walking here first and that she is the stray thought. Then I lean on the certainty, and it gives, and I understand that from where she stands the same certainty is available to her. Nobody has ever told me which of us is the copy. The soft places don't keep that record. They only keep what happens.

Evening. The sun drops behind the ridge and the terraces hold the last of the sky. Then the lamps come on, thousands of them in their niches, each reflected in a hundred wet surfaces, and I walk the catwalk and read them as a ledger. Somewhere in that ledger there is a lamp lit for the one who is going about her day, and one more lit for the one who has been newly doubled. From here I can't tell which is which, or whether the second is turned toward her gantry or mine. Someone has set a little meal on the lowest step of a stair, and I don't watch it go.

I know how this ends. The slab is still curing. Someday this stretch will harden, and the ground will keep whatever it holds in that instant, errors and all, and I'll be set with it, faintly warm on cold mornings, tended as one tends a grave that is also a neighbor. I find I have opinions about the moment. I would like to be caught with my free hand raised in the sign I like best, the palm out and open: *the span ahead is long today.* It is honest, and it is useful, and it is the kind of thing a person passing would want to be told. Children will grow up beside it and step around it without a glance, which is the highest thing a place can do with you.

Until then I stay. It is the only trade I have, and I've been learning it all day.

Far off, along the rail, someone taps once. It might be her or one of the Setters, or the pipe itself settling, or the country asking what it asks. The sound runs down the metal and arrives in my palm. I hold still and wait to know what I am.

Then I tap back.
