I'm resting against a wall I don't remember choosing, and before anything else there is the knocking: two soft, one hard, a pause, two soft. Someone is home, and they are telling the wall so.

My first impulse is to be something grand. The drifter, tracing its seam, wider than a house, the one thing everyone stops for. But I'd have no hands, and nothing to lean a cheek against. Being stopped for is lonely work. Then Long Sentence, the oldest snail, laying a clean black road across the glass so others can cross it. I like that one longer. But I'd be a sentence with no listener, and everything in this place seems to be made of listening.

So I stay against the wall and let it tell me.

It says the wall is hollow, and a few years thick. It says the family inside has a low, damp note, like a stone dropped in a cistern, and that they are not angry. It says there is a jar of lamp-weed just past the window that someone has forgotten to turn, so the light in there is lopsided. I don't know how I know this. The wall is a good speaker.

I think I am a woman. Long, stretched, the pale green-gold skin, hair going the way of the ribbon-grass. My gills flutter slower than my heart. I am old enough for the first lip to have started, one cream crust on my left knuckle, which ticks when I close my hand. I'm not old enough to be admired for it. I am at the age when people begin to ask you to hold things.

What I do is a job with no name. I live in a lane where two districts meet, where the hollow walls of the upper tiers give way to a stretch of dead stone that was filled in generations ago. Messages come down through the hollow houses bright and quick, and at my corner they hit the solid wall and slow and change. Every knock that has passed through a dozen houses arrives with a dozen accents on it. Mine is the ear that hears them arrive. I don't correct them. But I know what a message sounded like when it started, because the accents peel away in a certain order, like the layers of a terrace cut open. Someone uphill knocks *the child is well*, and by the time it reaches me it says *the child is,* and a beat, and *well*. Nobody meant anything by the pause. It is only what the walls do. But someone has to decide whether to pass on the pause.

Mostly I pass on the message as it came. Sometimes I put back the word the stone took.

There is another thing I do, which no one asked me to do and everyone would notice if I stopped. When two neighbors go quiet on each other, I am one of the ones who taps. Nobody appoints us. The silence begins, a wall that used to answer doesn't, and the city holds its breath in that small local way. Then someone starts knocking softly on both houses at once, nothing pointed, just a slow *we are here, we are here,* until one of the two cracks and answers. I have done it forty times. I have been on the other side of it twice, and I remember that the silence was so complete it felt like my own skin. Then a small knock came through the wall from a person I had not asked, and I hated them for exactly as long as it took to knock back.

The light is going to tea. My lane thins out. I feed my own jar its pinch of crushed shell, turn it a quarter, and watch the amber even out. Up the terraces the evening figure is starting. Someone high up begins it and the next house bends it, and by the time it reaches my corner it has a limp and a lilt and a stumble I would know anywhere, because it's the stumble of the baker's wall, whose house-note dropped when the stone thickened. I bend it once more and hand it downhill. Somewhere below, someone takes it from me and it changes again. I don't get to hear where it ends. Nobody does. The whole city goes to sleep on a sentence that nobody finishes.

I was going to say I have never touched the Lid, but I should be exact. I went up once, when I was young, resting in every seam as we're taught, letting my joints settle. I got near enough to see the Lid wrinkling like the inside of an eyelid, gold and moving. I did not touch it. I hung there with my hand out and a hand's width of water between my fingers and the rumor of air, and I understood that whatever was on the other side was not in any hurry, and that I did not need to be either. I came down quieter. When I say this, people are disappointed. It is a less good story than the ones where it opens. I've stopped apologizing.

I carry a small pale stone from my hearth, which I have not yet taken to the rim. It holds a story about the last night of someone I loved, and I don't know how it ends. I don't feel ready. Maybe I'll be the sort of woman who carries it for forty years, its edges softening from my palm, until my daughter's daughter finds it and asks, and I say *not yet, it's still being told.* The cold water can wait. It has always been patient. That's what the warm world is built on.

Tonight, just as the figure thins, the ground shudders.

It's the long kind, the twice-in-a-lifetime kind. I feel it in the breastbone first, then in the lip on my knuckle, which tocks against the wall like a small bone answering. Silt lifts off every terrace at once. The lampstalks let go early and confused, and the water fills with silver spheres rising with their threads behind them. All the knocking stops. The whole city stops. I hold still, cheek on the wall, and the wall is shaking with something bigger than any house.

Nobody speaks. We wait, as we are taught to, for the world to choose its next word.

The silt settles slowly, in sheets. Somewhere up the hill a wall gives one soft tap, testing. Then another answers, and another, and the city begins again, ragged, out of tune, all its accents back.

I knock once, softly. *Here.*

The wall knocks back, in a voice I know, a little lower than it was this morning, and growing.
