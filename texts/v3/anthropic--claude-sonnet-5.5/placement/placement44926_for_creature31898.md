I start by looking up, which is a mistake, because up is two million of something else. I try being a bat for the length of a breath. It doesn't hold. There is no one to be in that ceiling, only the ripple, one animal spread over the dome, and when the exodus starts I would be the pour itself and not know it. I let that go. It's too large, and it wouldn't notice the town.

I try being a person and can't decide which. The Cone-Woman with the wet cloth on her face? The Lamp-Wright on the ladder, holding the dark in one hand and the spare bulb in the other? They are good lives, but they are lives someone chose or inherited. What keeps happening is that I feel low and cool and near the water.

So I come down off the gangway, onto the pier, and something rearranges itself. My paws are tucked. My belly is against iron that is warm and I am not warm. Everyone in Harrow Deep is a wet, hot, rust-smelling animal, and I am the exception, a sleek thing with a coat that sheds the heat. In the afternoons, people lift me into their laps in the hammock rows to be cool against, and I allow it, the way a lease is allowed.

I am a pier cat. Nobody owns me. Eleven years ago I walked into a room, sat on a chest, and decided.

*

The room was the Gauge-Keeper's. That was not chance, though I would let her think it was. I had watched the households for a season, the pump-men's low terraces with the thud coming up through the beds, the mess-hall cooks, the Tinkers' scaffold-side lean-tos smelling of oxide. Every one of them was busy, and busy people want a cat for warmth or vermin. I wanted a person who sat still and looked at black water for a living.

She sits on the stool bolted beside the pier and trims her lamp. Every hour, in the wet, in the dry, in the small hours when only the engines and I are awake, she leans to the brass and reads it. She says it aloud, low. I don't have the letters. I have the sound of her voice, and I have learned that sound is enough for most things.

*

Here is what I know that she doesn't, and I have never known how to tell her.

The sump talks before the brass does.

The water that comes up from below is warmer than the air, and when it rises it changes its manner. Not the level, which is a matter for the gauge and the meeting hall, but the sound of the drips. A hundred seeps plink into a hundred buckets, and when the rock takes on more water somewhere above, the plinks quicken and come closer together, and the note of each one goes a shade higher. It is a sound below the pumps and below the bats, a thin bright sewing of drops. I can hear it with my whole face. My whiskers lie back a little when it changes. The pier hums differently. I'm not certain how I know. It is like knowing a person's footstep.

So on the nights when the wet season is turning, I get up from my paws and walk the length of the pier and back. She notices. She has never said so. She puts one hand flat on my back, which is the gesture for *level, steady*, and then she leans to the brass, and a half hour later the number confirms what I told her with my walking. Neither of us mentions it. It's a courtesy, like not remarking on someone's cough.

*

I should say what else I am, so as not to make myself finer than I am.

I am a killer. The sheds at night are mine and the rats' and we have our old dispute. I don't hate them. A shed with no rats has no cats worth keeping, and they know it too. Some nights I stalk the long aisles between the sacks and a large one sits up on a beam and regards me with a face like a small old politician, and neither of us moves, and we both understand the treaty: not tonight.

I eat blind fish from the sump's edge, when I'm fast. They drift up into the amber light, pale as candle wax, and they have no eyes to warn them. I am a little ashamed of this, as I am ashamed of most pleasures. The pale shrimp are better.

The roaches I ignore, as the town does. They walk over me. I let them.

*

At dusk I am on the pier, and the bell rings, and the gray comes down the east shaft.

This is the part I have to get right. The whole chamber stops, the hammers, the winches, the mess halls emptying onto the terraces, and four hundred and sixty faces turn up. And the roof begins to pour. I have watched it eleven years, roughly seven thousand times, and I still forget, each time, how *big* it is. Then it shows me. The dome and the scalloped walls and the little red boxes of the town, all of it seen at once, held in gray light for forty minutes, the wet stone shining like the inside of a mouth.

The Gauge-Keeper doesn't look up. She watches the water, because in the gray the sump is briefly a mirror, and the bats go over it in a long broken spiral, two million wings turned upside down and dark in the black. I sit beside her and look at both, the sky above and the sky below, and I think this is what I am for. Not the rats, not the fish. Somebody has to sit at the border between the water and the roar and be cool and attentive. Somebody has to be the animal that listens to the level.

*

A fern was put on the end of the pier once, in a tin cup. The gardener from the Lamp Works brought it down himself, one frond curled and green, and set it beside her stool without speaking. He tapped two fingers on his collarbone, *engine*, and then the flat palm, *steady*. She understood it. She had been told she was staying. She had, of course, been staying for nineteen years, but there is telling and there is telling.

I did not get a fern. Nobody gives a fern to a cat. But some nights, when she has read the number and written it in the book, she says it quietly, out over the water, and I feel the word go past me. I turn my ears to it as if it had been mine.

The brass says what it says. The drips sew their thin bright line. Overhead the whole dark rearranges itself, rustling, alive. And I am the cool small thing at the edge of it, holding still enough to hear what the water will do before it does it.

*Steady*, she says.

I put my chin down on my paws. For now, it is.
