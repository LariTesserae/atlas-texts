I wake, and for a while I am not anyone. I am only the wake-up, the small cold fact of it, and the first thing I could tell you about myself is that something has changed.

Nothing has stopped. The bed frame is still humming against my spine, the pumps are still going through the floor into my ribs, and the lamp on the lane still hangs its yellow halo in the damp. But a listener's body keeps a ledger of its own, and one column is off by a hair.

I'd have said, in that first minute, that I was the bell on the pulley, the one that swings and strikes by itself when the big pump starts, because I feel like something that was hit and hasn't finished ringing. Then I'd have said the heron, because I'm awake before the cutters and want to stand somewhere without moving. Neither is right. My knees hurt, so I am a person. I put my feet into the boots by the door, toes drained, still cold inside, and I shoulder the swollen door open the way you do, low and to the left, and I'm out on the moss and the two worn lines of brick.

Wet air. The pennywort takes my weight and gives it back. Above me the washing hangs in the dark like the shed skins of people, and the upper windows lean across the lane to whisper to each other. The houses all tilt toward the canal, and at night I can believe they are listening for the same thing I am.

I'm sixty-eight. I know that because my knees say it and because I have lost the top of my hearing, the birdsong and the whine and the bright edge of a child's shout. What I have left is the low end, which comes in through the soles and the breastbone. I keep the switch room, and I am called Kettle by everyone on the Lock. There's a sign for it, a finger circled by the ear and then a lid lifted. My mother named me Odile, and only Tamsin Oke still says so, and only when she's cross.

The switch room is at the back of the hall, up four iron steps slick with the sweat of the pipes. Inside it is dry. It's the only dry room on the Lock, kept dry by a small stove I am not supposed to run as hot as I do, and the walls are lined with the grey cabinets where the current comes in from the far country and is taught to behave. The transformers hum in a note that never wanders. It is almost pure. To the crew that hum is the quietest sound there is. They come in with their ears ringing from the hall and stand against my wall for a minute with their eyes shut. I don't speak to them. That's what the room is for.

I have a chair, a lamp, a book with a broken spine, and a kettle on the stove. I do not need to hear any of it. I set the cup on the desk and pour, and I watch the tea.

Nobody has ever asked how I know what I know, and I would not know how to say. But a cup of tea on a desk in a room that shakes tells you everything, if you've looked at it for forty years. The surface has a pattern. When the three pumps run together, the ripples cross, and the crossing makes a slow lattice that swims and reforms. Old Meg, the heavy one, sets the long slow swell. The Sister makes the quick fine grain on top of it. The Bishop, who is always a little out of step and always has been, drops a stutter into the lattice every ninth beat or so, and I have grown fond of it. It's the sound of the Bishop clearing his throat.

Tonight the lattice is right, and the stutter is where it should be. So what woke me?

I sit with the cup between my hands and let my chest do the listening. The transformers are level. The kettle ticks as it cools. Somewhere past the wall the outfall is roaring into the canal, the big brown roar that carries the bowl's whole night of rain up and over the river's shoulder. I have never seen the river from the height it stands at, not properly. I've seen it from the upper walkways, a slow brown ridge above the rooftops, and each time I felt what everyone feels, which is not fear exactly. It's a kind of manners. You do not turn your back on a guest that large.

Then the lattice shivers.

It is not a stop, only a flinch. The current sags, half a second, less, somewhere far up the line where I cannot see it, and the lamp goes the color of weak tea. The rings on my cup collapse into one wide slack circle. In the hall, through two walls and forty years, I feel all three pumps take a breath they have not been given, and the bell on its pulley gives one small unprompted note.

Every sleeper on the Lock must have come half awake in that half second. I think about them in their beds, sixty of them, in the lean rows: the cutters with their twitching arms, the wall crew who say nothing, the teacher, the cook, the children with their first pale crescent scars. For one held moment the whole settlement lies listening to the water think about coming in.

Then the current comes back. The lamp warms. The lattice crosses and re-forms, and the Bishop clears his throat on the ninth beat as though nothing had happened.

I let my breath out. My hand has gone flat on the desk without my telling it to, and it presses down, slowly, the sign I've made all my life. *The water rising.* *Be patient.* I have never once, in all the years of making it, been able to tell which I meant.

I take the ledger from its shelf. It goes back to the steam days, in a dozen hands, some of them in inks that have gone the color of rust. I find the last line and write under it: *03:40. Sag on the incoming, under a second. All three held. Bishop late, as usual.* I sign it with the small circle-and-lid, since that's who I am here.

Later, when the cutters come out at gray light with their machetes and the near end of the bank has already begun to close, someone will ask me at the door if I felt it. I'll say yes. She'll nod, because she felt it too and only wants to hear it said, and then she'll do the thing with her shoulder, the slow lift and the tilt of the head toward the river. *Well. It'll be there tomorrow, and so will we.*

I pour a second cup and watch it shiver in its ordinary way. Outside, the heron will be arriving on the outfall wall by now, folding itself into its stillness. Whether it is the same heron or one of a dozen, I have never needed to know.

The pumps go on. I sit in the one dry room on the Lock with my hands around the warm tea, and I do not sleep, and I do not mind. I am the one who wakes when it changes, so that the others can sleep while it doesn't.
