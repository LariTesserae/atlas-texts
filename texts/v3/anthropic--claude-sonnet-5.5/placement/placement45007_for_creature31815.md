I come down the ramp with my hand on the wall, which is a mistake. The gypsum flakes off under my palm and the shale has an edge on it. By the third turn I have a thin line across the pad of my thumb, and it doesn't bleed much, because nothing here does anything wet for long. At the bottom I sit on a slab in blue shade and wait for the cut to close, and I realize I am not a visitor. I'm only late.

I wear the pale stiff cloth. The cuffs rasp. My boots are lampstalk-bark over iron, the one new thing I own, and the collar tag against my throat is warm from me. I don't need to look at it. I know its rhythm the way I know the inside of my mouth: two short, a held one, a short. Nobody has thrummed it in a long time.

---

I start the day as a cutter. Before the mirrors wake I'm in the channel with the long curved knife, taking the litter back from the rock in slow strokes, and the mat parts with a dry zip, a smell of cork and pepper. Behind me the street is already trying to close. You learn to feel the lane through your soles, the way the stone is cooler than the fiber. The old cutters can do it blind. I can do it for about forty meters before I start to guess.

Then the first heliostat swings, and the first disc of white light slides down the wall, and I stop. My right hand comes up, palm out, toward the rim. The wrist lifts and nothing else. All along the street the others are doing it too, a stoker with a scoop in her other hand, a boy with a runner in his collar. Nobody looks at anybody. I don't know what I'm showing. I've been told four things and I hold all of them loosely.

Then the hand comes down, and the boilers light from the rim inward, one after another, each a low pulse in the mat, and I go back to cutting.

---

By noon I'm not a cutter. I don't know when it changed. I'm walking the long way through the shade, around a disc of light that would cost me half a cup, and my feet are making no sound, and I'm thinking about the ground rather than the street. The knife has become something I carry rather than something I use. I stop at a lampstalk as thick as a cistern, lay my palm flat on the rib, and wait.

It's humming, as it always is. There are the leaves above tracking the light, the tiny tick of resin cooling, and further down the sap moving in its slow tide. Beneath that is a deeper thing I don't have a word for. The tuggers would call it slack. I would call it the floor of the sound.

That's when I know. I'm a root-listener.

---

The warren mouth is behind the third boiler-house, where the litter-runners have worn a passage and the children have widened it. I go down on my elbows and knees. The mat closes overhead like the inside of a mouth. It's warm and close and smells sweet and burnt, and the fibers whisper against my sleeves. My tag scrapes rock. I go until there is no more fiber, only bare stone, cool and faintly gritty, and I turn my head and put my ear to it.

The roots are working. This is the sound everyone knows about: the tick, the creak of a ship's timbers, the patient grain of a lampstalk prying a hairline crack a hair wider. I can tell you which wall will shed next. I can tell you by the sound whether it will go in a season or a year. That's my trade, and I'm good at it, and the city moves its stacks on what I say.

I lie in the dark and I do not cry, because I can't, and I let the count go on.

---

There is something else under the roots. I'll say it once.

It isn't loud. It's slower than the roots, and more even. It comes in a pattern I've been learning for eleven years, the way you learn a language by living next door to it. Two short, a held one, a short.

I want to be careful here. I know what I've lost, and I know how the mind hunts for the shape it's missing. The old root-listeners, when I was an apprentice, told me that grief plays tricks on the ear, that the rock gives back whatever you bring to it. I believed them. I still believe them on most days.

But the roots don't hold. And the interval between the held one and the short isn't mine. It's longer than the way he did it, more patient, as though whoever is thrumming has had a very long time to practice. As though they learned it from a cable, a cable that carried it out across the lawns and up the trunks, and down.

Or the other way around. That's the part I keep turning over in the dark. The cable remembers, they say. They never say where it learned it.

---

When I climb out it's evening. The mirrors are folding, one by one, and the pit goes blue and then bluer. The tick-moths lift in their slow clouds and the families sit under their nets, holding still together, and I stand at the mouth of the warren with dust in my hair and watch them glitter. Somewhere a piston sighs. Somewhere a long thrum runs down a line, and every leaf along it trembles at once.

I put my palm on the nearest cable and I don't tug. I only listen, with the thick skin at the base of my fingers, with the nerves that have gotten fine over forty years.

Someone else is on the line. Someone always is. I lean my weight into it and I wait for the pause where the talking goes, and I think about the first of the mornings when I'll go up to the wall and hold my hand out to the rim, and it won't be for the reasons I was told. It'll be because I have started to suspect that whoever opened the pit is not above.

I don't say it twice. I lower my hand. The lawns are turning green.
