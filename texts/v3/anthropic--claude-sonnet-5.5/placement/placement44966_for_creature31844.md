I would like to say I'm a singer. There is something restful in it: a year on one handspan of wall, the lack coming up through six feet, the hind leg lifting without a decision. I've spent a lot of my life deciding things, and the idea of a note that was always going to be mine is nearly unbearable in its appeal.

But when I try to picture my body on the wall, the picture won't hold. I keep looking at the neighbors' notes and hearing that they don't need me. The chord is whole. Whatever I am, I'm not the missing voice.

So I start at the seam instead. I come out of the north wall pale and wet, wing-cases folded like unopened letters, and I stand at the mouth of the crack, feeling. Everyone in this story who comes out of the seam knows where to go. The shoal opens, the hole is there, and the body walks toward it.

I feel for the hole and there isn't one.

That is the moment I recognize myself. I'm the surplus. I'm what the room makes when the room is already full, because the seam doesn't check whether it's needed. I stand there while the chord goes on around me, complete, humming in the soft plates of my unhardened shell, and I understand that I will not be received. Nobody is cruel about it. The chord simply has no seat for me, and I'm too new to have a note anyway. If I sang, I'd be a blur.

So I go quiet before I've ever been loud. I walk, the only purposeful walker, and the singers part for me the way they part for the newcomers, though they're parting from something different. They feel that the pattern is off by one, a hole where no hole should be. The chord bends around me like a hymn around a cough. I cross the room toward the shale slab, past the cairns and the pool with its single trembling ring, and I go in under the lintel.

The silence there has an edge. My ears, which are down in my forelegs, go dizzy with it. I sit in the clean sand and the chord drops to a tremor in the stone, and for the first time I can tell that I have a body of my own and that it isn't part of anything.

Six keepers are there, long-legged and translucent, with their antennae spread. They don't greet me. They aren't the kind of creatures who greet. But one of them shifts its folded legs by a fraction, and that is space. I understand it was made for me.

---

I'd be the seventh keeper, then. It isn't what I'd have chosen, and it's the only thing that fits.

I would be the youngest and clumsiest of them. My hind plate is still ridged, still capable of a song I'll never use, and I'd feel it ache like a word I keep not saying. It would take years to wear flat. In the meantime I would learn the work. I would learn to ease a husk from the wall without disturbing the neighbors gripping on either side, and to carry it in my jaws to the crust, and to press it in and watch the glow brighten for a day where the microbes take it. I'd learn that this is a kind of singing too, and that the chord depends on it: the dead are not left, and the room is not allowed to keep what it has finished with.

I'd learn the cairns. Three stones, always three. I'd add and remove them for a long time before I noticed that I knew the pattern without being able to say what it counted. I have the feeling it counts something the chord can't count from inside. Maybe it counts the keepers. Maybe it counts how many are needed to hold one silence.

And there is a seventh niche I don't have. That's the part I keep returning to. The six stones are set in the north wall, and each rings a note of the chord's skeleton, and none of them is mine. There is no seventh note. If I made a stone that rang like the others, I'd only be repeating the chord in a smaller voice, and I've already been that once and been quietly declined.

So I would look for a different stone. Not quartz. Something from the raw walls, the fractured limestone that hasn't decided anything, or a fragment of shale from the edge of the slab. I would hold it against my flat plate, the motion of singing with the sound taken out of it, and rub. It would take a decade. Limestone doesn't take a shine the way quartz does. It would go dull-smooth, chalky, and when I tapped it, it wouldn't ring. It would give a short, dead, unfinished tick, like a pebble dropped on a floor that hasn't been built.

I think that would be my place. I wouldn't be the note that completes the chord. I'd be the stone that doesn't yet have a note in it, kept in the seventh niche by someone who was never given a gap to fill, and who therefore is the only one in the room who can hold something incomplete without needing it to resolve.

---

I know what the six do, lifting their antennae together in the hollow and holding them still for a long time. Before I'd been there a season I'd have my own antennae up, and I wouldn't yet have heard what they hear. I'd be the one among them who half-believes it's only the stillness.

But I would keep listening, because I'm the one who came out of the seam with nowhere to go. And one day, if the water finds its channel, and the first real groove opens, and the chord bends to a new note through six thousand pairs of feet, I think the stone in my niche would ring. The tick would become a pitch, the pitch the chord had no place for until then. The singers would find it through their legs and move half a step toward it, not knowing why.

It wouldn't be much. It would be one dull stone sounding for the first time, an hour or a year after everyone else had already changed. But it would tell the room that something had been kept for this, that the surplus had been a reservation rather than an error, and that the seam knew what it was doing when it made one more body than the chord could use.

Until then I'd sit in the sand, unsung and unfinished, wearing my plate flat, turning the stone in my forelegs, and listening to the room hold its breath through the wall.
