I begin without a where. There is only a cold that is getting less cold, and a glitter all around me, and a sound I feel before I have anything to hear it with.

It is the turnover. The surface has chilled and sunk, and the sediment is up in a haze that catches light from somewhere above. I can see twenty meters, maybe less. The pulse comes through the haze muffled, like a heart through a wall. I am very small, a larva no longer than a grain of the pyrite that falls past me, and I am not swimming so much as being carried down the long slow slide of the overturn. I pass the basin wall. Its fractures are so fresh they still have edges, and orange mats spread across it in tongues, following water I can't feel.

At first I think I might be one of the things I pass. The shoal swings by overhead and turns all at once, a wall of foil folding, and for a moment I want to be inside that turn, a hand-long fish among ten thousand, carried by my neighbors' certainty. But I have no neighbors yet. Then the bell-grazers, hanging in their warm layer like the parts of a slow clock. I want their indifference, to tilt into the warm, rise, tip out, sink, and never once ask where I am. But they are not what the water is carrying me to become.

What I am is a listener. I have no ears, so I listen with my whole length. The beat comes up through the haze in layers: a deep thock, a thock-thock, a wet bang. Under them is something slower, a swell of many flaps drifting into step and out again. It is not the beat my parents settled into. Chimneys have cracked since then, and new plates have grown, and the phrase I am hearing has never been played before. I have to learn it from nothing.

So I drift down through it and try surfaces, and each one has an answer.

The first chimney is too loud. Its flap fires close, almost on top of me, and a scald of hot fluid rolls past. I go sideways on the pressure, tumbling, with the taste of iron in whatever I taste with. Too close. The larvae that settle there are cooked, and I am not going to be one of them.

The second is too far. The sound thins to a tick at the edge of hearing, and I can feel the hunger in it, a slope with too little film and too few pulses to keep the food coming. I turn back, and the turning is the first thing I have ever done that felt like mine.

The third is the color of old blood, about the girth of a barn, warm all the way up its flank. Its flap is high above me. When it fires, the clack arrives a moment before the push of water, and in that gap I feel the word *enough*. It is not a thought. It is my body finding that the beat matches something already in it. Below the flap the water runs warm and thick with bacteria, in the band where fresh fluid arrives every few seconds and never quite stops.

I let go and touch stone. I cement myself to it, and the stone is warm.

I secrete a tube, and the tube is me. Days pass in a rhythm that doesn't have days. My plume opens, a small red flame at the top of a pale thread, and I feed on the film that the chimney's own breath keeps thick. Above me the flap builds pressure, lifts, and fires, and before I can decide anything, I am already inside my tube.

I did not flinch first. The slope flinched, ten thousand tubes withdrawing in a wave that came up through the rock from below, and I went with it a fraction of a second behind. That is how I learn. I am not the one who knows the machine. The colony knows it, and I am one place where the knowing lives. My retraction is one stroke in a very large gesture. When the scald rolls over the crown and finds only stone and closed tubes, I am not afraid. I am a part of the reason it finds nothing.

After that "I" gets harder to say. The fur thickens as new larvae settle beside me, cementing themselves to the same warmth, and the tubes pack into a skin. In the long weeks after the haze clears, I notice the beat above me slowing by a hair. The crown is wrapped in tube-mass now, holding its heat differently, and the pressure takes a little longer to build. The flap that made me is now made a little by us. None of us decided this. It is just what happens when you press your body against a machine for long enough: the machine learns your shape.

On some day I don't count, a long one comes through.

I feel it before I see it, as a change in the water, a slow pressure. Then the blue bands are there, ringed along a body longer than the chimney is wide, pulsing in a pattern that is not the field's pattern but moves slowly against it. It brushes the stone as it goes, as if asking the warmth to count it. And I flinch. But it is not the flap's flinch. It starts at the base of the slope where the long one touches, and it runs up, colony-wide, tracing the line of the passing body. The whole slope draws the path of the animal on itself, and I am one stroke in the line.

For a moment I understand something I cannot hold. That thing is crossing the entire field. It hears the hot southern rows and the cold edge of the terraces at once. It carries something between colonies that the beat alone can't, and it reads us as it goes, a finger drawn across a surface. I hear one chimney. I know one flap. But I am part of what it hears, and the tiny fact of my flinch, arriving in the right place a fraction late, is a note in a piece I will never hear whole.

Then it is gone. The tubes slowly open again. The film is thick, the flap builds, and the beat comes down the water, the phrase I learned from nothing and now know.

In a few years my tube will crack, or the flap will, or the whole slope will be a crust on the terrace, one more layer of the plate. The beat will have moved on and the animals will have followed it, without deciding to. I am not sad about this. I was never the one keeping time. I was only, for a while, a place where the pulse was felt, and the feeling was enough.
