I come down the argument between the staircase and the landslide with my eyes shut for the last hundred meters, because the light has stopped being something I see and become something I carry. A woman at the bottom takes my wrist without a word and puts a strip of dark reed over my face. Through the slit the world comes back in pieces: a causeway, a fog bank leaning on a terrace, a wall the color of a tooth with one long scar of notches down it.

I have none. I know it before anyone signs it to me. Nobody flinches at a stranger, but they look at my hands and see the trouble there. They are empty and unmarked, and they hang at the wrong height.

So the first thing I am here is *unfinished*, and I find I don't mind it. I have always suspected that being unfinished was my ordinary condition, and it is restful to arrive somewhere that has a word for it and a remedy.

The remedy is a trowel and a stranger's lintel. The lime is wet and heavy and smells of burned shells and cold. I lift it wrong. A wallwright, old, with a face cracked like a dry pan, takes my elbow and moves it four inches. Nothing else is said. By the end of the afternoon my forearms shake and a lintel that was failing holds, and she cuts my first notch into the wall of a house I will never live in. One upright stroke, one small tick at the top.

I touch it. It's the smallest thing I have ever owned, and it belongs to the wall.

---

I want to be a poleman. I say this in signs, badly, low and curled, and the wallwright gently lifts my hands to shoulder height, which is how I learn I have said it in a mumble. I say it again with my palms open. She considers me behind her visor for a long time, then shakes her head, not *no* but *not yet*, and points me to the back of a line instead.

The back of the line is where the second walker goes, and the third, and every one after, who carry no knowledge of their own. The poleman at the front taps and listens and turns her palm: *the ground ahead holds.* The next person repeats the sign. So does the next. By the time it reaches me it has passed through six pairs of hands and a hundred meters of fog, and I make it a seventh time, chest-high and flat, to the woman behind me, who has never seen the ground the poleman heard.

It takes me weeks to understand what I am doing. I am not reading the ground. I am *vouching*. Someone else's tap becomes my sign, and my sign becomes the weight of another person's foot. The whole line rests on a chain of people saying *it holds* about something they did not test, and every one of them means it. Nobody is lying. But the sign gets thinner with every hand, and the only thing that keeps it honest is that each person makes it as though they had tapped the stone themselves.

I make the sign large. I make it slowly. I don't make it at all if I didn't see the hand in front of me clearly, and once I stand in the fog with my palm raised and nothing to repeat, and the line stops behind me, all of them waiting, and no one is angry. A pause is permitted. The pause is the courtesy.

---

I do fail, though not in the way I feared.

It is a gray morning, the fog thick as felt, and the sign comes back to me from the front a little low, a little curled, the poleman tired or the sleeve of her cowl in the way. I could read it two ways: *holds*, or *holds, but.* I have half a second and I choose the cleaner one, because the cleaner one is what the line wants and I want to be someone who is easy to follow.

The woman behind me steps where I signaled and her foot goes through the crust to the knee. It's only clay under the salt, wet and cold, and she comes out of it with nothing worse than a ruined boot and a look I can't read through the visor. No one shouts. Of course no one shouts. Someone's pole comes down against the stone, three taps and a fourth, *stop here*, and the line settles on the causeway like birds on a wire.

What I feel is worse than being scolded. I keep waiting for the scolding, and it doesn't arrive. That evening the old wallwright hands me a trowel and walks me out to the oldest district, where the causeways stand a body-height over the sunken floor, and points at a wall that has been failing for three years. Its notches run out halfway up. Above them there is only the smooth plaster of a house nobody trusts.

I understand that it is the hardest wall in the eleven cities, and that I am being given it. I still don't know whether it is a penalty or a kindness. I've stopped believing the question has to be answered before the work begins. I lift the lime. I cut nothing that first night. I only mend until the shell wash sets, and when I look up, the glass snails have come on along the lower stones, the whole wall breathing its faint light, and my forearms are shaking, and I am not sorry to be here.

---

Something else I learn. I am a creature who wants to speak. It is not that I have much to say. Speaking is how I find out what I think, how I set my weight down in the world, and here the setting-down is done with the heel first, and then a beat of listening. I've been slow to give up the habit. Twice I nearly say something out loud, and both times the muscles in my throat close of their own accord, as though the basin had reached in and held them. It turns out to be less a prohibition than a discipline of attention. Before the hands move, you have to know what you actually saw.

The hum is different. The hum I am allowed. Late one night I sit on a threshold that isn't mine and shape the one held note, the breath-length, *I am here and I am not afraid*, and I am astonished at how much I mean it. I don't say it to anyone. I say it to the wall. It goes into the plaster and I feel it come back through the seat of my body, a small answering pressure, and I cannot tell whether that is the stone or only me.

---

Months in, or seasons, or some number the tally wall would know better than I do, the tone comes.

It is a still hour after the fog has burned off, the light flat and total, every face of every terrace throwing the sun into every other. I am on a causeway with a basket of shell. I feel it first in my soles, as everyone has always told me I would, a slow pressure moving from the rim toward the center, patient and enormous, not a sound at all.

Everyone stops. The wallwright lowers her trowel. A poleman plants his pole. A woman halts in a doorway with a child on her hip. I stop with my basket half-lifted and let it down, and bow my head, and close my eyes behind the reed.

I don't know what is being counted. I have asked, in signs, and the old say notches, and the young say days or the dead or the water itself, and the quiet ones who walk to the center pans make the sign with the palms turned down and do not explain. I find that I don't need it explained. I stand in the pressure of it, and I think that if the basin is counting, then somewhere in the sum is a small upright stroke with a tick at the top, cut on a stranger's wall by a stranger's hand, and it weighs what it weighs. It is neither larger nor smaller than it is. That seems to be all the ground asks, that a thing be counted at its true size.

The tone passes on toward the center. I open my eyes. The light is unbearable and clean.

Then I pick up the basket, and I set my heel down, and I wait for the ground to answer.

Ahead of me the poleman turns her palm, flat, at shoulder height, against the pale wall of the fog. I take the sign from her and make it larger than it needs to be, and hand it back to the one behind me.

*The ground holds.*

I mean it. I have tapped the stone myself, in my way. Tomorrow someone will need a lintel lifted, and I'll be out before the fog with lime on my back.
