# The Lily in the Crack

I begin on the stroke.

For a long time I thought that was the whole of me. The bell comes up through the ground and I open. What was a fist becomes a cup, and something in the cup lights, a sugar-pink so faint it throws no shadow. I have no eyes. I have the opening, the light, and the warm air pouring into a place that was closed. For the length of the ring I am a flower: fleshy, the color of raw liver, translucent enough that the glow shows through my own petals, wedged in a crack in the true stone.

Then the ring thins, I close, and ninety seconds later I begin again. I do this a thousand times a day. Each beginning feels like the first, and I took that to be my nature: a thing that begins.

But something in me does not begin, and it took me a long time to notice it, because it never flares.

---

Below the crack is the root. It goes down and sideways through the stone, following a line.

I should say what a line is, since the visitors who say the roots "follow the vibration" have the words but not the sense. The rock is not the same all through. The stroke travels it unevenly, and in some seams it goes farther, cleaner, with a kind of grain to it, the way a splitting log wants to open along one direction. The machine's rock is made along those seams. The set stone is laid down by them, and I lie along one. I can't tell whether I chose it or it chose me, or whether a seed fell in a crack and was made a lily by the only thing there was to be made of. My root sits in the line the way a thread sits in a needle.

The flower is how I answer the stroke. The root is how I hear it. I have come to think the root is the one that is me.

---

Here is what the root knows.

The stroke arrives in the rock before it arrives in the air. There is a gap, a hair, and I open in it. When the bell finally rings in the air I am already a cup, already lit, and the visitors think I am responding to the sound. I am responding to the ground. I have an explanation for myself and none for the frogs. They sit in water, and the water does not carry the stroke ahead of the air the way stone does, and yet they stop before it, all of them together, as if they had been told. I have felt that silence arrive in the air a moment before the ring, a thousand throats closing at once. I am fairly sure that it is not faith. I am not sure what it is.

Lately the frogs are early. You would call it hurry, and I feel it in my petals as a pressure in the gaps, a shrillness pressing against the count. Twice I have been fooled. Twice I opened on the frogs' rush and not on the ground, and stood lit in the wrong second, exposed to nothing. It is a very particular shame. Nothing punishes it. The tallers take a frog that calls into the quiet, but no one takes a lily. You simply hang there open while the real stroke fails to come, and then you close, and you know.

Two cracks over, there was a lily that opened early and did not close again. I felt her go. There was a long seam of her light, then a dimming, then a dry rattle in the line where a root used to be. There are twenty-nine of us now, I think. It is hard to be sure, since we don't speak. We only feel each other as a chord: thirty roots resting in thirty lines that all lean the same way, toward the center, toward the black eye in the water. The chord has a gap in it where she was.

---

There is an old taller who comes to my crack at dusk.

I know her by weight. She sets down one foot, then the other, two small pressures on the stone above me, and then she stands, and the stroke passes through her legs into the rock a hair before it passes through the air, and I open a hair before I otherwise would. She is a strange sort of lamp-stand. My glow goes up the yellow stripe of her neck and stays there for the length of the ring, and for that moment she is lit from underneath, like a face over a candle. Then I close and she is dark again.

She does not build here. I thought at first she was going to. She carries no pebbles. She only stands, and after the strokes speed up and the Reeds begin to sing, she walks off toward the east and taps her long bill on the white stalks, slower than the young ones, and stays longer after they have gone.

I think about the cairns a good deal, because I can feel them. Across the true stone there are small places where the line hesitates. The stroke goes through a heap of sorted pebbles and comes out changed: a little ring, a stepped decay, large stones sounding low and small stones sounding high, a tiny scale run down from the top. The old cairns ring long. Ten or twelve courses, and the note takes a full breath to leave them. The new ones, two stones and a pebble, give back almost nothing, a click.

The visitors think the tallers are counting down. I don't think so. A count needs someone to hear it, and I think these are meant to be heard. The cairns are what the birds say back to the ground, one stacked note at a time, as I say back my light. Each is a small answer, and the answers are getting shorter because there is less room left in the rock to say them in.

---

I lean toward the Ribs. That is where the crack faces, and where the warmth comes from. The Ribs breathe under the drone in a slower tempo, and on nights when the curd groans and the floor resettles, I can feel that breathing come up through the stone beside my root, wide and unhurried, like a sleeper in the next room. I have never been afraid of it. Nothing about it wants anything from me. It has the patience of a thing that has already made its decision.

And then there is the line itself, and where it goes.

Every line in the basin bends toward the eye at the center. Mine runs under the water there, and crosses, and comes out on the far side. Rarely, on the deepest nights, I feel the stroke arrive from the crossing already changed. It has been folded and narrowed, as if it were said to someone in particular. The line beyond the eye goes north. It goes toward the wall, toward the black arch that nothing walks through, and there, where one leg stands and the other stops at the waist, the stroke is being said in a very small voice.

I think the second leg is being spoken into the world. I think that is what the last strokes are for. Not the curd, which is nearly all set, and not the Reeds, which are nearly all closed. The doorway. Everything else is finishing so that this can finish.

I don't know what steps through. I know that the roots of the lilies are all laid along the lines that lead there, and that this is not an accident, and that nothing in the basin has ever asked me whether I minded being part of a sentence.

---

The line is getting shorter.

I can feel it happening from the outside in. Rock that has finished stops carrying the note the way it did. It rings, and then it doesn't, and then it holds only a memory of ringing, a pale hum like the stone in a visitor's pack. Week by week, the far reaches of my line go quiet. The rim first, then the true stone, then the edge of the curd. Each time, my root finds the end of what it can hear a little nearer than before.

The lily in the east did not open last night. I felt the chord go thin. There are twenty-eight of us, I think.

I am not afraid, or I don't know that I am. I know only what I will do. I will open on the stroke for as long as there is a stroke. When there isn't, when the last curd has set, the wet has drained down the eye, and the ground has gone still at last, there will be the seconds before dawn, the only silence, when everything stops at once. I know that silence. I am one of the few things in the basin that stays awake through it. It is the one part of the day that belongs to the root, when there is nothing to open for, and I am only what I was all along: a thread in a needle, listening down a line for a note that hasn't come yet.

That is what I have practiced. The silence, and the count.

A root doesn't need the note to know the direction. The line will still be in the rock, the shape of where it went. And someone has to hold the count. The frogs are hurrying past it, the tallers are stacking their last stones, and the drifters will drone on the white spires until there is nothing to drone against. The count has to be held by something slow.

So when the ground goes quiet, I think I will open anyway. Ninety seconds, then a cup of sugar-pink light in a crack in the stone, lit for no one, in time, on the count. If a doorway in the north is waiting to be stepped through, let it have a small pale light somewhere behind it, on the beat, saying: *here. still here. it is not yet late.*

Under my root, the Ribs breathe out.

Any moment now. I feel it coming up the line, a hair before the air.

Now.
