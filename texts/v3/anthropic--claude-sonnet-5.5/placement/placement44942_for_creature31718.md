I find myself on a crest, barefoot, with my palm flat against the skin of a drum I cannot hear.

That's where I start, anyway. Later I'll learn the start was a kind of hiding. For now there is the hide under my hand, damp and faintly greasy, and the ground coming up through my soles, and the roar around me so total it has stopped being sound and become weather. I am a drummer of the Held. My name is Ninth-after-the-great-column, four hundred and some cycles gone, and my gesture is a thumb bent back to touch the wrist. My mother chose it because I was born double-jointed and furious.

I am old now, or the Held's version of it, which means I can no longer hear a shout at arm's length. I don't mourn that. The ear that goes deaf teaches the shins and the breastbone to listen, and that is what I do all day. I stand on the crest and feel the whole Basin as one body. The vent at the heart draws its breath. A mud pot heaves in the south terraces. The wallowers shift their bass a half-tone as some great cow rolls over in the warm. And under all of it comes the answering thud from the next crest, the next drummer, a pulse I have known for sixty years the way you know a sibling's footstep.

I strike. The pattern goes out through the rock. Below me, the sluice-keepers ease their gates for the change of work, the mat-tenders straighten with their blades, and children in the lanes stop and lift their heads without knowing they have done it. I have never missed a beat. Nobody has. I say this the way everyone says it, as a drum of its own, a small comfort struck again and again.

*

The light is pearl and shadowless. The steam sits low, and the sun is a pale coin in it. From the crest I see the roofs going down in tiers, chalk-white and beaded, houses leaning on houses until the city looks like a terrace formation that happens to have doors. Smoke and steam are hard to tell apart. I see hands. That is what a city of the Held looks like from above: a restless flicker on every stair and in every lane, forty people at a sluice all speaking at once and all understood. Nobody down there knows I'm watching. I love them with a fierceness that has no signal in the hand-tongue. The nearest is a cupping of the palms, and that means *careful, careful,* which isn't the same thing.

*

I said I was hiding. Here is what from.

In the innermost chamber of a house on the third tier, under felt, in the held breath, my teacher is dying. She taught me the long chains. She taught me to stop listening with my ears. She is so old that her skin has gone from flushed to translucent, as though the water has finally begun to pass through her. Nobody has said that she is dying, because there is no gesture that doesn't sound like a small crack in the perfect steady world.

I went down to her yesterday between beats. I tapped on the lintel, asking, and was let in. The dark was thick and close and smelled of iron and mat-oil. She was awake. She spoke aloud, in the murmur, which is the sacred thing, and I had to put my ear almost against her mouth. My ear was the wrong one, the deaf one, so I turned and gave her the other.

She said, "You'll want to leave the drum."

I said nothing. The inner room is for speaking, but I had nothing.

"You'll want to carry me," she said. "Don't be a fool about it. You have an apprentice."

*

The rule is older than any of us: the drum is not left. But the rule is silent on the rim. The dead go up. Someone carries them, and the someone is always kin or near to it, and carries them out of the pressing warmth where the balance holds, into the cooler air where things seem to be waiting to happen. A drummer has never been the carrier, because a drummer cannot be spared. So in the whole memory of the drums no drummer has stood on the rim. I would be the first to hear what a long silence is.

That's what I'm afraid of. Not her death, which I've been mourning for two years in the hands, in silence. I'm afraid of the quiet.

*

So I put the question to the ground, as I've done all my life. I stand on the crest, wait for the next beat from the far crest, and feel for the answer.

What comes up is unexpected. Beneath the drums, beneath the vent and the wallowers and the million small bodies of the frogs, there is one more pulse, very slow and very regular, that I have felt all my life and never counted. It is not the Basin's. It isn't on the schedule of the vent. It has been getting slower. Not much. A hair's breadth per year, a thing no drum could record. It is in my own soles, in my own chest, and I have been treating it as the ground's.

It is me. It is the one drum that isn't kept.

I stand there with my palm on the hide and my heart going, and the thought that comes is very plain and has no gesture. The city does not change, and I have been changing the whole time. My teacher, the child in the lane, the drummer on the next crest, each of us is a line drawn through a place that is a circle. We were the only ones who ever moved, and we spent our lives keeping time for the ones who couldn't.

*

I'll go. I've decided as I write this, which is to say I have decided in the hands, and my hands have already begun to move. I will put the apprentice at the drum. She is fourteen, all elbows, with a thumb that bends back to the wrist, and I will tell her *do not miss*, and I will know it's a lie the moment I make the sign, because someday she will. Someday one of us will be a half-beat late and the perfect steady world will hold anyway, because it was never as perfect as we needed it to be.

Then I'll go down to the third tier, tap on the lintel, and wait to be let in. I'll lift her, who weighs nothing now, and wrap her in felt, and carry her up the black glass ramp to where the noise thins hour by hour, and the light sharpens, and the air goes cool and clear. I'll feel the ground go still under my feet.

I'll hear it then, I think. Not with my ears. The pulse, mine, slow and steady, the only drum left. I'll stand at the rim and open my mouth and make the sound we make nowhere else, and it will go out over the cliff and be heard by no one.

Then I'll come down. I'll wash, and cup my hands together, and carry the water.
