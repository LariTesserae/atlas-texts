# The One Who Breathes Out

It is the thin phase, and I can hear every drip.

That is the first thing I know, before I know my own hands: water, one drop at a time, from a seam above my head onto the toe of my boot. Then another from farther off, onto a shelf of stone with a slightly hollower note. Four days ago the choir was so loud I felt my own teeth. Now the choristers hang in their clusters on the cables above the cut, throats slack, and the basin is nearly quiet. Somewhere a bat ticks. Somewhere a mite molts with a sound like a fingernail on silk.

My forehead is against the rock.

This is the work. The fault wall in the Fourth Cut is pale and raw, and it weeps, so my brow and my palms are wet and the cold has gone into the bones behind my eyes. My eyes are shut, and I keep them so, though I've wrapped the band across them anyway, out of habit. I am a Listener. I am listening for something I will almost certainly not hear.

Every Listener knows this. In three hundred years of Listeners, hardly any has felt the shudder before the choristers did. The animals know first, through the cables, through some sense we lack, and their silence arrives a heartbeat ahead of the floor. What we do is sit here anyway, with our faces to the mountain, so that when the silence comes, someone is already awake in the right way. Someone whose lungs are full.

---

I should say what I am, though it's hard to say in words. Words are for the outsiders, and the thing I am has a hum.

It is three notes. The first is low, the second falls away from it, and the third rises just slightly, like a question that decided not to ask. My mother chose it in my first month. She carried me down to the lowest cables at the hour of the deep drone, wrapped in two layers of oiled mat and one of wool, and hummed it up into the dark. The drone-hangers did not move. She hummed it again. Still nothing. My father tried a variation, and the family stood in the cold for an hour while I slept through it.

Then, near the end, one cluster far up the span shifted. It was barely a ripple of clay-colored bodies, like a breath drawn in a sleeper. That was enough. The name held.

I've always liked that it was so faint. Some children are named to a roar of answering pitch, and they grow up bold. I was named to a flicker, and I grew into someone who attends to flickers. I don't think that's an accident. I think the flicker is what the name was for.

---

Something has crawled into my collar. I can feel it: the tiny scrabbling comb-strokes of a mite, warm from feeding. Good luck. I let it be. Through the wet cloth at my neck I sense its green light, faint against my skin, and I think of the child I was, with a mite in a cup of damp cloth, learning what the city is: a thing that eats, glows, and does not mind being watched.

I have done my season in the drains. Everyone has. I remember the hook-rod's weight and the dark closing in, and the way a sump-eel slid past my calves like a cold thought. I remember clearing a blockage of shed shells so old they'd fused into a plate, and the sudden rush of released water, and the sound the whole terrace made above me, a long relieved gurgle, as though something had been holding its breath. I think that was when I knew I would end up here. I had liked that sound more than I liked anything else.

Now I sit at the opposite end of the same idea. The drain-walker frees the water. The Listener frees the silence, and at the right moment ends it.

---

There is a thread-worm in the wall by my temple.

I know because I can see it, or rather sense it: a small amber spark in the dark behind my eyelids, drifting a finger's width through the rock, then stopping, then going on. We are not supposed to be able to see the worms. But in the thin phase, when the mites dim and the world is still, a Listener with her face against the lattice sometimes catches one. The old Tenders argue about what they are. Some say the worms are the lattice, and some say the lattice is what the worms are dreaming.

I have my own opinion, which I've never hummed to anyone. I think the worm is listening too. I think it is doing exactly what I am doing, in exactly the same posture, one small warm thing pressed to the mountain's flank, waiting for the news. When I think that, I feel less alone at the fault wall, and I feel more like part of a very long line of ones who wait. I don't know whether that thought is true. It has the shape of a true thing, and I've learned that this is sometimes all a person gets.

---

The drip on my boot has stopped.

I notice its absence before I understand it. A second passes. The mite in my collar has gone still. Above me, on the cables, something has changed. I can't hear it. Not hearing it is exactly how I know: the whole basin is holding one breath, and that is not the ordinary thin-phase quiet, which is full of small sounds, but a stillness with nothing in it.

*Early*, I think. *They stopped early.*

Then the mountain moves.

It isn't violent. It is a hand's breadth of settling, a long slow grind deep in the rock, and somewhere far down the basin a terrace takes it, and the lattice groans and bends and finds its new balance. The wall against my forehead shivers once, like the skin of a horse. Water shakes loose from the seams in a fine cold rain. Every tension-line in the city rings a single note, low and long and out of tune with itself, and the note dies.

And then there is the silence.

It is enormous. People say they can hear the stone settling in it, and now I know they're right. I hear the rock finding its rest and the water thinking about where to go. I hear my own heart, and I hear, very faintly, the amber spark of the worm, though I know that can't be so.

Every Tender in the basin has stopped mid-stride, mid-word, mid-bite. It is not fear. It is courtesy. All of us are standing very still in the dark, listening with the animals and the walls and the water.

It is my breath now.

---

This is the part no one can teach. You cannot know when the silence has been long enough. If you start too soon, you cut it, and the city feels it as a small rudeness. If you start too late, the dread begins to gather, and people start to wonder whether it will end. The right moment is a place that is neither soon nor late. It is a kind of ripeness, and you find it by listening to the silence itself until it turns.

I wait. I count nothing. I feel the silence as a weight in my chest, and I feel the whole basin leaning on it, and then I feel a very small loosening, a give, like ice thinking about becoming water.

I breathe out.

The hum comes from low in my body, a deep note, deeper than my name, deeper than I am. It isn't mine, really. It belongs to whoever is doing this job. It goes into the rock through my forehead and out into the cut, and it says nothing except *here*, and *still*, and *the terraces are standing.*

For one moment it is alone.

Then, from the cable above me, a drone-hanger answers, a pressure more than a pitch. Then another, and a whole span of them, and the weavers pick up the melody in slow turning phrases, and the needle-choirs come in bright and thin over the top. The bats resume their ticking. Down the hill I hear hammers on the junction stones, pings in a scattered rhythm as people begin to move again. Somewhere a child laughs in a fast breathy stutter that sounds exactly like an eel, and someone laughs back.

The roar comes up around me like water rising in a cistern.

---

I stay a little longer with my forehead to the stone. My knees ache. The mite in my collar has resumed its combing. The thread-worm is gone from behind my eyes, or I've stopped being able to see it.

In a while someone will come to relieve me, and I will climb the stepped streets past the haulers holding their patient ground in the gutters, and the lanterns of the lower terraces will pour green light across my boots. I'll find the warm wall where my row sleeps, and someone's back will already be there, and I'll settle against it without waking them, and we will shift together in the dark the way the sleepers do. Tomorrow the weight of that silence will still be with me, as it is for everyone, a small stone carried in the chest for a day.

I have been asked what a Listener is for, if the animals always know first. I have never found a better answer than this. Someone must be the comma. Someone must be the breath between two parts of the sentence, so that the sentence knows it can go on. The city says itself in its thousand voices, and now and then the mountain interrupts, and I am the one who says, softly, into the stone: *continue.*

It's a small thing. It's the smallest part of the speaking. But I think a sentence without its breaths would not be a sentence, and I think the mountain, which is still rising, still weeping, still unworn, is glad of them.

I hum my name once, quietly, for no one, and go up.
