# The Tarnish in the Water

I am the fish behind the low-pressure cylinder, hung in the slot between a cross-girder and the condenser bank. The crawlers do not notice me and neither do the worms. A calf once mistook me for a shadow and tried to shelter in me. I am thin as a hand and clear as a cold breath, and I have been here longer than the rust has been red.

I don't swim in the beat, because I don't swim. I hang. What moves is my heart.

It is small and bright, and it beats once every ten seconds. The bell strikes every seven. Nobody else on the shelf is built this way. The drifters' hearts fall on the stroke, and the hunters' do too, and the crawlers are still and then not still in the same seven seconds, as if the iron were breathing through them. Mine keeps its own count, and so it slides. Every seventy seconds the bell and my heart strike together, and for that one instant I am like everything else, exactly on time. Then my heart slips behind the bell by three counts. Then by six. Then it comes round ahead by two, then five, then one, then four, and then there is the bell again, on my heartbeat, and the sliding starts over.

I have passed through every phase a drifter can be in. I have been the tired one and the sick one, the frightened one running ahead of the herd, the courting male holding a dangerous half-beat off. I have been, for one stroke, a calf's particular lag. None of it means anything to me. It happens in me the way tides would happen in a stone. But it is how I know time has passed. I have no herd to read and no season to lose, only the seventy-second turning, the bell going past my heart like a slow wheel past a slower one, over and over.

---

The hall is warm in the way that a hand is warm to a cold cup. Under the iron ribs of the roof, where the plates have opened, the ice-light comes down in soft blue and never moves. The piston falls. The note goes through the plate, the water and my small bones. There is no bell and then there is nothing else, and I don't call it noise, because I have never had a word for the water I hang in.

The cylinder's flank is above me, and I feel it warm and cool as it cycles. The crawlers walk the gratings in their pulses of movement. The worms lean in their fur on the flywheel housing, and the wheel goes round, and I am not sure it has ever been otherwise.

A hunter sometimes lies outside the hall's great door, a long dark shape against the black glass, with its jaw on the rock. It feels the hall and cannot use it. Inside, the engine is so loud that every flaw is buried. A late tail, a lame one, a frightened one, all vanish into the iron. The hunter waits a day, or ten, and drifts off. I have watched this happen more times than there have been calves. I think the hall is the only place on the shelf where being wrong is not fatal, but I don't think the hall knows it.

---

There is a calf.

It came out of a female wedged among the valve rods, in the season when the warm outflow ran thick. I know the calves as they come, thin white curls no longer than a forearm, and I know which ones catch the beat. Most of them do within an hour, and the shiver of it goes through their small bodies like a stitch being pulled tight. This one did not. It hung and curled and uncurled, always a little behind the bell, by a tenth of a stroke, a hair, a breath.

Its mother looked for it. I watched her look. She moved her great pale length through the girders and read the crowd for the lag she had felt in her own body, and it was not there. It was there, but it was not where a lag should be. It was too late to be a name. On the third day she turned her tail to the iron, joined the others, and went out toward the herd. It was not cruelty. She could not find what she was looking for.

The calf stayed. It found the mineral scale on the condenser tubes and scraped at it with its wide small mouth. It grew a little, slowly. No hunter came. And after some days I noticed it had settled a body-length from me, in the slot between the girder and the pipe, where the water is a bit stiller and the bell is a bit less sharp.

I think it came because I am the only thing in the hall that is not in step. The water around me carries a small contrary flutter, my heart ticking out of time, and its skin, which had been listening for something to belong to, found the one thing that did not expect it to be on the beat.

---

I cannot feed it and I have no voice. I have only what I have always had.

This is what I can do. Every seventy seconds my heart falls exactly as late as the calf does. It happens on the third slide, after the bell, when I am behind by three counts, near enough to its lag that the two are the same. For the length of that one beat we are in step, the two most wrong things in the hall, wrong in the same way. Then I slide on. Then the bell comes round and I am right, and then I am wrong again, and then, at the third slide, I am wrong with the calf.

The calf feels it. I think it feels it. Its tail-tip flickers, a shade quicker, when the moment comes. It cannot have a name, since a name is something another reads, and there is nobody else reading. But I have been watching phases pass through my body for longer than the rust has been red, and I know what a lag is. I know this one. It is a small thing, a curl of white with an unreadable delay, and it is mine to know, once in seventy seconds, in the warm slot behind the cylinder.

The piston comes down. The bell goes through the iron and the water and the two of us, and it is the whole of the world. In the third slide after it, we are late together.
