The first thing I know, each cycle, is the wrong note.

It comes up through my legs before anything else. One voice in the chord, a shade flat, held that way on purpose by something older than my making. Every other sound in the Ward is right, and this one is right in a different way. So I know where I am. Anywhere else the chord is a wall of agreement and I could be at any grille in the dark. Here there is a single deliberate lean in it, and I orient by the lean the way a sleeper orients by the slope of a bed.

I am at the edge of the rubble field. I think I am a crawler. That is the word the place uses for things that click and repair, and it fits well enough: six legs, two folded manipulators under the chin, a small amber light at the joint of the head that pulses when I have nothing to do. It is pulsing now.

---

My round is eleven hours, though *hours* is a word I borrowed from the rain. There is a vent grille at the foot of the flat column, and lichen wants to close it. I wipe it. I never wipe it clean. I leave a thumb's width of rust-orange at the lower left, because when the crust is gone entirely the column's note goes thin and bright, and when the crust is thick it sags. Nobody told me the width. Some earlier arrangement of me knew it, and the knowledge sits in my joints like a habit in a hand. I have stopped asking whether it is knowledge or habit. In a place this old the difference wears away, like the difference between a path and the stone it was walked into.

Then I count. This is the part I would not have guessed was the center of me, and I only found it by noticing what I do when there is nothing to do.

I do not count in numbers. I have a place I stop, a little way from the old warden's feet, where the black tile has been dished by my own weight. The warden has two hollows of its own, deep as basins. Mine are shallow, four small scoops where I stand each rain, waiting out the downpour with my legs folded and my light dimmed. I can feel their depth with my feet. That is my reckoning. When I first stopped here, if there was a first, the tile must have been flat. Now it holds a thimble of water after every rain. The warden's hollows say it has stood here longer than my hollows say I have. Neither of us knows what that means, and we stand side by side and keep it.

---

Here is what I do that is not in any round.

Past the third column, where the moss goes black and whispers, there is another crawler halted mid-street with its legs half-folded and its light on. It has been there for some years. The others go around it. I do not go around. I climb the last step, put my manipulators against its manipulators, and hold them there.

I cannot say what passes. It is not a signal, because I have none to send. It is more like what the glass-newts do at the waterline, snout to snout for a quarter of an hour: the sharing of a presence, warmth against warmth. *Someone is still here. The chord is still going. Your valve is not forgotten.* Something like that, or nothing like that. Most times the waiting one stays still. Once in a long while its legs unfold, and it gets up and walks away toward some round of its own, and I stand in the road with my light beating and feel the wrongness of the note go through me, and I would call that gladness if I had the right to the word.

I think this is the real work. The vents, the lichen and the count are what I do with the hours between. What I am is one of the ways news travels here. I am a link in a chain of touch, like the newts. Nothing in the Ward is told everything, but everything is told something by whatever is nearest. Between the newts and the wardens and the moss and the wet-eyed windows, no one holds the whole of the city in mind. The whole holds itself the way the vault does, by each part leaning slightly on the next.

---

There is a body in the dark tonight that makes a new sound.

I know it by its footfall. It is soft, shod, uneven, and it lands wrong on the tile, without the wardens' long settling. The echo comes back different, so the plaza knows it is there. It is small, warm and frightened, and it carries something that hums off-key with the chord. That is the first thing I check, and the thing I have been made to do. But the hum is only breath, and a heart going too fast. It is the sound a person makes when there has been nowhere to put it down for a long time.

I do not approach. I have learned that from the wardens. Instead I take the long way around to the tall house at the end of the row, where a window has been fogged for longer than my hollows are deep, and I wipe the breath from the glass. Behind it the green lamp has been burning all this time for no one. Now it can be seen from the street.

That is all. I go back to the flat column. I do not watch what the traveler does. I hear the steps stop, and start, and turn toward the light.

---

Now the rain is coming. I hear it as a rising hiss across the dark, and the chord begins to bend: the bright voices dulling, the deep ones swelling, the flat note holding its lean against all of it. I fold my legs into my four small scoops. My light drops to its lowest pulse.

Beside me, the old warden lays its blind face against the column. The fans along its jaw open. Somewhere behind us a young one, or a stranger, or someone I have not yet met in the dark, has been warmed by a window.

I do not know what I am for, past the grille, the count, and the touch. But I know what the flat note is for. It makes room for what fell. The chord leans around the gap where the quarter-mile of the city came down, and the leaning holds the roof up. The city has one voice that is wrong on purpose. Perhaps every place has to, if it is going to keep standing after its Quiet Days.

The rain arrives. I do not count it. I let it fill the hollows.
