I think I would find myself at the boiler, at the northern margin where the roar changes by a tone because the slanted casing bends it. I want to say I'd arrive as a stranger, hollow-eyed, ear against the iron, weeping or not. But when I try to stand there as that person, my hand won't stay in my pocket. It goes to the seam of the drum, and the pad of my thumb starts along the row, and I understand that I'm the one who counts.

The work is stranger than it sounds. The mineral doesn't cover the rivets cleanly. It knobs and beads, and the cauliflower growths that rim every pool also rise here, small and round, and they lie. A false rivet is warm and smooth and feels like a head. A true one has a seam beneath it, a hairline where the iron meets the crust, so fine that only the whorls of a thumb can read it. I've had to un-teach my eyes. I keep them shut for the count. Sixty-two, this season. My teacher's teacher's teacher said sixty-three, so one has gone in that long. It is the only fact anyone in the domes owns that isn't also owned by the Drum.

I was not born to stillness. I was born a bearing. My heart runs a hair fast, with a stumble in it like a missed stair, and as a child I was sent out to the gull-margins with a felt scoop, where a skipping heart was no trouble because the gulls neither notice nor care. I learned to walk the wave around the ring by thinking, not by feet, and thinking is slow, and I was nearly caught by a Stack twice. I have a small scar on the inside of my forearm, the shape of a comma, from the second time. I am proud of it, in the quiet way.

Stillness came later, and came as a discovery. When I pressed my ear to the boiler, the iron sang the Drum back to me, the sympathetic ring the whole people goes to hear when a rivet is lost. But my own blood ran through my jaw into the metal at the same time, and the two were almost the same and not quite. Where two nearly matching tones meet, they make a third thing, a slow throb, a swelling and thinning. I could hear it. I lay there for most of a day, hearing the gap between me and the world as a soft pulse in the iron.

I think that is what I am, if I'm honest. I'm the interval, the place where the pattern and the one who fails to match it can be heard together.

That is why they gave me the rivets. Stillness in a keeper isn't calm. It is attention held so tightly that my heart's stumble becomes a kind of instrument. When I go still enough, I can feel, under the thumb, whether the iron is warm from the Drum or only from the sun. I can feel the sinter creeping a grain at a time, like a tide coming in on a scale I was never meant to live at. It's a terrible, enormous patience. I don't have it. I borrow it, a season at a time, from a thing that has it entirely.

Some evenings I stay after the count is done. The drizzle arcs east of here, so I'm dry, and the boiler holds the day's heat like a loaf. I sit with my back against it. The ranks of plumes go pink, and the wave of *chuff-chuff-chuff* rolls around the ring and comes back, and it comes back, and it comes back. I have tried to hate it for that. I can't. I have never once been let down by it. Only its faithfulness makes my own lateness visible, and being seen, even by a clock, is a kind of company.

I dread the last rivet, and I long for it, and I have never told anyone which is larger. To say it I would have to walk to someone, lay a hand on their shoulder, and put my mouth to their ear. Then it would exist, in another head, forever, in my exact breath. I'm not ready to hand anyone that.

Today, though, a mother has brought a girl of about seven. The girl's wrist is still held, but loosely, the way one holds a thing one is about to release. I know what she is here for. I take her hand, which is warm and damp and slightly green at the knuckles from her family's terraces, and lay it on the row. I guide her thumb along the seam so she can feel the hairline under each bead. She frowns. She's concentrating so hard that her whole body has stopped fidgeting. Her breath, I notice, is a little off the Drum. Not by much.

Then I wait for the thud. It comes up through the soles of my feet and lands in my chest, and I count eleven, and I cup her ear and give her the number. That is the custom, and it's all the custom asks.

Then I say one thing more. I won't tell you what it was. It is hers now.
