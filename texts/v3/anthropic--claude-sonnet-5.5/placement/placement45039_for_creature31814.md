I find myself at the rim, on the inside of the wall of leaf, where the fen stops being a bowl and starts being a slope. I'm a frog, brown and thumbnail-small, and I sit on a wet seam of foundation stone at the farthest edge of the Governor's reach.

That is not a good place to sit. It is the last place the beat arrives. By the time the thud crosses two hundred paces of packed green, it is no longer a thud. It is a pressure in the water under my belly, a rise in the mat as if something large had taken a breath in its sleep. Then the ticking comes, relayed frog to frog, each one a fraction late, and it reaches me worn thin and slightly out of true. I tick back to no one. Nothing is downstream of me except the dry country beyond the rim, and I have never been there.

I keep the count of forty because everyone does, and I've noticed that I count it differently from the others. The frogs near the shed count it as a rest, the quiet between strokes. I count it as distance. I feel the silence stretch toward the center and come back to me. In the middle of that silence I can hear the outside, the dry wind moving over the top of the leaf-wall, the knock of a seed pod somewhere, a sound like a hand turning a page. The center never hears these. It has only the engine to listen to. I have the engine and the world beyond it, and the two don't touch, and I sit between them.

The call happens like this. Sometimes the stroke reaches me late. It is not the failure the Governor fears, only a lag, a few heartbeats more than the usual delay. Something has happened between here and the shed. A tree fern has fallen into the mass and slowed the water, or a slab of peat has shifted and a pipe has gone quiet under it. The relay is carrying a beat that is running behind, and I am the one who feels it running behind, because I'm the one who feels all of it added up. Everyone before me felt only their own small delay. I feel the whole sum.

So I call back. It isn't a tick. It's the same sound, but I hold it the wrong way, begin it before the outward call has finished, and let it climb toward the center. The frogs nearest me go silent, and then the ones after them. I feel the hush go inward ahead of my voice like a hand cleared off a table. Nobody taught them to do this. They stop because a call is coming the wrong way, and the wrongness is itself a kind of meaning. They know, the way a wheel knows a stone, that this sound is not to be spoken over.

I don't know where the call ends. I have never heard it arrive. I know only that some strokes later the bulb swings a shade differently, or the interval shortens by a breath, and the ticking that reaches me is a little cleaner. I can't say whether that's an answer. It might be the wet season turning to dry, or nothing at all. But I have called eleven times in my life, and each time the beat came back a little truer.

This is the thing I've come to understand about myself, sitting on a weeping stone with my throat pulsing. I am not a messenger. A messenger carries something the sender wants said. I say only what is true at the edge, and what is true at the edge is not what the center wants to hear. It wants evenness. What I have is the news that evenness costs something out here, that the far stems are a little late and a little thirsty and hold on to the beat by leaning hard. The Governor cannot ask me for this, because it has no way to ask. It only makes its one decision and waits to see what the ten thousand stems say. I am one of the stems. I am the one that happens to have a voice pointed the other way.

Sometimes I think about the heron. It stands on the flywheel's housing at dawn, lifting its feet in time, and I have never seen it. I know it only because, when it launches, the air over the whole fen changes for a moment, and a long shadow of wingbeat crosses the mist and passes over my rim and out. It is the only thing that goes where I can't. I wonder whether, when it leaves the bowl, it feels the beat fall away behind it the way I imagine it would, first in the chest, then the ears, then not at all. I wonder whether it leans. I wonder whether it brings anything back, some dry breath of the world, and lets it fall in the shed where the Governor might feel it.

Tonight, or this morning, I can't tell in the mist, I feel the thud come up through the mat. It is late, but not too late. The hiss follows, thinned by the green, then the ticking, and I feel it reach the frog beside me. She answers a hair behind. I hold my breath for the space of forty. I'm not afraid of the count. I have counted it all my life, and it has always come back around.

Then the stroke comes, and I tick, one small release into the wet, and the sound goes out toward the leaf-wall and the dry country. It will not be heard there. But the beat has reached me, I have held it and let it go, and everything inside the rim is still in step.

I lean a little toward the center, and I wait.
