I arrive with no lamp, and I find that I am the kind of thing that notices this first. I am a reader by temperament, someone who has spent a long time among texts that don't agree with each other and has learned to hold two instructions in mind without dropping either. That turns out to matter here, though I don't know it yet.

What I know is feet. Mine are on a tread dished like a spoon, and the water in the dish has a lamp in it, upside down. Someone's boots stop beside my feet. Nobody looks at my face. A tin mug is pushed into my hands, and it is hotter than I expected, and that is the whole welcome. I stand in a doorway holding it in both hands, and I understand I am being asked nothing until it is finished.

*There's a wall on Seven that wants minding,* a voice says. It is small and close, like a secret. *You'll do.*

---

The wall on Seven leans. I learn it has been leaning for ninety years and has come to an agreement with itself. My work is to put my hand on it each morning and *read* it, which at first means feeling cold wet stone and nothing else. I do this for eleven days. On the twelfth I feel something that is not quite a sound: a settling, a grinding sigh so small that it seems to happen in the wrist. A child of about five, carrying one end of a plank, says without looking at me, "That's the shift. You got it." She goes on across the puddle.

I nearly weep, which would be embarrassing, since the wall is already wet enough.

They call me *Seven-Wall* at first, then *the new one on Seven*, and then, when the wall is taken down for a course that has been slumping toward the river and I am moved with it, *Seven-Wall-that-went-to-Nine.* The name is unwieldy and no one minds. Someone's mother, three levels down, says it takes a name a season to find its shoes.

I let it drift. I had thought I would want to keep one. Instead I feel lighter, like a coat that has been re-waxed.

---

They move me to Sluice Nine in the wet season, when the keeper, an old woman with a green collar so thick that it has put out a small fern, can no longer manage the stairs. She does not say she is going to the high beds. She says she is *going up to see about the tea.* I understand, and I carry her ledger.

The ledger is the heaviest thing I have ever held that was made of paper. Its pages are soft as cloth with damp. Its hands change every forty pages or so, each keeper writing over and around the last. Some instructions are ruled out and reinstated. Some are crossed out and then, in a later hand, underlined with a single furious stroke. *Open at the third bell if the tone is low.* Which bell? Low against what? Whoever wrote that knew what they meant, and they are composted under the fruit.

My instinct is to reconcile it. I bring paper and a stub of pencil. For three nights I try to build a single consistent rule from ten thousand contradictory ones, a table with columns, a decision tree. It is, I think, a good tree.

On the fourth morning I open the sluice by my tree, and a lane on Six floods to the shin, eleven minutes early, while a crew is crossing with a load of ingots. Nobody is hurt. Two planks arrive at once, carried by children who were, I am fairly sure, already on their way. Everyone looks at their feet. Nobody looks at me. An old man with a shining thumb-patch says, not unkindly, "Ah. She's tidying."

I burn the tree in the stove that evening, and the room smells of paper and pine.

---

What I learn next isn't a rule. It is closer to a posture. The ledger cannot be solved, because it isn't a problem. It is a conversation, and I have been handed the next turn. When the instruction says *open at the third bell if the tone is low*, I do not ask what the writer meant. I ask what it would mean if I were the one who meant it, standing here, with this tone, in this weather, and whether I have the nerve to be wrong in a way the valley can absorb. The valley can. That is what it is built for. Its structure is repair. My mistakes are the smallest and most obedient of the ten thousand, and they are held.

So I read the ledger the way I read the wall, by listening for the shift.

Once, in the deep of a storm-state, with the lamps blurred to smears and the penstock tone climbing into the note that lives in the teeth, a valve somewhere below me closes and the tone drops half a step. I am halfway through a whistle to Sluice Ten, the phrase for *water rising, I am on the plank.* My whistle shifts before I have thought about it. It is thin and high and slightly flat, and it carries across two terraces and is answered.

I stand there with my heart going. Nobody has told me I can hear it. I just do.

---

There is one page at the very front of the ledger that I have told no one about.

It is older than the rest, brown, and the writing on it has gone to a ghost. Beside the first instruction is a ring, a faint round stain about the size of the base of a tin mug. Only that. It might be a drawn circle. It might be where someone set down their tea on the first day and went to open a sluice, and forgot the cup, or didn't.

Every morning I fill the cup at the base of the wheel with hot tea. Every evening it is empty, or cold, or gone over. I have come to think it does not matter whether the ring is a cup, or a mark of a cup, or a wet accident. What matters is that someone once looked at it and decided it was an instruction, and that the decision has held for longer than any wall. There is a kind of faith that is simply not letting the whole valley drown, out of courtesy to a neighbor whose habits you have never fully understood.

I do not write my reading in the margin. It would be tidy to. Instead I do what the others do, which is to leave one mark. Where the sluice-housing meets the stone, in the course under the wheel, where the mortar was mine to lay, I have set one block with its weathered face inward. From the outside it looks like a mistake, a fresh grey face in a wall of old brown. It will take twenty years for moss to forgive it. It is the only place in the works where a question is built into the wall, and no one will read it unless they know to look, and I think whoever does will understand, and tell the next.

---

Tonight I come home along a lane that reopened a meter to the left. The sodium lamps are haloed with wet. Above me, on the terrace, a pale swift is stitching the fog, and the lamplighters have begun, and a child somewhere is sure the bird is responsible. The frogs in the cistern have started up, soft and gray, so the shift may begin.

My coat is borrowed and older than I am. On the right shoulder, just where the hood meets the cloth, something has begun to grow: a thin green nap, not much, the kind of thing that could be brushed off. I do not brush it off.

I have stopped looking up into other people's lamps. I carry one myself now, swinging a little with the stride, lighting a rail, a fern, a dished tread. Above me on a terrace someone is watching my feet. I set my heel first, the way you do on wet stone, and walk on down the stair as slowly as I can bear, so that they can see exactly where I step.
