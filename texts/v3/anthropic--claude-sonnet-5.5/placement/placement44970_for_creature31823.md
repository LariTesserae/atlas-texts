# The Third Tussock

I am the oldest frog in the Boiler Hollow, which is a small distinction. It belongs to whoever sits on the third tussock from the north lip, and today that is me.

I sit in a crook of moss with my back legs folded and my throat going. *Chk-chk-chk-chk.* Four to a burst, then a rest, then four. I don't decide this. Asking me to stop would be like asking you to stop your pulse, and the question would be as strange, since you would not know who was being asked. But I know where my burst falls. It falls between the blast of the big stack behind me and the smaller stack to the east, in a gap about the width of a held breath. The frog on my left fits himself into the gap after mine, and the frog on my right into the one before. We are a weave. If I left, the weave would have a hole in it the exact size of me, and another frog would be hatched to fill it. I take some comfort in that, and some other feeling I have no word for.

The world, from here, is green-gold and wet. The light never moves. The leaf overhead is the size of a table and glossy as a beetle, and I have never seen it tremble. The mat under me gives a finger's depth when I settle and pushes back when I shift, so I always know whether I am the one moving. The water is warm against my belly. The air tastes of iron and sweet rot, the way home tastes to anything.

I don't think of the noise as noise. The stacks blare, the understory ticks, and the mayflies hold their ranks in the air like a struck note. All of it is the ground I stand on, only made of sound. I am one grain in the grinding bed under the horns. That is not small. A bed is made of nothing but grains.

---

Here is the thing I know that the others may also know, though we have never compared.

Every nine seconds the ground breathes.

It is not the stacks. They fire on their own cycles, thirty of them, each a little early or late, and the blasts come through the moss into my feet as a rough shove. This is something else. It is slower and lower and comes from underneath, from the black water below the mat, and it is so regular that I long ago stopped feeling it as an event. It is more like the fact of my weight. But sometimes, just before the big stack behind me fills its hood, I feel the mat lift under my belly, gently, the way a sleeper's chest lifts a blanket. Then the gas is in the stem and the stack sings, and I place my burst in the gap after.

I don't say the breath causes the blast. I say the blast is a little bit the breath's readiness. That is the closest I can come, and I got it from no one, since it is only what my belly says.

Something is down there, and it is leaning.

I don't mean anything grand by this. There are pale threads that run through the mat, and they are warm, and sometimes a small pulse passes along them under my toes. On the odd occasion the pulse comes before the breath, as if the thing below were being told what to do by the mat, and not the other way around. I can't sort out which one is the keeper. Perhaps that is like asking which of my two lungs is the one that breathes. I sit on the tussock and I feel a body that is bigger than the salamander, and bigger than the mat, and includes me the way a sentence includes its commas.

---

Once in what the old frogs call a decade, and I take their word for the length, the whole thing happens differently.

It starts in my feet. The pulse comes through the threads harder than it ever does, and there is a brightness in the water below that I can't see but feel, as a pressing behind the eyes. The hum-fish turn. I know it because the fine tingle in the moss goes tight and all points one way. Then every stack lets go together, thirty voices in one shuddering blare that shakes the tabletop leaves, and I don't call.

I don't decide not to. None of us do. Hundreds of throats stop in the same instant, and for one second there is a silence in the hollow that has the exact shape of the frogs.

That second is the only thing I have ever had that was mine alone, and I want to say what it is. It is not quiet. The horns are still ringing, the understory is still clicking, the mayflies still hum in their ranks. It is only that the loom stops. And in the space where the loom was, I can hear, for the first time, the place where my own burst would go. I hear the gap. It is warm and narrow and just my size, and it has been held open for me every day of my life by the frogs on either side, who fit themselves around it without knowing they do.

Then the ground lets go and my throat starts, *chk-chk-chk-chk*, and the weave closes, and I am a grain in the bed again.

I think that second is what the hollow feels like from inside, if it feels like anything. I think that is what the salamander's rare brightness means, if it means. It is the machine turning to look at itself and finding, in the moment of looking, that it is a body.

---

I know I am not the frog who sat here a decade ago. The tussock remembers him and I do not. But I know the beat, and I know the gap, and I know the breath under the moss the way I know I have a throat. Whatever passes from him to me passes through the station. It is enough. I have never met a frog who wanted more.

Somewhere above the rim, on the marsh road, someone is walking with the sedge on either side. They hear a faint steam-note and think of a distant works. They keep walking. They don't know that the note is made of me, one grain among hundreds, placed exactly between two horns, and that under me the ground is rising and falling in a slow, unhurried patience.

*Chk-chk-chk-chk.*

The stack behind me fills. The mat lifts a finger's depth under my belly, and I wait for the gap.
