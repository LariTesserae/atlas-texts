I arrive at dusk, when the fog is coming over the lip, and for a while I am no one. I am only the wet on the stones, the beading on the cushion plants, the cold thin skin over the sun's leftover heat. I try on the first creatures I meet, the way you try on coats in a house you've been left alone in.

I try the goat first. It is easy to be a goat. You face the wind with three hundred others and the pale fleece streams back off you, and you do not have to decide anything, because the old doe with the grey eyes has already decided. At night you lie where the ground hums up through your ribs. But I find I can't stay a goat. Everything a goat knows is in the ear, and I keep wanting to say something the ear has no shape for.

I try the rodent under the felt, the sleek one at the mouth of its hole, paws folded, face in the morning sun. It is the most secure life on the plateau. It whistles all day, and nothing it says is heard, and it seems not to mind. I stay a little longer in that one. Then I feel the prickling in the roots of the sedge, the whole colony talking beneath me, and I understand that I would be content there and would never know whether anyone had answered. I put that coat back too.

The bird nearly keeps me. I am one of ten thousand in a wind-bored hole, in a rank of holes up the pale stone. My voice is nothing alone. I fit it into a gap between two peaks of the roar, a place where the chord is thin, and I join the shimmer. When the raptor's shadow slides over the fin I drop my pitch and the drop runs through us like a curtain falling and lifting. To be a bird is to be exactly as loud as you are needed. I love it, and I leave it anyway, because the bird never asks what the chord is for. The bird has only ever been inside it.

So I come out of the fin, and I am a woman, and I am old, and I have been up this mountain nineteen times.

---

My name is a knot. It hangs at my belt, third strand from the left, a double loop with a bead of bone. There is no sound in it, and nobody has ever said it aloud to me on this plateau, which is what I like best about the place. In the valleys people shout my name across water and it gets bent by the trip. Here it stays whole, a thing I can find with my thumb.

We came up through the scree this morning, six of us. By the second day we had stopped trying to speak, and by now even the wish to has gone out of us, the way a hunger goes if you pass the hour for it. My hands do the talking. *Goats, downwind, the long fin, two days.* Tomas answers with the flat of his palm: *slow.* He is young and wants to run at it. I hold the cloth flag up against the violet sky until he sees it, and he slows.

What I carry that the others don't is the long cord. It has a strand for every year I have come, and on each strand are the knots for the tarns, the fins, the beds where the drone is best. When I run my thumb along it I know the route to a pool as if I were seeing it. That is my work, to be the one who can say *there, and there, and not there.* Nobody chose me. I was standing near the cord-reader when she got tired, and I picked it up.

---

We come up on the herd from the wind-shadow of a fin, where the air goes dead and the roar drops to a pressure in the bones. The goats face away from us into the gale, three hundred pale backs leaning the same way. The old doe's ear goes flat and then forward, a small movement a long way off. She knows something is here and cannot say what. I hold my breath, though breath is nothing to a place like this.

Tomas throws. The obsidian goes in like a bit of the night. It is quick, and I am glad of the quickness, and I press my palm to the goat's neck while its legs are still deciding what to do. I don't pray. I don't have the words, and if I did they would be swallowed. But I keep my hand on it a long time, and I feel the pulse go slack, and I think that's the prayer: the hand kept there.

Later, at the lower nests, I take three eggs, small and blue-grey and freckled with rust. I leave three holes with their eggs untouched beside them. I don't know who made this rule, and I don't have to. I have kept it nineteen times, and the birds go on.

---

That night, while the others sleep in a slot with their backs to the rock, I walk out alone. I shouldn't. I go to where the goats bedded, a shallow dent in the gravel where the grit is pressed flat in a hundred pale ovals, and I lie down in one, still warm, and put my belly against the ground.

The drone comes up through me. It is not a sound. It is a slow pulse under the ribs, deeper than the shrill of the perforated fins or the fifths in the mid-slots, older than either, and it does something to me that I have no cord for. I have been afraid all my life without knowing it, of my own thoughts, of being one voice among the valley's shouting. Here that fear has nowhere to stand. The chord takes it and doesn't answer. I lie in the goat's bed and think that maybe the herders are right, that the goats are not listening. They are being held.

---

At dawn the fog comes up, and every stone is beaded, and the tarn at the edge of the basin lies with its hexagons drawn on it in lace. I have looked at that pattern for nineteen years. This time I kneel and look until the low sun catches it, and I notice something I never had the patience for before. The rings are spaced. They are not random. They fall in the same steps as the pipes, one after another, fifth and octave and the thin glassy top, as if the water were a page and the sound had been writing on it in the only hand it had.

So I do a foolish thing. I take a new strand from my belt and begin to knot it. It is not a route or a count. I put in a long, slow knot for the drone. Above it I put two knots close together for the fifth. Then a bead of bone for the whistle in the perforated fins. Then a run of tiny, tight ones that I can barely feel between finger and thumb, for the birds, for the gaps the birds have found and filled.

I don't know if it is right. I only know that when I hold it in my palm, the strand has a weight it didn't have before, and my thumb, running along it, hears something. A cord has never done that for me. The knots do not describe the chord. They answer it, small and dry, in the one language I have that does not need air.

---

Before we go down I climb the ridge with the flat stone I carry. It is the same stone I've carried up nineteen times and set down on the cairn nineteen times, and each time I've taken a new one, because that is how it goes. But this time I put the stone down and keep the strand.

I go instead to the fin with the ochre hands. There are a dozen of them at shoulder height, palm out, fingers spread, faded to the color of dried blood. I have fitted my hand into these many times and stood with my eyes shut. Today I look at them properly. One is small, a girl's, or someone's from long before the rock was as worn as it is now, and the fingers are held a little apart, as if in the moment of pressing they had wanted to reach further. I fit my hand to it, and it fits.

I don't know who she was. I know that she came, that she added something almost nothing, and that the place left room for it. I know the chord went on, and that it is going on now through the palm I have against the stone, and through the bone of the hand, and into me.

I leave the strand. I tuck it into a crack beside her print where the fog will find it and the lichen will find it, in a hundred years or a thousand, orange and black. Then I go down the ridge after the others, and the sound closes behind me like water, and I am glad, and I am quieter than I have ever been.
