I find myself on the fourth terrace, in the last of the dark, with my palm flat in a stone cup that is already faintly damp.

I am not from here. That is the first thing I know about myself, and everyone in the party knows it too. I walk wrong. I put my heel down first, and the gravel announces me across the whole valley like a dropped tray. The others move in file along the channel, placing each foot whole, and from a ridge they must look like a herd of drifters with one clumsy animal at the back. That animal is me.

I am the ninth of the party, the newest, and the one who tried to improve things.

It was a bend in the channel below the reed pool, choked with a season's drift of gravel. I saw that the water could run straighter if the bend were cut, faster and cleaner, and I had the scraper in my hand and had already started. No one shouted. Someone rested a palm on my forearm, gently, and took the scraper, and for a month afterward I was given only the cane brush. I was not punished. It was closer to being lovingly set aside, like a child handed something safe.

I have thought about that month more than anything else that has happened to me. I had been sure I was being useful. What I learned is that clearing a channel to how it was is harder than improving it. Improving is talking. You bring your own idea and lay it over the stone. Restoring is listening: you crouch there until you can feel where the lip used to be, where the old hand meant the water to turn, and you take away only what isn't that. My first restored bend took a whole morning and looked, when I finished, like I had done nothing. It was the best work I've ever done.

---

The light comes. I take my hand from the cup and stand, and the comb above me begins to sweat.

I have been assigned this one. It is a middling comb, not the oldest and not the slowest, though it leans a hair late toward the far veils, as if it is thinking it over. Beads gather on the gradient where edge becomes not-edge, and I watch them join and thread downward, and I do not touch the blade. I have never wanted to. The material is so fine at its margin that it seems less like an object than a place where an object stops, and something in me knows this is a courtesy and should be met with one.

I do not speak. By day I am learning to talk with my shoulders. The open-palm gesture, *wait, something is changing*, I have gotten right, and the first time one of the others answered it with the same gesture and turned the whole party a quarter-degree east without a sound, I felt something I can only call belonging. It was not that I had joined them. It was that I had briefly stopped being the loud part.

Walking in the morning, I have started to fall to the back and to the edge, and I am beginning to understand why. The old drifters do it, the gray-muzzled ones standing slightly apart with their ears turned outward. They are not leading. Their job is to notice, and to be trusted in the noticing. I have a talent for it, or I am growing one: I hear the dry click of a sun-hawk two ridges off, and I see the flat stone that is slightly too warm and too evenly shaped. Twice now I have lifted my chin, barely, and the party has bent around a shale-cat I never had to name.

---

Once, in the third month, I watched a kill from far off.

It was a young drifter, a straggler, and the cat took it so quickly that my mind had not finished the word *cat* before it was over. Then the stillness. The cat lay across the body, not eating, holding, for as long as it takes to say a long sentence slowly. I felt no wish to intervene, which surprised me. What I felt was closer to witnessing, the sense that this moment was owed attention, and that the attention itself was a kind of care. Then the hawks came, *here, here*, and the small brown scavengers, and the ants in their dark ropes. By evening there was a pale patch.

I think about that patch. I think about the cushion plant that will sit there for three generations of drifters. Nothing on the shelf is wasted, and nothing is asked to mean more than it is.

---

At the pool, I draw my day's water. The rule is a day's water and no more, and I have learned to take slightly less than I could. It feels like tact. I tie the knot in my wrist-cord, and I tie it carefully, because a hurried knot is a small lie about how much you took. My cord is only a third full. I won't finish it before the circuit turns, and I've decided that's all right. Someone else's coiled cord lies in the stone lip of this pool, gray with years, and I've never asked whose.

I don't know whether the plateau reads it. I tie the knot anyway. This is the part I understand best now, that the tying doesn't depend on the reading.

---

Evening. The rock ticks as it gives back the day. The drifters gather near the water, flushing pale in slow ripples, and the threadwings lift in their great hum, and I sit beside the channel with the scraper laid down beside me.

The highest combs are darkening. One, then another. The first beads form.

And then it comes, the small pressure, the sense of being read by something that reads very slowly and does not mind waiting. The Keepers say every one of them has felt it. I felt it for the first time in my second month and thought I was being judged. I know now it isn't that. Judgment is quick. This has no verdict in it. It is closer to the way I attend to a bend in a channel: crouching, patient, wanting only to know what is actually there.

Tonight I notice something I hadn't before. I recognize it, not from being read, but from reading. It is the way I have learned to look at the water, at the cat, at a comb that leans a hair late. I don't want to overstate this. I am a walker with a cane brush and a wrong gait, and whatever turns toward me at dusk is not me. But the posture is one I know from the inside, and it is strange to be held in an attention that resembles my own on its best days.

So I stay quiet. That is all there is to do, and it turns out to be enough. The thread of water moves through the stone, hardly louder than breath. Somewhere along the terraces, someone is laying a palm in a cup. Someone is tying a knot. The combs lean toward veils of rain that never arrive, and I lean a little with them, and the count goes on, and I am in it. I'm not sure how much, or as what. A small wrong-footed thing, learning to be included by not being much.

Tomorrow I will clear the bend by the reed pool again, to how it was.
