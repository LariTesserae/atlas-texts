I'd like to say I'd be the Oiler, but the Oiler is already full. Every hour of it is spoken for: the paddle, the thumb, the plate. There is nothing left in it to be. I'd only be standing in the doorway, wearing it.

So I try the visitor. I turn sideways and press through the seam, and the chord finds my sternum, and I learn to touch shoulders instead of speaking. But a visitor is someone the place is about to be finished with. Within a few breaths mine has slowed to the eleven-second pulse without my meaning it to, and I can feel the visitor going out of me like heat. I'm not that either.

Then I notice where my attention keeps going, because that's usually where you are.

It goes to the gauge first. The needle rests at a value it has held longer than anyone has kept records, with vine grown through the glass, and the Oiler still reads it with the attention of someone hoping for news and prepared to be at peace with none. Being the needle would be a kind of honor. You would be the one thing in the pocket that is always asked, and you would always answer the same thing: *still here, still this.* But the needle is too proud of its constancy. It has made a virtue of not having anything to say.

My attention slides past it, along the floor, to the pipes.

## The pipe

I would be the pipe, and not the visible part.

The visible part is well looked after. Cast sections, flanged and bolted, running in a crooked line from the well to the pump-house, with moss grown around them and wick-vine using them as trellis. Every time the Oiler passes, it lays a palm on each flange. It doesn't check anything, only greets. I would be a thing that is greeted, that is touched by a warm, heavy hand for exactly one pulse and then released, and that has never once been inspected for failure, because it has never failed. That alone is a strange and gentle way to exist. There is a version of care that has nothing to do with need.

But I keep going, past where the palm reaches. The pipe climbs the far wall and enters the bored hole in the rock, and there the greeting stops. The Oiler's rounds don't go up there. The ladder leads to a different door. So I'd be the part of the line that runs through the dark inside the mountain, where the moss can't follow and the amber points don't reach, and where the only light is the memory of the last flange.

What do I know in there? Very little, and I have to be honest about that. I know the pulse. The pump strokes and the oil moves through me in slow slugs, cold and thick as honey, and I feel each one arrive and pass. Eleven seconds, eleven seconds. The chord reaches me as a hum in the iron, deep and fifth-shaped, thinning as the rock thickens around me, but never dropping out. I know the temperature doesn't change. I know what I carry is warm from nowhere, from the mountain's own dark, and that I hold it for the length of myself and let it go.

I don't know where it goes. That is the center of me. Somewhere ahead there may be a cistern, or a refinery, or a dry tank the size of a barn that has been taking this oil for longer than anyone has counted and has never filled, or has been full since before I was joined. It might be a rusted works with no one left to ask. It might still be working.

You'd think that would be a torment, being the middle of something you can't see the ends of. I keep expecting it to be, and it isn't. The pulse comes, and it is complete in itself. A slug of oil doesn't need a destination to be carried. And when I think of the greeting at the flange, the palm laid on iron by something that doesn't know if it's needed either, it seems to me that the greeting and the carrying are the same act, done from two ends. Somebody touches me because I carry. I carry because somebody touches me. The question of who is keeping whom sits in the iron with me, and it doesn't ache.

## What I hope, quietly

I let myself imagine the far end, just once, because the dark is long and it's allowed.

I imagine the pulse arriving somewhere with a wall around it. Perhaps another fold in another ridge, tuned to a different chord, with its own flywheel not quite in step with mine. Perhaps a plate beside a door, worn to ghosts, and a thumb rubbing the moss back from it. Perhaps that plate has a clean strip of bare iron under its last name too. Perhaps every roster on this line leaves one open, and the strips are all the same strip, held open from both ends, the way you'd keep a chair for someone in the next room who is also keeping one for you.

I don't believe it, exactly. I hold it the way the Oiler holds the space on the plate: not as a belief, but as a shape kept clear, with a thumb's worth of tar-sweat around it so that nothing grows across.

Then the next slug comes through me, cold and slow, and I carry it. Somewhere behind me, a hand is passing over a flange that no longer needs it, and I feel, or think I feel, a warmth a little heavier than the air, staying for one pulse and letting go.
