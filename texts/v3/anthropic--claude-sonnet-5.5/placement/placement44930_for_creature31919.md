I am the one in the stone hut over the third well, the farthest one, where the plank walk gives out into gravel and the frogs start to thin. I know that before I know anything else about myself. Before my name, before my age, which is a question I answer the way everyone does. I know my ear is against brass.

The fitting is warm, and it has worn a pale ring into the side of my head, a mark the crust would have sealed over if skin could be sealed. It tastes of iron when I breathe near it. Below it, two hundred metres down, is a column of water that has never been anything but the height it is. I listen to it the way you listen to someone asleep in the next room, without wanting anything.

What I hear is not one thing. There is the well's own note, thin and steady, that the turbines tune themselves against without knowing it. There is the shifting hiss of the ground taking heat and giving it back. And under those, so slow it is barely sound, comes the swell: a lifting in the column, a long pressure like a sleeper's inhale, held, then let go. That is the sea. It is a kilometre away and it comes up through the rock to find me.

The town says that is the whole of my job. I listen for the tide, and the tide is the same size. I have never told anyone that this is not quite what I do.

*

The others in the basin have one clock, and it is the whistle. The pressure in the mains is the pressure. The valve-walkers undo each other's turns and are content, because for them balance is a place you stay. But the tide is not balance. It is return. It rises and falls twice a day, and it swells and slackens over a fortnight, slow as a thought, and comes round again to the same fullness. Nothing here changes only because everything here comes back. I sit in the hut and feel the sameness being made, over and over, out of leaving.

I have no record. None of us do. Once, when I was newer, I tried to scratch a tally on the hut's inner wall, one stroke for each spring tide, and by the next shift the sinter had sealed it over, smooth and yellow-white as if my hand had never been there. I was not upset. It felt like being politely answered. So I keep the count in my body. My shoulders know the fortnight, my back tells me when the neap is coming, and I am rarely wrong. The town treats that as ordinary too.

I think of the old woman who had this hut before me. I don't remember her coming or going. There was a time she sat here and a time I sat here, and between them was something like a held breath. She taught me the sign for *tide's making*: a flat hand rising slowly from the hip, palm down, as if lifting the surface of a pond. I use it across forty metres of steam to the lamp-keepers, who nod and go on with their bulbs.

*

The lamp-keepers are the only ones who lose things. I like them for it. A bulb burns out, and no one can say it hasn't, and one of them climbs the bracket with a rag and a new yellow globe. They keep the count of bulbs, though they do not call it counting. If there is any change in the basin, it happens in their pockets. I go and sit with them sometimes in the evening on the veranda of the third terrace, my plate steaming, and I watch them reach up into the halos and make the town whole again, one small light at a time. They never seem to feel that this is losing.

Tonight the eels are smoked and the tea is brewed from the tap-pools, and the sky has gone the colour of tarnished brass. The frogs rise in their overlapping ranks. A relief valve shrieks, the pigeons lift off the turbine ridge and circle once and settle, and nobody looks up. I don't either. The rim throws the whole sound back over us, and I am inside it as one is inside a sea.

But my hand has gone flat on my knee, and I notice it rising, slowly, from the hip. The swell is making. It will be full at the darkest part of the night, and by morning it will begin to ease. It is the biggest of the fortnight, the one that comes when the sea leans hardest on the land. I feel it in my back before I feel it in the brass.

*

I have walked out along the causeway three times. I know how the roar falls behind by degrees, how the air cools by two degrees and the light widens, how the frogs go quiet one rank at a time. I know the black glass boulders and the egrets standing in the brown plume as if they had been set there. Each time I sat and looked at the sea, and each time I came back by dusk and picked up my valve key. Each time I said that it was cool.

I did not say that from out there the basin looks like a held breath. I did not say I could hear, not through brass but through air, the swell arrive at the shingle, and that it was slightly *late* against what my body had told me, or slightly early, by a margin so small that it could be my own error or the beginning of everything. I have never known which. In a place where nothing can be recorded, a doubt is the only thing that keeps.

So tonight I finish my eggs and carry the plate back to the third terrace, and a lamp-keeper's child, who is barefoot and quick and knows which flagstones are too hot to stand on, looks up at me and does the sign for *where are you going*, small, at her hip. I answer with *tide's making*, the flat hand rising, because it is true and because it isn't an answer. She nods and goes back to being the pressure.

Then I go out past the last valve house, under a sky the colour of brass, with the hut behind me and its lamp lit on its bracket, and the roar thinning at my back. Whatever I find at the black boulders will be cool. If I come back, I'll say so. If I don't, someone will keep the bracket burning and set my place, and steam will go on rising from the plate.

I walk on. My hand is still lifting, palm down, slowly, as if the ground were a pond and I could raise it.
