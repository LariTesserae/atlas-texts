I begin as warmth on one side of my body and none on the other.

That is the first thing I know: the ground under my belly still holds the day's heat, gravel ticking as it cools, and the air above is only a little cooler. I don't start as a moth, though I feel the pull toward the gold drift and the lit tarn. I don't start as a furnace-back either. I would like to be that patient breathing hill, but I am too small and too restless. Nothing that large would crouch over a scorched patch of sand with its ear pressed to the ground, and I am doing exactly that.

So: knee-high, dun, long-fingered. My eyes are all pupil, and they are the least useful thing I own. I read the world with my hands.

The black sand where the lightning came down last night is glassy at the surface and gritty beneath. I tap it with one fingernail and listen through the bones of my jaw. Solid gives a dead sound. Hollow gives a small bright *tik*, like a thought being had. I dig and lift out a tube as thin as a reed stem and pale as a bone. I blow across it and it sings a high, thin note, perfectly ordinary and perfectly lovely. I set it in the pouch with the others.

There are five of us in the burrow, in the bank of a gully where the gravel is coarse. I know each of them by the note their footsteps make on the burrow's glass. The oldest walks with a hitch and a low tone. The youngest is all clatter. I am the one who wanders farthest, and they have stopped asking why. Every family has one who goes off to listen. It is not clever, the way the smelters are clever. I can't run a fire or lay a course of iron. I only keep finding that the world has more inside it than it shows, and I keep wanting to put my ear against it.

What I want is a low note.

It started with the reeds. Every gully has its own reed-song, and I know them the way you know voices: the tenor whistle in the burrow gully, the reedy double note two swells east. But days from here, near the equatorial edge, there is a gully where the reeds sing lower than any tube I have ever found. I heard it once, passing through, and it went into my chest and stayed. Since then I have been looking for glass that could hold that note. Long glass, thick glass, a tube that could answer. I have not found it. The long ones snap when I lift them. So I carry my short bright ones across the plateau, and I go on listening.

The walk takes many nights. I cross a flat of dark crust that hums under my feet like a struck pot, and I keep to the edges of it, because the hum gets into my teeth. I follow a fog river downhill for a day, drinking from the beads on the scrub. Once a furnace-back herd passes at a distance, and I feel their pulse through the ground before I see the ember line: *chuff*, and the long wait, *chuff*. I stand still and let it go through me. A calf is walking in the lee of its mother, cold and dull-domed, holding to her heat, and I feel a fondness for it that I can't account for. Then they are gone. Forty red points burn along the horizon, and I count them the way you count breathing.

Late one dark I stop and hold a tube to my ear. Inside the glass is the hollow ringing of my own blood, and under that, so faint I might be inventing it, the low hum of the smelters, tower to tower, a slow sentence being spoken across a very large room. I don't understand it. I listen anyway. It doesn't feel like eavesdropping. It feels like being let stand in a doorway.

The gully, when I reach it, is smooth-sided and floored with black gravel that ticks. The reeds are singing, and their note is exactly as low as I remembered. It is a sound like a large animal thinking about humming. I go down between them slowly. Everything here goes slowly. I notice that my own steps have shortened, and I don't remember deciding it.

The rail is half buried, longer than I am. It is ribbed with rust, and worn smooth by a very long time of not being anything in particular. The gravel has drifted against it. Moths have thinned out overhead, and I see only two or three, high and gold and uncommitted.

I have taken everything else the ground offered me. Tubes, bones, a bright scale that fell from a furnace-back's flank. I don't take this. I put my palm flat on the metal and it is warm, exactly as warm as the ground, no warmer, and it does not hum. That surprises me. I had expected it to be the source of the note. It isn't. It is only sitting inside the note, the way a stone sits in a stream and makes the water speak differently.

I put my ear to it anyway. I hear gravel and my own pulse, and maybe, far down, a very slow ringing, like something remembering the shape of a bell.

Then I do the thing I did not plan. I take the longest tube from my pouch, the one I have carried all this way without breaking, the one I was saving for the day I found the low note. I lay it along the rail, from one end toward the other, the way you lay a tube across the chest of one of your own. I don't know if the rail is dead. I don't know if it was ever alive. It might be a rib from something that took in too much iron. It might be a road for a creature so large it was weather. But it has been lying here a long time with nobody to say a word over it, and I am a thing that keeps ears on the ground.

The wind comes down the gully and the reeds bend and lower their song, and the glass on the rail begins to speak. Its note is high and thin, nothing like the low one. But it sits on top of the reeds' voice the way a small light sits on a large dark, and the two of them go on together, slightly out of tune, neither trying to be the other.

Then the sky cracks open, purple-white and branching, and for three seconds I can see all of it: the swells, the rust-ribbed bar, the pale tube laid on it, my own shadow thrown long across the gravel. Somewhere far off a herd lifts its ember line. Somewhere a tower breathes red. Then the dark shuts, and I hear the thunder arrive as pressure in my chest, and I am small and warm and crouched in a place where the animals step a little more quietly.

I stay a while. Then I stand and go back the way I came. The glass keeps singing behind me for as long as the wind holds, and after I can no longer hear it, I keep listening for it.
