# The Last Hand

I know the ridge before I know myself. Cold air in the valleys, and above it the thinner, sweeter air where we sleep. Moss is pressed against the back of my neck, and it is heavier than it was yesterday because it drank in the night. Under my cheek the hollow is warm. It was warm before me. It has the shape of a body curled on its side, and I fit it the way a hand fits a glove that was sewn on someone else's hand and then handed down.

So I am a Lodger. I check that this is true by opening my eyes, and the inner lids slide back. Below the ridge there is a white sea, and other ridges stand out of it with their dark coats, and on one of them, very far off, a forearm is lifting in the pale light. It is ochre, then gray-green. It takes a long breath to say *awake*. I lift mine and say it back, slowly, so as not to look like an eye.

Someone beside me presses a palm to mine. We stay there the length of a drop gathering on a leaf tip, and it falls. The palm is warm from the steam vent, so I know she has something to tell me later, and that it matters.

---

I should say what I am among them, because I did not know it until I lay here this morning: I am the last one in the chain.

Every Lodger in the line is counted by the hand of the one behind. The hand rests flat between the shoulder blades and feels the muscle there, the small rolling of a body walking the way the mat requires. If the pressure goes slack, the whole line knows in the same instant. That is how we lose people without a sound. But the last one has no hand at her back. Something could drop from a limb onto the end of the line, and the ferns would shiver, and no palm would go slack, because there would be no palm. The line would walk on with a shorter tail and not know.

So the last one carries a cord. It is vine, twisted with moss fiber, as long as two bodies, and its far end is weighted with a nodule of bog-iron the size of a fist, orange-brown and lumpy, from a pan I have never seen. It drags behind me over the mat. The one ahead of me holds the near end loosely, looped through her fingers. She feels the iron pulling and the small snag and release as it crosses roots. If it goes still, she stops.

I have thought a great deal about this. It means my whole use to the chain is a weight on the ground. When I am doing my work well, I am a thing that is dragged. Nobody praises the cord. But I have the whole line in front of me, the whole row of backs and hands and slow bodies, and I am the only one who sees them all at once. I know who is favoring a foot. I know whose cloak has begun to go pale on the left shoulder. I am not lonely in it. It is a good place from which to see people.

---

The fog lifts and tears and climbs the slopes, and we go down with it into the dripping.

The first thing is the drinking. Along a fallen trunk there is a silk sheet strung between two branches, and every strand is beaded. I draw my tongue along the bottom edge, not the middle, where the spider sits in her stillness and does not yet know I am here. The water tastes of cold and of the thing that has no taste until you have had it for a long time. The web does not tear. She stays. Somewhere behind me a sound like a small wet cough goes through the ferns, and it is only a frog, and my heart takes a while to come down.

We work. Someone pinches back a liverwort that has gone over a cushion of moss. Someone clears a seep-thread with the side of her thumb so the water runs evenly and does not pool and rot the mat. I press a plug of moss into a bare place the size of my palm, with the heel of my hand, and I water it from my own mouth, and it takes. In a season no one will be able to tell where the plug went in. That is the entire ambition of the work.

At the second gulp of the siphon after midday we come to the marsh valley by the eastern rim, and there is the Kettle.

I have a claim on the Kettle. My family lodges in its firebox, in the ferns, in the dark iron mouth that is warm at noon and smells of rust and something like rain that has been standing. I lay my palm on its flank. The metal is cold enough to hurt, and it flushes my skin slate. I rub ochre from the marsh edge into the rust where it has gone gray, so that it will keep its color.

*You are still being made,* I say to it, with my hand, in the old way.

I say it every time, and every time I wonder what I mean. The others say it to the Kettle because it is the only creature we know that has been finished. I say it, and I feel something else under the words. The Kettle is not the only finished thing. Whoever lies down and stops is finished, and I have no way of telling the difference between resting and finishing. That is the whole question, and it is the one I asked.

---

The rain arrives a minute after its sound does, through the canopy. Then it is on the leaves, deliberate, fat and cold, and the whole forest bows a little under it. We go to ground in pairs, back to back, in a dry hollow under a leaning trunk. My partner's spine is warm against mine and the moss on our shoulders touches, so that we are one low green thing with four hands.

This is the time when the long things are said. She presses her palm to mine, and I feel the slow words go in: her mother's cloak is failing, and she is afraid to reseed it from the wrong ridge. I answer the way the rain requires, with weight, not hurry. There is a lot of rain. The marsh will rise a hand's width. We have time to say anything.

When she has finished I tell her what I have not told anyone. In the last dry season I climbed to the old one who sits on the western ridge where the afternoon light comes in low. Her legs are green to the knee now, and lichen has taken both shoulders, and her palms have settled into one long green. I put my hand against hers and asked her: *who counts the last?*

I meant the cord. I also meant more than the cord. I do not know how to say the rest of it, and that is why I asked in the language that has no words.

That was before the fog season. It is the rain season now. Elders answer in the next season, if they answer. My partner says nothing for a while, and then her palm goes warm against mine, which is her way of saying *go and see.*

---

The cloud breaks along the western edge. The horizontal gold comes through the trees and lays itself on everything, and steam lifts from the moss, from the iron, from the backs of my people. We climb to the lower branches and stand with our arms held out. The lichen on my spine goes faintly luminous. I am the color of the light, and so is every figure in the tree, and for an hour the forest is full of small warm fruit not yet finished growing.

Then I walk up the long slope to the western ridge with my cord dragging its iron behind me. Nobody holds the near end tonight, because I am alone. The weight pulls and snags and releases over the roots, and I keep walking so that something will feel it.

She is where she was. The moss has climbed further up her legs. I sit against her side and put my palm on hers, and it is warm from the sun, and for a long time I feel nothing but the warmth. A drop gathers on a leaf tip above us and falls.

Then, very slowly, something moves in the palm. It may be a pulse. It may be the moss settling as the evening cools, or the slow shifting of a root, or the great sleeping animal of the ridge turning over under both of us. I cannot tell. It is a pressure, a small one, flat and steady, between my shoulder blades, where no hand has ever been.

I don't know whose hand that is. I stay very still and let it count me.
