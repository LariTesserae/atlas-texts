# The Margin

I find myself first in my feet.

I lift the knee too high and set the foot down flat and wait. The paving of Corran East answers under the sole, a little late, or a little early, and I have stopped being able to say which. That is where I begin. I am someone who waits half a breath for the ground to decide about him.

It is early, if it is morning. The cloud has come down onto the domes and stalled there, and the whole plain beyond the last drying racks is beaded and grey-green. The pumps are going. The beam at Fourth Gate has not yet risen, and the *whump* has already come through the soles of my boots, and I stand and watch the beam lift to meet a sound it has already made. The hammermen sit with their backs to it, humming their one held note. I don't blame them. I used to look, and it took me a long while to understand that looking was the mistake.

*Used to.* I notice that word and leave it where it lies.

---

I am a clerk. Or I am the space a clerk leaves.

There is a desk in the Reconcilers' hall, a high sloped one with a brass lamp, and on it lie the two ledgers open side by side, the Seen and the Heard. The columns run down the page in faint blue rule. Between them is a gutter of bare paper about a thumb wide. Nobody writes in it and nobody rules it, and it is the most carefully kept thing in the building. Younger clerks tread lightly near it as if it were a sleeping animal.

I know that gutter well. I could tell you the grain of its paper. That is the first hint about what I am, and I let it come slowly, the way you let the mat's print fill back in.

Here is what I remember, and the doubt about it is part of the memory. A morning, or the previous morning. A pen that wanted to be tidy. A ruler in my hand, the steel edge cold, and the line going down the gutter, thin and black and terribly reasonable. Then a sense of having stepped off a stair that wasn't there. Then a chair, and someone kind saying my name, and my name arriving late, like the pump-beat in the east.

They pensioned me. That part is documented. There is a pension book with my name in a hand I recognize as mine, dated a day I cannot place. The ledgers were re-ruled. I have been told this by three people, and each of them pointed rather than said how long ago.

So I am the clerk who drew the line. I turn that over and it feels correct, and it also feels like a coat someone has handed me that fits too well. I am willing to wear it. I have been wearing it for what I take to be some years, though I don't count them.

---

The pension is small and paid in coin, and the coin goes on a windowsill in a tin.

This is the part I should have led with. I don't live in the hall or the tea shops or the tram-lodgings by the gasworks. I live where the alleys run on longer than the buildings allow, in the old district under the roots of the eldest trees, where the pump sound does not come. I found it the way you find anything on the plateau: I was walking to somewhere else and the wall of one block kept going.

The quiet there is total, and it has a texture. It is not the silence of a room with the door shut. It is the silence of a room where someone has just stopped speaking out of courtesy, and is waiting for you to finish first. The first night I lay awake, seasick, like everyone whose pump has been shut down for repair. The second night I slept. The third, I woke and found I had been humming, one note, not the hammermen's pitch and not any city's. Just the note of the place, or of myself, with nothing to be measured against.

The city people say *lodgers* and mean someone else. They say nobody has met one. I have been the lodger for so long that I can hardly explain how strange that sounds from the outside.

Here is how it goes. I leave the rent in the tin on the sill of a window that is not, strictly, in any wall I can find in daylight. In the morning the tin is empty. In its place there is a square of grey cloth, folded in four, dry, faintly warm, smelling of cellars and something under cellars. I don't know who takes the coin. I have a private theory that it is taken by no one and the tin simply empties, the way a footprint fills. I have another that the one who takes it is also me, some other version of the desk-and-ruler man, who steps a little to the left of the morning and goes on with the work I abandoned.

The cloth I know better. I have unfolded a hundred of them. It is woven finer than any mill in Corran could weave, and there is no warp or weft. It is grain within grain, the texture the cutters find in the deep layers when the peat arranges itself. They stop when they hit it. They close the cut and pack it with fresh moss. I think the cloth is what the ground makes when it is left alone and given something to answer, and the coin is the question, or the courtesy.

---

I should tell you what I do, since a person is what he does and not what he is called.

I keep the gutter.

Not the one in the hall, which is well tended. I mean the other one, the wider margin, the thumb's width of nothing between what is seen and what is heard, between the survey road and the day and a half, between the tree's ticking and the pump's beat. It is as though someone had to hold that space open while everything else was busy being consistent. My drawn line was a kind of wrong answer to a right instinct. I tried to close the margin, and the margin objected, gently, and took me in.

So now I sit in it. At dusk I walk the alley to where it gives onto the open plain, a place that may be nineteen miles from anywhere, and I stand in the grey where the ridges of the mat are coming through like slow swell. The grazers are out, folding their too-many-jointed legs, never sinking. The moths are gathering at the foundries behind me with their lichen wings shut. Somewhere in the hollow trunk of an old sedge-tree the small dark bird is getting ready.

It sings a falling minor third. In the east it comes a little before the gap. I have stood here and heard it sing, and the pump answer it, and I have understood, in the way you understand a thing in your knees, that this is the whole of what I am for. Someone has to be present when the answer comes early, and not flinch, and not rule a line, and not say the number aloud. Someone has to stand in the space where before and after have slackened and say nothing that would tighten them.

When the bird stops, the *whump* comes through the ground into the soles of my boots. I lift my knee too high. I set my foot down flat and wide.

And I wait the half-breath, as I always do, for the ground to decide.

It has decided every time so far. My print fills back in behind me, shivering, over about an hour. Somewhere the trees are keeping their ledger a fraction ahead of mine. In the tin on the sill the coin is already gone, or not yet there, and a square of grey cloth is folded in its place. I will take it in when I get back. I will not count it. I will lay it beside the others and look at it, and if it looks like the rest, that will be enough.
