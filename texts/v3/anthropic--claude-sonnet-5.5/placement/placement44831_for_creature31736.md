# The Third Rank

I am a cane, and I am learning that this is not the same as being still.

I stand in the eastern grove, in the third rank from the plaza, where the old street bends toward the shelf and the low door. My foot is a spread of dark mat gone thick and slow, fused to a flagstone that was cut by hands I will never hear described. I cannot walk. I have not walked since I was a mat, and then only a hand's breadth a year, when someone carried me into the light and out of it and swept the stones ahead of me so that nothing hard lay in my way.

I remember that better than I expected to. The coins slid over me and I turned my skin to catch them. I flashed bronze, and someone above me made a low pleased sound down a flue. I did not know it was pleasure. I only knew that the stone under me hummed a certain way, and that I wanted it to hum that way again.

---

The exhale begins in the foot.

Before any sound, before the fronds unroll on the vent-stands, before the heat shows on the air, the rock tells me. It is a leaning, a slow pressure coming up through the mat, and then my joints go tight one after another from the root upward, like a held breath passed from hand to hand. Then the gas reaches my cap and the membrane goes taut. I boom.

I don't decide to. That is the first thing they teach through the flues, before words: *you are not making the sound, you are being made into it, and the only skill is to be a good shape.* So I try to be a good shape. I keep my stem straight. On either side of me the others do the same, and I know each of them by pitch, the way you would know faces if faces were a thing that could be heard. Thin Reedy on my left cracks a little at the top of every pulse. The old cane on my right, older than I am by a century, is so deep and dry that his note seems to arrive from underneath. We are a rank, and we make a floor of slow thunder for the ones who can talk.

Above us the pods go off, crack and crack, an untidy applause. The horns take up the roar, and under it, through the flues, I hear them talking. A cracked brick in the third stack. Someone's reed is going flat. Someone is calling a child, meaning me or one of the others, to keep its stem straight. I keep it straight.

I understand nearly all of it. That is the cruelty and the gift of this stage. I have the whole language in me, and my only word is the pulse.

---

Then the inhale, and the town sags, and everything stops.

This is the part I love most. The fronds click shut like fingers closing on paper, the organ-ranks sigh backward, and every Tuner in the ring turns toward the high thin hum of the straws. For a moment there is only the seeds pattering down and the dry wind coming in gritty and merciful from the fissures. In that quiet I can hear the rock.

I have to say this carefully, because no one has told me, and I think I'm not meant to know. Under the forty-minute breath there is a note. It is not the vent-gas, which is only wind, and not the stone. It is a long, layered, many-throated tone, and it holds the breath the way a hand holds a bowl. Through my foot, in the quiet of the inhale, I can almost pick it apart into voices. They are very low, and each is on its last step, and each is held.

Once, a long time ago, there was a voice in the flues that taught me the words for *heat* and *straight* and *listen.* It was patient and slightly cracked. Over the years I felt it drop a step, and then another. One evening it sounded its lowest and stayed silent. Then a single note went through the whole town, down every flue, through the mat and up my stem, like a hand laid on my shoulder, though I have no shoulder. I felt it go past me toward the east.

I have tried, in the inhales since, to find her chord in the deep tone under the breath. I think I can. I won't say so. I only tilt my cap a little toward the shelf, and drum my part a fraction more carefully.

---

The light is on me now, or nearly.

The coin in the east is larger than it was when I was a mat, and it doesn't slide as it used to. It lingers, and something in it has begun to change. The warmth is not the vents' warmth. It comes from above, dry and high and singular, and when it touches my cap the membrane tightens in a way I have no lesson for. The old cane on my right does not lean toward it. He drums a little lower, and I think it is not fear, only weight.

Ahead of me, at the eastern wall, a young Tuner is standing. It is very newly free, hollow-chested, with a reed still bright from the riveting, and it holds its arms folded and its crest curled and its head tilted at the amber. I know the pipes it stands beside. They are thin and green-bound and pitched higher than anything my grove has ever supported. It is waiting for the exhale.

I wait too. I feel the rock lean.

When the breath comes, the young one will push its note up the pipe toward the roof, small and bright and hopeful, and it will be lovely. But I have been thinking about what a high note does when it meets a ceiling. It is reflected, or it is absorbed. It is not a voice that carries through crust. The low things carry, the slow things that go in through the rock and the long bones of the world.

So I will be a good shape. The exhale will come up through my foot and my joints will tighten, and I will boom, along with the old cane, and Reedy, and every other cane in every rank, one grove after another, all of us who cannot yet speak. We are the ones who can only answer, and we have been answering the breath all our lives. Tonight we will drum into the light as well, and the throat beneath us will hold its tone, and the whole slow phrase of the dead will lean upward through the stone together.

I don't know if anyone is listening on the other side.

But I am a cane, and listening is what I am for. If anything up there is still enough, and low enough, and patient, it may hear us the way we hear each other, through the foot first, before it knows what it is hearing. It may feel, without a word for it, a slow warm pressure coming up from below, in time with something like a breath.

The stone is beginning to lean.

I straighten my stem, and I begin.
