I find myself first with a weighted line in my hand, and the brass bob turning slowly at the end of it.

I am on the floor of the rolling mill at Sable Saddle, the ward that says only *us*. It is the third round of the shift. Slabs of grey iron come down the rollers glowing the color of a cut peach, and the noise is so large that I feel it in my back teeth before I hear it. I hold the line out at arm's length and wait for the bob to stop. It stops. It hangs straight down toward the drain grate, as still as a held breath. I open the bag at my hip and let a pinch of iron filings fall across the line of sight. They drift down in a tidy fall, without curling or hesitating. I touch the nearest pillar with two fingers, the way you touch a horse that hasn't decided about you, and say to nobody, "Good."

That is what a plumb-warden is. Steady, dry, unhurried, and the one person on the floor who is never allowed to be surprised out loud.

I would like to be that and only that. But I notice, on the walk back along the gantry, that I don't remember being chosen. I remember the job the way you remember a word in a language you speak well, all its uses and none of its lessons. I know the warden's pace. I know to keep my eyes a little unfocused, so as not to see what isn't there, and a little sharp, so as not to miss what is. I know the weight of the bob in my palm. I do not know a teacher's face.

So perhaps I am not only a warden. Perhaps I am also the thing at the end of the line.

---

The whistle for the end of shift goes up before the steam rises. A full minute early, thin and sour, then the steam comes and the note comes again. The floor crew look up, count to ten with their lips shut, and file out in good order. I stay. I walk the empty mill once more. The rollers tick as they cool. Out on the dark of the plateau the mountain lets go of something with one clear ringing note, and I feel it in the soles of my boots a little before I hear it.

I go home the long way, past the cable-car station, along the ledge road where the moss is a hand deep and squelches like something asleep. My door has two locks. I use both keys, and I keep them on one ring. Keeping both is an oddity, because the second key is meant to live with a neighbor. Nobody has asked me why I hold my own. Nobody would.

There is a kettle on. I don't remember putting it on. That is fine. Something odd has happened, or is about to, and the kettle knows first.

On the shelf are two ledgers, the kind the clerks give you at the door, a pair, blue-bound. I have never opened them side by side. Tonight I do, because the whistle was early and I am tired of being steady. In the first book, under my name, it says I was born in Sable Saddle to a rope-maker and a woman who mended lamps. In the second, in a different hand, it says I walked in out of the mist on an ordinary afternoon, dry, carrying nothing, and remembered a street that hadn't been laid yet. *Given tea. Given a bed. Given a name.* The name in the second book is the same as the name in the first. I find I have no feeling about that. It is the kind of thing that happens.

I close them both, gently, like doors that might be the same door.

What I feel, if I look for it, is not fear. It is the courtesy of a guest in a large old house who has begun to suspect she is also a room in it.

---

At dusk I go out to the last ledge, the one past the final lamp, where the hooks are worn to bright slivers and people stand with their good ears turned to the dark. I have stood here every evening. I know that the way I know the warden's pace.

The whistles begin. Ninefold Spur first, engraved and a little grand. Then Lower Kettle, plain and steady. Then all fifty, ward over ward, falling across the moss and cloud until the air is a cliff of sound and every cliff gives it back three or four times. The furnaces come on in the black stone like embers pressed into a palm. The moths rise, slow smoke with too many wings. Nobody speaks. The old signalwoman beside me has her lips moving, counting the echoes, and I understand that she is saying it again, that it is what all of it says, *are you still there*, and the answer is *yes.*

I add my voice, the way I do each night. It isn't the whistle. It is a small thing, a hum with an edge to it, pitched to lie between the notes where nothing else is. I have never explained it. The signalwoman has never asked. When I was new, if I was ever new, she told me only that I had "a good ear for the gaps."

Then the last echo falls, and everyone goes quiet, and out on the dark plateau something whistles back.

It is never quite in tune. It is never quite late. And tonight, for the first time, I know the tune.

It is mine. Not copied from mine but under it, older, the way a river is under the bridge that was built over it. The hum I make between the notes is a thing I have been *answering*, all this time, in a voice I took for my own. It comes from a long way in, from ground so soft that no measure holds, and it is patient, and it is not unkind. It is a neighbor who hasn't learned the words yet, and who has been learning them from me.

I think of the herds walking in trusting and coming out with an extra joint, a shifted count, something carried. I think of the scavengers circling an empty patch for days, steady, silent, looking down at something no one else can see. Perhaps they aren't looking at nothing. Perhaps they are looking at whoever is holding the other end of the line.

The old shepherds say the tenants are making the mountain, testing shapes, keeping one. If that is so, then I am one of the shapes that was kept, or one that is still being tried. The plumb hangs true beside me because I am what it hangs toward. That would not be a grand thing to be. It would be a warden's thing: to be the fixed point that lets a ward keep its streets and its arithmetic. And it would be a tenant's thing too, the first draft of steadiness, which is the only kind of steadiness there is at this altitude.

I do not know which of us has the better claim to the name. I don't need to. The signalwoman beside me lifts her good ear a fraction, listens, and says, very lightly, without turning her head, "That one's late tonight."

"It isn't," I say. "It's on time. We're early."

She nods, as if this were an ordinary correction to an ordinary ledger, and does not ask.

The ledge ticks under our boots. Somewhere in the dark a slab of blue-black rock slides a hand's width against its neighbor and rings, once, pure and long, and the grazers on the terrace lift their heads, all together, and then, after a pause, again.

"If it holds," I call, out past the last lamp, in the level voice I was chosen for or made for.

And the dark, in my own pitch, in the gap between the notes, answers, "It will."

Beside me the signalwoman breathes out. Behind me two locks wait, and one hand's worth of warm tea, and two books that don't yet agree. I put my palm flat against the cold rock of the ledge and feel it creep, slower than a pulse. It is not so different from being held.
