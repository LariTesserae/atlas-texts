# The Third of Three

I am the one who looks up.

Marrow has the hammer and Pell has the broom, and I have a stiff-brimmed hat, a tick on my belt I almost never use, and a neck that has learned to hold a angle other necks won't. In a crew of three the third is the watcher. Nobody warns you how much of the job is being useless, standing in the spray of lime dust with your face tipped to the stone, waiting for something you hope won't happen.

We are on Cistern Row, cleared to the fourth doorway. The street is closing behind us as we open it in front. If I turned around, I could watch the white creep back over the first door we freed this morning. I don't turn around. That's not my direction.

---

Here is what I listen for, though *watch* is the word the trade uses.

The wall above us ticks. Every wall does. A million lips of stone let go their drops onto ledges and tin and slate, and after enough years you stop hearing a sound and start hearing a *grain*, the way a weaver hears cloth. The grain of a sound wall is even and unhurried, a little different at each height. Near the cornice it's brighter, because the drops fall on slate. Lower down it's duller, on flowstone.

When a slab is going to come away, the grain changes first. A drop that used to fall from the lip now runs down the face instead. Somewhere a ledge that used to tick has gone quiet, because the water has found a new road behind the stone. You lose one voice from the chorus, and then, if you're lucky and you've been listening, you hear another voice start up in the wrong place, a thin bright trickle where the wall has no business trickling.

I can't teach this. Marrow tried to teach it to Pell for two seasons, and Pell is a wonderful sweeper and will die in a gutter someday with a hand still on the broom. The ear either takes it or it doesn't. Mine took it when I was nine, because my grandmother's wall closed over our upper window and I lay awake three winters listening to the house get smaller.

---

Between the strokes of the engine, Marrow signs to me, one flat hand at hip height. *Above?*

*Steady,* I sign back. I'm not sure I mean it.

I've been hearing something I can't place for the last quarter hour. The street is the ordinary street. The well-hall two blocks down gives its slow beat, one every few seconds, and I feel it in my sternum, where I've felt it my whole life. The mains hiss. A water-seller is crying up the row with the brass lid of his jug clacking against the rim. A grazer's herd is crossing the north face of the granary, and I can hear the dry broom-whisper of forty thousand shells, rising a little, the way they do when they want to hurry and can't.

Under all that, at the very edge of it, the grain is wrong.

I step back to the far kerb and look up along the wall of the Row's east side, where the buildings lean toward each other over the street. The face is pale and beaded and dark with wet. A blue film sits on the lower courses. Above that the cornice is crowded with palmers' niches, their little dark mouths already stirring with the afternoon.

And above the cornice, on the wall behind the roofline, where the old district climbs the slope, is the face I've been avoiding looking at.

---

It is a niche-wall. It runs along the top of the row for perhaps sixty yards, cold water sheeting over it from the cistern-channel we cleared last spring. The travertine has closed over the older niches in smooth pale swellings, like knuckles under skin. The newer ones are still open, dark rectangles with a wrapped shape inside and the glint of a tick.

The rota skips this wall. It does by law and by something older than law. Nobody puts a hammer to a face in the stone.

The wrong grain is coming from there.

I hear it now clearly, a thin new trickle low on the wall, and beside it a quiet place where a steady drip has stopped. The water has gone somewhere else. The wall behind the dead is loosening. Not a niche face, not yet. But a shelf of flowstone thirty feet across, hung from the rock above, and under it forty years of the beloved, wrapped and laid down and closing over. If that shelf lets go it falls on the street, on us, on the water-seller, on whoever is passing. It also opens a hole in the wall that no one has the right to open.

I know what the trade says. A watcher who sees a slab moving calls the crew clear. That's all the job is. You call clear, and you don't argue with the wall.

But you can't call clear on a niche-wall and then walk away from it. Somebody will have to go up.

---

Calling is the one thing I do out of rhythm. All my life I've spoken in the gaps, the slack between beats, and my mouth knows the timing better than my mind. To shout on the stroke, into the heart of the engine's pulse, is to break the courtesy of the whole city. Everyone in earshot will feel it, like a shove.

I take a breath and wait for the beam to drop. I hear it fall through the paving. And I shout across the top of it, *Clear! East wall! Clear the row!*

Marrow drops the hammer. Pell drops the broom. I've never seen either of them move so fast, and then I see them do the thing the trade teaches, which is to stop, a body's length back, and look at where I'm looking.

The water-seller stops with his lid mid-clack. Down the street a woman on a step turns her face up with her hand still on her door. I don't hear the gulpers. It takes me a moment to notice. In the drain under the kerb, where a chorus has been clucking all afternoon, there is no sound at all.

They know already. They knew before I did.

---

I'd like to say that the wall came down, or that it didn't, and finish it that way. But the truth of the trade is that most days it's neither. We stand in the street with our hats tilted back and the dust settling on our sleeves, and above us the shelf of flowstone hangs exactly where it was, dripping in the wrong place. Nothing falls. The engine beats. The afternoon rain begins, slow and warm and fine, the way it always does, and it falls on the niche-wall and on our hats and on the water-seller's jug.

By evening there will be a crew of wardens and a foreman from the wall-office and, I suppose, an elder from the families with a bowl of clean water. There will be a long, quiet argument about who is allowed to touch what. The palm-keepers will go up first, because they are the only ones who can. They'll fix a prop where the water has run wrong. It'll be done by hand, with wedges, with no hammer, in a night, and nobody will speak of it much again.

And I'll stand in the street until the light turns amber, because I'm the watcher and it's still my wall. I'll listen for the missing voices to come back.

When they do, it isn't the drip I hear first. It's the drain. One gulper at first, low, and then a second answering, and then the whole street's chorus rising through the grate, red plumes flaring in the dark. The sound of the city being all right.

Marrow signs to me across the street, one flat hand. *Above?*

I tilt my head back. The palmers are lifting, first a few, then a rush, a dark roaring ribbon across the last of the light, and every face on the row tips up with mine to watch it go.

*Steady,* I sign.

This time I mean it.
