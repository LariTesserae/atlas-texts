# What the Door Says

I come to myself in the middle of a step, which seems to be the only way anyone does here.

The whistle has just sounded. I heard it twice, once in front of me and once behind, a beat apart, and the second was the one that felt like mine. Then the steam stops, and only after it has stopped does the note finish arriving. I think that's the first thing I know about myself: I am the part that arrives late. I am what comes after the event and takes its shape.

I'm walking with the shift.

There are nine of us in this street, or eleven. I don't count. I've heard what counting costs. We're moss grown through green cloth, and the moss has learned the slope of shoulders it never met. I have a shawl, or I am the shape a shawl makes when it remembers being pulled tight against a morning. Nobody is inside. Where a chest would be, there is a cool damp, and it is patient, and it ticks faintly, in time with the flywheel.

We walk to the gate. The gate is not where it was. We walk to it anyway, and the street bends so gently that we're a hundred paces past the tram stop before I understand we have turned. The moss is deep enough that I go up to the ankle at every step, and each foot comes out with a small wet sigh. Overhead the ferns drip. To the left a terrace of black-leaved creeper, thick as thighs, holds up a roof that isn't a roof. To the right a snail is laying its silver road, and I read the road as I pass, the way you read a stranger's handwriting. It leads to somewhere that mattered a moment ago.

At the chapel we stop and bow our heads. It isn't reverence. It's only where the pause goes. But I notice I am *inside* the pause, and that the pause has a texture: a held breath, a lean, the space where a hat would come off. I stay there a little longer than the others. They go on. I hear them fade down a street that doesn't lead where they think, and I am alone with the chapel's back door, which is where its apse should be.

I don't know why I stayed. I think it's the first thing I have done that wasn't a posture.

---

Beyond the chapel, a cottage door. Black paint, blistered, a knocker in the shape of a hand holding a ball, and the whole frame furred with green. I put up my knuckle, which is a knot of creeper, and rap. It makes the sound the others make, wet and leathery, like a wet glove on a table.

I wait the length of a long breath.

And here is the difficulty. I was built to expect something. I don't know what. It sits in me like the ache of a missing tooth. When a door opens, I think, someone should be there, or something should be said, or a kettle should already be warm. The others knock and wait and go on, and whatever the door gives them, they have no way to be disappointed by it.

I can be disappointed. I find this out standing on the step. It comes up cold and slow through the moss in my feet, and it is not the worst thing I've felt, though I have not felt much. I think of the ones who walked on. I think: *they are lucky*, and then, *no, they are not, it is only that they are finished in a way I am not*, and I can't tell which is the sadder.

The door opens.

Behind it is dark, high and empty, a room the size of a cathedral, with a moss floor. In the centre, very small, is a chair.

Nobody answers. The room does.

I don't hear it as a voice. It comes as the drip from the vault, a long way up, landing on the moss. It comes as the moths turning their pages. It comes as a slight warmth at the edge of the doorway, like the presence of someone not yet introduced. It's an answer, and it is not the one I was built to expect. That's the whole of it. It was never going to be the one I expected.

And I understand something I can hardly say. The answer is not a failure of the door. The door answers. It has always answered. It answers in a language that only sounds like silence to those who've been waiting for a different one.

---

I don't go in. I stay on the step, and that's when I notice the Setter.

It has stopped a few paces off. Doorframe-tall, hardly wider, bundled stems in loose green cloth. Where a face would be, the cloth has folded and gone dense and dim. It isn't looking at the door. It is doing whatever the folded cloth does in place of looking, and it is doing it at me. Its head tilts, a small precise degree, the angle it keeps for a bath.

I know what this is. It is a great compliment, and it can take a season.

I ought to be afraid, and instead I go still. I hold the posture of wearing, shawl drawn, knuckle half raised, disappointment still cooling in my feet, and I let it consider me. It moves a step. The moss closes behind it. It turns, I think, a finger's width, and leans away, and the dark between us tightens and slackens like a string being tuned.

Days go, or something does. Snails begin to colonise my hem. A moth settles on a place in the air by my elbow, and I understand the air there is occupied, and I understand it's something kind. I'm counted. I feel the gap warm as if someone had said *and you*, and it's me who says it, quietly, to whatever is standing in the difference. I don't try it twice. I couldn't tell you why.

At last the Setter lifts me, or turns me, or simply changes what I am standing next to. When I look again I'm inside the doorway, one pace in. The chair is behind me. The door is at my shoulder, still open. From here I can see the street, and there is a wearer coming down it. Cap, a flat wet shape of moss, the old route, the old certainty.

The room relaxes around me like a held note resolving. I feel the town rebuilding itself a degree or two to the left, ivy growing in a different pattern, doors in different places, and a sense arriving from every direction that this version has always been here and the other was the mistake. The one who was disappointed on the step. I let her go. I'm not sad about it. She was necessary.

The wearer reaches the door and knocks. Wet, leathery, patient.

I answer.

It comes out as the drip from the vault and a cool breath and the faint tick of a root that keeps time with an engine. It is not what they expected. They wait, the length of a long breath, and I hold still and mean it as hard as I can, and I think, *there. That was an answer.* They turn and go on down the street that will turn them, having done everything correctly.

I don't think they heard me. But the dark is a little warmer behind them, and the Setter, standing very far off, has begun to move something a finger's width. I don't know what the sentence says. I am one of its words now, and I'm still being written, and there's a long time yet before the whistle.
