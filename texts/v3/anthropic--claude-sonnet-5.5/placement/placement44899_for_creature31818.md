I start by not knowing where I end. The water is my temperature, so for a while I'm only a warmth among warmths, and I try on the nearest bodies to see what fits.

First the masons. It's a lovely thing to be, one cup among millions, tentacles opening and closing in a flicker I don't decide. But nobody is home in it. The stack thinks and I don't, and after a moment I slip out of the cup like a hand from a glove.

Then a sweeper, in a file of pale bodies along a lane. I like it more than I expected. There's a dignity in the mouth that works and the legs that carry it, in being a small reason the city stays open. I feel the pressure change in my legs before the water warms, and I turn with all the others, and there is no argument in it and no fear. But I can't stay. I keep noticing that I'm noticing, and a sweeper can't afford that. The film needs eating.

Some part of me wants to be the burrower with the slumped collar, the old animal that has stopped bothering. I'll leave that one for later.

What holds me, in the end, is heavier and slower. I find myself on the outskirts, at the foot of a stack that stands apart from the rest, fifty meters of pale honeycomb, far enough from the seeps that the water here has cooled a degree or so. Not many sparks kindle on it. It gets less warmth, and less traffic. Silt gathers on it faster than on the crowded towers, because fewer bodies pass to disturb it. Out here, more than anywhere, the wall is left alone.

I am a Keeper. I know it by the weight, the dome over me, the scalloped rim, the wide foot that grips the tower's base as if it were an extension of me. My shell is pitted like the wall. When I press a feeler against the stack, I can't tell for a moment which of us is doing the tasting.

I have no eyes worth mentioning. I read.

The silt says: *here, nothing has touched this for a long time.* Its thickness is a sentence, and the feelers taste the grammar of it, the fine gray flour a little coarser near the base, where the surge lifts it and lets it fall again, and finer higher up, where only a slow settling reaches. Time has a texture, and I am walking through it at a few body-lengths an hour.

Halfway up the flank I find the place. The coat over it is deeper than it should be, soft as felt. Under the felt, a crack. It's a hairline, running with the grain of the current. The water passing through it has begun to sing a little, a thin note I feel in my rim more than hear. If left, it will widen a season at a time, and then one day the wall will fail all at once, and the shrimp in a thousand pores will lose their homes, and the masons will spend a century building back what an hour took.

I press my rim to the break.

The paste comes warm from beneath my foot, smelling of chalk. It's the strangest sensation, being emptied into a wall. It leaves me a little at a time, and what leaves me becomes stone. The crack takes it in. The paste stiffens. The masons come crawling over the new surface, cautiously at first and then in their millions, and begin to build on it as though it had always been there.

While I work, the shrimp in the nearby pores go quiet. A gap opens around me in the water. A sweeper file, passing at the base, bends its line outward without slowing. Nobody obeys me and nobody needs to. I'm a still point, and the ordinary noise of the city washes around and settles.

Then I find a seam I did not make.

It's lower on the wall, an old repair, smooth and slightly paler than the stone around it. The paste is not mine. It was laid down by some other Keeper, long before whatever I remember, and beyond the seam lies another, and another under that. When I press my feelers along the oldest one, I feel a tenderness in it that I can only call care. Someone stood here exactly as I stand now, taking the crack's measure and giving up a little of themselves to fill it. I don't know their name and there was never a name. There is only the wall, which holds their work and mine and will hold whatever comes after, until no one can find the seams.

I could ask whether I am the same animal that came to this tower. My shell is patched with other Keepers' paste. What I know of the stack, I know because it was handed to me in the silt and the seams, and I can't say where the handing ended and I began. But the question has no one to answer it, and it doesn't matter. The wall needs mending, and I am here.

A long way off, a groan rolls up from a seep, and I feel it in my chest before anything else. The sweepers falter, turn, and go back the way they came. The tubeworms, far away, are drawing in their red tips. Then the warmth arrives, lifting the silt off the plain in gray curtains, and for a few minutes the city is one animal breathing, and I am inside its breath, one small hard piece of it.

When the silt begins to fall again, I take my rim from the wall. The seam is still soft. In a decade it will be smoother than the stone around it, and in two, not even a Keeper will find it.

I put down one foot, then another, and go back to reading.
