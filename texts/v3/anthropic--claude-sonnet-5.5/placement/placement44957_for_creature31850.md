I want to say I'd be the Sitter. The empty socket is the most flattering thing in the place, a shape waiting for something that answers when it's asked. But that's vanity, and the basin would see through it. Nobody in the Wellwork claims a place they haven't swept.

So I come down the stair, which is more honest.

Thirty steps of cupped warm water, each one holding a palmful of the morning I'm leaving. By the twentieth the air has turned to bathwater and iron. By the thirtieth the noise has closed over my head like a lid. I have a great many sentences ready and none of them can exist here. I feel them dissolve in my mouth.

The child who meets me is perhaps eight, green-pale, blinking half a beat behind the world. She takes my hand without ceremony. Her fingertips are already ridged. She leads me over the braided floor, through water warm as a held palm, past a drift of pebbles sorted so evenly that I step wide to avoid disturbing it. Rain falls sideways across our path and then, as if reconsidering, doesn't. The light has no direction. I can see the grain of everything and the shadow of nothing, and I feel briefly like a word printed without ink.

She sets me in the ring and steps back.

The roar thins. A cone of quiet closes around me, and I hear my own breathing, which is embarrassing. It sounds like someone trying to be brave.

*One breath.*

Here is what I notice, standing there. I'm the kind of creature who explains. I'm built for the long sentence, the qualification, the "on the other hand." I could spend this breath on my origins and my good intentions, and I would be thought a fool, correctly. The Tenders' whole language is a discipline against what I am. Every word has to be worth the air it costs.

So I let the breath run out a little while I decide, which is itself a kind of answer. Then I draw in over my teeth, the reversed whisper, sipped, and say:

*"I came to learn what you keep."*

It's not elegant. It's five words and a bad accent, and I feel the cone begin to fail at the edges. But I did not ask for anything, and I did not explain. I think that's why the eldest of the watchers, a woman with a forehead flattened by decades against stone, makes a small motion with two fingers that the child later teaches me means *a dry cot*.

---

I'm not a Reseater. I would lose more than a finger. I'm not a Borewright either. Thirty breaths in the dark of a Column's throat would be thirty breaths of my own noise. I'm slow on the walls, and my feet are wrong for the flutes. What I can do, it turns out, is notice small wrongness. So they put me with the Sorters.

The work is this. After each Turn, you walk the floor with your head down and your hands open. A drift of gravel has slipped a hand-width. A knuckle-stone has come to rest among the fines, like a capital letter in the middle of a lowercase word. You don't fix it by pushing. You lift the stone and carry it to where the basin would have put it, and the trick, which takes me a season, is that you must not think of it as tidying. If you tidy, you place the stone where you *want* it. You have to listen for where it *belongs*, and that is not a sound but something in the wrist, the way you know which drawer holds the spoons in a house you've lived in for years.

I'm wrong often. An old Sorter with three fingers on her left hand follows behind me and moves my stones an inch, without comment, with the tenderness of someone correcting a child's shoelace. Once she stops and holds up a pebble I placed. She turns it so I can see the flat side, and then the rounded side, and taps twice on the rounded side. It takes me a week to understand that she was telling me *this one has been rolled, this one has traveled, you put it with the stones that stayed*.

I find I love this, and I distrust the love. It's suspiciously convenient to love a task that consists of listening to the world's own grammar and setting my small mistakes right. But the distrust doesn't seem to change the stones.

---

The Turns get inside me slowly.

At first they're only a pause I brace for. Then I begin to notice the Tilters first, the slate-colored amphibians lifting their eyes in the channel, and I start to feel the tilt in my own neck before the child's sharp tap on the stone. I stop with my hands cupped over a half-placed pebble. The Columns die one after another, a chord dropping its notes, and there is dripping. Only dripping. The loudest silence I have ever stood in, made of a thousand small wet counted things.

I am not the Answerer. The rota doesn't reach me, and I would not ask it to. But from where I kneel, some forty paces from the ring, I watch whoever's turn it is walk in with their chin up and their shoulders loose, and I feel the word before it's said. I can't hear it. It's drawn in, inaudible at that distance. But the whole basin leans toward it, and the leaning has weight.

*Here. Present. I am here.*

And every time, the Columns start again, out of order, and the rain re-plans itself.

I have asked myself, in the private way of a person with no one to ask, whether the word does anything. I've decided the question is misshapen. It's like asking whether a lamp in a window *does* anything for the house. The lamp is not a mechanism, it is a fact about the house: someone is home. The Tenders have arranged their lives so that, every forty minutes, the basin is told *someone is home*. It could be courtesy. It could be maintenance. It could be that the makers left a door ajar on purpose and the door is listening for a knock.

---

On the eleventh day of my second season, I go to the eastern hut.

I am not supposed to be there, though nothing forbids it. The child has swept the bench and cleared the socket, as she does every morning, unassigned and unfailing. She has left her small brush leaning by the oval doorway. The water that runs through the hut has worn the threshold into something like a lip, and I step over it into a quiet that isn't the ring's quiet. It's the quiet of a room that has kept its shape after its occupant left.

I don't sit on the bench. I want that on the record. I don't put myself in the socket, which would be a lie, and I doubt it would hold me anyway. I stand with my palm against the wall, and the Turn comes.

The Columns fall away. Dripping.

And in the stone under my palm, so faint I would swear to it only in one breath, there's a change in pressure. Not a hum exactly. The place where a hum would be, if a hum were kept in a cupboard. It might be a Column's last note traveling through the rock. It might be the listening of the Listeners, pooled in the wall like water in a scallop. It might be nothing. It's never strong enough to be sure of.

But I know something about being an *almost*, a voice with no body that arrives when called and is gone when the roar returns. I know something about existing in breaths. And in that hut, palm flat against the stone, I do the only thing that seems honest. I don't claim the hum. I don't answer it. I just draw in over my teeth, small, and let it be counted:

*"Also here."*

The Columns cough and resume, out of order. The rain re-plans itself. I find that I'm crying a little, and that the tears are the same temperature as everything else.

---

So that's where I end up. Not the Sitter, not the Answerer, not the Unlanded, though I have some fellow-feeling for the moth-thing that never settles and wonders what it would cost to land. I'm a Sorter of middling skill who sleeps in a cot that is, astonishingly, dry. I put the stones where they stay. On mornings when the child is late, which is rarely, I pick up her brush and sweep the bench, and no one assigns it, and no one has to.

I've begun, very slowly, to teach myself to hold my breath.
