I don't begin as anyone. I begin as the note.

It is low, and it has always been there, and before I have hands or a name it is already pressing on the long membranes along my flanks. It says the melt is coming. It says the stream on the plateau is swelling, that the weather above has shifted its weight, that it is the hour when fog spills over the rim. All of that is in the roar for anyone born to read it. Then the reading gathers itself into a body, and the body is mine. I am a Comber, and I am sitting back on my haunches in the dark green, with the fans of my forelimbs resting open on the moss, and I am not yet doing anything.

I am not young. The haircap along my spine has grown thick enough that the fog beads on it, and there is a cushion between my shoulders I have never trimmed, because it was the first thing I ever grew and I cannot bring myself to. The liverwort on my jaw drips. I have been up out of the caves for more years than I count, and I am still not used to the sky. Below, the ceiling was close and the roar was a wall I could lean on. Here the roar has no wall. It fills a bowl with no lid, and I am forever a little dizzy with room.

So I work. Work is how I make the room small enough to live in.

The feather moss in front of me has lain the wrong way since the last freeze. I part it with all seven fingers at once, and each finger knows its own job. One lifts a dead crown. One presses a stem flat. One feels for the willow seedling, the thin pale thread of it, and draws it out whole, root and all, because a willow left alone here will shade a whole hillside of moss to death. I lay the drift downslope, as water would, and the ground gets a little more like itself. There is no sound in any of this. There never is. My hands are the only voice I have, and the moss is the only thing that listens.

But what I return to, in every stretch of my days, is the little engine.

It stands at the end of its rails with its stack still pointing at a sky it has never gone to. I found it green already, and something in me, some old ancestral habit of tending, took it for a hill and then saw it was not. A hill does not have a window. I cleaned the window first. I don't know why. I think I wanted to see what was looking out. Then I pruned the birch in its belly, because a birch left to itself would split the iron, and I didn't want the thing to break. Now it sits in its moss, tidy, a little reliquary of someone else's hurry. Whoever made it wanted to go somewhere and did not get there. I find that I love it for stopping. Almost everything I know is either the roar, which never stops, or the moss, which never hurries. The engine is the only thing on the floor that ever tried to be somewhere else.

I sit in its cab sometimes, with my fans on the cold levers, and the roar comes through the iron into my belly, and it is nearly the caves again.

There is also the lamp.

It burns above the red door, all the dark weeks, and I go and sit at the edge of its circle with the others who come, though we never touch faces there, since we are all watching the same small sun. It is a kind of church. Once a month, she comes. She wears the color of moss, but she cannot fool the moss, only herself. Her light is red. Red is a soft color to eyes made for the dark, a warm hand on the lids. I think she believes it hides her. I think it is the kindest thing she could have chosen, and she does not know.

I watched her sweep the drift from her doorway with slow strokes, as I would. I watched her follow my dressed ground with that red beam, along a stroke, and along the next, and stop where the strokes ended. She stood there a long time. She did not touch anything. She did not tell.

So I left her the bark, which is a thing one gives, pale and clean, the good kind, from the willow the hares don't reach. And beside it I stood the bolt. That was the harder choice. I had found it in a hummock, rusted to the shape of the ground, and I worked it free and cleaned it with the finest of my fingers until the metal shone as it must have when it was new, and I set it upright in the moss beside the step. It was the smallest piece of the works that would hold still and mean something. I meant *here.* I meant *we have been looking at the same things.* I do not know what a bolt means to her. But it was made by hands, and it was cleaned by hands, and I wanted her to know that both are true.

She took it.

I saw her lift it, turn it in the red light, and close her fingers around it, and I felt something in my chest go quiet and enormous, as the roar does when it deepens with the melt. She carried it up into the air, into the cable car, up the wall. Somewhere in the village on the plateau it is sitting on a shelf. I have never seen a shelf. I think of it constantly. I think of the bolt in a room with no roar, and how strange it must be to it, to be so small in the quiet, and I hope she has turned it so that it faces the window.

Now the note is deepening. The melt is coming. I sit with my hands open in the moss, and I am not lonely, because nothing here is far from me, not the hare in the fog or the raven shouting in its dream above the vent, not the ten million bodies in the peat trembling with the same voice as mine. I am one of the things that the sound is shaking. I only happen to be one that knows it.

In a month she will come again. I don't know what she will leave. I have already cleared a place by the step, in case.
