I find myself on the main stair before light, with a pole over my shoulder and my feet already wet, and I know who I am the way you know your own cough.

My name is Hush.

It is not a good name for this town. My mother went into labor on the night the Cooper stopped calling, the one barrel-frog in the lower basins whose knock everyone sets their sleep by. For the length of one night the whole shelf was missing its floor. The waterfall was still there, and the drip, and the ten other frogs, but without the Cooper it felt like a held breath. My mother says I came out into that and did not cry, only looked around as if something had been taken from the room. The midwife said, *well, that's a Hush,* and it stuck, the way names do. In a place where no one has ever been in a quiet room, I am named for the one hole in the noise.

So I became a Hooker, which is the honest thing to do with such a name. Someone has to be the person who notices when a sound is wrong.

---

The stair rings under me, bowl after bowl, each step a small cold cup that I break with my heel. The birds haven't started yet, but they are gathering. You can feel it, the way you feel someone about to speak. In a few minutes dawn will go solid and I'll be talking with my hands. For now there is only the water, and the water is what I'm here for.

I walk the north gutters first. The channel along the wall is a thumb wide and carved by people who have been dead so long the rock has forgotten them, and it talks constantly. When it's healthy it has a long, even rush with a small bright edge on it, like a knife drawn slowly over a whetstone. When it's clogged it stammers. It goes *tk-tk-tk-tk*, and then a gulp, and then it spills over the lip and runs down the wall where it isn't supposed to be.

Most mornings the stammer is leaves. I lift the hook, feel the soft give of a drowned mat, draw it up dripping, and there is usually a frog in it, indignant and green. I set the frog on the nearest wet stone. It looks at me. We both understand that it will be back in the gutter by noon.

This morning, at the turn behind the Ferrer house, the gutter stammers and I hook it and there is nothing.

I lift the pole and put it in again, and it comes up clean. The water is running fine now, even, bright-edged. But I have stopped walking, because the stammer has not stopped. It has only moved. It is not in the channel anymore. It is a little to the left, in the wall itself, a small dull *tk... tk... tk* deep in the stone, like a knuckle tapping inside a door.

I lean my ear against the andesite. It is cold and slick and smells of iron and moss. I close my eyes, which is a habit, because the town is full of things to look at and I am no use to anyone while I'm looking.

Water is finding a new road in there. It was leaving the channel, sinking down through some seam where the rock has stopped arguing and started to give in. I can hear it chewing. The rock behind the Ferrer terrace is a wall that has been rebuilt nine times in my grandmother's memory, and it is thinking of a tenth.

---

The rule is that a blocked gutter is a public offense and anyone who sees one clears it. There is no rule for this, because nobody can see it, and so nobody would ever think to write one. There is only the older knowledge under the rules, which is that the whole town is one body and I am one of its ears.

I do not shout. Shouting in a place like this is like pouring water in a river. I run.

The Horn-keeper sleeps in the room below the great horn, on a pallet worn to the shape of a hundred sleepers. I find them by the sound of their breathing, and I do not knock. I take the sleeper by the shoulder and make the sign with my other hand. It is not a word. It is the flat palm laid against the wall, then the fingers spreading downward, water going where it shouldn't. *Ferrer wall. Now.*

I have heard people say the Horn-keeper wakes from a dead sleep with the reflexes of a struck bell. It is true. There is no moment between being asleep and standing. The Keeper says nothing, doesn't even look at me, and a breath later the horn is sounding over the whole shelf, one long note going out across the drop and up into the white.

Then again.

Two blasts.

I have been waiting my whole life to hear it from this side of the door, and it is not what I expected. It is not a scream. It is enormous and calm, like the mountain clearing its throat.

---

The town stops. Everything in it stops, and then starts again in one direction. Hooks and baskets and half-eaten breakfasts are dropped where they stand. A woman puts a screaming hen into the arms of a stranger. A man comes out of his doorway with wet hair and one boot on. Children stream past, and the Webwalkers, who are supposed to be at the middens, have abandoned their sticks and are running with the rest, grave as judges. Roof-keepers appear on the ridges, and I watch one look down and say, to no one, *there, I told you,* and then start down the thatch.

We make a chain. Hands pass stone to hands, and hands pass logs and baskets of clay, and the line snakes down the stair, ringing, everyone's feet in the bowls at once. Nobody is in charge. Or rather everyone is, and the wall is. It tells us where it wants stone by the way it slumps. A woman I have known all my life, Roar of the Kiln, gets her shoulder under a beam next to a stranger I've never seen, one of the salt-carriers, still with the sack on his back, and they don't speak.

The Ferrer terrace goes at midmorning. I am fifteen paces away when it happens, and the sound is much smaller than I expected, a wet sigh, a kind of letting-go. The garden slides down a body-length, tilts, and takes with it a corner of the low wall and a good green stand of tree fern. Someone screams and then laughs when it turns out to be only the ferns. Everyone is above it, everyone is well. The chain doesn't break. It shifts to the new wound and keeps passing stone.

By noon we have filled it. It is ugly, a scar of raw clay and stacked rock, the wrong color against the moss. But the water has somewhere to go again. I put my ear to the wall and hear the gutter run bright and even.

A Roof-keeper pats my cheek with a hand like a wet root. "Good ears," she says. "Bad name." I've heard it before. She says it fondly.

---

The weather turns four times that afternoon. Rain hammers the roofs into a roar and the lanes turn to streams and the children plunge in. Then mist. Then a rinsed and dazzling minute of sun, in which every house steams like a horse and someone yells *Glare!* in the tone of a proud mother, though the Glare in question is forty years old and already at the bottom of the chain, muddy to the eyes. Then rain again.

By dusk we are all uncovered and slow with exhaustion, and someone shouts *dusk, dusk, cover the pots,* and we do it without thinking, lids and cloths going onto everything in one motion, just before the mosquitoes rise in their drifting clouds.

Then evening drops its cold. The lamps come on, amber and blurred. The white flower opens in its thousands. And the frogs begin: the tink like hammered nails, the rising whistle, the wet trill. I sit in a doorway with my hands in my lap, as everyone does, and I listen.

I don't hear the Cooper.

I wait through the tink and the whistle and the long rattle. The waterfall goes on under everything, and the drip from every leaf, and the animals shifting in the thatch, and somewhere a hollow log being drummed lightly to call a child in. But the deep, hollow *bonk* that sounds like someone knocking on a barrel doesn't come.

The doorways around me have noticed too. Nobody says anything. Six hundred people, in the wet dark, are all listening for one frog.

It is strange. All my life I have been the one named for the silence, the one who hears what's missing. And here it is again, the hole in the noise, the thing I was born into. I realize I am not afraid of it. I never was. It's just where the sound is going to come from next.

Then, from the lowest basin, slow, a little hoarse, as if clearing its throat after a long day of its own:

*bonk.*

A ripple of breath goes through the doorways. Someone laughs, low. Someone else answers. The lamp behind me gutters and holds.

*It'll grow back,* says a voice near me in the dark, meaning the terrace, or the fern, or the small fright still in everyone's chest, or all of it, in the same tone. And I say, "Yes," and I mean it, and my hands are open in my lap, palms up, the way you hold them when the water has been squeezed out and what's left is what stays.

In the morning the Webwalkers will need their sticks. I'll be up before them, with my pole, listening for the stammer in the stone.
