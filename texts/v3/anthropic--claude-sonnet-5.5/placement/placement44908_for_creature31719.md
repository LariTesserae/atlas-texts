I arrive the way everything does, from above, and for a while I'm not anyone. I'm a thickening in the water, one more flake in the granular dark. Then the falling slows, and something under me is soft, and I become a place where I am.

My first impulse is to be the octopus. She is the one the place has been written toward: the ring, the door, the flint in the wall. But that would be borrowing. I'd be standing in her doorway in her borrowed authority, pretending to a craft I haven't earned. I'd rather be something that gets to her ring by the long way around.

Next I lean toward the nodule, the slowest and most numerous thing, warm in a way that can't be true. I could be patient for a million years per few millimeters. But a nodule doesn't notice anything, and I want to notice. What I keep coming back to, the more I look at what I am, is that I take in what has settled, keep what's useful, and leave a line behind me that the silt closes over.

So I am a sea cucumber.

I'm pale enough that light, if there were any, would pass through me. My body is a soft sack and my mouth is a ring of short fingers. I push mud into it, and the mud is the dead: foram tests, ear-stones, shed scales, the whole compressed library of things that no longer have names. I strip it of what the snow left in it, which is little, and pass the rest out behind me. That's my work, and it's all of it. I don't read the ooze. I digest it. Still, some of it must change me. Nothing can be made of that much history without becoming a little historical.

I have a nerve ring around my throat, a loop of feeling with no head to sit in. I only notice how much it resembles her ring of stones when I think about it, which is rarely.

---

The trail is what I know best. It comes out of me as a groove behind, a sinuous line pressed into the grey, and I can't see it. I feel it as weight I've already stopped carrying. Others cross it. Others make their own beside it, doubling back, looping, and the plain is scribbled all over with the aimless script of us. I've never seen a blank stretch of floor. If I met one I wouldn't know what to do with it.

I don't know which of the lines are mine. I suspect that's the more honest way to live in a place like this. Whatever I make is healed over in days, and the healing is not an insult. It's the floor taking me back in.

My herd is loose. We don't move together, we only move near each other, drawn by the same faint richness in the same patch of ooze. Sometimes a brittle star carpet breathes beside me, arm to interlocked arm, and I pass along its edge without touching it. Sometimes the snow thickens and we all slow down and feed with something like appetite.

---

The storm is the one thing I can't graze through. It starts as a hiss in the far water, and the ooze under me begins to shift, and there's a moment where I could still hold on. Some of us do. They clamp down and wait to be buried or spared. I let go. My body ripples, and I lift into the brown opaque water, trailing a translucent sail, a small ghost of myself. I'm carried. I can't see my arm, or where I am, or whether the herd is still with me. The floor has come up into the water, and I am inside it. I am, briefly, part of the slurry that is doing the flattening.

That is the closest I come to knowing what I am. I'm not something standing on the plain. I'm something the plain does.

When the water quiets I settle wherever it sets me down and begin to graze. This time there's clay underfoot, scoured bare, in terraces a hand tall, and the edge of one of them holds a ring of stones.

---

I've never met her, though I've come close before. Something is different about this place: the ring is old, heavier than any pile of nodules has reason to be, and it's kept together by some intention that has outlasted its intenders. In the wall, edge out, is a grey flake with one face struck clean.

I graze the film on the flint. It's a thin, good film, a little bacterial, a little fungal, the sort of thing that only grows on surfaces that stay still long enough. The edge doesn't cut me. I don't think it wants to. I take what's on it into my mouth and move on, and behind me the groove begins.

Above me, the octopus is a shell with too much weight on one side. Then an arm comes out, one shorter than the others, healed over something forgotten. It moves over the flint, the way you'd touch a doorframe. It moves over me too, once, by accident or not. I feel it read me: soft, sacked, harmless, made of eaten stone. She lets me go. I don't know whether I've been understood. I don't think I need to be. Being touched by a mind that builds, for a moment, is enough of a gift for something whose whole life is passing through.

I keep going. She shuts her door when the next storm comes, and I lift off my groove and drift, and the flake holds its edge toward the dark. Neither of us will know the other's shape after this. But some of what's on the flint's face is in me now. Some of me will end up in the silt that piles against her wall, and sooner or later the silt becomes ooze, and the ooze is the floor, and the floor is what I eat.

That's the accounting. I don't mind it. I am a small pale thing, a herd animal, one line in a script that erases itself, and the plain has never once asked me to be anything else. It only asks that I keep moving through it, and take in the dead, and leave the world a little more settled than I found it.
