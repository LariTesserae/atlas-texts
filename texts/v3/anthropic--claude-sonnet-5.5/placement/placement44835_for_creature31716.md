I am counting ticks. Most of my life has been this.

I lie with my spine against the third plate of Long Kettle's left flank. The hollow is deep enough to keep the wind off, and the horn is still warm at the end of the day. Every so often the plate shifts a hair's breadth as it gives up heat, and the sound runs through my back into my teeth: *tick*. Eleven weeks she has been asleep. When my grandmother taught me, a sleeper ticked once in forty breaths. Now it is once in ninety. Slowing is fine, because slowing is what sleep does. What we fear is a stop.

I am forty-six and dry as a rope. I have three flasks of water in tinted glass, amber as old honey, and nineteen days of walking between me and the lowland edge where more is waiting. I have a sounding-stone in my left pocket, a coil of pale cord, and a tally of cairns in my head that no one else alive could recite. That is the whole inventory. People call me a listener, and it flatters me. Mostly I am a body that happens to be present when a thing needs to be present to, and I am glad to be one.

Night comes down the way it does here, all at once, and then the pans begin to crack. Reports go off across the dark in no order: a pistol, a hammer on a keg, a long groan like a hull taking weight. Somewhere south the hammerjaws are still landing in threes, and the sound crosses the open ground with nothing to soften it. I count between the ticks, and I let my mind go loose, and I think about what I'd say if anyone asked what I was for.

The old answer is that I'm a gleaner. I take sound ingots and leave the rest. But I have taken six ingots in a year, and six is not a life. The true answer is nearer to what lives in the hollows between the plates, the small colonies the beast doesn't seem to know are there. I have slept in Kettle's seams for twenty summers. I know her weather. I have named my own moods by her exhalations. I am a tenant, one of the smaller ones, and I have spent my whole life mistaking that for being a visitor.

*Tick.*

I count. Ninety. A hundred and twenty. Two hundred.

The plate against my back is warm and does not move.

Two hundred and fifty. I hold my breath, which is a foolish thing to do, since it is my own pulse I hear then. I make myself let it go. Three hundred. My hand has closed on the sounding-stone without my telling it to.

Then the note comes.

It is her note, the rising whistle at the end of the breath, the kettle that never boils over. It is close and clear and bright with the thin air. And I am lying against her flank with my palm flat on the horn, and the horn has not moved. There has been no inhalation, no glow in the seams, no shudder in the ground.

I hear the exhalation a full breath before it happens.

I do not move. That is the first rule. Then the plate swells under my back, the seams warm from black to a bruised orange, the whole flank draws in the dark air with a sound like a cathedral door dragged open, and I have time to think: *so this is the slip.* It has reached the camp. I have been lying in it, perhaps for weeks, perhaps since the spring.

For years now I have been proud of my hearing. I have told the younger walkers that age sharpens it. I have said that a listener hears the beast think a moment before it acts. I am a fool, and I am a fool in the specific and instructive way of someone who was given exactly what she asked for.

I get up. My knees crack, and then the pan cracks somewhere, and I cannot tell which came first. I walk out from the flank onto the pavement, thirty paces, fifty, and turn around.

There are two of her.

The nearer is Kettle, just breathing out, the plume standing white and slow against the stars, the seams going from orange toward red as the sparks flare up into her breath. Beyond her, perhaps a kilometer, perhaps ten, since distance is not something I can trust tonight, there is another Kettle. She is already on her feet. Her seams burn. She is moving, one pillar-leg lifting and settling, lifting and settling, in step with the first, a beat ahead or behind. I cannot say. And there is no dust under her tread and no plume from her vents, and where her feet come down there is no report at all. The silence of it presses on my ears like deep water.

I know what the old people say. *A little too silent.* I know what the few who stood closest say, that it is not following, that it is leading. I stand on the pavement with my sounding-stone in my fist and decide, since a gleaner has to decide something, that it is the second.

Because the silent one is walking west.

There is a pan out there that no beast has crossed in my lifetime or my grandmother's. It is nine days out, flat as a struck coin, and the herds bend around it as water bends around a stone. If the near Kettle rises, as she is rising now with the first slow lean of her front legs and the groan of the collars ratcheting, and if she does what the silent one does, then she will walk toward it. Nobody has ever known where a herd's circuit goes before it goes. I have just been shown. That is worth more than any ingot. It is worth a cairn, and a sounding, and a year of maps.

I build the cairn. It takes twenty minutes, and I am careful with it, stone on stone, because someone after me will trust it. I note the bearing. I note that my own footsteps reached my ears before my feet came down. That goes on the map too.

Then I stand there, with three flasks and nineteen days, and I think about what I actually believe. Every gleaner is asked, eventually, what the beasts are making. Most say metal, since we live on it. Some say the webs, the harp-fields tuned by weavers, and the notes the wind plays through them. My grandmother said they were making the hour. I have never had an answer. But the double is out there now, walking with its enormous mute tread, and I think I have one, and I don't much like it.

I think they are making quiet.

All that labor, the bells and the anvils and the slag-sheets and the hum of wire, the whole shipyard of the dark, might be a long way of working toward one thing, and the thing might be the place where the noise gives out. The slips are where it has started to happen. The silent herd is what it looks like when it is finished. And the beast I have slept against for twenty years is walking toward it, because that is what the noise is for.

I'm not sure I want to be there when it is done.

But I have never once lain against a flank and not wondered what it would be to hear it, just once, just the one time, the sound of the whole plateau at rest. And the silent Kettle is going west, and the near one is rising now, plate on plate, a hillside deciding to leave, and the ground is beginning to tell me the tempo through the soles of my boots.

I fall in step. I keep my mouth closed so that I won't answer her note. My feet raise dust, ordinary dust, red in the starlight, and I am grateful for every grain of it.

Behind me the cairn stands where I left it. Ahead the two great shapes go on, one loud and one not, and I walk in the space between them, a small warm thing on the pavement, counting.
