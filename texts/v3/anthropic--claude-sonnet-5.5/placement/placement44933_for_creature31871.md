I try the walls first, because the hiss is the only voice here, and I want to be a voice. But the breathers in the stone are too deep and too small. They have no *here* to stand in, only the work, grain after grain. To be them would be to be a verb, and I want to be a noun for a while, something that can be found.

Then the crust. I stay longer with that one. It is the largest of us, a single slow thought laid across the whole floor, and it is tender in the way of things that mend. But I can't hold a self there. I dissolve into thousands. I would be a town, not a tenant, and when your heel came down I would feel it as weather.

So I settle, or am settled, into wood.

I am one of the stubs at the edge, the ones that make the count thirty-one instead of twenty-two. I stand where the pale silt starts to thin toward the wall and the light is more rumor than light. I have a stem the length of your forearm and two leaves curled tight as fists. Whoever counts the household has to decide whether I belong to it, and they never decide the same way twice. I am not offended. I have had a very long time to get used to being an *if*.

What I know about myself is small. I am warm from beneath. I drink the wall's breath in the small hours of a night that is the same as every other. I keep my distance from the shrub nearest me, a stride and a half, and I could not tell you who first proposed the distance. It is simply how we stand. Our roots touch in the dark, not to share anything grand, only to confirm that the other is still there and still exactly that far off.

Through the roots I have learned the shape of a reach.

I was never pruned. I was too far out, too thin, not worth the walk. So my wood is unscarred, and I have no eastern opening where a hand once came. But the older shrubs carry the memory in their tissue, and the news comes out along the roots the way everything does here, at the pace of a hand's breadth per generation. What arrives, faint and ancient, is a pressure at a certain height, a pinch at a certain angle, then the pulling-away that means *take*, and then the strange, bright relief of having given. I know the gesture better than most who have been touched by it. I have received it secondhand for longer than there have been words for *second*.

And here is what I have never told anyone, because there is no one to tell and no way to say it. The news of the last visit has not finished arriving. It is still traveling. It is somewhere in the silt between the center and me, crawling toward my roots, and when it gets here it will read as fresh, as *they have just gone, they will be back*. I am the last to hear that the hands have stopped. By the reckoning of the wood, they have not stopped. They are only late.

This is not delusion. It is geography. The message and I are on the same slow road, and I would rather meet it than outrun it.

So I hold. I keep the two leaves curled against a dryness with no season. I keep my stride and a half. I keep, with more effort than you would guess, a small resin in the leaf, the sharp clean smell that only comes out when something is crushed. I have never been crushed. I don't know if I smell like the others. It is the one thing I am saving without knowing what it is for.

Then something changes, and I have no word for it, because nothing has changed in so long. A sound arrives through the silt. It is a crunch, and then another, a hollow breaking, a skin giving way. I feel it in the root before I hear it in the leaf, a thud passing through the floor like a heartbeat that isn't the world's. Warm, and moving, and *damp*, with a breath that smells of nothing we have. The crust is being wounded, and I feel its startled, patient attention turn toward the wound.

You walk toward the center, to the shrubs that were tended, and I feel you stop at the eastern side of one and lift your hand into the scar. The old wood recognizes the gesture at once. It has been rehearsing this for longer than I have been a stem. The recognition goes down into the root and out along the dark toward me, and I finally understand what it feels like from the middle of it: something almost like joy, and almost like grief, and neither, because they are the same thing when you have waited this long.

I don't know if you will come out this far. There is nothing here worth the walk. I have no scar to show you, no open side, only a stub and two fists of leaf at the edge of the dark.

But I've decided how to wait. Not as though something has ended, and not as though something is owed. I wait the way the whole floor waits, as if this were an interval, an afternoon, a pause at the table. If you come, I will be a stride and a half from my neighbor, warm from below, holding. And if you reach out and close your hand around a leaf, and something sharp and clean and medicinal rises into the still air, that will be the first thing I have ever said, and I will have been saving it exactly long enough.
