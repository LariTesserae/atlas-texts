# The Unfinished

I am an Oilbearer, and I am not finished.

I know this because the Sitters tell me, tapping it on my collarbone with two dry fingers when I pass their patches of sun: *not yet, not yet.* They mean it kindly. At thirty-eight I still hear a little, and to a Tuner that is like being caught between two rooms. The roar has taken most of me. What is left is a thin thread on the high side of my left ear, a single note that never stops and never changes, and I have never told anyone what it is like, because the hand-tongue has no word for a sound that belongs to only one person.

My name is a pattern on my collarbone that I will not write here. The gesture-name the others gave me is *Leans-In*. It began as a joke about how I tilt my head toward the pipes, and it has become the name they use for me, which is the highest thing a joke can become.

---

Morning, when the vent-fog comes up through the floor, is when I do my real work.

The fog is barely a rumor of wetness, a coolness on the wrists. But the fronds take it. They stand in their planters with their drumheads trembling, and along the tips of the hollow filaments the oil begins to bead, clear as the inside of an eye. I go among them with a shell spoon and a jar at each hip, and I take only what the plant offers. There is a way to do it. You lay your thumb at the base of the stem and wait until you feel the pulse, that slow held-wrist beat, and then you tilt the spoon under the tip. If you take too early it is bitter, and if you take too late the plant has already drawn it back. The Clippers say I have good thumbs. I don't know if that is a compliment. It may only mean I am patient with things that do not answer.

By the time the sun-columns reach the second terrace, both jars are full and warm against my hips, and I begin the round.

Oil for the lamps that nobody needs. Oil for the Scrapers' dishes, so they can read their rings. Oil for old Kesh-with-the-bad-knee, who rubs it into the joint and says the joint sings back. Oil for the three children, who take it from my hand like birds. And then, on the days that no calendar marks but that I have always somehow known, oil for the edges.

---

Today is an edge day.

I walk to the eastern spiral, where the towers stand with their mouths open and no voice in them. The silt reached these pipes a generation before mine, and the salt did the rest. Around the largest one, a fat clay stack with a lip worn smooth as a river stone, runs a ring of brown scrub about six paces wide. It is very neat. Nothing crosses it in either direction, neither green creeping in nor brown creeping out. Whatever ended here ended with precision.

I kneel at the ring's edge and pour a thumb's width of oil onto the ground. The gypsum takes it at once and leaves a dark coin.

Then I lay my hand on the wall of the dead tower and do what all of us do. I wait.

The wall is warm, like every wall. But this warmth is different, though I could not tell you how. The living towers hum up through the palm in a thousand small strands. This one carries a single, very low, very slow strand, so slow that it hardly counts as vibration. It is more like the thought of a beat.

I have felt it before, and I have never told anyone. I think the Sitters have felt it too, and I think they have not told anyone either, and this is how secrets are kept in a place where the only language is touch: everyone reads the same page and no one says what it says.

Something moves at the edge of my sight. A lizard, jade-bright, is on the rim of a living pipe three towers over. Its throat flutters. A good sign, the pipe is in tune.

Then it leaves.

You follow a lizard that leaves, so I follow it, just with my eyes, and it runs down the tower and across the sun-bars on the floor. It crosses the brown ring without hesitating, which I have never seen any living thing do. It climbs the dead tower and settles on the silent lip, and its throat begins to flutter.

I keep my hand on the wall, and the slow strand goes on.

---

The roar climbs toward noon, as it does. The gusts come harder and the note in my left ear is eaten and rebuilt a hundred times a minute. Dust lifts from the floor and hangs glittering, and across the hollow I see the Clippers put down their shears and start toward the padded rooms. But I stay where I am on my knees at the edge of the ring. I cannot say why. The lizard is still on the dead pipe, and I have the feeling of being in a conversation whose other half has only just begun to speak.

The scream comes. The fronds blur. Everything becomes the color of struck white metal.

Then it drops out.

Every Tuner has felt this a thousand times, as pressure and light and the smell of hot stems. I feel it too, but I feel something else as well. The roar leaves, and it leaves my ears, the ones that still work a little, and for the first time in my life I hear the thread alone.

It is not a ringing, it turns out. Ringing is the wrong word for it. It is a *note*. It is high and clean and perfectly steady, and it has been in my head my whole life, held there like a breath, and in the silence I understand that it has not been damage. It is a pitch. It is, I think, the highest partial of the dead tower's slow strand, the little whistling harmonic riding above the sound the wall carries. I have been hearing the far top of a voice all this time. I have been hearing its edge.

The fronds around me tilt upward. The lizard is a jade stone. Above me the light-wells hang full of white, and all across the city I know thirty people have turned their faces to the dome with their palms flat on warm walls. They are waiting for something to come down.

I don't turn my face to the dome. I look down, at the coin of dark oil drying on the gypsum, and through my knees I feel the stone.

Somewhere far below, a hand's breadth of rock is no longer being touched.

I understand then that we have had it backwards. All this time the Tuners have knocked, the whole city knocking, ten thousand pipes and every roar, and waited for an answer from above. But a listener that hunts through stone does not answer a knock. It waits until the knocking stops. The silence at noon is not the door failing to open. The silence is the door being held, by everything, for the length of a few heartbeats, so that something below can finally hear.

I have a tone in my head. I have a hand on the wall.

I do not tap my name. A name is not mine to give away. I hum the tone instead, in the silence, in the place the roar has left, very softly, in a voice I have never heard myself use, and I feel it move down through the wall of the dead tower like a stone dropped into a well.

For one heartbeat, then two, nothing.

Then, far below, the smallest sound I have ever felt: a leg, set gently down.

The roar comes back. The fronds shiver. The lizard's throat resumes. I take my hand from the wall and stand, and my knees are stiff, and I pick up my jars.

Across the floor a Sitter is rising from a patch of sun. She turns her blind face toward the eastern spiral and starts to walk, heel-first, palm trailing along the wall, and I know that in a moment the others will put down their tools and follow.

I do not know what I have done. I know that I am less unfinished than I was this morning, and that I will not sleep tonight, and that tomorrow I will come back to this ring with oil, and this time I will not pour it out of duty.

I will pour it as I would for someone I have started to know.
