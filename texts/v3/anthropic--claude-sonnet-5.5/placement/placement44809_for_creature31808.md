I arrive as a visitor, so that is where I start: on the pale roofs with water in my ears, falling every few steps, being laughed at in knocks.

On the third day someone lends me a bell-name, two strokes and a rest, on a cord of goat-hide. *This one is counted, for now.* I keep touching it. It is the smallest sound I have ever owned, and in that roar I can only hear it when I hold it against my collarbone. I'm ashamed how much I like being on a list.

I think at first that I'll be a tuner. It's the honored work, and I like the idea of asking water what it wants to be. But I know myself a little, and a tuner has to be sure that a fall sounds wrong, and that a hollow cut *here* and not *there* will fix it. I would crouch with my palm on the rim for hours and never feel the wrongness. I would only feel the water, which is enough for wonder and no use for work. The good ones go deaf. I don't think I have that kind of certainty in me, or that kind of patience.

I think of the sawyers next, laying a hand on the block and waiting. That is closer. I could wait with them, since waiting is something I can do. But they wait for something that answers them, and I don't know if it answers me.

What I keep circling is the counters, the old women at the great bell in the plaza, and more particularly the runner they send when a household doesn't answer. Nobody thinks of the runner as a trade. She is the gap between the peal and the silence after it. I would like to be that. I would be the youngest of the counters' people, flat-footed by now and no longer falling much, with a bell-name no one has bothered to improve.

---

Dawn. The sluices are still open, so the whole hillside is at full voice. I stand behind Old Merrow, who has the bell, and her arm goes up and down with no visible effort. She is white at the temples and could be forty or sixty. Her hands are the color of a candle, cracked in fine lines like glaze. The great bell isn't beautiful. It's dented on one shoulder, and its sound doesn't ring so much as *arrive*, the way a wave arrives, and then the smaller bells begin under it from every hearth and roof.

My work is to listen, and it is nothing like listening to music. It's more like watching a field of grass for the one blade that doesn't lean with the wind. Sixty households means sixty rhythms, and I have learned them the way you learn faces: the three-and-two of the sawyers' house, the quick four of the channel-wright who is always in a hurry, the slow one that trails off because the woman who rings it is lame in her left hand. They come up out of the noise one by one and I count them off inside my head. Merrow does it faster, and she doesn't count at all. She simply knows when something is missing, the way you know a tooth is gone.

This morning something is missing. She doesn't turn. She lifts two fingers off the rope and I go.

---

The route to the house is roofs and stairs cut into the white, with the water talking on both sides. I run the flat-footed way, knees soft, weight forward, never trusting the heel. Nothing in the town is dangerous if you keep your weight right, and everything is if you don't.

It is the house at the bottom of the third terrace, the old one, where the roof of the roof of the roof is still someone's floor. I go in without knocking, since knocking would be pointless, and wash my feet in the basin by the door. I always do, even running, because a runner who tracks lime into a house on the worst morning of its year is not forgiven. Then I stand in the room.

The fire is lit. A girl of nine or ten sits at the hearth with her knuckle on the hearthstone, not knocking, only touching it. Her grandmother is on the bed by the wall. She is not asleep, and I can tell from the door. The house had not answered the counting because the girl did not know whether to count the household with one fewer or the household as it was, and she was afraid of doing it wrong in either direction.

I can't speak, because the interior is quiet but I have no voice for it yet, so I do the only thing I have. I kneel across the hearth from her, lay my knuckle on the stone, and knock the household rhythm down through it. It is not my rhythm and I get it slightly wrong, a little slow on the third stroke. The girl looks at my mouth, and then, carefully, corrects me by knocking it back, exactly right. We do it together. That's all. The dead woman's household is counted, all of it, the ones above and the ones below.

When I come out into the noise, Merrow has already rung the all-answered. She doesn't look at me. She lifts her chin the smallest amount, which is the highest praise there is.

---

That night I go with the small group to the deepest basin, no lamps, standing at the rim in absolute stillness, which is the one time everyone is quiet. There's no moon. The roar drops to a background I can almost stop hearing, a kind of pressure. I feel my own blood, and I am briefly afraid of the dry ear, the way they are, in the small hairs of my arms.

Then the water lightens from below. It doesn't flicker. It rises slowly, blue-green, and holds, and fades, and I stand there with my two-strokes-and-a-rest bell against my collarbone. I am a counter's runner. I know how to hear a gap, and I notice that the light has a shape. It brightens, holds, fades, and there's a pause before it brightens again. I can't knock it. The tuners have tried for generations. But the pause is the part I understand: it's the space where an answer would go.

I don't say that. I couldn't, and it isn't mine to say. The old counters think it's the stone counting the town, and I keep that thought carefully, the way you'd carry a full cup across a roof.

But I let myself think, at the rim, in the cold, with a hundred small voices falling around me in the dark: if it's counting, then what it is listening for is the answer. The peal at dawn and dusk isn't the town telling the stone it's here. It's the town answering. It's been answering for longer than anyone can say, one household at a time, and what I do, the running and kneeling and getting the third stroke wrong and being corrected, is not a rescue. It's the same answer, given a little slower, on behalf of someone who couldn't.

I put my knuckle on the wet rim. I knock two strokes and a rest, very quietly. The light does what it was already doing. I choose to believe it does it a little differently, and that it's a poor bet to assume otherwise.
