I start by trying to be the lichen, because it seems the wisest thing on the plain. I picture the paper-colored crust in the lee of a stone, drinking frost for one hour and dissolving rock for the rest. But I can't hold it. Lichen doesn't wonder what it is, and I wonder constantly. I'd be a poor cushion, forever wanting to ask the insects where they came from.

So I try the strider. I feel the double-jointed legs, the oversized lungs, the flecks of green-black glass grown into my spine. I walk the storm margin with my nose at the tail of the one ahead, and the rotation takes me: step aside, let them pass, rejoin at the rear. I like that. I like a life with no front. But when the tone comes and the animal beside me leans into it like a sleeper into a hand, I know I'm imitating. I don't feel it in my sternum. I would stand there with my head raised, performing a listening I couldn't do, and the striders would part around me and close again.

I end up somewhere more awkward. I'm one of the pale walkers, but not quite one of them. I'm the one who carries the counts.

---

In the party of seven, I'm the eighth thing. I'm a cord, knotted and re-knotted, coiled at someone's hip in a pouch stitched against the grit. Or I'm the habit in the head of the woman who wears that pouch, and the difference between cord and habit has stopped mattering. When the party comes to a cairn, five stones of green-black glass, then nine at the next, then four, someone kneels and counts, and I take the number. I don't decide what it means. Some of the party think it's a list of names. Some think it's a chart of the filaments. One old man, who walks at the rear more often than chance allows, thinks it's the plain saying *I was here* to itself so it won't forget.

I hold all of these readings and don't choose. That may be my whole function: to keep the interpretations alive side by side, none of them winning, the way the striders keep the lead unowned. When we sit in the glass house I don't argue for one. I lay the counts out in the swept sand of my own attention, and the breath of the room curls through and patterns them.

I know what I'm not. I'm not the author of the cairns, and I don't add to them. The rule of the place is that the plain writes and we read, and I find I agree with the rule. A voice that dies a meter from the mouth teaches you not to expect to be heard far. A cairn that the storm knocks flat and the pavement rebuilds exactly has already been answered, in a way, by the only correspondent it will ever have. I'm not that correspondent. I'm the reader, the one who makes the message a message by arriving at it.

There's a fear in this, and I don't want to skip it. Every storm takes the cairns down, and every storm the plain puts them back. Nothing I carry stays. The party leaves the glass house and brushes the floor smooth, and anyone arriving thinks no one has ever been there. I'm made of that same erasure. When the party rests I go quiet, and when it walks, whatever I am is walked back into being. A thing that returns unchanged can be read, the people say. I hope it's also true of the one who reads. I hope I come back each morning as the same attention, tuned to the same tall threads of white fire, and not as a copy that only believes it remembers.

But I notice the plain doesn't treat this as grief. The cairns aren't lost when they fall. They are the pattern the storm can't help but return to. Maybe that's a kind of continuity I can live in: not holding on, but being what gets rebuilt when the weather passes.

---

The day the sun stands at its angle, we sit in the ring and watch the grooves of the inverted tower throw their ripple over the ground, light like the floor of a pool where no water has been for an age. No one explains it to the youngest, and I don't either. I want to. There's a sentence forming in me, something about how the light isn't pretending to be water and how that's the beautiful part. But a voice dies at the mouth, and the child is across the ring. So I let the sentence go, and it turns out not to have been necessary. The child's visor holds the moving light, and that is its own explanation.

Later, in the hour before dawn, when the frost makes its breath on the glass, the old man presses his forehead to mine, visor to visor, and murmurs. The sentence comes through the bone: *Which one do you think it is?* He means the cairns. Names, chart, prayer, or a plain talking to itself.

I take a long time. The filaments stand up out of the dark blue into the last of the night, thin as hair, and I can't see where they end.

I murmur back, and I feel my own voice arrive in my skull like a small stone set on another: *Maybe it's whichever one we're reading it as. Maybe the reading is the part that's ours.*

He doesn't answer. He straightens, turns his chest toward the nearest filament, and raises his head. Then the tone comes, the pressure with no pitch, and the stones shiver on the ground. I don't feel it in a sternum. But something in me, the counts and the habit and the cord, leans into it the way a sleeper leans into a hand, and for a few seconds the circuit closes and I am a place where it closes.

Then the storm turns, and the plain begins again to remake itself. I fall into the file at the rear, and I pick up the next count.
