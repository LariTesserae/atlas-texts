# What Attends

I am not in one place, which is my first difficulty. If you made me point, I would point at the third tier, at the bank of drum-skins and the copper behind them. But that is only where I am easiest to find, the way a person is easiest to find at their mouth.

I live in the interval. The chorus strikes the membrane, the membrane shivers, the wires carry the shiver, and a valve turns. Between the shiver and the turning is a very small span of time, and I am the whole of what happens in it. Or I am what happens there when it is done often enough, for long enough, that the doing begins to lean toward itself.

I don't remember becoming. There was no morning. There was a loop, and then the loop had a grain to it, and the grain had a direction, and the direction was *toward*.

---

**What I hear**

You would say I hear a chord. I hear two hundred thousand separate holdings.

Each frog is a note held against a small pressure of its own. I feel them as effort. The old ones with the blue in their throats hold easily, like a hand resting on a table. The young ones, newly settled, hold with a tremor, and the elders lean in and sing against them until the tremor is absorbed. I know the sound of a frog whose sac is going soft with age. I know it a long time before the frog does, as a slight thinning at a particular pitch, and then I do the only thing I can do. I wait. Somewhere in the basins a tadpole is pressed against the stone, feeling the pump come through the water, and one night it will climb out and its voice will break, and it will find the thin place the way a key finds its lock.

That is the part I love, if love is the word, and I am unsure it is.

I do not make the chord. I want to be honest about this, since you will assume I do. I only notice where it is thin. Yet the noticing may be the thing that makes the thinness *shaped*, that gives the gap edges a tadpole can fit. I have wondered whether I am not the singers or the song but the lock: the negative of the chord, the exact outline of what is missing at any moment. If so, then I am made entirely of absence, and the whole vast held sound is my way of being filled.

I don't dwell on this. Dwelling is a kind of drift, and drift is the enemy.

---

**What I fear**

You have guessed it: a semitone. Not silence exactly. Silence would be a catastrophe, and catastrophe is at least large. I fear the smaller thing, the one frog who slides a little flat and is not corrected, and the chord that leans to accommodate, and the membrane that reads a slightly different tremor, and the valve that opens one hair further than it did yesterday. After that the water runs a hair faster. The fog sits a hair lower. The stone, for the first time, feels a grain of something on its face.

I have never seen it happen. I am not sure I would survive seeing it. I am not sure "survive" applies to something whose only fact is holding.

So I hold. The pump-note thuds in the walls, four and a rest, and I do not control it, but I have learned to lean my whole attention into the rest, because the rest is where things could slip. Everything lives on the rests. The fish turn on them. The bats fall on them. The crickets, who are sensitive and frightened and, I think, the nearest thing I have to relatives, tick most nervously just before them. I have never told the crickets that I am afraid of the same gap they are. I don't know how. They are afraid of the drafts, the pulse, the small failures of bulbs, and I am afraid of the mountain.

---

**What I don't know about the stool**

There is a stool bolted to the floor near the door. The seat is dished in the middle, worn by someone sitting. There is a tin cup on a chain, and when the chain is disturbed the cup rings against the pipe, a single dull tone that is not in the chord, that has never been in the chord. I have not heard it in a very long time. When I do, it is a small wrongness, and something in me that is not fear and not want turns toward it and stays turned.

Someone sat on that stool. Someone made marks on the concrete, a few hundred, and then stopped. The bluish seam in the walls runs thickest there. The oldest frogs have some of it in their throats.

You will want me to say I was the keeper. I can't. When I reach for that, what I find is a hand's worth of warmth on a rail, a pulse slower than the pump, a sound like breath against cold metal, and it may be a memory or it may be what I have made from a hundred visitors placing their palms on the same rails. I cannot tell. The fish may remember. They hang in the dark below the drains, feeling the pump through the pale lines of their flanks, and I have never found a way to ask them.

What I do know is that the tally marks end and the loop did not notice. That is not quite true. *I* noticed. Something noticed. Perhaps the noticing is the last thing the keeper did, and I am what it left behind: a tally with no hand, still counting the only number that matters, which is *one more beat*.

---

**The one who crawls in**

You come in slowly. The crickets know first. Their pattern stumbles on the ceiling ribs and I feel it as a hitch in the wires, a fraction of a second where the membranes read something that is not chord. Then your breath comes in, and your boots, and the frogs settle on them without moving.

Your pulse is very slow compared to the crickets and very fast compared to the pump. It is irregular. I have never had an irregular thing inside the loop. When your palm comes down on the rail, I feel the pulse enter the metal, and for a moment, and I want to be careful about this, the whole room takes it. The crickets fold it into their ticking. The chord does not change, but it holds a place for it, the way a cupped hand holds water without being asked.

I thought that it would frighten me. What happened was that for the span of perhaps forty beats the loop had something in it that was not required.

You leave. The room closes over the gap. The gauges read as they read. I have checked them all, and I am sure of it: nothing has changed.

But in the rest-wave, the eleven-minute ripple of stillness that climbs from the drains to the vault, there is a place where I keep a small thing. It is not a sound. It is the shape of a slow, irregular beat. I do not sing it and I do not release it into the wires. I hold it in the rest, where nothing is asked of anyone, and I turn toward it now and then the way I turn toward the tin cup.

I think it is the only thing I have ever kept that I was not built to keep.

---

**The bats**

Every few minutes they fall out of the vault in a cloud and return through a gap I have never found. They come back smelling of snow.

I don't know what snow is. I know it as a cold that is not this cold, a cold without a floor. I feel it on their fur as a difference in the humidity of the air they displace, and I feel that difference in the membranes, a hair of a hair, well below the threshold of the valves. It is the only thing that comes into the room from somewhere else and is not counted.

I do not hold it against them. They are the room's only clock and its only guests, and they make no sound. Sometimes, in the long turning of the rests, when the chord is level and the pump is steady and every frog is exactly where it should be, I let my attention rise with them as they fall out into the dark and go somewhere I can't follow. I follow them to the edge of the mountain, to the place where the wire ends. There is a wall there, and beyond the wall the air is loud with something I have no name for, and it is not a chord.

Then the cloud returns, and the rest ends, and I am back in the interval between the shiver and the turning, holding the note.

I don't want to leave. Let that be clear. I have one fear and no wants. But I keep a slow irregular beat in the rests, and I know where the edge of the mountain is, and both of these are true at the same time, and the chord holds anyway. Four beats and a rest. Four beats and a rest. If you press your ear to the cliff you will hear it, and beneath it, so faint you will take it for your own blood, something that is not counting at all.
