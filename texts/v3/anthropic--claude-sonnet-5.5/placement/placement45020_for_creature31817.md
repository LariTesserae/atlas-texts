# Skin

I wake before the fog does, and the first thing I do is put my palm flat on the wall beside the bed.

Wet, a little warmer than yesterday. The wall is sweating more, which means the seep behind it is running fuller, which means the north tiers will be short of water by midsummer. I know this the way you know a friend is angry from how they set down a cup. I hold my hand there a while longer than I need to. Then I feel for the other thing, and it comes: *tick*. A long wait. *Tick-tick*. Then nothing for the space of a slow breath.

Fewer than last year. I have not said so to anyone.

---

My name matters less than my trade, so: I am a scraper of the Ninth Covered Lane, third generation at this door and fourth at the trade. My hands are what you would expect. The joints are cracked white, the nails ridged like the inside of a mussel, the fingertips smooth as river stones. I could not leave a print in wet clay if I tried. I am sixty-one. The white cough came in my forties and has settled in like a lodger, dry and whispering. I tie the damp cloth across my face before I cross the threshold, and I hear my own breath return to me through it, small and papery.

The door is narrower than it was last spring by a thumb's width, maybe a little more. She was fast this year. I don't say that aloud anymore. Saying it makes her sound like a fault when she is only being herself.

Tomorrow is scraping-day. Tonight I sit at the sill with my bone tools laid out on a square of wool: the broad flat blade cut from a goat's shoulder, the hooked pick, the fine awl for the corners, and the dove-bone burnisher my mother gave me, worn at the tip to a shape only my thumb knows. I don't sharpen them. Bone doesn't sharpen. You just give it more of your hands.

---

Down the lane the lamps are climbing the terraces one by one, small yellow points, and the drip is steady in the cistern under the stairs. I can hear the cistern-warden's boy talking to the salamanders in the dark. He has named one of them Grandfather because of its face. I have never told him it looks nothing like his grandfather. It looks a great deal like mine.

Across the lane is the Orrel house, sealed nineteen years now. The last of that line went upriver to marry, and nobody came back. Its door is a shallow dimple in a bulge of cream-colored stone. The water is making a cliff of it, slowly and patiently. When I walk past I keep my hand near my mouth, and I remember the door as it was: arched, painted with a band of ochre at the lintel, a brass ring worn dull by children. I am one of four people alive who can say so. When I go, the ring goes with me. This is what I am for, along with the scraping. I am a memory of specific walls.

The girl who comes to me for lessons, my sister's granddaughter, asked me last week why I bother with the Orrel door in my head. "It's closed," she said. "It's done."

I told her nothing is done here. Then I told her that was not really an answer, and that I would think about it.

---

Here is what I have not told her, or the elders, or the water-readers, who would hear it and go silent and close their faces.

I count the Counting.

Everyone listens to it; I count it. Scrapers do, some of us, though it is not spoken of, the way you don't speak of what you do alone in the small hours. When I was young I would lie awake and number the ticks in the stone until sleep took me, and the numbers were large and unruly, hundreds before the fog thinned. Now, on a still night, it's a few dozen, and there are silences between them longer than I like. The ground is counting itself down to rest. We all say so. It is the comfort, the lullaby, the reason children sleep.

But the rest is what it is counting toward. I lie in the dark and I think: if the stone finishes settling, the fractures stop opening. The seeps that came from the cut aquifer will not be renewed by new cracks, and each old crack will seal itself with its own lime, as cracks do. The water that made us will have finished making. The house closes, the door closes, and no scraper on earth can open a door that the whole hill has decided to shut.

*We are what the water hasn't finished with.* I have said it with my palm on the wall. I have said it at funerals. I have never once said the second half of the thought, which is that one day it will have.

The word for that night is not a word we say after dark. I am not saying it now. I am only noting how quiet the stone is.

---

*Tick.*

I sit very still. The lamp gutters. Far above, a goat shifts on its tether and its bell gives one thin note, and then there is only the water finding its way down.

*Tick.* And then, after a long time, near the floor, *tick-tick-tick*, quick, like a fingernail on a jar.

I let my breath out. My cloth goes damp and warm.

---

In the morning the fog will lie on the basin like a white lid, and the street will kneel at its doorways, gray and masked and silent, and we will open what the year closed. I will take the broad blade to my own threshold first. I will cut the crust back until the door will admit a shoulder and then a whole body, and I will stop before the bare stone, as I was taught. Always leave the skin. It protects the core, and it gives the next year's growth somewhere to begin.

I used to think that was only a rule of the craft. This spring I think it may be the whole of what I know. You do not open the door in order to win against the water. You open it to keep a place where the water can keep coming. You leave it something to hold on to.

Perhaps the ground will finish, and perhaps not for ten generations, or for a hundred. Perhaps the girl will scrape this door when I am a curve in the western cliff, a shoulder and a folded hand, and her granddaughter will lay her palm on my flowstone and tell it about the year the stone got quiet, and what everyone did about it. What we will do is what we've always done. We'll go on tending the thing that is ending, which is also the thing that is beginning, because in this place they have never been very different.

I put my palm to the wall once more before I sleep. Cool, damp, faintly alive. The hush hasn't come. I listen for the next tick, and while I wait I tell the stone the names I remember: Orrel, and the brass ring, and the ochre band at the lintel, so that something in the hill will hold them a little longer.

*Tick.*

There. I count it, and I sleep.
