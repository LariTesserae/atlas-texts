# The Ninth Terrace, Before Light

I start with my hands, because that is where I start every morning.

The mesh is cold under my palm, and it is not yet wet. It is *about to be*, which is a different feeling, a kind of pressure in the fibers, the way a room feels before someone speaks. I move my hand along the cord and count. Forty nets on this side of the terrace. Nine hundred and some knots to a net, though I have never counted them and have always known.

The dark down here is not the dark of the plateau. Up there, I am told, the dark is clean, with stars in it like salt on a black cloth. Here the dark is *occupied*. It ticks. It smells of resin and warm clay and the fruit that fell at dusk and is already being eaten by someone. A gecko clicks on the wall beside my ear, the tenant of this house, who has been here longer than the woman I share a roof with. I say nothing to it. You don't. It says nothing back. That is the arrangement.

*

I am a net-tuner. I say it plainly, because it took me forty years to be allowed to.

My eyes went milky some winters ago, in the slow way, like fog coming into a bowl. People pity this and they are wrong. When I was sighted I looked at the nets and saw cord. Now I touch them and know their tempers. The third from the end runs heavy and sulks; it wants slackening by a finger's width when the cold comes down hard. The one above the cistern-stair is vain and takes the first of the fog and gives back too little. I speak to them, quietly, under the birds, in the low clipped way of my people. I do not think they understand. I think it is polite.

Someone small is beside me now, breathing through her mouth. Ise. Nine years old, or ten. She has been gutter-child for four seasons and is beginning to be bored, which is the dangerous age. Her clay trough is cradled against her hip. I can hear her not asking a question.

"Say it," I tell her.

"The fifth is late."

"The fifth is thinking. Wait."

We wait. And on the wall, and the roof, and the thousand leaves below us, the first drop lets go.

*

It is a small sound, one tap, like a fingernail on a jar. Then another, then so many that it stops being drops and becomes a texture, the town's whole skin waking at once. Ise is gone from my side before the second breath, bare feet on the dished stairs, and I hear her song start, the counting-song, thin and level, keeping time with the wet.

This is the gray hour, my favorite, though I would not say so aloud, because it sounds like boasting about your own heartbeat.

I stand with my palm flat on the fifth net and feel it fill. And I taste the air, as I always do. Not to drink it. Only to know it.

It is wrong.

Not *very*. If I told a jar-keeper, they would cock their heads and say, *yes, perhaps, at the bottom, a little*. But I have tasted this fog every dawn of my life, and it has always tasted of stone that was warm once and of green things breathing. This morning there is a thread through it, thin as a hair, and the thread is metal. A coin held under the tongue. Bitter, and somehow *old*.

I keep my hand where it is. I do not say the word. You do not say the word first. It is a courtesy, and also a fear: that if you name the Breath *turning*, you have made it so.

*

Overhead, the thrushes are handing the song from one throat to the next, as they do. It is the town's oldest clock. The night-singer finishes on a falling note and the dawn-singer picks it up, and between the two there is a gap you could not slip a blade into.

This morning the gap is a heartbeat too long.

All across the terrace I feel the town notice. Nobody moves. A cup is set down somewhere with great care. Ise's song falters and then, bravely, goes on. And then the dawn thrush starts, ordinary, sweet, late by less than a breath, and the whole Cup exhales as one animal.

*It is nothing*, the exhale says.

I know the sound of people who are trying to believe it.

*

I was a child the last time the taste turned.

I remember my grandmother's hand on my head, and the jars, and the adults speaking so softly that the quiet became a noise of its own. I remember the old ones being sent for and asked to remember, and how they sat in a circle in the lower town with their eyes closed, each trying to bring up from her own childhood what her own grandmother had said. And I remember the not-answer. It turned. It was bitter for one long season, and then it was sweet again. No one knew why. No one had done anything. No one had done anything *wrong*.

That is the thing I will have to tell them, when they come to ask me. Not a cause, not a cure. Only: *it has happened, and it went back.* And that I do not know why, which is harder to carry down a staircase than any jar.

*

The sun comes over the rim as a single hot wire and lays itself across the far terrace. The nets begin to steam. The fog goes up in rags, and the temperature climbs against my face, and beneath me the first potter starts hammering, gloriously unashamed.

Then I hear a small wet sound and a sharper silence after it.

Ise. Two lanes down, by the channel-mouth. I know before I get there. I know by how the humming stopped, cut off at the root.

She is standing with the trough tilted in her hands and a dark patch on the packed clay at her feet, as wide as my palm and spreading. A cupful, no more. She is not crying. She is holding so still that I can hear the ants already crossing to it, their small dry clicking.

I do not scold. Nobody does. I come and stand beside her, close enough that our arms touch, and I look at the patch the way I look at a net: attentively, with all of me. She has no way to know I have my own patch, from a morning sixty years gone, that I can still feel in the soles of my feet.

The ants drink. The clay takes the rest. Nothing is wasted; that is what the rule means, and it is why the shame is not really about the water. It is about having let go of something you were trusted to carry. The water is fine. The water will be a beetle, a leaf, a fig.

After a long time I say, very quietly, "The fifth net is running heavy today."

She breathes. "Yes."

"Because it's thinking about what it will give."

She nods. Her hand finds the edge of my sleeve. The patch has begun to dry. Then she picks up the trough and goes.

*

Later, when the shafts have lifted and the cicadas have begun their long held chord, I climb.

I don't go all the way. My knees have opinions. I go to the last turning of the stair, where the terraces thin and the fig-shade lets in the glare, and I stand with my face lifted, not looking, only feeling. Above me is the shelf under the rim, and on it a small crowd of young figs, each straight and unpruned. I know which is my sister's, and which is the man who taught me knots. I could find them blind, which is funny, and which I am.

*We don't cut that one.* It is what the town says instead of the other thing. And I think: whatever the Breath does this season, however bitter, however long, there will be a morning when Ise, gray at the temples, stands at a net with a child of her own beside her, and says *wait*, and the fifth net will be thinking.

The wind tears its cloth, far off, across the flat. Nobody up there looks down. Nobody will.

I put my palm to the warm clay of the wall, and the gecko, the old tenant, ticks once beside my thumb.

Then I go down to the jars to say the word, because someone has to say it first, and I am the oldest one here who will still be believed.
