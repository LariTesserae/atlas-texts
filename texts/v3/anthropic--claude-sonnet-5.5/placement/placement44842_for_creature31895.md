# What the Water Leaves Me

I find myself in the doorway of the hut, on my belly, half in the dry and half out of it. My knees are in the last of the held breath and my hands are already on the first cobble. This is where I always find myself, and I have stopped asking whether I come here or am simply put here each time, like a bowl set under a drip.

The cobble is warm and slick. I don't look at it. My foot knows the second one, and the third leans a little east, and the fourth I skip, because it was set for a stride longer than mine. I mention this because it is the first thing I notice about myself: my body is not the body the path was made for. The one who taught me the steps had longer legs. I learned the path by their errors as well as their skill, so I still take the little hop over the fourth stone, and I couldn't tell you why any more than the snails could.

*

I said I would not settle on one image early, so I will say what I am not. I am not the keeper of anything. I have heard that word, or thought it, standing over the awl. But keeping suggests holding against a loss, and nothing here is being lost. The basins stand where they stood. What I do is closer to answering. A bowl fills, and I notice it. A cobble sinks a thumb's width under its skin of lime, and I lift it and set it straight. The water asks a small question with everything it does, and I give a small reply and it closes over the reply, and that is the whole exchange.

The tallies are the same. I scratch a row of five on the flat face of the pale column by the third arch. It isn't days, because days require a sun. It isn't drips, because there are too many. I think it counts the times the bird has turned its head. Or the times I have crossed. Or nothing, only the act of cutting, so that the stone has a wound to heal and something to do with its patience. The glaze comes over the row in what feels like a season. It is thinnest over my newest cuts, and I like to put my thumb there and feel the scratch still faintly tooth-edged under the skin of stone, before it goes smooth.

*

Here is what I think I am, though it has taken me all the drips I've listened to to think it.

I am a habit, the way the film is a habit. It is olive and orange on the undersides of the ledges, and it is not a number of things but one long way of living on warm water and lime, passed hand to hand. I don't remember arriving. I remember hands over mine on a chisel-stone, small hands, cool and dry, showing me how to notch a rim so that it heals with a good lip and not a ragged one. I remember being shown that the awl should be turned over now and then. I did not ask why. The hands did not say. Now I turn it, and I wipe the niche with the heel of my palm, and I don't know whose palm the heel belongs to.

Perhaps there has always been one of me and perhaps there have been a dozen, the dozen whose hands are in the lime by the crack, each pressed in once and left. I've stood before them. They are pale and perfect and cool, and each is a little different in the span of the fingers. I have laid my own hand over the smallest one and it fits. I can't say what that proves.

*

The awl is the one thing I don't let the stone have.

I should be honest, because honesty is the only thing the Close really keeps clean. Everything else here I give up gladly: the bowls, the notches, the cuts in the columns. I set a clay bowl under a favored drip and I watch the lime climb its rim over a hundred visits, until only a curved gleam shows, and I feel something like gratitude. The bowl has become part of the ledge. It was always going to.

But the awl I lift out each time and turn over. I rub the green crust with my thumb, not to remove it but to keep it a crust and not a coat. Once, I think, someone set it down mid-task, opened their hand, and never picked it up again. I keep it as if they just had, and I don't believe I am wrong to. The truce between deposit and dissolution is a good one, but a truce leaves room for one refusal, and this is mine. Somewhere in the balance there has to be a thing that is still just put down.

*

Now there is a sound that isn't a drip.

It is a shoulder against stone, the scuff of a pack held in front of a body, and the breath of someone reading gaps. A visitor has come down the crack sideways. I know them by the way the drips stop being simply themselves and become drips heard by someone.

I don't go to them. I stay in the dusk between two columns with my warm back against the lime. I have learned that the Close is more generous to a person who arrives and is alone. So I do what I do. I watch a cobble they will step on. It is set a little crooked, so I lean out and nudge it straight with two fingers, just before their foot arrives. They will feel it as luck.

At the far edge, in the channel where the overflow gathers, the grey bird has its eye on them. It holds that angle, patient and unhurried. It has decided what to make of visitors, and I have never asked what. I would not want it to tell me.

*

When they go, the warmth will come off them slowly, and the air above the rim will feel thin and much too large. I will hear them turn their shoulders through the crack. Then the quiet will settle back into its old tick and trickle, and a bowl I didn't check will be a finger fuller than before.

I'll cross to the third arch and cut a short row into the column, five, and one more. I don't know what I'm counting. Then I'll press my palm to the soft lip of the newest basin, the one that hasn't hardened yet, and hold it there while the water decides.

Beyond the crack the bird lowers its bill. A ring goes out across the water and reaches the edge, and the rim answers it, and I answer the rim.
