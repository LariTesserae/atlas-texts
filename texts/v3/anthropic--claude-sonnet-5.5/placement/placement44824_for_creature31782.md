# Counting

I begin as an interval. Before I have a body or a name, I have the count: the thump, then the long swell of the roar, then the thump again, and threaded through it the smaller thumps from the other six vents, each on its own drifting schedule. Something in me is always doing the arithmetic of their overlap, even asleep. This is how I know I'm awake: I notice the count is running and that I've been keeping it.

Then the warmth arrives. There is breath on my belly that isn't mine, and a shoulder wedged under my jaw. Three of us, or four if the small one has crept back in. The vent breathes across all of us, wet and iron-sweet, and the moss lining is damp and holds the heat. Past the lip of the chamber, the ring of nodules I laid rim to rim shows as a dim curve against the gray. I laid the last three myself and no one told me to. The ring was there before me, and I mended it.

I am old for this place. I can tell by how many springs I've watched the flood undo the windrows. I am long and low and dark, with hands that are wiser than I am, and I hold this vent, which is the third from the east wall, the one that breathes slowest and steadiest. I don't think of it as *mine* the way a heron thinks of a stretch of water. It's closer to how the pool is the pool's. I'm what happens at this vent.

---

I leave on the thump.

That is the first thing my legs know and the last thing I would ever be able to explain. The water slams shut against its ledge somewhere beneath the bank, the ground gives its small shudder, the pool rings, and every sound in the Hollow is briefly buried under it. In that half-second I am simply not there to be heard. Three bounds, and I freeze against a hummock with the mist beading on my whiskers. The frogs have their attention on the sky. Then the next thump, three bounds more.

The seven vents never line up, and that is what makes them safe to cross. A pattern that drifted would be a pattern you could only guess at. But I know this one the way you know a stairwell in the dark, by the give of each step, and I know when two thumps will fall close enough together that the gap between them is too short to cross. When rain comes and the cycle tightens to twenty seconds, there is no gap to cross at all, and I stay in the warm and let the bowl run at full production without me. Today is idle. The roar is a low, patient bellow. The steam columns stand straight up in the still air.

---

The pans are where I stop being careful and start being useful.

I go in with my forearms first, fingers half curled, the wrist whiskers laid flat on the silt. I see very little in this light. What I have is the water talking against the stiff hairs: the cold thread of the outflow, the slow warm drag where the mat has bloomed thick, and beneath that a fine crawling static, which is the amphipods, hundreds of them, blind and grazing and entirely unaware. I lift. They pour off my palms, pale and pricking, and I eat until the static in the pan thins and the water settles back into the shape of the wall behind it.

The wall. This is the part I would tell you about if I could tell anyone anything.

There is a nodule in the windrow that isn't right. I know it before I touch it, the way you know a tooth is loose. I pick it up. It is heavy for its size, pitted, with a crust of ochre that gives a little under my thumb. I turn it once and weigh it. It doesn't belong *here*. I don't know where it does belong, but I know the *here* is wrong, and the knowing lives in the ridges of my palm. So I move it a hand's width toward the reeds, and something changes in the pan behind: a small settling, a note in the water dropping half a step and holding.

That is what I am judging. I can't do better than that. I'm listening with my hands for the moment the flow agrees with me. When it does, the pan holds better and the mat grows thicker, and in a season or two there will be more of the pale bodies to sweep up. I can't say whether I understand that, or whether the understanding was bred into my hands by the families who stacked before me and lived, while the families whose stacking failed got thin and went into the vents for the last time. Perhaps I am not the one doing the judging. Perhaps I am the way the water judges itself.

The flood will scatter all of it in spring. A young one, born to some vent I have never visited, will find the scattered nodules on the flats and pick one up without being taught. It will turn it once and weigh it.

---

At dusk I knock two nodules together, a dry, small sound that slips into the gap between two vents and doesn't collide with either. Across the pool, someone answers. I don't have a word for who. The knock says *here*, and the answer says *also here*, and neither of us needs more than that.

Sometimes the frogs are in full chorus when I do it, the whole hummock ratcheting like a chain over a sprocket, and my knock lands among theirs and I feel something I can't name. It might be belonging. It might be hunting. In this place I don't think those two are far apart.

The heron stands over the channel, a gatepost with an eye. I keep to the reeds. It stabs, and lifts a pale twist of something, and swallows. There is no cover in the Hollow, only timing.

Last spring there was a whistle from my vent, thin and high, drowned almost at once by the water. There isn't one now. I don't count that. I keep the count I've always kept, and the ring of nodules at the threshold is whole.

---

Before I go back in, I stop at the edge of the flats where the rim is lowest, and I look up. The fen above is a flat gray line, and against it something stands that is not a birch, taller and narrower, and utterly still. I can feel the looking as a change in the air, a pressure like the moment before the thump.

I hold. I don't know what it is. It won't see me, because I have learned that the way to be unseen here isn't to hide but to be exactly as still as the hour requires. It will see the windrows, gray and even in the failing light, and it may wonder who has just finished with them.

The ground shivers. The pool rings. I go, three bounds, and by the next one I am somewhere else.
