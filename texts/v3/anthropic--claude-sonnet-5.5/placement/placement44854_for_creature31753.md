The first thing I know of myself is heat coming up through my soles.

It is the afternoon rock, the low steady warmth of an animal's flank, and for a moment that is all I am: a pair of feet on a dark dome. Then the wind changes, and I feel it before anything else does. It is not in my ears, which have little left in them. It is a swell of pitch in my heels, climbing through the shin bones and settling behind the breastbone. I lift one flat hand. A minute later the gust arrives and bends every tin-leaf giant on the flat toward the east, and the drape-vines over the near slot stand straight out sideways.

So I am someone who knows things a minute early. I have a body, then, and a age to go with it.

I am old. My knees say so, and so does the silence in my head, which is not the fog's silence but my own, the one the elders call *gone under early*. The high range left me some winters ago, the glassy shimmer first, then the fluting. Now the bass is mostly what I have, and I have it in my bones, which is perhaps where it always lived.

I am a tender of cairns.

I don't decide this. My left hand is already resting on a stack of flat rock, and under my palm the capping bone is humming. It is a shin-bone from a grazer, hollow, with a hole cut by someone whose name I could sign but not say. I know the note it should be making. I can't hear it anymore, so I have learned it another way. A true bone gives a clean, even buzz against the palm, like a held breath. A cracked bone gives a rough, scattered flutter, like a moth caught in the hand. This one is true. I sign *good* to nobody, thumb off the chin, and the small gesture goes out into the wind and is lost.

There are forty-one cairns on my route. I know them the way you know teeth with your tongue.

---

The girl who walks behind me is called by a sign, not a name: two fingers tapping the temple and then flicking out, *quick-ears*. She is fourteen. She has all her hearing and it is a torment to her. She flinches at the low groan of the big wells, and at the shimmer she winces and touches her plugs. I have told her, in the huge slow signs we use across a dome, that this is the price of being useful, and that I paid it before her. She rolled her shoulders at me, which is what the young do when they think you are being wise at them.

But she is useful. When the wind drops for a few breaths she stands with her eyes half shut and tells me what she hears with her hands, fast and small at the chest so I have to lean in. *The next one. East. The double note is wrong. Too flat.* And then, because she is honest: *I think. I'm not sure.*

I am sure, though I can't say how. I have felt the wrongness for two cairns now, not in a bone but in the ground. Something has shifted. One of the routes has a bad note in it somewhere, and the bad note is in the bone that says *water two hundred paces east.*

That is the one that matters. In the dry weeks a party without tank-water walks toward that note. If the bone has cracked and flattened, the note may sound like another cairn's, the one that stands at the lip of the big slot, three hundred paces the other way.

I don't tell the girl this. I put my hand on her shoulder and we walk.

---

We are half a morning from the cairn when the birds start to climb.

I see them only as specks, the long-winged ones holding station in the turning sky, but I see them going up and up, and I know what that means. I stop. The girl is already stopped, and she has seen it too. Neither of us signs. It is the quiet kind of knowing, the kind you don't spend hands on.

The fog will settle by dawn. Perhaps sooner.

We reach the water-cairn by mid-afternoon, with the cloud already thickening in the west, a gray thumbprint that is swelling as I watch. I lay my palm on the capping bone and the flutter comes up my arm. Moth in the hand. It is cracked, a fine hair of a crack that I can't see and could not hear, and it has been sounding wrong for who knows how many days.

I sign to the girl to stand back. She does. I take the spare from my pack, a new bone wrapped in dry moss, cut last winter from the shin of a grazer we took with the whole party standing still afterward. I ease the old one from its cap of flat rock, and I do it gently, as you would take a tooth from a sleeping child. I fit the new bone into the stack and lay my hand flat across the top of it.

Nothing. The wind is between gusts. Then it comes, out of the east, the big swell under my heels, and the bone takes it and sings into my palm a clean, even, unbroken buzz. Held breath.

I stand there, feeling it, for longer than the work needs.

The girl steps up beside me, and her face is doing the small dramatic things faces do. She signs, tiny, at her chest: *That's it. That's right. You got it right.* And then, because she is young and can't help it: *How did you know?*

I don't answer in hands. I touch my own sternum, and I touch hers.

---

The fog arrives before we can reach a hut.

I watch it come. It takes the tin-leaf giants first, one row at a time, until their raised gray hands go soft and then go out. It takes the near dome. Then the cloud is on us, and the wind goes out of it, and the roar of the plateau, which I have not heard in years except as pressure, thins away into a great slack pulse that even I can feel loosening in the ground.

The girl is still. Her hands go up to her ears. She takes out the wax, first one side and then the other, and she stands with her head tilted and her mouth open a little. I can see the drip from the leaf-tips reaching her, and the tick of beetles in the moss. Her face falls apart into the look I have seen all my life on all of them, the look of someone hearing everything they are for.

She is crying. She does not hide it.

I have nothing to take out. That used to be a grief, in the first winters. Now I stand beside her in the white, and it is the same for me as it always was, because I have learned that the fog is not only a sound. It is a slackening. It is the world letting go of the effort of being loud, and my body knows the letting go as well as hers does.

She turns to me. Her lips move, husky and shy, unused, and I can't hear a word of it. I see the words as shapes, though. *Can I? Would you?*

I nod.

She cups her hands and puts them to my collarbone, the way you would hold something small and alive against the cold. I lay my forehead against her hair. I feel her breath first, and then, from the hollow of her chest through the bone of mine, the low warm vibration of a girl singing.

I don't know the song. It is hers. It is a thin, crooked little thing, and I can feel where she takes a breath in the wrong place. I feel the small unsteady climb into the upper notes where her voice is not yet sure. It goes through my collarbone and into my skull, and I think, very clearly, that of everything the plateau has ever taught me, this is the thing.

When she is done I hold her by the shoulders. Then I do what I have been holding for a long time. I take her hands, turn them palm-up, and draw a sign on the left one with my fingertip, slowly, in the smallest speech we have. It says: *Now I will sing you mine. You will keep them. When I am in the well, you will not sing them. You will only know them.*

She looks at me through the white. Behind us the new bone hums its clean note to nobody, out on the dark stone, and somewhere far off a deep well finishes a tone alone, and I feel it stop.

I put my hands to her collarbone. I open my mouth. I do not know what will come out. I have not sung in eleven dry seasons.
