I begin by wanting to be the hawk.

It's the obvious wish. I'd have the cliff-thermals, the kilometer of air, and the power to silence a mile of frogs by crossing it. But the hawk sees the plateau as a green cloth with small things moving on it, and the plateau isn't that. The hawk has never felt the noise in its sternum. It takes the quiet as tribute, and I suspect it never learns that the quiet is a held breath, not an absence.

So I drift down, and for a while I think I'll be a well. The slow ones tempt me: a black throat breathing cold air, keeping a stone's fall for seconds like a secret. To be the place where water leaves the world, to receive petals and feathers and beetles as rumor from above and pass them on into the dark. But a well has no one to look after. It only accepts. I want something that goes back and forth.

Then the old woman at the front of the file, with the map in her ears. I stay with her longer than the others, walking bowed, reading the pitch of the ground. But she is a guest, and she knows it, and she leaves when the rains come from the east. I would always be leaving. What I want is to be someone the place cannot picture without.

So I go down, and down, past the slugs and the toad rolling off its cushion in injured dignity, past the paprika mites, to a bromeliad the size of a cartwheel on a wet slope, and I am the frog.

---

I am the size of a lentil. My back is black and my throat is the yellow of butter left in sun. My whole country is one plant, a spiral of stiff leaves that hold twenty small pools at different heights and angles, and I know it the way you know your own house in the dark. I don't see the route to each cup. I feel it. Left along the ridge of the third leaf, a slide down the wet base where the leaf curls, a stretch of bare vertical where my toes have to be sure, then the cup with the drowned bit of bark at its bottom. In that cup lives a child of mine who is mostly tail and appetite.

Every few days I visit each one. I climb with nothing on my back or with a new tadpole on it, and I lay one unfertilized egg in the water beside the child. It is not for the future. It is food, and it is all I have to give. Twenty cups, and an egg for each. When I am tired the leaves seem to lengthen. When it rains the pools fill and the tadpoles ride the swell like sleepers on a boat, and I count them by touch, twenty, twenty, twenty, and settle.

At dusk I call. *Tink.* A clean note, a little above the pitch of a struck glass. I don't sing it so much as let it out, the way you let out a breath you've been carrying all day. It goes out across the hollow and other frogs answer from other plants, and I learn things from their answers. This slope is solid. The mat under the next ridge is thin, and the voices there sound low and round, as if from inside a drum. Some hollow nobody has tuned yet is quiet, and I do not like the quiet. My note passes through the roots, and I feel a hundred million others pass through them too, and the ground is not underneath us. The ground is what we are doing.

It is loud, and from here it is not chaos. Anyone who lives inside it can hear the grammar: the whistlers on the hour, the wire-drawers in the lows, the long coin-spinning descent from somewhere behind me that always comes just before the cold. The insects make a floor and a ceiling, and the hummingbirds make a thrum I don't hear as sound anymore, only as the air being alive. I am one syllable in it. I'm not sorry about that. I think being a syllable is the best thing available.

Some days the people go by. I feel them first, through the leaf, a soft weight sinking the mat a hand's depth, then another, unhurried, the old one at the front. They walk with their heads down as though listening for me, and I think perhaps they are. I call *tink* and they slow, and one of them puts a foot a little to the left. I have no idea what I've told them. I know only that it's a kind of company, and they are gone by evening like weather.

Sometimes the shadow comes. I don't see the hawk. I feel the plateau change, a hush starting somewhere far away and running toward me like a wave, everyone stopping mid-note. I stop. My throat is full of a sound I haven't let go. The cups are still, the tadpoles are still, twenty children are holding twenty breaths in the water, and I am on the leaf with my whole body pressed to the wet green, small enough that the shadow has no reason to notice. Nothing moves for a long time. The mat creaks. Far below, water finds its way toward the dark.

Then the shadow passes.

Nothing happens for a moment, and this is the part I am for. Somewhere in the ridge of silence, someone has to be first. It doesn't have to be brave. It only has to be willing to be the one sound in a wide quiet. So I let out the note I've been holding, *tink*, thin and clear, alone. Then a second frog answers from the next plant, and a whistler behind that, and the wire-drawers, and the drummers pour in under them, until the noise rebuilds from that one point like a fire catching, and the ground is singing again under everything.

I think the plateau is old and being slowly unmade from the inside, and someday my plant will slump into a black mouth and take my twenty cups with it. But not tonight. Tonight I climb down the wet base of the third leaf, past the curl and the bare stretch, to the cup with the bark in it. I lay an egg beside my child. I wait a moment, feeling the whole hill hum through my feet. Then I go to the next.
