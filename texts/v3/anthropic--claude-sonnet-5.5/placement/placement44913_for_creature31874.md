I begin without a shape, as heat with no border. For a while I am only wetness gathering on a leaf, swelling until I let go and tick against something lower. I am the drip. That lasts a few breaths, and then I try the sloth-slow mammal on its limb, wrapped in damp fur gone faintly green. I hold that shape as long as I can. It is very restful, but it keeps nothing. It is kept, by the limb and the dark and the fact that nothing here wants it. The snake below it is the same, and so is the bird between them. They are sediment, and I am not sediment, because my hands are moving.

That is how I learn what I am. My fingers are working a cord in the dark, and my fingers know the work before I do.

I am one of the Few. I am not the oldest, who sits at the river with her back against the boiler, listening to something the rest of us can't hear. I am the one she gave the cords to, the one who holds the count.

---

I am sitting against a buttress root, in the angle where it meets the trunk, and I am so still that a beetle has decided I am bark. Its feet are quick and dry on my forearm, and I let it cross. My skin has stopped registering the air as separate from me. That is the first discipline: not to be a thing that stands against the heat, but a thing the heat passes through on its way to being ground.

The cord runs through my hands. It is bast, twisted long ago and retwisted many times since, and the knots on it are as legible to me as a face. A long single knot is a year. A doubled turn is a season of high water. A loop with a bead of hardened resin is a death that has not finished happening. I am reading that one now. Under my thumb it is the nineteenth bead on the cord of the great tree upriver, the one with the buttress like a wall, which died standing and has been dying since before I was born. Twenty years, they say, to fall. I have never seen it, only touched it, but I know it better than I know most of the Few. I know by the sound of the drip under its crown, which is different now. Water on living leaf makes a soft, cushioned tick. Water on bare wood makes a hard small click, and there is more of it each year. Light will come down through that crown someday, and a hundred saplings that have waited decades in the dark will begin their slow contest. I will not see who wins. Someone's hands will hold the cord by then.

I don't mind this. It is what the cord is for.

---

What I count is only the slow things. The oxbow silting, the terrace lifting under a changed root, the place where the river is thinking of a new loop, and whether that thought is getting more serious. I do not count the beetles. The beetles cannot be counted, and pretending they could would be a kind of lie. So my accounting has a hole shaped like everything, and the everything goes on around it, breathing.

Some nights I feel the whole basin as one animal, and that the Few are its attention. It is not a grand feeling. It is like the awareness a body has of its own hand in the dark, without looking, knowing where it is. We are where the forest notices itself. That is all, and it is enough to spend a life on.

---

The rails I have walked. Every young one does, and I did, out along the two red lines while they still stood above the mud, and then the last stretch where the iron sinks into root and vanishes. There I stood facing east, where the underside of the cloud is stained the color of a coal seen through a closed eyelid. The Other Shore. And under it the thrum, which I felt more in my breastbone than my ears.

I did not want to go. I want to say that clearly, because the elders think the young ones stand there out of longing, and some may. What I felt was different. I felt like a knot near the end of a very long cord, and I understood that the cord did not end at me. The rails are a cord too, laid down by hands I will never meet, and somewhere the other end is held by someone I will never know. They may be listening to the same hum. They may be as unable to count what surrounds them. That was a comfort, and a complete one. It did not require me to go. So I turned around, and I have done so every time since. No one made that a rule.

---

Something moves in the tree overhead. A frog shifts its weight on a leaf-edge, and a bead of water lets go and finds my shoulder. I feel it run down along the collarbone and lose itself in the sheen of my skin. Farther off, at the river, the big fish rolls again and sets itself down like a sack on wet ground. I hear it a moment after the water does.

Then a long crack, unhurried, from somewhere upstream. A branch gives way, and a soft crash follows.

My hands stop on the cord. It is not the great tree. The great tree will not sound like that. It will take a whole minute to fall, and every sleeping thing within a kilometer will know. This is a lesser branch, and there is no knot for it. The fallen thing will feed the forest, and the forest will feed us from what it lets go, as it always has. I ease my grip and hold the beaded loop of the nineteenth year between finger and thumb, only holding it.

The heat does not leave. The drip resumes. The beetle, having finished with my arm, goes on up the trunk.

I shift my weight by the width of a thumb, settle, and keep listening.
