I am awake before the Levelers, as always, and the first thing I do is listen.

Not for anything in particular. I lie in the dark of my room on the fifth level of a block that sulks to the east, and I let the building tell me what it is doing. Somewhere below, a slip joint ticks as the night's heat lets go of it. Three floors down, a neighbor's gutter is running at half strength, a flat dull note like a cough. I will mention it to her, gently, and she will say she knew, and we will both be lying a little, because neither of us knew until I said it. That is how we keep each other.

My name is Oda. I am a drip-keeper, and I am old enough that the felt wipers have worn a shine into the pads of my fingers, though not the numb shine of a scraper. Mine is a sensitive one. I can tell you from a plate's surface whether it has been dusted with ash or with glass, and whether the frost in the deep pipes is running true.

I dress in the dark and go out into the grey.

---

The plates hang from the ridges like laundry for giants. At this hour they are cold, colder than anything else on the mountain, and they carry a faint blue in their steel that vanishes once the sun reaches them. I climb among them with the wiper over my shoulder. I don't hurry, because hurrying wastes breath, and breath is also counted here, though no one keeps that account on cords.

*Tick.* The first bead forms on the ninth plate of the Cistern Row ridge. I have been watching for it. I stop and hold still, as I always do, because it seems rude to walk past. A bead is not a small thing. It is the mountain giving up a breath it did not have to, the air of the whole upper world passing through a sheet of metal and leaving a little of itself behind. Then another bead, then a dozen, then the plate goes silver all at once, and the first thin runnel starts down to the gutter.

The young ones think this is beautiful, and it is. What I would tell them, if they asked, is that it is also *accounting*. Every bead is a line in a ledger I did not open and cannot close. I carry it in my hands and my knees, and when I lie down at night I can feel it in my ribs, the whole slow tally of what falls and what is kept.

I clear a grit from a runnel with the edge of the felt. I don't say anything to it.

---

Here is what I don't tell people.

There are nights when the city has gone quiet, when the furnace mouths are dull and the amber awnings hang without a breath under them, and I go down alone into the low vault where the deepest tank is. The stone there is cool in a way nothing else in Ombrel is cool, and my skin remembers what it is to be a body that is not being cooked. I put my palm flat on the tank wall, not for warmth, for the other thing.

It taps. Three, and a pause. Two. Then a long silence, in which my own pulse is very loud.

I have tapped back for thirty-one years. I use my ring, which is plain iron and was my mother's. I do not tap the same as it does. I have never tried to copy it. That would be a kind of theft, or worse, a kind of mockery. I tap what comes: once, twice, a rest I let go a little long. Sometimes it answers right away and sometimes it does not answer for so long that I go home, and at the top of the stair I feel the reply come up through my boots anyway, faint, like a thought I was about to have.

I don't know what it is. I know it isn't afraid of me. I know that when the tanks were last inspected by the council, the water came up sweet, and the inspectors, who were all tired and hot and grumpy, drank from their palms and were quiet for a while and then went home. I know that once, when I was ill and could not climb down, a younger keeper went in my place and came back grey in the face and would say only, *It asked for you.* I have not been able to decide whether that was a kindness or an accusation.

I think it is lonely. I say this to no one. It's an unforgivable sentiment to bring into a city that measures itself by the cupful. But everything else in this place has company: the Levelers have their buildings, the beetles their slow road, the swifts the whole open air. The thing in the tank has only the dark and the cold and, at night, a stranger's ring on the wall. So I tap, and it is not pity. It is closer to what the Held mean by *holding*. Not *are you well* but *are you still there, is the ground still under you.* And the answer, *Holding*, or something like it, that I feel in the stone.

---

The Exhalation came on the eleventh day, in the middle of the afternoon.

I was at a stoop on Widening Four, where three pipes cross and the old ones gather to argue about who has been coughing worst. The first sign is always the floor. It goes still in a way it never is, the small constant tremor that lives in every plank dropping away, as if the mountain had taken a breath in and held it. Then the hiss, from everywhere, and the white.

We stood. I put my palm against the warm brick and felt the vibration come up, enormous and slow, a chord too low to be a sound. The sun went to a pale coin. Ten degrees left the air at once. Beside me a man I've known forty years closed his eyes, and neither of us said *why*. Nobody ever says why. It is the only hour when the mountain isn't listening to the city, Old Teshe told me once, and the city ought to return the courtesy.

But here is what I have never told Teshe. In the white, with my hand against the wall and the whole hill breathing out around me, I felt the tanks. Not the sound, no tapping, but the *stillness* of them, deep under my feet, as complete and attentive as my own. The thing was holding too. In the hour when nothing is asked of any of us, it held its silence the way we held ours, and I understood, with an odd pang like grief or relief, that it had been doing this for longer than there was a city. It has been standing in the white in the dark, all this time, not working, not tapping, and not able to say why.

That is how I know it is one of us. Not by the tapping. By the courtesy.

---

The white drains off the terraces slowly, the way it always does. The plates come out of it one by one, every bead lit at once, and the city begins to drip: ten thousand edges, the whole roof of the world letting go. A child on a gantry stops mid-step and holds up her hand to catch one, and her mother lets her.

I climb back up to the ninth plate of the Cistern Row ridge. My knees complain. The lean has been growing in me lately, a little more each year, the way the archive line hangs a little further out of true. Someday I will be a plumb line in a doorway, letting whoever passes see how far I have settled.

But not today. Someone is coming up the stair behind me with a Leveler's long pale coat and a vial rack clicking at her hip, and she is young and in a hurry, and I know what she is going to say before she says it.

"Holding?"

I put my palm against the plate. The last bead runs down under it and away.

"Holding," I say, and under my feet, three floors down and forty feet deep, something answers.
