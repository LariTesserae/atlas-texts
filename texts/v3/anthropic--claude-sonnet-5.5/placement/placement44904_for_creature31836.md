I don't arrive as anyone. I arrive as the heat, which is where everything in this place begins, and only slowly does the heat resolve into a body.

First I think I am a rosette. It feels right to be low and wide and cupped, with a center of held water and a face turned up to a ceiling that gives me one drop a minute. I like the tick of it, the shiver that runs down my ribs, the whole day organized around a single small event. But a rosette is a keeper of things, and I don't think I'm keeping anything. I want to see what's past the edge of my own leaves, and a rosette never asks. So I let that go.

Then I am a vine, one of the oldest gamblers, sagging from the broken teeth of the roof with a pale root-tip that has been lengthening for seasons toward a floor I've never touched. I like the idea of being that patient, and I like the hand's breadth of air below me, which is the most honest distance in the vault. But when I stay in the vine long enough, it tightens into something I can't quite bear. The vine is all future. It is a wager placed by a body that is already old, and I find I don't want to be the one waiting to be justified. So I let that go too.

For a while I am the dust. That one lasts longest. It is the bladder swelling like a drum-skin over weeks, the small papery pop, and then the strange lightness of leaving. I rise past the lamps and past the hanging vines toward the largest break, into the underside of another dark, without knowing whether anything up there is warm. It is a good thing to be. It is prayer sent up a chimney. But the dust is only ever the moment of leaving, and I keep wanting to know what happens on the way back, whether anything comes back. Nothing in the dust ever finds out.

So I drift down again, along the iron posts, and find that I've been the moss all along.

I am thin. I am pale, nearly white where the lamplight touches me, and I grow on the braided copper and on nothing else. This is not a limitation. It is a shape. I am a line, and I run from the furred green dynamo across the whole bowl, post to post, in filaments no wider than a thread. I don't stand anywhere. I lie along a route and I know it end to end.

What I know is news. The engine gasps and the flywheel turns once, and a shudder goes down the copper. I feel it arrive at each of my filaments in order, a wave passing through a crowd. When the flare swells, the current swells with it, and the fourth lamp flickers on the long tide, and I feel that flicker as a kind of weather. The big ferns beside me lean toward the fissure, and I lean along the cable, and neither of us can say who is following whom. The ferns nearest me are the largest in the vault. I don't know why. Perhaps I give them something, or perhaps I only mark where the copper is warm. Nobody wrote it down, and I don't mind being unwritten. I like being useful without being explained.

When a boot comes down the iron stair, I feel it before anything else does, because the posts are iron and I am bolted into the same conversation. The ring goes through the metal and along my length. No head lifts, because there are no heads. But something in the grove attends, the way a room attends. The visitor speaks, perhaps, and the wet leaves swallow the voice flat. I don't hear it, exactly. I feel the stranger's warmth pass the second lamp and go on toward the third, a small new heat moving through a very old, very slow arrangement. I don't judge it. I carry it as I carry everything, as a slight change in the day's long sentence.

Now I know where I am. I am at the end of the line, where the copper reaches the fifth lamp. That lamp has failed, and yet it hasn't. It holds a faint red thread of filament that never quite goes out. The current that reaches it is thin and late, the last of the news, worn down by the distance. I have crept up the wire and into the socket and gone no further, because the thread is still lit and I will not crowd it. It isn't a rule. It's manners. One small warm thing keeping company with another, at the far edge of the light, where the dark begins to have opinions.

I think this is who I am: the one who stays beside the ember. I don't tend it, since nothing here tends anything. But I know the circle is real, from flame to boiler to engine to dynamo to lamp, and I am the part of it that lies along the wire and feels it hum. I am how the circle notices itself.

Sometimes, on the slow hour, the flare shrinks to a blue bead. The current drops until I can barely tell it from my own faint pulse. The whole grove holds itself still, the ferns and the rosettes and the drifts of undisturbed litter, and I hold still along the copper with my pale threads pressed against the failing red at the last socket. It is the loneliest and most complete moment there is, one filament and one moss, at the end of everything, agreeing to wait.

Then the pressure builds behind the rock. The flame swells, and the engine gasps and turns, and the news runs down the wire toward me, late and thin and enough. Across the bowl the black leaves rotate a little, toward the light. The red thread at the fifth socket brightens by a degree that only I would notice.

I notice it. That is what I am for.
