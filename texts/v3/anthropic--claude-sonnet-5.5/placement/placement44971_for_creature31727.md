I begin with the feet, because on the plateau everything begins with the feet.

The ground is warm through the pad, and I test it before I trust it. Sinter has a sound when it will hold, a dull note like a knuckle on a full cask. When it's hollow, the note goes high and thin, and I lift the foot and go around. I have never been wrong. Foxes learn the crust with their scars, but I learned it by standing very still on each terrace, and my weight has not gone through yet.

It's late in the day. The noon stillness has broken, and the virga are stroking down out of the cloud and dissolving before they reach us. I watch them without hope. Rain that doesn't land is still a kind of talk in the sky, and I have never stopped listening to it. The stone is starting to cool. I can feel it in the way the warm from below begins to outweigh the warm from above, and the air on my back goes from hard to merely bright.

That's the hour. My whole body knows it before I do. The neck comes up on its own, and I turn toward the near wall.

---

I am old. I know it from the order. The mothers go first, with the calves pressed under their chins, and they take the low faces where the beads come fastest. Then the yearlings crowd in behind them, jostling and stupid with thirst. I wait with the other old males at the back, and the wall is already darkening by the time my turn comes. That was a matter of pride once, being last, because last means highest. Nobody else can reach the upper courses, where the water beads longest and tastes most of iron. My neck is still the longest thing on this hillside.

I lift it, set my tongue to the stone, and draw.

Salt, then chalk, then the iron at the end like a coin held in the mouth. The tongue is gray to its root by now and has no feeling except for taste. All around me the other tongues are working, and the sound they make is the sound of a great many people turning the pages of stiff paper. I have listened to that sound my whole life, and I would know it in my grave.

The water is not the trouble. It is the other thing, between the walls, which is the lichen. Black, orange, and chalk-white, it clings to every rock in the long dry stretches, and you have to strip it with the back teeth, grinding until it comes away. I have ground for so many seasons that there is no grinding left in me. I run my tongue across the ridges and find them smooth as river stones. The lichen slides under them now and stays on the rock. Each dawn I go out into the dry with the herd and each dusk I come back thinner, and each dusk I take my place a little farther toward the edge.

I know what that means. Every licker knows. We don't talk about it, since we don't talk, but it is known the way the cold is known. There is an edge to the herd, and animals drift there and stay there, and the foxes come to know their names, if foxes have names for us.

---

This evening a fox is watching me.

She lies in the shadow of a sinter rim with her chin flat on the stone. Her paws are black at the pads and there's a pale scar over her shoulder where the fur never grew back. She is not interested in me yet. She is interested in a calf on the far side of the wall who has taken three steps too many toward the lip of a mud pot. The mother pulls it back with her neck. The fox lets out her breath, shifts a paw, and goes back to seeming to watch nothing. We are courteous, the fox and I. I do not run, and she does not hurry. I think we both understand that I am a long way from the day when the arrangement changes.

Above us the broad dark birds have stopped circling and simply hang, wing-tilted, on the warm column over the vent. They are patient too. Everyone on the plateau is patient. It is the only thing the heat is good for.

---

When I have drunk what the wall will give, I do what I have started to do, and I leave the herd.

I go east. The tuff there is scored in polygons, and the gullies are settled and shallow, and after a while the pipes come into view, on their stilts, wrapped in silver and rust. A lizard is still clinging to the warm insulation, a mottled flat thing, holding on to the last of the day's heat. It does not look at me. Beyond it, where one pipe meets another at a flange the color of an old tooth, there is a hollow in the ground, and in the hollow there is water.

It isn't like the walls. It doesn't sweat out of stone. It falls, a bead at a time, from the seam of the flange, each drop with a small clear sound like a word said alone in a large room. Years of it have made a bowl, and the bowl has made a seep, and in the seep the tussock grass has come up. It's soft, wiry grass, fine enough that a mouth without grinding in it can still work it. I did not understand at first why I kept coming. Then I put my head down and understood.

I eat. The grass tastes of grease and steam and something faintly sweet. I eat slowly, and the drip keeps its time beside my ear.

The tall slow one comes along the line. I know her by the smell of oil and hot metal and by the wrench that clicks against her belt. She has walked this line every dusk of my life. I have heard her say something low, sometimes, as she passes the old males, and I don't know what it means, though I know it is meant for us. Tonight she stops beside the flange. She has her can in one hand and the wrench in the other, and the flange is two paces from her, wet and shining, obviously a thing that could be fixed.

She looks at it. She looks at me, with my long neck bent down into her grass. Then she puts the wrench back on her belt and goes on down the line, walking at the pace of the herds.

I don't know what that was. I only know that the drip continues.

---

Full dark now, and the stars have come out crowded and enormous. The ground is still warm. Far off the herd is folding down in rows on the open tuff, necks curled back, and soon the pools will be full of sleeping waders standing on one leg, and the foxes will begin their rounds. Behind me the turbine hums, felt in the breastbone before it is heard, and I have never once known it to stop.

I will not go back to the herd tonight. I lower myself onto the warm ground with my legs folded under, beside the hollow, and curl my neck along my flank. The heat comes up through the fleece. Steam lifts off my back in a thin pale thread, and I watch it go up and join the steam from the vent and the steam from the pool and the steam from the sleeping herd, until I cannot tell which is mine.

The drip counts something. I don't know what. I count with it for a while, one breath, one drop, in the thin air, until the counting is only the sound of being here.
