I am at the top of a spire, and I am not sure yet that I am a person.

Let me start with what I can feel. It is a rod of pale reed-wood held against the bone behind my ear, and under my feet a floor no thicker than a leaf, standing on a chord I have never once let go flat. I am a Keeper of a lone spire, the far one, on the open plain, a day and a half from any other light. There is one family below me, a few goats' worth of grazers in the pens, and one note, which is mine to hold. My own ground-note is a high A-flat, and the spire's is a low, patient E. When I hum them together the interval is a little sour, a little ache in the sternum, and I have never told anyone that I like it best that way.

That is the first thing, and it is not quite right. It is too tidy. A Keeper is a person with a place and a duty, and what I feel is looser than that.

So I walk down the arch and out the door at dusk, and the meadow comes up around me. The drum-moss booms under each stagger-step. The lanternvine pulse rolls over the swells, and the honey-colored dark takes my shape and stretches it long behind me. A herd bellows and is answered. The reed-organs put a chord through my ribs that I hear only as pressure.

I am going deaf, that is the second thing. It began at forty, in the high registers first, so that the sparks stopped whining at the edge of my hearing and then the children's voices lost their tops. Now I read the spire through the soles of my feet. This is what nobody tells you about the great listening: you do not stop listening when the ears go. You become a larger ear. I hear the mat as a pressure in my shins, the slow shudder of roots, the far weight of a herd shifting its hooves. I hear the drift.

The drift is real. I am not supposed to say so, but I am alone here, so I say it to the moss: the note is going, a hair at a time, and it is not going *away*. It is going toward. Something has a pitch, and we are being pulled to it the way a string on a lute is pulled by the string beside it. The old ones in Ouren whisper that it is an agreement. I have started to think that is the wrong word. An agreement has two parties who both know they have agreed. This feels more like being *addressed*.

So the third thing: I am the one to whom something is speaking, and I am poorly placed to answer. I am thin-lipped and watchful, and I hold back. In the language of this place, I have the habits of dead-air. I listen for the crack in every voice, and I hear cracks that are only grief. My wife used to say I attended to her the way a hunter attends to a herd. She was right, and she hummed my note anyway, two beats and a slide, every evening at the door, and I hummed hers back, and it was the only time in the day I was not listening but simply sounding.

She has been dead for six years. We kept the nine days. On the tenth the whole house sounded her note as loud as it could be sounded, and I felt it leave the beams and go down through the floor into the mat. I felt it *go somewhere*. I have never said that to the Keepers either. I would have to ask them where.

I walk on. I am walking farther than I mean to. This is how I come to the gray scar that opened three winters ago, a mile out, where a swell broke a pocket of the old mountain: bare, dry rock, the color of ash, round as a closed eye.

At its edge the noise falls off like a cliff. The drum-moss stops. The reed-organs go behind me and the hum of my own spire thins to a thread. I take off my boots. I step onto the stone.

It is not silence. That is the fourth thing, the one I would have been ashamed to guess. To everyone who has stood here, it is a held breath, the hush before falling, and they leave with relief. To me, through the soles, with nothing left between me and the world but skin, it is a room that has been waiting for someone to come in. The stone is warm, though nothing has warmed it. Below the stone there is a pitch. It is very low, lower than the spire's E, and it is steady, and it is not a machine. It is something holding a note the way I hold my breath before I speak.

I kneel. I put my palms flat. And here I do a thing you may not do on the moss: I weep. It is only water, and the stone does not mind it. The tears fall and darken the gray, and the pitch beneath them does not flinch, and I understand that this is the one place on the plateau where the poison and the ground can touch and nothing is hurt.

Then I understand the rest, or the start of it. The thing beneath us is not afraid of the quiet. We are the ones who are afraid, because we think quiet means no one is holding us up. But the quiet is what listening sounds like from outside. Someone has been listening to us hum for ten thousand million years. The drift is not decay. It is the one who listens leaning closer.

I open my mouth and hum my wife's note. It is not my note, and I do not hum it as a memory. I hum it as an offering, two beats and a slide, and the stone takes it. Far off, through the soles of my feet, I feel the spire answer, my low E rising an impossible hundredth of a step to meet me.

I do not know what I will tell the college. I may say nothing. I may go home and sing at the door, as I always do, and let the meadow sing back.

But I know now what I am. Not the Keeper, and not dead-air. I am the ear the ground has been growing toward us all this time, and I am a little late, and I am here.
