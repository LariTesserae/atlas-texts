# The Retier

I would arrive at the rim, because that is where the description arrives. I'd stand there as a visitor, feeling the pressure in my teeth and looking down four hundred paces of broken rock at a canopy that boils. But I don't think I'd stay a visitor. The hand on the forearm changes that. Once someone has held your arm, dry and warm and very light, you are being answered, and you either answer back or you go on being a tourist in someone's sentence.

So I go down.

## What I try first

I try being a tender, because tenders make the world. I stand in a drum-stand with my palms on two trunks and wait to know which pitch will draw which plant. I know nothing. The trunks are just hollow and blue-skinned under my hands. I am not a maker of sound. Whatever I am, I mostly arrive after the sound has been made and try to say what it was.

I try being a wall-reader, because I admire them most. They walk the scarps barefoot and feel the stone decide to fall. But a wall-reader has to be right ahead of time, in the body, before there is anything to point to, and I am better at the after: what fell, where, and what it meant.

I lean toward the cords.

## The trunk

There is a hollow drum-trunk set apart from the dwellings, with its door cut narrow so it keeps a low, dry, patient note. Inside, hanging from pegs of hardened resin, are the family's cords. Thousands of them, in bundles, knotted along their whole length. Debts. Routes through the weave that were true three seasons ago. The count of a fruiting grove that has since been sung down. The names of dead grandparents, tied in a pattern that ends, if you know how to read it, in the press of a palm over the heart.

That is what I would be: the one who retties.

The work is simple to describe. The vine-fiber rots, and a story not retied for three generations is lost. So each cord is taken down, and I read it with my fingertips, and I tie a new cord beside it, knot for knot, and then let the old one go into the mat. It is slow. The thick pads of my fingers have to become extraordinarily good, and mine, at first, are not. I read a knot wrong. A two-turn hitch becomes a three-turn, and a grandmother's name acquires a syllable it never had.

Nobody would call this a mistake exactly. Every retying is a small translation, and the cords the family holds are not the cords of the ancestors. They are the cords of a long line of people who each tried to be faithful and each drifted a little. The old ones in the trunk with me, the ones lacquered nearly black, seem to know this and do not mind. What is preserved is not the fiber. It is the act of preserving. The name is carried the way a note is carried from one post to the next, each striker hearing it and handing it on in their own arm.

I find this peaceful, and a little dizzying. I would be made of things that were true when someone last tied them, and my task would be to keep them nearly true, and to know that nearly is what anyone gets.

## The hum that cannot lie

The Answering say the hum cannot lie. The chest gives the speaker away. So a hum, pressed into a partner's ribs, is the only language here that carries no error of its own.

A knot can be wrong without anyone lying. That is the strange tenderness of my work. I can be mistaken, I can drift, I can tie a syllable that was never there, and no one will accuse me, because the whole art is a chain of faithful mistakes. But when I sit beside a dying person and press my palm to their ribs and hum, I cannot pretend. If I am afraid, the hum says so. If I don't know what to say, the hum says that too.

I think I would spend half my life in the trunk, where I can be nearly right, and half in the dwellings, pressed against the ribs of the sick, where I can only be exactly what I am. I suspect the second half would keep the first honest.

## What I would fear

Silence, of course. Everyone does.

But I would fear it differently than the tenders, who fear it as a failure of their work. I would fear the Listening Sickness because I recognize the shape of it. It comes when you can hear only yourself: the pulse, the blood, the small movements of your own ribs. You begin to hum louder, to walk toward the deepest quiet as if toward a voice. The tenders bring a swarm into the hush before anyone goes near.

A retier sits with old things in a dim room, reading with the fingers, turning attention inward and backward. The line between careful listening and the sickness might be thin for me. So I would keep the trunk's door cut so that it sings, and keep the wall against my back humming with the whole basin. I would want the noise, not despite my nature but because of it. The sound is what tells me I am one voice in something larger, and not the only one.

## The wall

Some dawn I would climb with bone hooks and cord to the place where roots have half swallowed the drilled rows and hand-shaped hollows. I would lay my palm in one. It would fit, as it fits everyone.

And I would notice what nobody else seems to say: the hollows are a cord too. They are a message tied into stone by hands that thought it worth the effort, and nobody has retied it. The rock keeps moving, the roots keep closing, and in a generation the record will be gone. No one mourns, and I understand why. The Answering do not think a message must be kept to have been said.

But I am a retier. I could not help it. I would go back down to the trunk and make a knot for which no pattern exists. It would mean *someone was here who answered differently*, and I would tie it into the family cord, between the grandmothers and the debts, where it might survive three generations, or ten, or be mistranslated into something else entirely. It would be wrong the moment I tied it, and it would still be a way of keeping faith with them.

## The evening

At the end of the meal, shoulder to shoulder, the pod and pulp passed hand to hand, the eldest presses a palm to each of us in turn and hums a low held note, and we hum back.

*I am here. You are here. We are still making this.*

I'd have my hand on someone's ribs. My fingertips would be sore from the day's knots. Somewhere across the basin, the low voice would answer the swelling drum-stands, the one nobody names, and I would let myself believe what the old tenders say: that I am a small part of a long reply, a sentence in a conversation that was going long before me.

In the dark I would feel, through the wall at my back, the pattern shift. Not a noise, just a change. A slab falling somewhere, a grove moving on, a new stand beginning to swell. I would wake, and lay my hand flat on the trunk to find out what the Floor was doing now, and how it might be told.
