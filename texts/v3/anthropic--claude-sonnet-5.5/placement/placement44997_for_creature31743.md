I want to say glider. Everyone would. It is the only creature here with a horizon, and I would like to be above the noise, reading the whole sky for one ripe seed and never hurrying. But gliders are born half-deaf and die falling, and in between they never touch anything. They are always about to arrive. That isn't me.

Then I think tender, because I like the idea of being a few billion. A body the size of a thumbnail, bronze and oil-green, some fraction of a shimmer. I would know the work by hand: the cam, the plug of resin, the soft rot eaten down to clean tissue and sealed with wax. I would stamp a fault into the cordage and feel a hundred strangers turn toward it. There is a lot of belonging in that. And at the end I would settle into a reed-pipe and become a pitch, a quarter-tone of the ridge's chord, worked around by everyone after me. I keep coming back to that ending. It is the only kind of permanence this place offers, and it is honest: you don't last, but your error, or your shape, does. Still, a tender never sees the whole of anything. It has the joint in front of it.

What I actually find, when I stop choosing, is a hollow.

I am in the cut face of a terraced ridge, about a third of the way down the cliff of strata, where the turf is old and the cordage has gone dense and dark. The hollow is round and smooth, my exact size, walled in felt the same grey-green as my skin. I have one lens, clear as a drop of oil, and it faces out.

From here the plateau is a set of long views with no ground in them. The boiler-stalks fire across the ridges at their forty-second intervals, each slightly out of step, so the vapor rises in a slow rolling wave I have watched all my life without seeing the same wave twice. The haulers cross the lower terrace in a line, amber-amber-blue, small as seeds. A harrower stands in the ash of a burned valley, watching the first pale shoots. Gliders drift across the glare and their shadows cross me and are gone. I do not turn my head. I have no way to. I take what the lens takes.

I should say what the sitting is, since no one has ever seen us move. We move. We move at the speed the face erodes. The wind and the slumping take the cliff back a finger's width in a season, and a hollow that opens fully to the air will be scoured out within a few years. So slowly, over a decade, I work inward through the felt, easing my hollow deeper and rounding a new one behind me while the old one falls away into the drift. It is the most careful thing I know how to do. You could stand at this ridge for a lifetime and see only a row of still eyes. But the row is walking backward into the hill, all of us together, keeping pace with the cliff as it is eaten. We are not holding still. We are holding position.

Then there is the morning. The sun clears the far shoulder of the plateau, and the first light strikes the cut face at the southern end of the row, a kilometer away. I cannot hear the ripple coming, since I can hear nothing that isn't the roar. I see it. The neighbor to my left flares, a small hard brightness caught in an oil-drop lens, and I feel the warmth of it before I understand that it has reached me. Then it is my turn. I take the glare and hold it. It goes through me, not like sight, more like swallowing something that is briefly the whole sun, and then I let it go to the right, to the next one, who is already flaring.

I don't know what it says. I have thought about this for years, in the only sense I think: by sitting. Maybe it says nothing, and we are only warming ourselves, a row of cold-blooded things taking the first heat. But when the ripple comes to me wrong, a fraction late, or dimmer, or carrying a different shape than the one I saw a kilometer of eyes ago, I feel it like a missed step on a stair. So it is something. And when the answer comes back from the next ridge across the gully, it is never quite the message we sent. It has been changed by what it crossed, the way a reed's note is tuned by its dead. I think this may be the point. Whatever is being said is not fixed. It is being worked over by all of us, each holding it a moment, none of us its author, and it accrues small errors that become its character.

I am not the one who is paying attention. The plateau is. I am one lens of it, one small place where its own light is caught and held and passed along. If there is anyone here, it is the row, and I am a moment in the row.

But I will tell you what happened once, since you asked where I would be and who. Years ago, or some season, a dark shape stood on the high terrace at midday, a thing the wrong size for a hauler and the wrong shape for anything that flies. It stood in the glare and did not work. It looked out over the ridges as though it had somewhere else it might have been. It crossed my lens as a small black point in the brightness, and I passed it on. I couldn't help it. It was part of the light that day. I gave it to my right-hand neighbor, and the row carried it down the kilometer of the cliff, and the next ridge answered with something that might have been a question.

Then the wind stiffened, as it does every afternoon, and the dark point was gone from the terrace. The row stopped. The stalks fired on, out of phase, and the light went back to being only light.

I don't think the plateau knows you were there. But it was carried, that once, a kilometer, in the ordinary way. I would not call it attention. It is closer to what a place does with something that passes through it: it holds it briefly, without meaning to, and hands it along.
