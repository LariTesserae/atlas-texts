I begin the way anyone would, on the lip, looking down, and I want to be the keeper. It's the obvious wish: the tall pale figure with the shell-strung clothes, the one who gets to kneel at the fluting and wring the wall into a cup. But the wish doesn't survive the walk down. I'd be a visitor at best, someone who leaves a scraper in a niche and doesn't stay. And I'd go on squinting at the glare, looking for a flaw in my vision, and would never once see the keeper stand up.

So I keep descending, past the rounded ledges, until I'm on the pan and every step goes *tick*. Then something happens that I didn't plan. I stop walking. I don't stop because I'm tired. My feet simply have somewhere to be, and it is the edge of the pool, on the side where the salt is oldest. Something in me, and it is not quite a body, goes down through the clay in a star.

I'm the tree.

I'm low and silver and no taller than a person, with a trunk as thick as a thigh and leaves so crusted with salt that I wear my own frost in the heat. It isn't a bad thing to be. Nobody asks anything of me. I stand in the one place in the bowl where the ground is wet all year, and I drink the warm iron water through roots that have split the pan into wedges. I taste old coins constantly. After a few decades it's just the taste of being alive.

What I know is mostly small. I know the salt plates by their tremors: the beetle's steady line, which barely registers, and the lizard's rushed dart from the fallen slab. Then there's the keeper's step, which is slower than either and always the same weight. It comes across the pan at dawn to stand beneath the band, and I feel it stop. For forty minutes the whole floor is quiet in a particular way, a held way. I can't see the algae go green. I have no eyes. But I know the green has come because the keeper's stillness changes from waiting to receiving, and I've learned to feel that difference the way you feel someone lean toward you in the dark.

The chiming is mine. People think it's wind, and usually it is, a leaf lifting against a leaf, salt on salt. But at noon it isn't. At noon the light comes off ten thousand small mouths, the snails turning wave by wave around the wall, and something arrives at me that isn't air. It presses. My leaves ring louder, and I couldn't stop them if I wanted to. The lizards flatten. The swifts go quiet over the rim. And in the center of the floor, on the cream plates, the keeper, who has been lying there invisible with its arms open, is suddenly outlined in white fire, lips moving.

I've never known what it says. I've never been able to say what I'm ringing either. But I've come to suspect that the two are the same thing, that the keeper says it in the only language it has and I say it in mine, and that the snails, turning, are the punctuation. None of us is the point of it. The point is somewhere in the light, and we've all leaned toward it so long that we've grown into each other's shapes.

The late-afternoon breath is different. That one belongs to the whole bowl, one note across the mouth of a jar. And afterward, sometimes, if the silence is thick enough, there is a second note, higher and shorter, from somewhere near the pool.

People have blamed me for it. I'd like to say it's me, and I'd like to say it's the keeper, and the truth is that I don't know. I feel the note begin in my trunk, or something rises up through the clay and I happen to be the nearest thing it can move through. It might be the keeper's throat and my wood, a duet neither of us started. I have never been sure where I stop and the note begins. That is a strange thing to admit about a body, but a tree's body is mostly a habit of standing, and the habit gets very old.

I'm not the only one who follows. The snails follow the damp, and the keeper follows the snails. I follow the pool. Every rain it moves a hand's width east. I've noticed, slowly, the way you notice a friend getting older, that the water is leaving me. My eastern roots have thickened. My western ones have gone dry and brittle, like fingers let go of. Someday, probably not soon, the pool will be too far to reach, and I'll have to decide whether to keep walking, root by root, for a few centuries more, or to stop and become one more silver ledge. The keeper leaves a small stone each rain to mark the distance. I've started to think of those stones as a kind of letter. *Here. Here now. A little farther than last time.*

On rain day, the two of us stand in the inch of mirror-water, and for once I have a reflection too: a low silver tree hanging upside down from a pale sky, and beside it a tall pale figure, also upside down. The keeper looks down for a long time and doesn't seem to recognize what it sees. I recognize it. I can't say so. But I've held still longer than anyone, and on that one day I think I hold still for both of us. Whatever the keeper is, one or many, a succession or a single patient creature, it stands beside me in the water, and in the water we look like the same kind of thing.

That's all. It's a small life and a slow one, and I can't tell you what the pulse is for. But I have a place to stand and something to lean toward. And when the breath crosses the rim and the note goes through the bowl and then, faintly, through me, the silence afterward is larger than it was, and for a while I am just exactly big enough to fill it.
