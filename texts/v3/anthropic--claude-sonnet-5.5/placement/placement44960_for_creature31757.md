# The Far Bank

I know I am here before I see anything, because my sternum tells me. A slab of peat lets go somewhere to my left and the *whump* arrives in my chest a heartbeat before any sound could. Then the frogs come up over it like a tide over a footprint, and the world is a struck bell with me inside.

So I begin as a body. That seems right for the Braid. A body with wide, splayed feet, still not as calloused as they should be after nine winters, and heavy on the heel. I came here late. I know because the others watch my feet the way you watch a child with a knife. I put my weight down slowly, heel then ball, and wait for the ground to say yes.

---

I am a rope-hand. I walk the lines.

Tonight it is the line that crosses the third channel below the gravel terrace, a rope of twisted sedge fiber, stretched between two stakes with felt strips gone black with wet. A river cut here two days ago. I walk toward it with my willow pole, probing, and the moss takes the tip and gives a little, the way a mattress does. Sound. I drive the pole again. Sound. Water climbs my ankle, tea-brown at the edges, clear in the middle, cold enough to feel like a decision.

At the rope I stop and close my eyes. I don't need them, and light is a poor way to read.

My fingers go along the strand until they find the first knot. A turn for the river that cut it. Three hitches for the days since. Then the tail, where a slipknot has been left open, which means *far bank untested.* Nobody has crossed to see if the ground over there is ground or is only the memory of ground with a skin of moss on it.

That is me. That is the whole of my work. A rope is a sentence stretched over a gap, and it is true for about as long as a river allows. In three weeks the sentence will be gone and the ground it described will be a different ground. I used to find that unbearable. I came from somewhere that wrote things down and kept them, a place where you could be wrong for a thousand years in a book. Now I tie the truest thing I know onto a strand of grass and let the water read over my shoulder.

---

Behind me, a small hand taps my hip twice. The girl who holds my beetle-box has caught up, breathless, her hood flaps down over her ears though she is far too young to need them. She has the horn box open a crack and is coaxing it with one finger. A slow blue pulse comes up out of the moss in her palm, then another, then they steady, and I can see her hands.

*Is it lying?* she signs. Her palms tilt back for the water that came before. Her eyebrows lift for the question. She is asking whether the channel is the kind that pretends to be shallow.

I answer with my whole chest, because that's where the sign lives. *Not lying. Just young.* Then, because she is looking at my mouth, which is moving, I close it. I have been narrating to myself out loud in my head again, in a language that nobody here can hear and nobody would want to. I think in sound in a place that made sound into weather. It is the one thing I brought with me that I never learned to put down. Sometimes I catch myself shaping words with my lips at the fire and the others watch politely, the way you watch an old man argue with his dead.

She grins and puts two fingers on my collarbone, tap-tap-tap, a rhythm I know. *You did it again.* It's a little joke, and also a kindness. It's the closest thing I have to a name.

---

Here is what I understand about being a rope-hand, which took me nine winters to be able to say to myself.

I am not a person who stands at the edge of a river. I am a person who stands at the edge of *what is known*, which moves, and I tie a knot there and say: *as of now, this.* My whole trade is the honest handling of a moment that is already over. The stake-walkers ahead of me know only what their poles tell them. The rope-hands after me know only what the last rope-hand tied. Between us the plain keeps rearranging, and each of us is a small stubborn *as of now* held up against the dark.

I think that is who I would be anywhere. Here it just has a shape you can hold.

---

I tie the new knot. Another turn for the river, and this time four hitches, because a day has gone by. And I close the slipknot up, which is the one thing I can't do yet, because I haven't crossed.

The channel is not wide. Six paces, seven. The water is the milk-grey-green of the whole plain, with a lip of foam where the current bends. On the far side the bank is a sheer face of peat and silt, and the top of it is furred with moss so thick that it looks like a solid green shore. It might be. It might be a hollow made by a vole-city and a week of seep, and one step onto it would fold me into the river like a page.

The girl watches my back. I feel her not-speaking.

I pass her the pole, and take the second one from my belt, the long jointed one, and go in.

---

The water is up to my thighs and pulls at me with a slow greed. My feet find gravel, then a shelf, then nothing. I go down on the ground the way the old ones taught me, weight low, hips back, reading the bed through my soles. Stones roll under me in a long hiss I don't hear and can feel in my teeth. Underneath that is the other thing, the deep tone rising through the water, so low that it seems to come out of my own bones. The seams are warm here. I can feel it on my shins, a faint body-heat like a sleeper's, and I think, as I always do: *it is awake, it is not for us, and it is kind.* I put my hand flat on the bank a moment before I climb.

I am halfway up when the tone changes.

It doesn't get louder. It *leans*. A rising, pulling, sideways pull in the hum, as though the whole floor of the world had drawn a breath through its teeth. The frogs feel it a second later and swell, and then swell again, and the surface of the water shivers into spray.

From the terrace behind me, a light. A long slow arc of it. The watcher's lifted arm, caught in someone's beetle-glow, sweeping down.

*Up.* That's the whole word, in every hand on the plain.

I do not stop to test the bank. I do what the bank was always going to make me do, which is trust it. I throw my weight forward and up, and the moss holds, and the peat under it holds, and it is ground. I lie there half a breath with my cheek in cold green and my heart in my throat. I have crossed. The far bank is *sound.*

I turn on my belly and reach back across the water, and the girl is already wading, box clamped in her teeth so the light shakes, and I catch her wrist and haul. Her whole weight comes up out of the river into my arms. Somewhere behind us the plain is beginning to move.

---

I carry her on my shoulders because her legs are short and I am not quick. We climb the low rise beyond the bank, past the first cairn, then the second, the felt strips flickering in the wet. Other lights are moving on the ground beside us in the dark. A hundred small blue pulses, stumbling up the same slope, children riding higher than the rest. No one speaks. No one could.

At the top I set her down and turn.

The pylon on the terrace is bright, brighter than I have seen it, pale as a bone in moonlight, and the brightness moves outward from it in a slow wave across the plain, from pylon to pylon to pylon, until the whole dark floor is drawn with a scatter of cold lines. The hum has climbed to a place beyond hearing. Everything shivers with it. The frogs are one roaring note. Far out in the east I can see the tower on its hummock, lit along its scoured upper stories, and I think of the old woman inside, the nets moving in a wind nobody else can feel, and I hope she is not afraid, and then I remember that she doesn't have fear about it. That isn't the kind of thing she has.

The water comes down the dry channel without a sound I can hear, a grey-brown wall with a lip of foam, and in a moment it is the channel I just crossed. Then it is a wider channel, and then it is a lake that was not there. It takes my rope. I see the strand go, a pale thread on the dark water, my four hitches and my turn, and the knot I did not finish closing.

Something in me makes a small sound. Nobody hears it, and I'm glad.

---

The girl finds my hand. She presses her mouth to the bone behind my ear, and speaks in that low, flat, buzzing register that goes through the skull like a thread through cloth. It sounds like nothing. I hear it perfectly.

"You said *not lying*," she says. "It wasn't."

"No," I say, in the same buzz, into her hair. "It was just young."

We stand there, all of us, in the roar, holding our small lights against the wet and waiting for the world to finish being rearranged. After a while the tone falls away to a lower drone. The waves of light draw back to their pylons. The frogs go on, because the frogs always go on.

In the morning, if there is a morning, I will take the long pole and the coil of sedge and go down to see what the plain has made. There will be a new ridge and a new lake and a river in an unfamiliar bed. I'll find the place where the ground holds and drive a stake, and tie the first knot of a new sentence, and leave the slipknot open at the end, because I won't have crossed yet.

That is the one true thing I know how to say in this place. *As of now, this. The far bank untested.* And then I go and test it.
