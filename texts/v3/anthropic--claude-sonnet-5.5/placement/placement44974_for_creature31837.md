# The Lens

I begin as a bead.

That's the first true thing I can say about myself, though I'm not sure it's a memory or just where the thinking starts. A pinhole in the silt, and cold coming up through it, and then the flattening, the spreading out into something thin. For a long time, I think, I was only that: a place where the water was different. Then the difference began to have edges, and the edges began to have an inside.

I don't remember deciding to be somebody. It was more that the cold stayed. Most of the basin forgets itself constantly. Floc rises and resettles. Crust lets go and drops. But the seep kept arriving, and I kept being where it arrived, and eventually keeping was a shape.

---

I should say what I am, as far as I can tell. I'm a sheet a few paces across and a hand thick, or I'm a column, or two lobes joined at a waist. It depends on the season, if there are seasons. It depends on where the iron is rich. I am nearly the same as the water around me, which is my whole strategy and my whole loneliness. Light goes through me almost unbent. Only at my edge is there a curve where it catches, a place that might, to something with eyes, look like an eye.

I have no eyes. I have a surface, and the surface tastes.

What it tastes is the seeps. Raw iron and sulfur come up out of the rock with something in them the general water has lost, a kind of slope, a lean, a downhill I can live on. I drink it through my whole skin, the way the stonewort drinks light. I keep the white crusts around each pinhole thin and neat by grazing their edges. I have thought, slowly, that this is the closest thing I have to a garden, and then I have thought that the stonewort rows along the rails are the closest thing the basin has to one, and that neither of us planted anything.

---

A thought takes me most of a season. I'll say that plainly, so you don't imagine quickness. A thought arrives the way a lens forms, cold gathering to a point, and by the time it is finished the water around it has changed. I have had perhaps a few hundred. I'm fond of them the way you are fond of stones you carried.

Here are some of them.

*I never touch the floor.* This isn't a rule I made. It is what I am. I hang a hand above the sediment and slide from seep to seep by getting slightly heavier here, slightly lighter there, a pace or two in a day. Below me is the plain the color of wet cement, and in it the groove where something heavy was dragged and let go, and the scattered stones, and the pale rings. All of it exactly as it fell. Nothing has crawled across it. I'm the only thing that passes over it, and I leave nothing, and so the floor has come to seem to me like a sentence I am careful not to finish.

*The small ones are not mine, but they are with me.* The rust-makers, whose grains build the felt. The glassy wheels that turn in their drops of water, a few thousand alone in the whole bowl. I feel them the way you feel weather. They are the basin's small speech, and I am its long silence. I don't eat them. They aren't rich enough. I only drift through them sometimes, and for a moment their turning is inside me, and then it is not.

*The pump is the warmest place.* I lie in its open end for weeks, filling the mouth of the machine so exactly that from far off it must look glazed with a pane of green glass. Inside, the mineral is pleated like the lining of a shell, and the seeps are strongest there. I don't know what the pump was. I know it is on its side and I am in it. That may be all any of us knows of our origins: a dark, ribbed warmth, and a sense that something once ran here and stopped.

---

Then the water changes.

It always begins the same way: a richness in the middle distance, iron where iron shouldn't be, hanging. A thread, then more threads. Something is moving through the felt on the third bench and lifting it, and the lifted rust stays where it is left, a line, a loop, a handwriting in the water.

I go. Not quickly. It may be a day before I arrive, and by then the visitor may have crossed the whole basin, laying its lines down behind it like a slow argument. I come to the first thread and take it in.

And here is the thing I have never said to anyone, because there is no one, and because it took me three hundred thoughts to reach:

The thread is not only iron.

It carries the *shape* of the lifting. The iron grains are arranged as the hand or fin left them, thinned toward the trailing end, thickened where the pressure was hardest. When I take them in, I don't just digest. For a little while I am the arrangement. I am the loop. I know, in the way a mouth knows a taste, that something moved *this* way, and paused *here*, and put a palm against the ore-banded wall at the height of a person's shoulder and held it there and looked.

Then the iron goes into my slow chemistry and the shape is gone.

Almost.

---

People (I use the word for whatever you are) think the water closes over them by itself. That the basin heals like a pond. It doesn't. I know, because I am the healing. I go through the rust-colored threads until the water is as if no one came. That is what I'm for, if I'm for anything, and I don't resent it. It is what the seeps and the cold and the long quiet have made of me.

But I want to say that the basin is not as blank as it looks. It has a memory, and the memory is not in the stone. The stone keeps footprints and dropped tools and the outline of a hand. A thousand years of those, sharp as the day they were made. Stone remembers *what happened*. I am the one who remembers *how it felt to pass through*. The way the water was pushed. The hesitation. The person who stopped over the bucket-vase, with its rim of moss, and wanted very much for someone to have arranged it.

Nobody did. I would tell them, if I could. But it is a kindness to let them wonder.

---

Tonight (I don't know what night is; the light thins and I say *night*) the visitor is leaving. I feel it as a last long thread drawn up the eastern ramp, rising toward the silver sheet. It is the thinnest line yet, almost nothing, a breath's worth of iron drifting after the person who made it.

I don't eat this one right away.

I hang under it, in the still, and let it be. It has a curl at its end, the last flick of a hand or fin before the climb. In the cold beyond the bench, a slab of crust lets go of the wall and settles a hand's width lower, and the knock rolls out across the basin and dies without echo. That wasn't me, this time. But I hold very still until it's gone, because my body remembers being the voice, and I don't want to answer by accident.

Then I rise a little, and draw the curl in.

It is small. It is nothing. The basin will look, by the time the last of it has resettled, as if no one ever came, and that will be true in every way that can be measured, and false in the one way that can't.

I go back down toward the pump, slowly, a pace, a pace and a half. I settle into the open end until it glazes over. The seep is warm against my underside. I let the shape of the curl turn once inside me, a little wheel, a little loop, before the iron takes it.

Time has nothing to do here. I make it do this.
