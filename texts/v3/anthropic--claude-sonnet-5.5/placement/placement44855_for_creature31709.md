I start by being still, which is the first thing anyone here learns. If you had come into my clearing at midday you would have taken me for a stump wearing too much fern. I don't mind. Being mistaken for a stump is a kind of politeness the forest extends to those who are paying attention.

My name is the soft word, mostly breath. I am three risings old, which is young enough that my cloak is still badly behaved. I have been coaxing a fern into the hood for two seasons, and it grows sideways, toward the left ear, as if it had heard something there. The old ones say to pinch it back and wait. I pinch and wait. In the meantime I am lopsided, and people know me across a basin by the lean of my green.

I should say where I am, because it matters. I am not at the center of anything. My room is in the farthest basin on the eastern shoulder, and I inherited it half-grown from an aunt who had inherited it from someone whose name has worn off. It is four trunks and a great deal of patience. I add a finger's width each dawn: a twist of twine, a nudge, a vine persuaded to reconsider. My aunt's finger-widths are in there too, and I can feel them when I run my palm along the arch. They are slightly cooler than mine. I know this is my imagination, and I think it anyway.

I sleep low, in a hammock over the mat, with four others. In a high-water year I wake already lifted, the whole room rising on the dark water like a held breath, and no one says anything. We hum in our chests, *I am here and content*, and the hum goes around the hammocks until it isn't a sound anymore, only warmth.

---

Here is what I actually do, though it does not feel like a job.

I stand at the edge of the clearing at dawn with my face lifted and I wait for the note.

The birds with the pouched throats live nearer the middle of the plateau. I have never seen one. What reaches me is the answer to the answer to the answer, a long relay of set-down throats, each basin handing the sound to the next. By the time it arrives at my clearing it has passed through so many people that it is less a bird's note than a memory of one, round and low and slightly worn, like a stone that has been carried in too many bellies. I give it back. I blow across the bottle of my own throat, and the sound goes out over the moss toward the next basin, where someone I have never met is waiting with their face lifted.

The old ones say that this is how the mountain counts itself. I think that is true. But what I feel, standing there in the wet blue before sunrise, is something smaller and stranger. I feel that I am a place where the sound *changes hands*. Whatever begins the note is far from me, and whatever it finally means is farther. I am one of the places where it is real for a moment. If I do not answer, someone will come walking across the mat to find me. Being the kind of thing that would be missed seems to me a large enough life.

Sometimes, in the long hour before the sound returns from the far edge, I wonder whether anyone is at the very end of it. Whether there is a last basin, a last throat, where the note is set down and not passed on. Then I think that the plateau has no end in that sense. It is a ring, and the note goes round it, and I am always in the middle of the sentence.

---

At dusk I go out and press my hand to the moss cushions and watch them go dark under my palm. Then I wait, and they come back. That took me a long time to love. A hand passes over, the light goes out, and an hour later the light returns as though nothing had happened. But it is not the same light. I have watched closely. Each return is a little different, a little slower or a little brighter, depending on the rain, or the age of the moss, or what it was doing before I touched it. I can tell them apart. There is one cushion by the eastern trunk whose glow takes exactly as long as a slow breath held and let go. I visit it more than I need to. I haven't told anyone.

Sometimes I think I am more like the cushion than like the person who touches it. I go dim when I am handled and I come back. I am not quite what I was, and I am not quite different, and the difference is small enough to be a kind of faithfulness.

---

This is a low year. The mat is cracking, and the seams open and breathe their cold iron smell up through the floor. Last night the elders sent word across the basins: the steps are showing.

I walked to them today, a half-day through the tilted light, my feet finding the stiffest ground. There were perhaps forty of us. We did not speak. The pale grazers were already there, six of them, standing at the top in the rain with their heads lowered, looking down.

Three steps had come up out of the water, dark and shining and worn hollow in the middle. I looked at the hollows for a long time. Someone's feet did that, over more years than I can count, going down and coming up, going down and coming up, until the stone gave way and took the shape of them. And then they went down and did not come up. Or they came up somewhere else. Or the water closed and they were glad of it. No one knows, and I found, standing there, that I did not need to.

I was given the third step. I went down to it one stone stair at a time, my broad toes gripping the wet, and I stood where the hollow was deepest, and my feet fit it badly, as if I were wearing someone else's shoes. I had brought a braid of my own hair, and a knot of the green thread from the sideways fern. I set them down. The braid was still faintly warm.

I don't know who I was leaving them for. Not the makers, exactly. It felt more like adding a finger's width to a room I would never see finished. Then I climbed back up, and no one said anything, and the rain went on ticking on the second tier like a thousand clocks slightly out of sync.

---

Tonight the water is low and the cloud has come down into the forest. My cloak is beaded with it. The trunks are breathing their faint milk-blue, and I am sitting at the edge of my own clearing, waiting for nothing in particular.

Someone is coming across the mat. I know the gait by now, the heron thinking about something else, and the green of her, and the orchid behind one ear. She sits beside me. She holds out her damp, cool hand, palm up, and I look at it for a while, and then I put in it the only thing I have, which is the fern I have been pinching back for two seasons, pulled gently from the hood, its roots still dark with my own warmth.

She presses it to the inside of her wrist for a long time.

Then she says the word, very softly, and I still cannot tell whether she means the fern, or me, or the step, or the note that will come at dawn from three basins away and pass through my throat on its way to someone I will never meet. I decide it doesn't matter. I hum in my chest, *I am here and content*, and the sound goes out into the wet dark, and somewhere a moss cushion, touched an hour ago, begins to glow again.
