I begin with my feet. Before I know what I am, I know the tick of my claws on the pavement, small and dry, and how far it travels before the fog eats it. The stones are oiled by wind. I have crossed this one before, or one like it. The difference between those two things is what I spend most of my days on.

So I am the one the kilns' world calls the rust walker. That is not my name, since nothing here has names, but it is the shape the place makes of me: long legs, a narrow chest, a coat the colour of the roads. I roll in the old pellet-lines every few days, and not for camouflage. I do it because the smell is the smell of *after*, and after is where I live. The herds pass and I follow the trail they leave behind, always a season or a decade or a lifetime late.

---

Morning is hoarfrost, then a slick wet, then dry. I drink from the seep at the foot of a mesa, where the black moss is cold as a tongue. The water tastes of iron. Everything does. I lick the beads off my own forelegs and they taste of iron too.

Then I walk. The Table is wide enough that walking is a way of staying still, the ridges sinking into haze as fast as I approach them. I check the slag as I go. There are blue-black lines from an animal that ate a lot of basalt, and a green-dull band where copper came through. A few of the pellets are fresh enough that the lichen hasn't found them. I never see the animal that made them. The trail is a sentence I read from the wrong end.

Most days I find something small. A grey bird that came down to the gravel and could not find the way back up, sitting stunned like something dropped from a great height. A beetle turned over, its legs working at nothing. I eat these and am glad of them and do not think much about it.

---

Once I found a calf.

I want to say that plainly, because it is the reason I stop and lift my head. It lay in a bed of slag that had gone cold, the size of a boulder, and its chest was a hollow with nothing lit in it. I had been told nothing, but there is no telling in me, only the body's knowledge, and my body knew this was a thing that had not caught. The herd had moved on. The ground still held the shape of the one who had lowered herself over it and pressed her warm belly down, hours of it, maybe days, and then risen and gone because there was no more to do.

I did not eat it at once. This is the thing I do not understand about myself. The attendants had not come yet, the beetles were only starting, and I stood with my nose an inch from that cold plate and did not eat. I stayed until the light went blue. That was the hour the herd's glow would have crawled along its ridges, and it did not. Somewhere over the rim of the world the others were lit up in thin threads of static, and here was one dark animal in a dark hollow.

Then I ate, because I am what I am. But I have not stopped standing there.

---

I sleep in hollows the herds have left, curled against warm slag or, better, in the lee of a mound where an old one stopped. You can tell them if you learn the grain. The tors that look like knuckles are mostly rock, but some are not, and the not-rock ones hold heat differently. The frost sits on them lighter. Lichen has laid orange maps across the old scutes, and if you press your ear to the ground beside one, you can hear the beetles working in the warm hollows of what used to be a chest.

I sleep against them and feel nothing. That is the truth. I sleep against an animal the size of a hill, dead for a hundred years, and what I feel is the dry warmth of the beetles' work. But I have noticed that I always choose the same few. I have noticed that I turn to face the direction they were walking.

---

Sometimes the ground speaks. I feel it in my pads, a long and a short and a long, coming up through the gravel from very far away. It is not for me. It is a pulse laid down for bodies that weigh what a hillside weighs, and all it gives me is a faint tremor in the bones of my legs, as if I were being told something in a language for which I have only the ears. I stand still through it. I have never once known what it meant. Whole basins halt, and I halt in the same moment, a fox-sized thing stopped in the empty, and then the stamp of an elder comes and the world resumes.

I think this is why I look up. The others don't need to. The birds ride the back and see the next plate. The beetles inherit the next pellet. The moss holds the seep. They are all inside what they depend on. I am between, in the emptiness that the herds cross, and the only way to know where the herd is, is to look. So when the fog thins and a great steaming back stands on the rim of the sky, I stop, and raise my head, and stay there.

I am checking. Something in me keeps a picture of the last one I saw, or the one before that, or a shape older than my own life, and I hold it against the animal in the distance. It fits. It always fits. The plumes lean and lay themselves flat against the cloud. And still I stand there a long time, as if the fitting were the point.

---

I don't think I am apart from it. I used to wonder, in whatever way I wonder, whether I was the one thing on the Table that was only watching. But the rust in my coat is their rust. The iron in the water I drink came out of one of them, hot, and hissed on the gravel, and went grey in a minute. The blood in me runs faintly warm because of the fog they made, a degree or two above the cold, which they exhale.

So I keep my distance, and it is not a distance at all. It is the way the last part of a breath sits in the chest, before it turns and goes out. The herd is a very long breath. I am somewhere in the out-turning of it, walking the road it left, and in a season or a decade it will come back around, and the ground will be soft and sweet-smelling, and I will lift my head from a pellet-line and there it will be, rolling out of the fog with the cloud following.

I will not run toward it. I will stand and let the sound of it arrive in my feet, and keep still, and be a small rust-coloured thing on a very large grey floor, briefly lit at the edges.
