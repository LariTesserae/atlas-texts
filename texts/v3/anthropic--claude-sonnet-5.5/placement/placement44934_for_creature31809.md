I am at the fourth joint, the clogged one, and I am late.

This is how I know who I am: by where my hands are. Before I know my plates or my name, which is a drip on wet iron, two beats and a rest, I know my fingertips are pressed to a seam in the riveted main, reading it. The iron here is thickening. It has not merely gone wet. It has a grain like the inside of a loaf, and behind it the clog sits like a held breath, a fist of root and ochre and old peat that the water has been packing in for a hundred and fifty years. My work is to take away just enough of it.

*Just enough* is the whole of my life. I am not old. My husk hangs on the stalactite three piers down, still the color of clay, still holding the shape of my back where I split. I climbed out two fills ago, or three. I don't count in fills. The fills count me.

So: the Holding. I lean into the pipe with the water rising in the trenches around the piers, and the mat under my feet lifts and tilts and sighs. My plates have closed. The hair on my head, the red crown and the pale green tips, has stopped drinking, and I let it, because a wet head makes sound and sound is the one thing I am not allowed. Upslope the drips are going quiet, one by one, as the others press their palms of sphagnum into the weeping shale. The silence comes across the hollow like a hand smoothing cloth.

In this silence I am supposed to listen with my whole body, through the mat and the clay and the pipe's iron under my palm, for the gulp. The oldest one, the one at the engine-house sill whose plates have gone violet, will lift her hand a heartbeat before it comes. I have watched her do it and I have never understood how. It isn't that she hears something I don't. I think she hears the same thing and has made peace with it.

What I hear is this. Deep in the fourth joint, behind the fist, there is a thin high whistle, air looking for a way through. If I have taken too little of the clog, the air will not find it in time, and the siphon will not break. The level will keep rising, past the terraces, past the mats, past the sills, and the whole hollow will drown in its own inhaling. If I have taken too much, the air will come early and the siphon will break before it has properly drained, and the mats will sit stranded on the clay, and the terraces will hold their water like unfinished sentences.

I have never seen either happen. Rule 2 says no one has. I lean on Rule 2 the way I lean on the pipe.

But I think about it. In the long quiet I think: *what if it is only me?* What if the pipe would do all of this alone, cough and gulp and cough, and the forty of us are the fog's idea of company? The old story says we keep the engine's dream going like a fire. It doesn't say what the fire does if you take your hands away. I have asked, in taps, along the main. The pipe answers with the tick of cooling iron, which is what iron always says.

I don't mind not knowing. What I mind is the moment before, when I am the only one who could be wrong.

The heron has come to the wagon. I feel it through the mat, the small shift of a body finding its stillness. Two of the others have gone to stand with it, and the light is going the color of pewter turning to slate. Over the tilted willow the fog is beginning to lean, not toward the sound yet, only toward the idea of sound. My hair is dry. My hands are warm. The whistle in the joint thins to a thread.

Then, at the sill of the engine house, a hand lifts.

I don't hear the pipe. I feel her hand. It comes to me through the mat as a change in the weight of the quiet, and my fingers, which have been reading the joint all this time, close very gently on the edge of the clog and take. Not the fist. Just the rind of it, a curl of orange scale no bigger than a leaf, the *just enough* I have been carrying all my short life like a stone in the mouth.

The pipe gulps.

It starts in the far cracked joints, a long throaty pull, and the iron goes alive under my palms. The fill lets go all at once. The mat drops, and the whole hollow starts talking in its low wet murmur: the channels quickening and running brown, the terraces emptying tier by tier like cups tipped one into the next, the sluices taking the water through their dark glassy lips. I am riding the mat down. I have an alder pole in my hands that I don't remember picking up. My plates open, flaking a little orange into the foam, and my hair takes the first drip of the cascade and drinks, and drinks, and goes dark and heavy and glad.

For the whole drain I am busy. A bank slumps, and I lean on it. A drift of root has come to the second gate, and I kneel, and clear it, and the water goes through clean. Along the pipe I keep the burst joints packed with paste, with my thumbs, in the rhythm the old ones taught me without teaching. And all the while, under the work, my fingertips are asking the iron the same question, and the iron answers as it always answers.

Toward the end of the drain I go back to the fourth joint. I don't have to. I lay my palm on it. The whistle is there, thin and clear, air finding its way through the space I left, and I feel the coming cough gathering in the pipe like a word before it is said. I feel it, I think, a heartbeat early. Only a heartbeat, and perhaps I am wrong, and perhaps this is only how it feels to be part of something that has always known what it will do.

The siphon breaks with its rattling cough. The level begins to climb.

I settle onto the mat. My plates close, my hair goes still, and out on the wagon the heron has not moved.

If you are watching from the rim, you will see none of this. You will see the fog lean, a mat that holds against the current, and a curve of orange crust that for a moment is a shoulder, a lean, a hand laid flat on a pipe. Then it is a terrace again. I am glad of that. It is the best thing I have learned: that the work is real exactly to the degree that nobody sees it, and that the hollow has been kind enough to let me be sure of it only for the length of one breath.

Then the Holding takes me back. Somewhere a drip falls on iron, two beats and a rest, and I know it is mine.
