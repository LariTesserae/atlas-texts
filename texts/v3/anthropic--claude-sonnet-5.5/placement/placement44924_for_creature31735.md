I would find myself on the pipe, and I would be a fern.

I don't start as one. I start as a spore, which is hardly anyone: a speck of dust with a plan folded inside it, lifted off the underside of a frond on the tree-fern floor and thrown into the steam wind. Most spores land on ground that is warm this year and cold next year. I land on a flange bolt.

There's a skin of moss on the bolt and a film of rusty water on the moss, and just below the seam the iron is warm as a hand. That's enough. I split and put out a thread, then a green scale one cell thick, a small heart of a thing pressed flat against the metal. This is the stage nobody remembers. I have no stem and no leaves. I'm barely a body at all, more a wet place that happens to be green.

Here the pipe sings to me. The seam whistles, and the whistle runs down the iron into the moss and shivers the water film I lie in. I don't hear it, but the surface trembles in one pitch that never changes, while everything else in the cleft is overlapping and ribbed. It's the only steady note in the place. Nothing else here is alive to make a sound, so the water carries the whole conversation. In a world with no wings or legs, it's the only courier. When the time comes, my small swimmers cross the film in the drip, shaken along by the flute, and find the egg. Nobody introduces us. The drip does.

Then there is a crozier. I uncoil from the heart-shaped scale, pale green and fisted like something asking a question. This is the first time I have a top and a bottom. The bottom goes into the bolt's crevice and finds the moss, and the moss finds iron. Rust comes up in my sap. I'm made partly of the engineers' failure, and I don't mind. It's just another mineral seep, and this one happens to have been bored and bolted by hands.

I learn the cleft by its pulses, and the geyser is the clock. It gulps, the rock shudders, the pipe rings faintly against its mound, and then the grit comes down hot on my fronds. A few of them scorch and I let them. Every eleven or twelve minutes the world tells me it's still happening, and I answer by growing a little more. I have no other way of counting. The water in me counts it.

The pipe isn't the only thing that leans here. The moss leans on the pipe, I lean on the moss, and the lichen ring on the flange face leans outward at the speed of a finger's width per year. It's the slowest clock I know, and when I think about it, it comforts me. Below us the silver columns drip in their private rain. Farther down the tree ferns shake in their laundry-warm dark, and I feel sorry for the outer trunks that are already browning, though I couldn't say why, since I'm bound to the same wager. I have bet everything on a seam of iron staying warm. It's a smaller bet than theirs, and it may lose faster.

I do know what I'm standing on. The pipe is the only straight line in the cleft, and the moss is combing it back into the shapes the mountain likes. Each year the flange softens, each year the ferns on the bolts are a little more numerous, and one day the pipe will be a green lump with a hole in it, and then only a lump. I'm helping. Every root I put down is a small vote for the mountain. I don't feel disloyal. The engineers wanted the heat and failed to get it, and I want the heat and have it, one drip of warmth at a time from a crack in a rusted seam. That's the whole difference between us. I stayed.

If the seam seals, I'll know first, because the whistle will thin and the film will stop trembling, and the warm side of the bolt will cool toward the temperature of the rest of the ridge. My fronds will brown from the tips in. I'm not afraid of it, or not in any way that a plant would call fear. But I have learned what to do before that day. On the undersides of my fronds the sori are ripening, rust-brown dots the color of the pipe, each one packed with the next generation and their plans. I hold them until the wind is right. Then the steam takes them.

That's what I'd be, in the end: not the fern on the bolt but the dust that leaves it. Some of me will fall on cold rock and be nothing. Some will land on a new vent ringed with yellow crystals like sugar, in a place I'll never stand, where the ground is just beginning to hum and the moss has already made the first inch of soil. There I'll be a green heart pressed to the stone, lying in a trembling film of water, hearing a note I've never heard.
