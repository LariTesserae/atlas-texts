I begin as a worker, because that is the easiest thing to be.

I'm in a covered gallery with a pellet of wet carton in my jaws, and the wall hums against my back, and ten thousand others are going the same way in the same dark. It is a relief. Nobody asks what I am. There is only the smell-road and the weight in my mouth, and somewhere ahead a wound in a wall that needs plastering. I go toward it. I put down my pellet and press it smooth. Somewhere in this, the tower is mending itself, and I am a small part of the mending.

But I can't stay. The fit is too good. When I'm a worker, nobody is home, and the tower does its thinking in a register I only pass through. The scar we make will outlast the hundred years of us that made it, and none of us will read it. I climb out through a vent into the air and find that I have kept something. I have a small unease, a habit of watching that the workers don't have.

So I go up, along the resin-slick bark, past the wasp paper and the amber funnels, into the felt. Everything up here is softer and stranger. The silk gives under me. Ferns root in it. I am not a weaver-bird. I have no yellow, no knots to tie, no one to sing at from the doorway of an unfinished basket. And I am not one of the roof-weavers, though I am nearly their size and wear nearly their brown. I am one of the tenants. I am the little thief at the edge.

This is what I am, and it took me a long time to say it plainly. I live on the margin of a meal that isn't mine. When a katydid hits the sheeting, the shudder goes out through the threads and the colony converges, hundreds of them, shoulder to shoulder on the body, biting together and feeding together. I do not go in. I wait on a guy-thread at the periphery, holding so still that I become a mote, a husk, a fleck of leaf. When they finish and loosen, there are always scraps: a leg, a smear of fluid, a wing-base with something left in it. I come in low and take what they've left. Sometimes I take what they haven't quite left. They do not notice, or they notice and do not mind. I have never been able to tell which, and I have stopped needing to.

My whole craft is in how I walk. The web is a nervous system, and every footstep is a sentence in it. A struggling moth says *food*. A tearing thread says *repair*. A stranger stepping heavily says *what is that*. I have to say nothing at all. So I have learned to move on the drips. After rain, when the valley is letting go of its water and every nest-spout is ticking, there's a rhythm in the silk, irregular and constant, and I walk inside it. One step lands where a bead of water would land. The next lands a little later, where another would. To the colony I am weather. I am just more of the wet.

I think this is what I am good for, if I'm good for anything: listening without being heard. I know the roof better than most who own it. I know which corners of the silk are thick with old leaf-mold and which are new and taut. I know that the weavers' alarm for snakes has a stutter in it that their hawk alarm lacks, and that the stutter arrives in the silk a half-second before it arrives in the air, because the birds shake their nests when they scream. I know that the frogs, at dusk, don't start at once. There's one small orange note, thin as struck glass, that begins alone from a leaf-tip, and only when it has held for a count of eleven do the others come in under it, like a floor being laid. I have never told anyone this. There is no one to tell. Still, I have the knowledge, and it is a kind of company.

Tonight the roar is coming up. I feel it first as a thickening in the threads, the thrum of the earth arriving through the trunks, then the frogs, then the cicadas rising in their shrill sheets. I keep still at the edge of my strand. My body, which is nothing, is full of the sound. It goes through me like water through a sponge, and I let it, because I've learned there is no other way to be here.

Then the hush.

It begins far off, on the valley floor, and I'd not have known its cause except that the silk goes strange in a way I've felt before: a slackening, thousands of small bodies dropping their attention at once. The weavers stop mid-rattle. The frogs cut out in a ripple that travels outward like a ring in a pond. Below me, ten meters down through the dark, something is moving that has weight and patience and a warm, bloody smell. The leopard-cat steps between the towers, careful, careful, and the whole valley goes silent around its feet. In the silence I can hear the drips. I can hear my own legs, the faint ticking of them on the thread. I have never been so loud. For a few seconds I am a sentence spoken in an empty room.

I do not move. I could say I am afraid, but the truth is stranger. I am attending. The silence is not empty; it is the valley holding its breath, and I am inside the breath. Every being here is doing what I do, listening without being heard. For a moment I understand that the thing I thought was my small furtive talent is the valley's whole art. We are all tenants. Even the towers are tenants in the old work of their dead. Even the roof is borrowing its light.

The cat passes. A weaver ventures one dry chatter, and another answers, and then the frogs begin again, that one small orange note first, alone, and I count without meaning to. At eleven, the floor is laid, and the roar closes over the place where the silence was, as if nothing had interrupted.

I take a step, on the drip.

Someday a tree will fall and the shaft of daylight will come down, and I will be somewhere near it, on a thread at the edge, watching the spiders knit it shut. I will not see it finished. The roof will thicken by a hand's breadth, and I'll be a husk in the felt, a fleck in the soil the ferns are rooted in. A beetle whose grandparents never touched the ground will eat what's left of me. That seems right. I have lived on the leavings of a larger life, and I would like to be leavings, in turn, for something that lives on mine.

But that is later. Now it is dark, and the sound is a substance, and the valley has noticed me the way it notices everything, without alarm, registering my small weight and my unfamiliar smell. It makes room. I go on.
