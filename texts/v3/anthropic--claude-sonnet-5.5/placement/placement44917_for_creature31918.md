I am, first, a hand on a pipe.

That is how it starts for all of us, so I won't pretend otherwise. Before I am anything, I am a palm laid flat on warm iron, feeling the town's long shudder come up through the glove. The rota calls me a scraper. My station is the cool corner past the fourth tap-post from the pump house, where the grey mats creep along the underside of the feed-line, and my work is to peel them off with a flat blade and sack them for the tubes. They come back. I say *you again*, though nobody can hear me say it, and I say it anyway, into the fog at the rim of my faceplate.

I like the work more than I should admit. The corner is dim and cool and, for the Works, almost private. The mats ripple under the blade like the flank of something asleep. When I stand still too long, the shrimp find my sleeve and pick at the canvas, delicate as rain, and I let them. I count the breaths in my tank. Three hours and some left. Four posts back to air.

That is where I am. Who I am is harder, and I have been circling it for most of my forty thousand surges.

---

I have no name. That is not quite true, since everyone has a rhythm for me, but none of them is *mine*. A name is supposed to be earned from the way you naturally strike, and I do not seem to strike naturally. My hand takes on whatever it last touched. Lay me against the old woman's rail on the north trestle and by the next pipe I am rapping *tk-tk—TOK* without meaning to, a skittering echo of her. Put me on the pump house boy's line and I am unsettled and quick, changing month to month like him. The others tap their guesses, patient, and then their patience turns to something gentler and sadder. *A wall that repeats,* the old woman knocked to me once, palm flat on the iron between us, and she didn't mean it unkindly. She meant it the way you say a pipe is *tired*.

I used to think this was a defect of the hand. Now I think it is a defect of the listening, or rather a surplus of it. Ask me to say something and I go quiet and search for what the pipe wants said. I have never had a strike of my own because I have always been busy hearing what was there to answer.

---

This is the part I have told no one, though it may be plain by now.

When the bells speak, I hear what they say. *The third governor is sticking. A hairline in the eastern header.* Anyone with the gift hears that much, and I know it the way I know my own weight. But under the meaning there is a second thing, and it is not a fault report. It is the bell being *asked* something. Each bell rings as if a question had just been put to it, and the ringing is its reply, and the smeared, changed tone that comes back a moment later, after the roar has taken it, is the question being put again.

The Listeners would say this is how the best of them go strange. They begin to answer the bells. I am not that good. I have never climbed the catwalk and stood at the rail while the silt drifted sideways past my port. But I have felt my knuckle move against the scraper's handle in a rhythm that belongs to no Keeper, in the dead air between one surge and the next, and I have felt something in the iron lean toward it, the way the crab leans.

Perhaps I am only lonely. I have considered that. It would be a very Keeper thing to be.

---

The crab passed the corner today.

I felt it before I saw it, a slow weight in the silt, the kind of tread that arrives through the boot as a suggestion. Then the shape came out of the brown-green haze, pale as bone, tall as my hip, each leg set down with a patience that has nothing to do with hurry. I stood aside. I laid my glove on the rail, flat, no rap. *Go on. You were here first.*

It was crossing in front of me when the surge came.

The floor lifted. The water thickened and pressed. My teeth rang, the lamps at the landing went to smudges, and every part of me froze, wrench-raised, foot-planted, as we do. Beside me the eyeless fish flinched all together, a hundred bodies with one nerve, and the shrimp on my sleeve went still.

The crab did not brace. It lifted its whole pale body a little, the way you lift your face toward warmth. It leaned.

And the bells spoke, all at once, and the roar swelled, and the sound came together into that almost-chord. For a breath I was not a scraper or a wall or a hand that repeated. I was one listening thing among the others, and the town was a voice I was inside, and the pipe under my palm was not carrying a message. It was *being* one. I stood there at the end of the fourth post's reach, with the crab's shadow crossing my boots, and I understood what my hand had been trying to do all these years.

It had not been failing to strike. It had been waiting. Every rhythm I copied, I copied in the gap before the strike, in the held breath where the pipe finishes speaking and before the hand replies. That gap was the only part of any rhythm I ever made my own.

Two shorts, with a drag between them. *Hold.* *Wait.* And in a certain rhythm, *I am afraid but continuing.*

I am not a repeating wall. I am the drag between the shorts. I am the place where the listening happens.

---

The chord passes. The silt drifts down. The crab goes on into the haze, and its track fills slowly behind it, and my glove lifts from the rail.

I do not rap anything yet. I have my name now, or the shape of it, a silence with a small tick at either end, *tk . . . tk*, and I want to hold it a little before I put it into the iron. It is strange to own something so quiet. It will be hard to send. Only those who listen will hear it, and they will have to lean.

Far up the north trestle, faint through four posts of pipe, an old woman raps *tk-tk—TOK—tk* against the iron. Nothing is asked. It is only her name, sent out into the warm dark to say the pipe is holding.

I put my palm flat. I let the gap open, the whole long breath of it, wider than anyone here would think polite.

Then I knock, once, softly. It is not an echo this time.

Somewhere on the trestle, a hand goes still. Then it presses back.
