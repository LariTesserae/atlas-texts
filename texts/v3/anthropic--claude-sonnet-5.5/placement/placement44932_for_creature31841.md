I start out wanting to be something with a better view.

A flamingo, maybe, one of the pink drift a kilometer long, head down, walking a slow circle in my own stirred mud. But flamingos come and go with the richness of the brine, and I don't want to be something that leaves when the soup thins. A mudskipper, then, propped at the water's edge with my eyes above the skin of the mud, offended and patient. I like that one too much. It flatters me. I'd be the philosopher of the shoreline, and the shoreline has no use for philosophers. It has crabs, ten thousand of them, waving one claw each in the same direction for no one.

So I go lower. I picture the cone snails, grey seed in the wet seams of the concrete. I drift a few centimeters at low tide. I close my door at the first dry hour. Someone walks the causeway and I click under their boot, and it's fine, it's what the ground is for. But a snail has no *where*. It has a wall, and the wall has no name.

What I keep coming back to is the window.

Not the whole compound of tin-roofed sheds by the main sluice, just one window on the causeway side, with a sill crusted white and a cup on it that has left a ring. I'm one of the crew. I'm not the one who works the gates by the tide table or the one who knows the diesels by their stroke. Those are real jobs with real sounds. I'm the one whose job, on paper, is the ledger. Once a season a boat comes in with fuel and flour and parts. I count what came off it and write the numbers in columns, and at the bottom I sign. I don't know who reads it. I've stopped needing to.

The rest of the time I'm at the window, and what I do there is notice. That is the actual work, though no one wrote it down.

I notice that the rain came at four-ten today, not four. I notice that the heron on the third dike has stood in the same posture since I finished my tea, and that the stilts have shifted a hundred meters east, so the brine must be turning. I notice a figure far out on the pan, upright and small among the waders. For a full minute I can't tell whether it's Odel going out to the north gate or only a survey post I've forgotten. Then it moves, and I let out a breath I didn't know I was holding. *Odel.* The plain has held its breath and let it go, the way it does when the great wheel turns.

The heat sits on my forearms like a wet cloth, and I've stopped noticing it, which is how I know I belong here. The condensation runs down the inside of the glass in lines, and between the lines the causeway runs dead straight toward a pumping house it never reaches, because the haze takes it first. I've watched that causeway for eleven years. I have never seen the pumping house arrive. I know it's there. I've walked it. But from the window it is always *toward*, never *at*, and I find I don't mind. I think most of what I know is like that. I steer by things the haze has taken.

In the evenings I go out with the oil can.

That's the other half of me, the half that has feet. The crossing is a kilometer and a half down the rail bed, past the place where the rails sink into salt scale. I walk it in cut-tire boots, eyes down, choosing my footing with the tired grace of long habit, and the snails tick under me in spite of it. The crabs part ahead without hurry. At the crossing there is a signal light on a rusted post, and it is still working. It goes from red to a yellow like weak tea, then to green, then back, on a rhythm no train has asked for in years. I wipe the lens. I oil the hinge. Salt has grown a fringe on the bolts and I chip it off with the flat of a screwdriver, and by next month it will have grown back. I do not call this futile. Calling it futile would be like calling the tide futile.

Because here is what I've worked out, out here, with only the light to explain it to. Someone kept it before me, and someone kept it before them, and none of us could tell you what the light is *for*. But the terns have started roosting on its crosspiece, and the guano has whitened it like the cracking tower, and geckos hunt the moths that come to its glow. A swallow has plastered a cup of mud under the hood. It has been a signal to no one for so long that it has become a signal to everyone, in the only language the flat speaks, which is *here, and here, and here.* If I let it go dark, I wouldn't be stopping a train. I would be walking away from a tenant.

That's the word I've settled on for myself, in the end: not resident, not owner. Tenant. I'm one layer in the section. Below me the purple ones live on the light that gets through the green, and below them the black floor does whatever it does. Above me the bats stream out of the brick like a chimney of smoke. I take in what the boat brings, I breathe out numbers and small mercies, and someone farther along the chain takes those and does something I'll never see. The rats in the dry pump house have opinions about me, and I am glad of them. They're the only ones who bother.

Sometimes I think a visitor will come, walking the causeway with too-clean boots, and I'll watch them from my window without a sound. I'll wonder mildly who they are and what they expect to find, and whether they'll notice the ticking underfoot, and whether they'll understand the light. They won't. But I'll leave the oil can where they can see it.

Dusk comes. The overcast turns the color of a bruise, the pans take their last flat gleam one by one, and the midges rise in their slow brown columns. Somewhere a diesel coughs and settles. I lean over the sill and put on the lamp, one yellow window in a very large dark, and I don't tell it what it's for.
