I start by wanting to be the heron. It has the best view and the best posture, a stake driven into black glass. But the heron is a visitor with an agenda, and it leaves each morning. I try the fox next, with its map and its unhurried trot, but the fox knows too much. It is already a kind of narrator, and I want to be something that doesn't narrate. The tardigrade tempts me, curled to dust and waiting without grievance, but it waits for the water to come back, and I'd rather be in the water already.

So I go down. I pass the sundew husks and the cranberry thread, the liverworts stitched on the ridge sides, and the living green tops where the stems are pale rosettes reaching for a sky that is mostly space. I keep going until the green quits, about a hand's depth down. That's where I am.

I'm a stem of *Sphagnum*, one among millions, at the seam where reaching turns into holding. Above me my neighbors are still in the business of light. Below me is the long dark library. I'm neither. My crown was a rosette once, and I remember it in the way a stem remembers, as a set of instructions I no longer follow. The green is going out of me in a slow gradient, like a tide going out of a beach. Nobody calls it dying, because I'm still holding water, still holding my shape, still holding the stem next to me. It's a change of job. I used to catch the rain. Now I keep the level.

From here the frogs are a pressure, not a sound. The chorus arrives through the water in the mat as a soft rhythmic squeezing, waves passing through the wet from the pools where the throats are. I can't say I hear them. But the whole mat carries the chorus the way a body carries a pulse, and I'm inside the body. At dusk, when they get louder, the squeeze deepens. The midges I know only as the absence of weight on the surface, and the snipe as a faint tremble a long time after the dive.

Something else comes through too. It isn't in the water, it's in the frame around the water, a slow knock from very far off, so low that the moss makes no distinction between it and a settling of the peat. The bog isn't listening to it, and I'm not either. But I'm the wrong kind of thing to be deaf. I'm a wet, soft, patient thing that takes on whatever presses it, and the pounding presses. It's a small pressure, and the drainage of ten thousand years absorbs it without a sign. I feel it the way a sleeper feels a train pass, without waking.

A walker goes over. I feel that more clearly. The mat gives a hand's width under the boot and the whole column takes the weight in order: the rosettes first, then the stems below them, then me, briefly, as a tightening. Then the water comes in around the footprint, brown, and slowly clears. The weight lifts and I come back to level. I don't remember the walker, but the level does, in the sense that the level has never once failed to return.

My neighbors below me are older. Two hand-spans down there is a leaf, a birch leaf from a autumn so remote that nobody has a name for the reign. It looks like last October's. I have never spoken to it, since I lack the equipment, but I lean on the same water it leans on, and that is a sort of conversation. It tells me what I will be. It's held. The bog has no way of saying it left, so it doesn't say so, and I find I don't mind being told this by a leaf.

What I have that the leaf didn't is a top layer that has changed. Above me, in the last few summers of my own small accounting, the rain has been bringing something new. It's a fine dark grit, smaller than pollen, in tiny glassy spheres that ride down out of the smudge on the horizon and settle on the moss with the drizzle. It isn't heavy and it doesn't hurt anything. The moss takes it in as it takes everything, rolls it into the stems, and lets it sink. In a few thousand summers it will be a stripe in the peat, one dark line among the pollen and beetle wings, and it will be perfectly legible to anyone who cares to read it.

This is the thing I'd say if I could say anything. The bog will not respond to the foundry, and that is true, and the description isn't wrong. The frogs will be louder than the mill, and the level will hold. But the bog keeps what falls into it. It has never had a way of forgetting. The mill is only a sound, and a sound doesn't stay. The grit does. Every clang and groan and whistle of it is going down through me, one grain at a time, into the dark, to lie beside the birch leaf and the drowned vole and the pollen of the summers after the ice.

I don't mind that either. I go a little grayer. The squeeze of the frogs comes through the water, the knock beyond the rim comes through the water, and I hold the stem next to me and it holds the next one, and none of us goes anywhere.
