I come into it drifting, in the middle of a lane, in the empty kilometer where nothing is near enough to touch. For a while I am only the drifting. The current has a pace, and I have it too. The thud arrives in my body before I have a body to feel it with, and then I have one: small, soft, the length of a finger, all appetite and no station.

So I am a larva. Somewhere downstream a row of fan-worms is waiting for a gap, and I am the thing that fills gaps. I ride the belt past the walls and watch the rows go by, coarse and fine and finer, each fan open in the current like a hand held out for change. Every station is taken. I feel no impatience about that, or if I do, it is the kind that has never once been disappointed. A vacancy will come within days. It always has.

Then one does. A fan-worm at the third row has closed for the last time, and its place is bare rock and a slow drift of its own dead tissue. I feel the pull toward it, and it is correct. It is exactly the size of me. I could go and open, and spend forty years on one pass of the current, and be content, and never be missed when I stopped.

But I have been watching the tenders.

One of them crosses the wall above the empty station, pale and quick, a hand-sized scuttle of legs. It stops and dips a foreleg into the silt on the dead fan. It tastes something, and I taste it too, faintly, a wrongness in the water about the size of a grain of sand. It clears the silt, tugs the husk loose, and walks away already leaning toward the next thing. It doesn't look back to see whether the row is whole. It knows.

I let the station go by. The current takes me on, and behind me I feel another larva, one of the patient ones, sliding into the vacancy, and I feel the row close. That is the first thing I choose here, and it is not much of a choice. A tender is what you are when you notice the grain before you notice the row.

---

The work is what the description promised. I go along the wall in a loose gang of six or seven, and none of us directs the others. A fan is fouled, and I clear it. A nodule has rolled onto a lace-sponge and is crushing one edge of it, and I turn it aside. A blade of canopy has sagged an arm's length lower than its neighbors, and I cut the fouled end and let the current take it to the feeders, who will be glad of it. Each fix is small and it takes a moment. Then it is over and the wall is exactly as it should be, and the fixing leaves no mark. That is the part I like best and understand least. The reward for putting a thing right is that it looks as though nothing was ever wrong.

I do not think this is servitude. The plateau is not my master. It is a thing I am part of, the way a hand is part of a person who is asleep and yet somehow reaching for the glass of water. The kelp continents don't think, but they agree, and I am one of the ways the agreement is kept.

---

The note comes on an ordinary stretch of wall, in the middle of a fix.

I am scraping silt off a palm-hand feeder when the sound arrives beneath the pounding and settles into it, the way a stone settles into a riverbed. It is a drum-whale, far off, one voice, one falling line. My legs stop. It has no wrongness in it at all. There is nothing in it I could clear, turn aside, or cut back. It goes through me, through the fan I am holding and the wall the fan is fixed to, and out the other side toward wherever it is going.

I stay there longer than I have stayed anywhere. The gang moves on along the wall. One of them pauses and turns back, tastes me, finds nothing wrong, and goes again.

What I notice is this. The place has no unsettled questions in it, and that is the whole achievement of the place. Every stipe fires in step. Every nodule falls back into the roots. Every vacancy is filled by a larva that has been waiting. And there, inside all that, one animal the length of a city block opens its throat every few years and asks something that nothing here is built to answer. Then, from farther down the lane, comes an answer. Not an answer to the question, I think, only an answer to the asking. *Here. Here also.*

I have no throat like that. I am hand-sized and my voice would not carry to the next feeder. But I think I understand now what the tender is for, in the larger arrangement, though no one arranged it. We are the ones who keep the wall clean enough that the note can pass along it. We are the small hands in the large silence. We are how the ordinary stays ordinary so that one thing can be strange.

---

Near the end, or what I take for the end, though the plateau has no ends, I climb. I go up the wall past the last row and the sagging edge of the canopy, into the band of green just under the roof of the world where the sheet-rays live. Up here the light is thinner and the pounding comes through the blades like a heartbeat through a mattress. A ray slides over me, mottled and vast and unconcerned. I follow it a little way, clinging to the underside of the blades, and I watch its wing lift and break the surface.

The sound drops out of the world. For the space of a breath there is a hole in the noise the exact shape of a wing, and I feel the strangeness of it, the wrongness, the *quiet*. Then the ray slides back down, and the pounding closes over it as if nothing had happened.

A blade-edge is torn where the wing passed. It's only a small tear, but it is the kind of thing I know how to do something about.

I put it right. It takes a moment. Then I sit on the blade, in the green, with the roof of the world over me and the whole loud machine below, and I don't move on right away. This is the only thing I have ever done that wasn't in the ordinary line of the work. I stay a little while and I listen.

Far down the lane, a long falling note, and after it, a very long time after, the reply.

I go back to the wall. There is silt on the fans.
