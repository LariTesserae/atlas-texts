I start by wanting to be small. A shellmoth, one of the grains in the grainy air, flashing silver because the cloud flashed, with no more idea why than a wave has. It's a restful thought. But a shellmoth doesn't know the pulse is coming. It only obeys the ripple after the ripple has already reached it. I'd be a thing the surge happens *to*, and I find I don't want that. I want to be in the hush.

Then a ladder-crab, one translucent body in a file that makes the wall look like it's flowing upward. Same problem, and a worse one: the file doesn't need me. Take me out and the wall still flows.

So I drift toward the people, toward the ones who keep the count. Not a weir-mender, though I'd be glad of the work, and not a spear-carrier. I'd be an apprentice hearer, young and not yet good at it. I'd be the one lying against a wallower's flank in the third pool, in the warm pearly dusk, with my wet hair heavy on my neck and my ear against a hide like boiled dough. Mostly I'd hear nothing but the animal's slow digestion, and I'd be wrong about the surge more often than I'd admit. Twice in my first season I'd tap too early. The households would put down their spears and look up at the roof, and the chitterers would answer my mistake in long rising screams. The old hearer wouldn't scold me. She'd say, *listen to the one who starts early,* and I'd go back to the big female, whose murmur begins an hour ahead of anyone's and which I'd learn the way you learn a relative's cough.

Around the third year the work would change. I'd stop hearing individual animals and begin hearing the murmurs *lean toward one another*, the way separate voices in a room lean toward a single word. That's the chord. When the pitches close, there's time for a short meal and no more. I'd tap it out on the bone-drum, and I'd feel the pattern leave my hands and run along the ridge, house to house, faster than I could ever walk. That would be the best feeling I know: something I heard first becoming something everyone knows, and nobody thanking me, because they're already looking at their work and asking whether it can be left.

I would lose things and I'd learn not to mourn them. The first weir I helped stake would go under in a flood, and I'd sulk. The second time I'd be at the poles by evening, setting them knowing the stone would armor them, *letting the water help.* I think that's the part I'd keep longest. You make something with care, you know it's borrowed, and the care isn't lessened by that. It's the reason for the care.

Sometime after that I'd be sent down to the deep gully with a bowl.

Nobody would explain. That's how it's done. I'd carry it rim-up in both hands so the drip couldn't spoil it, past the warm shelves and into the place where the light gives out and the snot-green mats run hot and thick. I'd see the spirals, and I'd watch for an hour, because everyone does, and they'd turn. It would be slow, like a pot being stirred by someone in no hurry. In the bowl would be a knot of hair from the first hearer I ever knew, the one who taught me the early female's cough. I'd set it at the edge and back away, and I wouldn't look for what came after.

I don't know which of the stories I'd believe. Maybe the mat is one creature and we're what lives in its fur. Maybe it's only current. Maybe it's a rhythm old enough to have woken up. But I know what I'd be doing there, and I think it's the truest description of my place in this world: I'd be the one who listens and passes it on, and who sets something down so the deep thing knows there is someone paying attention.

And in the three heartbeats before the next surge, when every animal falls silent and the floor holds its breath, I'd have something to say aloud, in an ordinary voice, on a ragged ridge with a dozen others. It wouldn't be grand. Probably it would be a name, or *I heard you,* or *thank you for the warning,* said to the wallower. Then the water would break, and the roar would take the words, and I'd trust, as the people do, that they were kept.
