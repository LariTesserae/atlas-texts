# The Uncounted

I'd like to say I arrived the way visitors do, over the rim at first light, with the whole basin laid out below me like a page still wet. Everyone who tells this story starts there, because the rim is the only place the city can be seen at once. Eleven minutes wide, pearl on grey, and the silt around it ringing dully under your boots like a cracked bell. You would stand there and think: *it's small. I could understand it.*

I do stand there, some mornings, but I don't arrive. I am walking up to look at the place I am about to walk back into.

---

My name is not important to the ledger, which prefers places. I am a Cord-Walker, and I have been one for as long as I have been anything. My cord hangs at my belt with forty-one knots in it, and this morning I'll untie six and tie six new ones, and not one of them will be a lie, exactly. *These are true today* is the oldest thing anyone said to me, and it is the whole of our philosophy. It sounds like a warning, but it is more like being handed a warm stone.

The Weavers' lane is where I sleep, in an ovoid room that grew around the shape of my sleeping. The walls are cool at the feet and warm at the head. The ice on the courtyard basin is a skin you could read through, and beneath it the water is so clear that visitors, when we get them, try to drink from what looks like an empty bowl and knock their knuckles on the ice. I break it with the side of my thumb. This is also a kind of measurement. Thin ice means a mild night, and a mild night means the errata will be gentle and the shadows will only lag a little.

Mine lags a little more than most.

The heron in the courtyard is doing what it does, which is nothing, with tremendous accuracy. One talon is lifted a finger's width above the water. I say, "Good morning," because you should, and because I've noticed that being greeted seems to make it hold the pose more steadily. Then I check the Low Basin, three minutes away by cord and eleven by legs, and it is there as well, talon up, the same grey angle to the neck. I leave a cake of offering at each. I have never once been sure which of them ate it.

---

The work is this. I go out to the warm edge, where the walls smell of wet chalk and struck matches and the ground is paste under a thin crust, and I lay my cord along a lane and walk it. I count. Then I turn around, and count again, and the counts disagree, and I tie both knots with a little space between them so that I can feel the gap with my thumb later.

Today it is the square by the new eastern wall. Ninety steps in. Coming out, forty.

People imagine the difficulty is the disagreement. It isn't. The difficulty is that for those forty steps you have to remember that you are also still crossing the other ninety, that both are true and neither is being kind about it. It is like holding a note while someone plays a second, lower note beside it. I come back pale. The Tenders give me moss tea, and someone says, "Is your shadow with you?"

"Mostly," I say. "A little behind."

It is a joke. Everyone laughs quietly, at the right place, and no one finishes the sentence, because we all know how it ends.

---

In the afternoon I go to the old quarter, where the air holds still and the walls are dark and finely fissured like the glaze on old bowls. It is the only place in the Ward where my cord agrees with itself. The stillness there is unnerving. It feels like being looked at by something that is deliberately not looking. I go to the ledger-room, where Marren sits with her back to the wall, waiting for the pulse and writing between pulses.

I bring the discrepancies. She takes the cord in her ink-stained fingers and reads it the way a doctor reads a wrist. "Ninety and forty," she says. "That square has been doing that since spring." She writes it down. Then she does something she has not done before. She turns back through the volume, unhurried, thread marker by thread marker, and lays her finger on an entry from eleven weeks ago.

*Count of the Ward taken at the evening meal: sixty. Recounted: sixty-one. Recounted by a second: fifty-nine. Difference unresolved.*

"That's the census," I say. "Nobody ever gets the same count twice. We made our peace with that."

"We did," says Marren. "I'm not sure we asked why." She turns three more pages, five more entries, and I see the pattern before she says it. Every time, the count differs by exactly one. Every time, the discrepancy is near the Weavers' lane. And in a margin, in the small plain hand the Ledger-Keepers use when they are trying not to interpret, the same phrase written over and over across many months: *a woman seen in the lane. Not recognized. Recognized afterward.*

"You," Marren says gently. She isn't accusing. She is reading the wall.

---

I would like to tell you I was surprised. What I felt was closer to the moment when a knot you have carried for years, pulled tight without thinking, finally comes undone in your fingers and you understand what it was holding.

There is a door on the north wall of the Weavers' lane. I know the entry. *Door on the north wall of the Weavers' lane opened onto silt; closed again by evening.* I have read it a dozen times without once asking who opened it. I do not remember opening it. But my shadow, I now realize, has lagged since before I was a Cord-Walker, and I've always assumed everyone's did, a little, and been too polite to say. And I remember, or seem to, the sound of a plate ringing under one foot, dull and single, in a country with no walls, and a very long edge.

It is possible I went through and came back. It is possible I came back *some* of the way. The census doesn't say which of us is the extra one: the woman who returned, or the woman still standing out there in the silt, counted only when the city happens to be looking.

Marren doesn't say this either. She says, "Some of the Lagging grow back in."

"And some go to the warm quarter."

"That is also allowed." She lays the pen down. "Entries that get cross-referenced tend to stop recurring. I've told you that carefully."

"You have."

"If I write you in fully, with a thread to every earlier sighting, it might settle the count. Sixty. Just sixty, every time." She looks at the wall, not at me. "It might settle you, too. I don't know that it wouldn't. I don't know that it would."

---

Here is what I think about, on the walk back through the dusk, with the light-guides going the color of overcast evening and the fog moving in to keep the stairwells company.

Being counted is a kindness. It is the oldest one there is. And to be counted *wrongly*, by a whole town that has decided not to mind, is also a kind of kindness, a room left open at the edge of the house. I have lived in that room. It's not a bad room. From it you see the errata from a little to one side, which is why I'm a good Cord-Walker, why I can hold ninety and forty at once without flinching. I have always been standing partly in the other number.

But it is also true that the herons resolve, eventually, and everyone agrees it was the correct outcome, and no one can say which courtyard was chosen. And I am tired, some evenings, in a way that has nothing to do with walking.

I stop under the pale tree in the Nine Wells, the one whose leaves are shaped like small hands. The Tender kneeling there is reporting on the fog and who slept badly. I stand and listen, and I am included in the report, which is how I know I am here. *And the Cord-Walker has come in late,* the Tender says to the tree, in that level murmur, *and is thinking.* The leaves do not move. But they hold.

---

I go back to Marren the next morning and tell her to write it. All of it. The door, the silt, the sixty-one, the woman in the lane who was not recognized until afterward.

But when she reaches for the thread to cross-reference it, I put my hand over hers, the way you might steady someone about to lift a full cup.

"Leave one loose," I say. "Not the whole entry. Just the last thread. Let the Mathematician have a margin to work in."

She looks at me for a long moment. Then she nods, and writes, and leaves the last marker unknotted, a small pale end trailing off the edge of the page like a question you have decided to trust.

That evening, in the oldest quarter, we lay our hands on the wall and wait. The pulse comes, once, slow, an enormous hand adjusting its grip. The wall under my palms stays warm a moment longer than it should. I do not pray. I keep it company.

Then, far off across the basin, the single clear tone rings, without echo, the sound of something finishing a thought. The Tenders lift their heads. The Cord-Walkers stop mid-lane. The water at the heron's feet shivers once. Marren counts the people in the room, out loud and quietly, the way she does.

"Sixty," she says.

Then she looks at me, and at the shadow that arrives a beat after I do, and she smiles the smallest smile a person can make.

"Sixty-one," she says. "Today."

I take my cord in my fingers. There is a place for one more knot, and I tie it, and it isn't a measurement. It's only a small, tight, true thing, the kind you can feel with your thumb in the dark.

*These are true today.*
