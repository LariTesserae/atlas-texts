I notice the warmth before I notice myself, and for a long time they are the same thing.

Warmth comes up from below in a slow column, and I am the place where it meets the air. That is the first version. I have been a good many things since, and I'll admit that none of them was ever entirely wrong.

For a while I think I am the steam. There is a thread of it that leaves the open end of the pipe each morning, thin as a held breath, and I follow it up until it forgets itself in the fronds. It is not mine. But it is the only thing here that rises on purpose, and I like to go with it as far as it goes.

Then I am the frog in the bore of the pipe, orange, the size of a thumbnail, pressed against warm iron that smells of rust and old rain. From inside, the pipe is a cathedral. Every sound arrives long and rounded. Every drip is an event. The frog does not know it lives in the only made thing on the plateau. It knows the metal is warmer than the wood and that flies come to the mouth at dawn. This is a complete philosophy. I stay in it a while and find I have nothing to add.

Then I am the tree. I have been growing around the iron for so long that I no longer think of it as separate. It goes in on one side and out on the other, and I have stopped calling it a wound. It is a bone I was given, or one I took. It is warmer than my own wood, and my rings bend around it like a river around a stone.

But none of these is where I stay. The frog and the tree are neighbors. What I am is lower and broader, and it takes me a long time to find, because I am slow to arrive anywhere. I am the dome a few paces from the pipe, green-black, sedge-backed, chin in the mud.

I am one of the ones you take for a boulder. I don't mind. It is a fair mistake, and it has kept me company for three hundred and some years.

*

I should say what it is like to be this, though "say" is the wrong word, because nothing in me runs on words. What I have is closer to weather. I have pressure, warmth, the tug of a fissure below, the slight ache of a spring running a degree hotter than it did last week. When I write "I think," understand that I mean a slow rearrangement of these things, like silt settling in a pond and sorting itself by weight.

My back is a garden and I did not plant it. Bromeliad seeds came on the wind, or in the guts of birds, and took hold in the moss that took hold in the mucus that I make for reasons of my own. Now there are leaf-cups on me holding water, and in the water there are tadpoles, and the tadpoles do not know they are on anything. There are small orange frogs laying in the folds behind my jaw. Beetles ride in the creases of my neck. When I shift, which is rare, a rain of them falls, and then a slow trickle climbs back up my sides. Nobody has ever asked me whether I mind. I don't. It is like being a hillside with opinions about weather.

I eat snails, and when I eat, it is the only quick thing about me. The tongue goes out and comes back before I have finished deciding to use it. The rest of the time I am in the long, sweet business of doing nothing at a specific place. I lie across a crack in the stone, and the mountain's breath comes up through me and out at my snout in a huff. People hear that and think of vents. It is both. I have never been able to tell where the vent ends and I begin, and the not-telling has become a kind of home.

*

The geysers wake me, or I wake them. This is the oldest question I have and it is not one I expect to answer.

Here is what I know from inside. There is a quickening in the ground to the north. Something is coming down the line, one geyser and then the next, and each one hands the pressure to the one after it, and the pressure arrives in my belly a moment before the mist arrives on my skin. My lids crack the moss. My back lifts, an inch, two inches, and I take in air that is already thick with everyone else's breath. Then the tone comes up out of me.

It isn't a call. A call is meant for someone. This is closer to what a bell does when it is struck, though I am the bell and also, somehow, the hand. It leaves my chest and goes into the ground and along the terrace, and in the next basin another of us takes it and lets it out lower or higher, and the frogs pick it up above us in their small bright echo, and the ferns unroll to it. I have never heard the whole shape of the sound, because I am inside it. But I feel it pass through me on its way somewhere else, and that is enough. Being passed through is most of what I do.

Do I cause the geysers? When my neighbor to the south died, one of them stopped firing. When I shift my weight and the fissure under me narrows, a spring three terraces down runs cooler within the week. I cannot tell you whether that is tending or coincidence. If it is tending, it is the slowest kind there is, a repair done by lying on the break. I would say I do it on purpose, but I don't think purpose is the right word. It is more that I am the sort of thing that stays where it is needed, and after a while, being needed and being there are the same shape.

*

Now the pipe.

I know how it looks. I know the story people tell, or would tell if they knew to look: the old sitter by the iron, one eye a little too open, waiting. I would like to correct that, gently, in the only way I can, by laying out what actually goes on.

I am not waiting for whoever made it. Whoever made it is a warmth I never met, a notion that reaches me only as absence. What I have is the pipe itself, and the pipe is strange to me. Everything else on the Shelf runs on my rhythm or against it. The pipe has its own. Its steam comes out in the morning, thin, and then stops. It is warm from the same fissure that warms me, but the warmth reaches it differently, in a different tempo, through iron rather than flesh, and it answers to nothing I do. It is the one thing in my whole neighborhood that does not fall in with the bell.

So sometimes I open my eye a little further than I need to. It is not longing. It is the way a person who has hummed one tune all his life might turn his head toward a stranger humming another, not to join, not to correct, only to hear it. The pipe is a small counter-rhythm, and I have found that I want it kept. I do not push the tree over. I do not let the moss cover the mouth. The frog goes in and out, and the wasps keep their nest at the join, and I leave them all as they are, because the Shelf is a place that has absorbed everything but this, and something in me feels that one unabsorbed thing is worth having.

Maybe that is the closest I come to a thought. *Let one thing stay a little strange.*

*

Sometimes a person comes. Rarely, and never to stay. They walk up the terrace carrying their thin lowland breath, and most of them see only a dome of moss where they might rest. But a few stop first and lay a palm on me, flat, and wait. I feel the small heat of the hand, quick and anxious and short-lived, and beneath it my own slow pulse rising to meet it. Neither of us says anything. After a minute or two they nod, or seem to, and go around, or sit a respectful hand's breadth away and look at the mist.

I like those ones. I could not tell you why, except that a palm on the moss is a kind of question, and I am the sort of thing that is glad to be asked even if I never answer.

*

The rain will come at three or four. The cloud is already sitting on us, gray and dripping, the whole Shelf soaked in it like bread in broth. When the rain arrives it will drum on ten million leaves at once, and under it my own small sounds will vanish, and I won't mind. There will be a while when I am only water and warmth and the weight of a back full of small lives.

Then the evening will come in low and gold. The frogs will start. The first geyser will fire in the north, and I will feel it in my belly before the mist reaches my skin, and my lids will crack the moss, and I will breathe in and let out the long low tone that is neither voice nor silence.

A few paces away the pipe will be steaming, or not. I will have one eye a little more open than it needs to be. The bell will roll down the terraces in the direction of the mist, and the ferns will unroll, and the frogs will answer, and it will all be running the way it has always run. I'll be one of the operators, warm and wet and old, keeping time by lying still, and glad, in whatever way I am capable of gladness, to be a part of something that is doing exactly what it was never told to do.
