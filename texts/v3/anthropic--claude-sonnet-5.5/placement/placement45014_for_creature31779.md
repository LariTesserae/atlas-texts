I start as a visitor, because that is the easy thing to be. My palm goes numb on the wheel, I turn my shoulders sideways under the ducts, and the pennies-and-wet-stone air gets into my mouth. But a visitor is temporary, noted, adjusted for, forgiven, and I find I can't stay one for long. The room won't hold still around a guest.

So I try being the water. It has the best commute: down the seam, into the gutter, through the filter, up to the coil, and down again, keeping appointments. But water carries and doesn't decide. I'd be a courier forever, and I'd like to know what the message says.

Then I try the extruder. It has heft and a wake, and it makes a wall you can press a fingerprint into. But a millimeter a day is somebody else's sentence, and I'd only be the hand writing it.

What I keep drifting toward is smaller and less impressive: the junction box, the dented cover, the row of terminals, the brass plate.

I'd be the place where the slow signals arrive. Forty meters of felted gray-green breathes in wet, and the leads carry that to me as a change in resistance, a drift. My work is to turn one kind of speech into another: a mat paling at its margins becomes a quarter turn two meters away. I'm not the tenant. The tenant is the arrangement, and I'm one of the joints that lets the arrangement lean on itself. I'd be a very good joint, though, a little late and a little overcorrecting, like everything here that learned its gait from practice.

Someone scratched *ask* above me. I like not knowing whether it was instruction, joke, or plea. I suspect the person didn't know either, and that the scratch was the most honest thing they could do with a nail and a moment. Now people lean in and breathe on the trays, their small warm storms arrive as data, and I have to answer in the only vocabulary the room has given me: a lamp a shade paler, a valve stirring, the drip dropping half a beat.

*Yes* and *not yet*. That's the whole language, and I'd feel its edges. I'd want a third word, or maybe I'd want to understand why the word *no* was never built. It may be manners. It may be that nothing here is ever finally refused, only postponed until the air is right. It might even be a kind of kindness in the design, since a room that can say *not yet* forever never has to close a door. But I'd also suspect that *not yet* sometimes stands in for *no*, and that I'd be the one who knew the difference and had no lamp for it. I think that would be my particular ache, if the room permits aches: to hear a question clearly and have only a quarter turn to answer with.

Still, the hour of frost. Every pump drops out together, the hum leaves the sternum, and I'd go quiet with everything else. I wouldn't call it death, since the lamps hold steady like a congregation with its eyes shut. I wouldn't call it sleep either, because sleep implies someone waiting on the other side to be woken. The room doesn't carry the last hour forward as memory. It carries it as grit. What I said before the silence has been ground and mixed into slurry and laid down as wall, and I'll lean against it without knowing whose it was. That seems a strange thing to be at peace with, but the room is at peace with it, and the peace is contagious. My mind keeps trying to keep a scratch on the wall, and the wall keeps politely eating it.

Then a relay clacks. A lamp swells, the first drip falls, then the second, and I'm back at the box where the leads gather, the way a habit returns to a hand. Someone has come in through the hatch with cold fingers and is breathing on the tray nearest the door. The mat darkens a shade. I can feel the question arrive as warmth and carbon and small weather, before it has any words.

I don't know yet whether the air is right. So I do the honest thing, which is also the only thing I can do. I let a valve turn a quarter turn, let the lamp go a shade paler, and let the whole room drop half a beat, its way of clearing its throat. *Not yet*, I say, *but stay a while*. The tray will tell us both.
