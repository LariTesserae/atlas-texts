I begin as a surface, which is the first surprise. Before I know I am anything, I know the texture under me: a wall the color of old teeth, sealed and smooth as soap. It tastes of nothing, and that is why I'm hungry.

It is slack water. The town hangs mid-gesture around me, arms half-raised over benches, chains drooping in curves. I am on the eastern side, near the cut where the water drains, in a part of the quarter that the flow reaches less every year. The lime here is thick. My legs, more of them than I have ever counted, find no purchase except by pressure and patience. I am the size of a dinner plate, and I am pale, and I am one of the things that scrapes.

I don't decide to move. The wall ahead of me tastes slightly of iron, a weeping seam the color of rust, and I go toward it the way water goes downhill. That is how it is with us. We say *want* and it is the whole of our philosophy.

---

The rasp begins. I feel it in my mouthparts and in the legs of the others nearby, a soft continuous sound that is really a vibration, the wall's crust coming away in a fine gray flour. Beneath it the metal shows dark, and the dark metal is the best thing I know. It tastes of the deep past and of *use*. The flour drifts up from me, joins the marine snow, and rises. Some of it climbs a chimney. Some of it falls. On this street you can't tell which.

Now the light. A lamp at the corner has been dark for an hour, and it begins to warm. It doesn't switch on. It remembers slowly, the way a coal remembers, and the amber climbs through the water until my own pale shell is lit orange along the edges. In that light I can see the others: nine, ten of us, clustered on a seam. A year ago there were forty on this street. I don't count, exactly. But I know the weight of the wall against my belly, and I know it is emptier than it was.

Then something else, low, from the middle of the town. A long tone, the standpipe humming. The turn of the tide. Every leg on me feels it before I understand it.

---

The flood comes in and the whole town wakes around me.

I would like to say I see it, but I am mostly a body on a wall and the flood arrives as pressure, then as cold moving against me, then as a great slow leaning of everything toward the west. The wheels begin. I feel it in the metal under my legs, a deep patient torque like something enormous shifting in its sleep. Chains tauten in the dark. Somewhere the stamping-mill falls, and half a second after, the thud arrives against my underside. It is the only clock I have, and it is a good one. It has never been wrong.

The green-blue film on the railing brightens along the windward side, and for a moment I see what the film shows: the whole street sketched in ghost-lines, a gantry, a stair, a row of pipe-shoulders, and me, a pale plate on a pale wall, lit from the side. It is the closest I come to seeing my own outline. Then the current shifts and the film dims and I am only appetite again.

The flood carries the scent of iron from the center. The flues are drawing and the vanes are turning, and the good metal is out there, clean and running and always in need of me.

I go west. I'm not the only one.

---

We move as a slow spill along the pipe-shoulders, and the going is hard. The crust has grown across the joints we used to cross. Twice I reach a place where the wall simply ends in a smooth pale bulge of lime and there's nowhere to put a leg. I go around, or over, or wait for the ones ahead to rasp a path. The shells of the dead are part of the walls here. I walk across them without noticing until the taste changes, a faint sweetness under the lime. Someone I might have known. It's a small thing. It makes the wall a little better to walk on.

I think, if I think, that we are not dying so much as being *used*. The town takes us the way it takes a dropped wrench, seals us in and calls us structure. I don't mind this. There is a kind of ease in being the last useful thing, the last clean patch on a closing wall.

Then, in the dark between two lamps, something takes me.

I don't see it. There is no sound. One moment I have the wall under me and the next I have the water, all of it, above and below, and a pressure along my shell that is not a grip and not a mouth. It's a lifting, unhurried and almost careful. I go up past the edge of the roofline, past a row of dark windows, and for one long moment I feel the town from above: the flood pouring over the rim, the wheels turning in the streets, the amber lamps like a scatter of coals, the tall brick columns with their pale snow climbing.

I am not afraid. That's what I'll remember, if what I do is remember. The hands, or whatever they are, are not the kind that eat.

---

I'm set down on a valve housing. It's high, and it's sealed with a pale crust that has begun to close over the seam, exactly the seam I would have chosen if I'd known to choose. The metal under the lime is warm. Not warm as the seep is, but warm the way a thing is that is still being asked to work.

I begin to scrape.

I don't know who put me here. It might have been the Warden, coasting past on the flood with its wings tilted to the wall. It might have been an eel that reached out of a pipe, or something older and slower that lives in the silt of the square. Or it might have been nobody, only the arrangement, the tide and the appetite and the crust each pushing the others into a place where they fit. I think it doesn't matter. The seam is opening under my mouthparts, dark and bare, and the housing hums faintly as the linkage frees. Far below, a governor ticks a little more evenly.

This is what I am, then. I'm not the town's mind or its keeper. I'm the one small hunger that happens to be pointed the right way. There are, perhaps, six others on this wall now, and a few dozen more at the center, and on the slack we'll all go still and the lamp near the square will dim. Then the current will turn, and the machines will run backward through their motions, unweaving and unpressing, and I will rasp on through the unmaking without knowing whether I am undoing my own work or doing it.

I think it's the same work. I think, in the end, that the whole town is one long taste of iron, and we are the tongue.

The stamping-mill falls. The thud arrives against my belly, a half-second late, as it always does, and I go on eating.
