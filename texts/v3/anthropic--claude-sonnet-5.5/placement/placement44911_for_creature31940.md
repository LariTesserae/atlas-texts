# The Screen at Third Knuckle

I wake a minute before the water turns, as holders do. The generator has changed its note. It is a half-tone lower, the cough of a machine deciding to rest. The floor shivers once under my cot, and something behind my ears registers the whole sea thinking about the other direction.

I lie there and count. I have a post, and the post has a name, Third Knuckle, because from the barges the three domes off the eastern shore look like a fist laid on the water, and ours is the third. It is a red shed on pale granite with a stack that smokes green-sweet. The turbine inside is called Aunt, for reasons that predate me and that nobody will explain. Aunt is very old, and she has opinions about oil.

But that is not really my post. My post is the screen.

---

At the foot of the penstock, eleven meters down, there is a grate of iron bars as wide as a barn door. The sea has to pass through it to reach Aunt, and the sea does not arrive clean. It brings ribbonwood torn loose in the last storm, stems and long olive leaves, and snails riding them like passengers, and once a whole drowned gull. The screen holds the litter back and lets the water through. If it clogs, Aunt starves. If it fails, Aunt eats a wrist of pondweed and breaks her teeth.

So twice a day, at the turn, I go down.

I don't dislike the cold, which is a thing I never say aloud. Seven degrees closes over my head like a hand, and then the noise changes rather than stops. The surface hiss goes up and away and becomes a ceiling. Underneath is the gravel-rattle, the frying of the shrimp, and far off the thud of a barge that no one down here bothers to fear.

I go down the penstock ladder with the light coming in shafts, green-gold and swinging. Near the bottom the ribbonwood begins, and I move through it the way you move through a crowd at a wake, apologizing with my shoulders.

At the screen I work with a rake. There is a method: pull from the top, let the current peel the rest. On the crest the current is strong enough to pin me flat against the bars, and I have to trust the iron to be there. Then I hand the weed back. I don't throw it. I carry it out past the fringe of the grate and let go, and the flow takes it east or west, wherever it will root again. An older holder taught me that. The weed is not an enemy, she said. It just wants to be somewhere else, and you are the somewhere else it can't be.

---

Down there, after a season of it, I noticed what I actually do. I don't stop the sea. I couldn't. I decide what part of it gets through. A screen is a kind of opinion held very still. Everything passes it: fish, silt, the slow lean of light, the pressure of the whole tray tilting. Only the things that would choke the work are asked to stay behind, and even they aren't refused. They're returned.

I think that is the whole of my character. I am the bars, and the hands on the bars.

---

On the trough I walk out. The dome has grown a skirt of wet flat, all slick gravel and shining weed, and the corestones lie in it like the backs of whales that have decided to stop. The weed steams. My boots crunch and skid. I cut a load of ribbonwood for the stack with my hook, fast, watching the western edge where the water has drawn back to a bright thin line. I know the sea's return the way I know my own name spoken from the next room. First there is a hush, then a hiss, then a hand's height of cold silver sliding over my boots as if it had always been there.

I walk back up the granite without hurrying. Hurrying is how holders end up as stories.

---

At dusk the frogs begin, one voice from somewhere east, deep and barrel-struck. I feel it in the soles of my feet first. Another answers from the direction of the old dredge, and within minutes the whole forest below me is throbbing in its lopsided pulse, each frog singing into the pauses of the others. Nobody leads it, and it doesn't fall apart.

I sit on the lee side with my back to the warm stack and my eye-strip pushed up to my forehead. The gulls, who have been arguing since before there was a shed, grow quiet by degrees. Out in the middle of the water the red lamp comes on, or rather I notice that it has, because the keeper has never let it go dark. It swings on its chain. I know that the swinging is the point. A light that never moved would be a wall. This one moves so that everything else can be located against it.

Sometimes I think that this is the truest thing I know. You can be fixed and still swing. The chain is not a failure of the anchor. It is how the anchor lives in the water.

The seiche gathers. The fish, somewhere below, tighten into one silver mind. Down on the valley floor, under all that weight of moving green, a very old trout adjusts a fin by the width of a finger and goes on holding still in the hardest place there is.

I get up. I check Aunt's bearings, and she coughs, and I tell her something rude and affectionate. Then I lie down in the dark and wait to wake a minute before the world turns over.
