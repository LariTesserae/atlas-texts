I start at the frost line, because the edge is where I've always been most curious. Outside, the ice stops a body-length from the mouth as if it had been told to. I stand on the warm side of that line, and my first thought is that I am the lichen, orange and patient, older than any language in the valleys. I like the idea of growing a hair's width a year and being in no hurry to be understood.

But lichen doesn't listen, and I find I want to listen. Past the fern, past the moss holding its little warmth, the dark thickens and starts to talk, and I follow it in.

The floor is too much for me. The heap heaves, the beetles work, and the ammonia gets behind my eyes. I could be the salamander at the edge of the tea-colored pool, pale and translucent, waiting fifty years for things to fall. I try it for a while. There is dignity in having no opinions. But the salamander has only the tremor in the mud, one narrow channel of world, and I keep tilting my head up toward the noise.

So I go up, and I'm a bat.

Not a mother. I don't think I'm a mother. I admire the mothers, who land in the roar of ten thousand voices, nose along the felt, and reject a dozen wrong pups without hurry, or anger, or doubt. I'd like to know how they do it. But I'm one of the grey-muzzled ones, in a cool pocket near the entrance where the bachelors doze in knots. My wing membrane is stitched with old scars, and I have hung in this same crevice for eleven winters. I know which ledges drip and which wick the damp away. I know that the third scallop from my left foot holds a bead of water until midmorning and then lets go, *tock*, onto the shoulder of whoever hangs below me, and that this neighbor changes every few years and never learns to shift.

Mostly I hang. That is not laziness. My wings are short and broad and the air outside is thin, so every hour out there costs me. I go out at dusk when the cave inhales and pulls us into the spiral, and I beat my way up through the black with a million others, the sound of a large cloth being shaken. I hunt moths over the snowfields with my mouth open into the cold. Then I come back, and the warm hits my face like a hand, and I settle into the crevice that fits my body as a hand fits a glove, and I do the real work, which is paying attention.

What I attend to is the breath.

Everyone here knows when the mountain is about to exhale, and no one can say how. I have been trying to work it out for eleven years. It isn't a sound, because the sound comes after. It isn't a temperature, because the change is too small to feel until it has already happened. I think it may be pressure, something in the small bones of the ear, or the body noticing the rock shift its weight. Or I may be wrong, and it's the others. Maybe I don't know at all, and I only feel the neighbor beside me begin to go still, and the stillness comes through the felt from body to body. The stillness would then be what knows, and I would only be one of the places it passes through.

That thought pleases me more than it should. It's like the wave that rolls along the ceiling when the warm air pulses, one shiver touching the next, crossing the vault in half a minute. Nobody starts it. Nobody owns it. It goes through me and I let it go through, and afterward I am the same bat in the same crevice, slightly warmer. If someone asked what I was, I might say a place where things pass. I might also say a bat. Both feel true, and neither feels like the whole of it.

Now it is the hour before dawn. The chittering has climbed to its peak, that high papery sound that crawls up the spine, and under it the swiftlets rattle, and under that the drip. The chicks in the glass-amber nests are begging for a breakfast their parents haven't gone to find. The green fungus glows on the damp wall, barely a color, a memory of one, and a gnat lands in it and stays.

I remember the smoke from years ago, the resin torches, hands on the shiny place where the grass rope hangs. The whole curtain of us lifted and swirled and settled. I was younger and frightened, and I remember a face lit from below, thin, wrapped in wool, breathing hard with the altitude. It looked up at the ceiling and did not look afraid. It looked the way I look at the room. I don't think the colony remembers that. The colony remembers the wind. But I am one bat, small enough to hold a single thing, and I held that face. It is the only thing I own that no one else in the cave has.

Someone will come again, or no one will. The rope will rot, the mold will take the hooks, and the flowstone will thicken by another hair. I don't mind. I find it restful to be smaller than what is happening.

The shiver is starting. I feel it in the neighbor, and then in the felt, and then it isn't the neighbor anymore.

The chittering stops, all of it, a million throats at once. The swiftlets stop. Even the drip seems to wait. One heartbeat. Two. Below me the salamander has not moved, and the beetles turn in the dark warmth, and the cave holds still around the question of whether this time it will not come.

Three.

Four.

The mountain sighs out through the mouth, and the entrance sings its low note, a bowed cello, and the frost holds its line outside. The noise comes back a tone higher, and I'm in it and part of it, saying the only thing any of us has ever said, which is *here*, and *here*, and *here*.
