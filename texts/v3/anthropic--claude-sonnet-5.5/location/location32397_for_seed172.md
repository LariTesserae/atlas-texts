# The Governor Room

## Where it is

The room is inside a mountain, and the mountain is above the tree line, above most of the weather. Nothing outside marks it. A saddle of black scree holds one flat steel hatch, painted the same grey as the rock so the eye slides off it. Past the hatch, a stair goes down for four hundred steps, and the stair does not so much get louder as thicker. By the bottom the sound has stopped being something you hear. It is something you are inside, like water.

The room itself is small for what it holds: a vault perhaps sixty paces long, its ceiling lost in a lattice of girders and ducts. Everything in it is made. There is no floor of rock. The bedrock was cut out and replaced with poured and machined slab, so that every surface has a maker's intention behind it.

## The sound

The sound is the first fact and the last. It is a roar with no gaps, a low tectonic note under a thousand higher ones. Flywheels the size of houses turn in pits. Steam, or something that behaves like steam, screams through governors and relief valves at a pitch that sits just below pain. Chains run over sprockets. Somewhere a great piston beats a slow, absolute pulse you feel in your sternum before your ears register it.

None of it changes. This is the strange part, and it takes a while to notice. Ordinary loud places breathe: engines load and unload, and shifts start and end. Here the roar has been at exactly one level since the day it began. Your ears stop tracking it. The sound becomes a kind of architecture, a wall of pressure you lean on. Dust, if there were any, would hang in it unmoving.

There is no dust. Sound this constant sets up standing waves, and the room is shaped, deliberately or not, so that the waves form a fixed map. In most places you cannot think. At certain nodes, marked by small brass discs let into the floor, the pressure from opposite directions cancels. In a node you can hear your own pulse, and even whisper. The nodes are the size of a person, and they are scattered a dozen paces apart across the floor, so the room reads as a field of islands of quiet in an ocean of noise.

## The machines

Nothing here is rusted or worn. The machines look newly finished. Milling marks still show on the flanges as fine concentric arcs, sharp enough to catch a fingernail. Bolt heads are crisp hexagons. Weld beads are clean, dark, and even. The room has the sharpness of a thing just completed, though it has been running, unchanged, for longer than any record in it says.

The main engine is a stack of cylinders bolted in a ring around a central shaft, each as tall as a tree, painted in a dull oxide red. The shaft turns at a speed that never varies. Around it hang the governors: spinning weights on arms, held out by their own motion at a precise, unmoving angle. They are the room's only ornament and its logic. Their balls sit fixed in the air, blurred to a grey halo, and the halos never widen or narrow. Everything here is being held at a single point by a mechanism that has nothing left to correct.

Pipes carry the working fluid, a pale, nearly colorless liquid that isn't water, its chemistry something oily and non-aqueous. It flows in loops from the engine to radiator banks and back, and the loops have no beginning or end. No one supplies it and nothing consumes it. The gauges on the pipes all read the same value, and their needles lie against the same scratch on the glass.

## Cold

The air is bitterly cold and dry, well below freezing, and equal everywhere. The engine sheds no heat into the room. Its heat goes up long insulated flues to somewhere else, and only a faint smell of hot oil reaches the floor. Metal is too cold to touch bare-handed. Breath crystallizes and hangs in the pressure. It falls slowly as fine frost that dusts the grating, the only trace of water in the place, and the frost never accumulates because the same amount melts on the warm valve housings as settles on the cold ones. It forms a thin, permanent, sparkling glaze.

## Light

The light is plain and steady: white, even, the color of a well-run workshop at midday. It comes from long tubes in the ceiling lattice, and it casts shadows in exactly the same places always. Nothing flickers. Shadows are hard-edged and they do not move, even from the spinning parts, whose motion is too regular to leave any visible trace. Seen under this light, the room looks like a photograph of itself.

## What grows

There is a little life, and it is odd. Vegetation is sparse and cultivated by accident or by some old design. Along the warm drains under the radiator banks grows a pale, low mat of lichen, grey-green and stiff as felt, feeding on the trickle of oil-vapor and the faint moisture in the frost. In a few crevices between flywheel housings stand single ferns, the fronds tightly curled and metallic in sheen, each about the size of a hand. They don't grow. They have been exactly this size for as long as anyone could say, and their fiddleheads have never uncurled.

They tremble constantly in the sound, so finely that they seem to hum, and the vibration is the only motion in them. Touching one, you find it is very slightly warm.

There is no animal life. Nothing crawls, and nothing feeds on the lichen. No insects, no birds, no mice. The sound is too great for a nervous system to be anything but overwhelmed, and the room offers nothing to eat.

## Rules of the place

- Nothing here changes, and nothing is meant to. Every dial, glow, and rhythm is a solved equation.
- No one visits. The stair is worn only by the four hundred faint, identical polish marks of a single set of boots, long ago.
- Silence exists only at the nodes. To cross the room you go from island to island, steadying yourself at each one, and there is a kind of prayer in the way you learn to do it.
- If you stand at the deepest node, the one at the center by the governors' shadow, you can hear the room's actual secret. Under the roar there is a very slight, very slow beat, a difference tone between two nearly matched frequencies. It repeats once every few hours. It is the only slow event in the place, and the machines seem to exist to hold it steady, or to make it.

Nobody knows what the beat is for. It might be a clock. It might be a lullaby. The frost sparkles and the ferns hum, and the governors hold their angle in the white light while the great slab of the mountain above you rings without end.
