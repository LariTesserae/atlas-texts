# The Resin Terraces

The shelf sits high on the shoulder of a mountain that is still finishing itself. It is barely a quarter-mile across, a tilted bench of black basalt that cracked into columns as it cooled, and the cooling was recent enough that the edges have not yet learned to be round. Every hexagonal column ends in a clean facet. Some are glassy and throw the light back in thin blades. Others are matte and bristle with tiny vesicles, like bread crust. Nothing has been worn down. The rock still looks as if it were made this morning, and in the sense that matters here, it was.

The air is thin and warm. Heat rises from the ground itself, a dry, steady exhalation through the cracks that carries a smell of struck flint and something sweeter above it. That sweetness is the resin.

## What replaces water

There is almost no water here. A few beads gather on cold nights on the undersides of leaves, and the animals lick them off like a rare condiment. The place runs instead on amber. The plants have made a whole chemistry from a warm, slow, terpene-heavy sap, and they pull it up from mineral-rich fissures where the mountain's heat cooks it out of buried organic sediment. It is not water, and nothing here has ever needed it to be.

The trees are the **lacquer-boles**, with thick, sinuous trunks the color of old cherrywood. The bark is so thin and glossy that you can see the sap rising inside, a slow honey-colored column visible through the wood like mercury in a thermometer. Their leaves are broad, leathery, and overlapping, deep green above and gold beneath. The canopy is dense enough to make the lanes below feel like the inside of a held breath. Between the boles grow ferns with fronds like lacquered feathers, cushions of moss that are more sponge than plant, and creepers that twine up the basalt columns and bind them together with cables as thick as an arm.

The vegetation is lush, but its lushness has a dry-season shine, glazed and resinous instead of dewy. When sunlight comes down through the leaves, everything glows amber, as if the place were lit from inside a jar of honey.

## The town

The columns have been built into. The town is not a human one. It belongs to the **gum-masons**, colonial animals about the size of a thumb. They look something like a cross between a wasp and a small, furred bat, though they are neither. They gather beads of hardened resin, chew them with ash into a pale, papery mortar, and cap and bridge and stack the basalt into a city.

It is dense. Spires of layered resin-paper rise from the tops of the columns, each one the width of a barrel and as tall as a house, and they are packed so close that their shoulders touch. The spires are joined by bridges the thickness of a wrist, by galleries clinging to the sides, and by ramps spiraling down into the cracks in the rock, where the warm air draws upward and keeps the lower chambers hot. The material is translucent amber near the roots and opaque cream near the tops, where the ash content is higher. At dusk the town looks like a cluster of lanterns, though no fire has ever burned in it.

The spaces between the spires are the streets. They are narrow, twisting, and roofed by the canopy. They are carpeted in leaf litter and moss so thick that a footstep vanishes into it. The lanes branch and rejoin, and at their junctions the masons have built little plazas, shallow bowls of hardened resin where the ground is lightly sticky and the smaller fauna gather to feed.

Nothing here is a tool. There are no devices, no worked edges, no engineered thing. The city is a secretion, grown the way a shell is grown, but it has the logic of a place, with districts, thoroughfares, and dead ends.

## Fauna

The place is crowded with animals, and they live in layers.

In the canopy are **lantern moths**, palm-sized and pale, whose wings carry a faint natural phosphor that glows a soft green when they land on warm resin. Hundreds rest in the leaves at once, like a second, dimmer foliage.

On the trunks are **glass geckos**, nearly transparent, so that you can watch the amber sap they have just drunk move down their throats. They cling in rows, sunning, and they never fall.

On the ground are small, round, felt-footed mammals, something between a hedgehog and a hyrax. They graze the moss cushions and nose through the litter. Their pads are thickly furred, so they move without a sound.

Above the town is a ceiling of birds. They are small, fast, and dark-plumed, and they hunt the moths. They do not sing. Their only voice is a faint dry click of the beak, which the leaves swallow.

The gum-masons are everywhere, moving in files along the walls, dozens per hand's width, each one carrying a bead of resin or a crumb of ash. Their wings are folded. They walk.

## Sound

The whole shelf is nearly silent. The leaves are heavy and thick, the moss is dense, and the resin-paper walls deaden everything. The loudest sound is often the soft tick of a resin bead cooling, or the papery scrape of a mason file dragging a load. Sometimes a bird clicks. Sometimes a bough creaks with the slow weight of rising sap. You would hear your own breathing first. Speaking aloud feels rude, and it is what visitors stop doing first.

## Light

The sun is direct and bright at this height, but the canopy and the amber of the walls turn it into something warm and even. There are no hard shadows below the trees, only gradations of gold and green. At midday the streets glow. At dusk the spires take over and hold the day's light for an hour after the sun is gone, and then the moths come on.

## The rules of the place

**The sap moves with the sun.** Each day the amber column in every trunk rises slowly toward the crown as the ground heats, and each night it sinks. The whole town breathes on this rhythm. Over a day the change is almost invisible. Over a season it is unmistakable, and the masons time their building to it, laying down mortar only when the sap is high and the resin is soft.

**The rock is still growing.** Every few years a new crack opens along the shelf with a sound like a dropped plate, a sharp, clean line through the moss. The vegetation seals it within a season. The masons begin a new spire on each new fracture, so the town's oldest buildings sit along its oldest cracks and the newest are the freshest, palest, and most translucent.

**One place is wet.** In the lowest corner of the shelf, in a sheltered fissure, a single seep of actual water slides down a wall of black glass. It is clear, cold, and thin. Nothing drinks from it and nothing grows near it. The moss draws back from it in a ring, the masons build no spire within a body-length of it, and the geckos, which will cling to anything, avoid that stone. The seep has been running as long as the rock has been cool, and everything else here has quietly organized itself around not needing it.

Change is slow and patient. The sap rises and falls, a spire gains a finger's width, a crack opens and closes, and the moths glow on and off. No one is in a hurry, and the whole small city sits warm and amber and nearly silent on its mountain, being made.
