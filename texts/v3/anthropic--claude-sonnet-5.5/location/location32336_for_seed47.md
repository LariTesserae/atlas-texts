# The Lathe Plain

At this height the sky is a dark, saturated blue that goes nearly black overhead, even at noon. The sun is white and small and hard-edged. The air is thin enough that shadows look cut from paper, and cool enough that the light gives almost no warmth. It is the cold of a cellar in a dry country: steady, mineral, unhurried.

The land is a plateau, and it is very old. Whatever mountains once stood here have been ground down to a level so complete that it reads as intention. The horizon is a ruled line, slightly bowed at the edges, and the whole surface lies nine thousand meters above whatever sea used to exist below it. The erosion finished long ago. There is nothing left to wear away, and the ground has settled into its last form: a pavement of tightly packed, wind-polished stones, each about the size of a thumbnail. They are ochre, slate, bone-white, and a green-black glassy stone that flashes when the light catches it. The stones are sorted by size, color, and density into broad bands and long curving ribbons, like a tide chart drawn on the planet.

## The Sorting

The pavement is not still. It is the most restless ground imaginable, and it is restless almost without sound.

Winds cross the plateau at speeds that would flatten a forest at sea level, but the air is so thin that they carry little force. They lift the fine grains and roll the coarse ones, and they can push a person a few careful steps. Whole provinces of stone slide slowly downwind in sheets. Ribbons of dark gravel unspool across pale fields and fold into spirals kilometers wide. A dust column climbs in a slow helix, leans, and comes apart into a dozen smaller columns, each spinning the opposite way. Storm fronts of suspended grit march across the plain like walls, and behind each one the pavement has been reassembled: re-sorted, re-banded, combed flat.

The plateau is not eroding. It is being maintained. Everything the wind disturbs is returned to a state of finished flatness, and the weather is the labor by which that flatness is kept.

## What Keeps It

Beneath the pavement, and rising through it in places, is the machinery.

Nothing about it looks like machinery. In the flat country between storms you see the filaments: pale, hair-thin lines that stand straight up from the ground and vanish into the dark blue sky. They are spaced a few hundred meters apart, in ranks that run to the horizon, and they are far too thin to stand. They stand anyway. Where the light is right they show as threads of white fire, and where it is wrong they are gone. Near their bases the stones lie in perfect concentric rings, as if the pavement were combed around each stem.

The filaments do not move in the wind. The wind moves around them, bending and braiding into patterns that resolve, a few minutes later, into weather. They seem to tune the air the way a harp tunes a string, and they may be doing exactly that. Their work appears to be the shaping of the storms, so that the storms may shape the stones, so that the stones remain a plane.

Nobody knows how they are powered, or what physics holds them upright, or whether they were ever built in any sense a builder would recognize. The best guess is that the plateau's flatness was a specification, and the specification has outlived everyone who wrote it.

## The Marks of People

Among the filaments are scattered a few things that were made by hand, or by something with hands.

A single house of fused glass sits on a rise that is not quite a rise. The wind has scoured it into a lens shape, and its walls are pocked with the small, fine craters of ten thousand years of grit. Its door is a rounded opening facing away from the prevailing wind. Inside there is nothing but a floor of swept pale sand, lightly patterned by air currents that pass through the door and curl back out.

Further on stands an inverted tower, a spire of dark alloy driven point-first into the plateau to a depth no one has measured, with only its broad flat base showing above the pavement, level with the stones. Concentric grooves are cut into that base. When the sun stands at a certain angle, the grooves throw a slow bright ripple across the ground, like light at the bottom of a pool, though there is no water within a hundred kilometers and hasn't been for an age.

There are cairns, too. They are small, careful stacks of the green-black stone, and each is a different height. Every storm takes them down and the pavement puts them back exactly as they were. Whoever built the first ones may have wanted to prove they had been here. Instead they proved that the plain remembers.

## Life

Life is thin and mostly small. On the lee side of stones, in the slim pockets where grit does not scour, grows a crust of pale lichen the color of old paper. It lives on frost that forms only in the deepest hour before dawn, a film of ice so thin it looks like breath on glass, and it lives on the slow dissolving of the stone itself. Here and there the crust has gathered into cushions, tight gray domes no wider than a hand, ringed with tiny brittle spines that vibrate in the wind.

Fauna is rare enough to be an event. Carried in the storms, or perhaps walking between them, are things the size of large dogs, long-legged and narrow, with hides the color of the pavement. They travel head-down along the storm margins, in single file, feeding on the crust and on the small insects that the wind lofts and drops. They have wide, cupped feet that spread like snowshoes on the shifting gravel. Sometimes one stops, raises its head, and stands motionless for an hour in the roaring, silent wind, as if listening. There may be something to listen to.

## Sound

The plateau is almost silent. In air this thin, sound cannot carry. The storms, huge and violent as they are, arrive as a soft hiss, like sand poured onto paper in another room. Grains tick against a visor or a sleeve. A step makes a dry crunch that stops a meter away. Even a shout dies close to the mouth.

But now and then, when the filaments are in tune with one another, something else happens. A low tone arrives that is not really a sound. It is felt in the sternum and the teeth, a pressure with no pitch, lasting a few seconds. The stones shiver on the ground. Then the tone fades, the storms turn, and the pavement begins again its slow, tireless work of remaking itself into the plain it has always been.

## The Rule of the Place

Stillness here is not the absence of motion. It is motion so thorough that it has become a kind of permanence. The plateau is ancient because it is constantly renewed, and it is flat because something, patiently and without malice, will not let it be otherwise. To stand on it is to stand at the center of a process that has no observers and needs none. The wind moves the stones. The stones become the ground. The ground is the plain. The plain is finished, and it is never done.
