# The Sump at Harrow Deep

## Arrival

You go down by a cage lift that takes eleven minutes and drops you past the last daylight in the first ninety seconds. The shaft walls sweat. By the time the cage settles, your shirt is wet through, and the air has the weight of a held breath: forty-one degrees, close to saturated, smelling of warm iron, ammonia, and wet limestone.

The chamber opens below you like the inside of a bell. It is perhaps four hundred paces across and lies well under the water table of the country above. The town exists because of that fact. It has been fighting the water for sixty years and is not winning, but it is not losing quickly either.

## The Rock

The walls are raw. This is the first thing to understand about Harrow Deep: it is *new*. The chamber was a flooded void until the pumps drew it down, and the rock still looks freshly exposed. The limestone is pale gray-yellow and scalloped by old currents, with sharp edges where the water only recently left. Nothing has had time to weather it. Flowstone is just beginning, in thin translucent skins the color of candle wax over the scallops, and in soda-straw stalactites no longer than a finger, each holding a bead of water at its tip.

Everywhere the rock weeps. Seams in the ceiling and walls bead and run in slow threads, and the floor near the walls is a ribbed pavement of wet stone, slick with a film of pale mineral mud. The town's drainage is mostly a matter of collecting these threads before they join up.

The floor is uneven and terraced by hand into broad shelves stepping down toward the center, where the **sump** lies: a long black pool, always a little steaming, fed from below by water that comes up warmer than the air. Its level is the truth of the place. Everyone knows it to the centimeter. A brass gauge is bolted to a pier, and children are taught to read it before they are taught letters.

## The Town

The buildings are scattered on the terraces, with wide bare stone between them. Nothing crowds. There are perhaps forty structures, but each is dense in itself, built up in layers: a stone base course, a riveted iron story above, then corrugated tin patches, then a later wooden addition. Everything is bolted, sealed, or painted against rust. The paint is the same oxide red on almost every surface, because it is cheap and it holds, so the town reads as a scatter of red-brown boxes on pale stone.

Streets are raised gangways of grated iron, running above the wet floor on short piers. They ring underfoot. Pipes run alongside them, lagged in rags and tar: steam mains, drainage, compressed air. In places the pipes cross over the walkway on arches, and you duck under them, and they radiate heat and drip.

The principal works:

- **The Pump House**, a brick hall on the lowest terrace, holding three triple-expansion steam engines. Their flywheels are taller than a person. Two run at any time, one rests. They breathe with a slow **thud–hiss, thud–hiss** at about the rate of a sleeper's pulse. Without this sound the town would be silent, and the residents say they would not be able to sleep. If it stopped, they would wake.
- **The Boiler Row**, four squat riveted drums fed by coal hauled down the shaft, whose chimneys go up through a bored flue to the surface. The chimneys give the chamber its only steady updraft, and a faint pull of air along the ceiling that sways the hanging lamp-chains.
- **The Guano Sheds**, long low buildings at the north wall where the harvest is dried, sacked, and hoisted. This is the reason for the town.
- **The Lamp Works**, a small generator hall with a dynamo driven off the pump shaft. It powers perhaps two hundred bulbs in the whole chamber, strung on sagging cables. Each casts a dim amber pool and none reaches another. Between the pools the town is dusk. The ceiling is entirely dark.

## The Animals

This is what the place is. The town is a small thing living inside a very large animal population.

**Bats** are the headline, and they are everywhere. Roughly two million roost in the domed ceiling and in the high crevices, so densely that the roof seems to be a moving surface, a brown fur that rearranges itself in slow ripples. The sound of them is constant: a dry papery rustle, high chittering, and, under it, a clicking too fast to follow. The bats make the ceiling a sound source, and it never stops. Twice a day, at dusk and dawn by the surface clock, they leave and return by the old collapse-shaft on the east side. The exodus takes forty minutes and is a slow pouring, a dark spiral rising through a well of gray light. The town stops work and watches, every time. The fall of returning bats at dawn makes a noise like sustained applause.

Below them, the guano has piled in cones on the chamber floor, some of them six meters high, and these are the second ecosystem:

- **Cockroaches**, several species, brown and glossy and thumb-length, in numbers that make the guano piles seem to shimmer. On bad days the pale stone walls of the sheds are coated to shoulder height. They are ignored, mostly, the way weather is ignored. Children race them.
- **Beetles**, small and carnivorous, that eat fly larvae in the guano and are tolerated because of it.
- **Crickets and camel-crickets**, pale, long-legged, springing in thousands from the wet rock. Their song is a thin, layered ticking beneath the bat noise.
- **Centipedes**, long and reddish, hunting the cockroaches along the gangways. Their bites are known and respected. Boots are shaken out each morning.
- **Geckos and small pale frogs**, on the pipes and around the seeps, where moisture and insects both gather. The frogs' throats pulse in the amber lamplight. In the wet season, their call joins the rest.
- **Swiftlets**, small birds that nest on the wall in glued cups of saliva, and echolocate by clicking. They add a rattling to the air.
- **Blind cave fish and pale shrimp** in the sump, thousands of them, drifting at the edge of the warm water. Cats fish for them from the pier. The cats are numerous, half-feral, and thoroughly at home; they are the town's actual pest control and the only creatures that don't seem hot.
- **Rats**, large, who share the sheds with the cats in an old, unresolved war.

All this life produces an atmosphere you can taste: a musk of ammonia and warm fur, of sour mud and hot metal. Newcomers get headaches for the first week. Workers wear wet cloth over their faces in the sheds, and everyone's eyes are red-rimmed.

Nothing here is strange. It is simply too much. Every surface is claimed, and where a person is not standing, something else is.

## Vegetation

Very little grows, because there is almost no light. What does grow lives in the pools of lamp-glow or near the vents:

- **Green felt** of algae and moss on the stone under each bulb, sharply bounded, so that every lamp has a circle of green beneath it, like a lawn the size of a tablecloth.
- **Ferns**, a few, planted deliberately in tin tubs near the Lamp Works and treated as valuable. A fern is a gift.
- **Pale fungi** on the guano, in gray shelves and clusters, and a slick white bacterial skin on the wet walls near the seeps.
- **Roots**: thin dangling threads of tree roots, pushed down from the surface through cracks, hanging from the ceiling like hairs. They drip. Some have reached the sump.

## Sound

The chamber is loud in layers. From below to above:

1. **The pumps**, thudding, hissing, and shaking the gangways.
2. **Steam and water**: the whistle of relief valves, the clank of pipes as they expand, the constant trickling of a hundred seeps and the plink of drips into buckets.
3. **Human noise**: hammering, hoist winches, boots on iron, shouting, because in the pump hall you must shout. A brass bell for shift changes.
4. **The animals**: the dry bat-chatter overhead, the ticking of crickets, the frogs, the rat scrabbling.

The dome holds all of it and returns it altered, so the town is always in a low, thick roar. There are no echoes as such, only a diffuse reverberation. Conversations in the open are conducted close, with a hand cupped. The only quiet in Harrow Deep is on the far southern terrace, where the ceiling drops, the bats are few, and the pumps are muffled by rock. People go there to talk privately, and to cry.

## Light

Dim, amber, low. Lamps hang in pools. The furnace doors glow a stained orange when opened. The sump is black and reflects the lamps as long broken smears. Each morning and evening, a dim gray wash falls through the east shaft, and for the forty minutes of the exodus the whole chamber shows as a shape: the great curve of the roof, the pale scalloped walls, the red town, all of it briefly *seen*, and then dark again.

## Rules and Rhythms

Change here is slow, and everyone lives by long cycles.

- **The level.** The sump rises slowly in the wet season, when surface rain finds the cracks, and drops in the dry. A rise of two centimeters in a week is a matter for meetings. A rise of ten is an emergency. The pumps' third engine is kept warm for it.
- **The harvest.** Guano is dug in rotation, sector by sector, so that each cone has years to regrow. It is a patient economy: the bats make it, the town collects it, and the surface buys it for fertilizer.
- **The stone.** The town is quietly shaped by the rock's youth. Slow trickles have carved small new channels, and a wall that was smooth when the founders arrived now has a ribbed pattern of miniature gullies. Every few years a slab sloughs from the ceiling with a sound like a dropped anvil. Nobody has died from it yet. There is a wall in the meeting house with the dates of the falls.
- **Heat.** Work is in short shifts. Everyone drinks salted water from cisterns. People sleep in the afternoon, in hammocks slung near the vents where the air moves. Clothing is thin and constantly damp. Children are pale, quick, and unbothered.

## A Moment

A woman on the eastern gangway stops to wipe her forehead, the pumps thudding through her boots. A cockroach walks over her shoe and she doesn't look. Above, the whole roof is stirring toward evening. A bead of water swells at the tip of a stalactite the length of her finger, the only thing in the chamber that seems to take its time, and the bead falls, and the sump takes it, and the black water, a fraction of a millimeter higher than it was, holds a smear of amber light.

Somewhere behind her, the bell rings for the shift change, and the bats begin to go up.
