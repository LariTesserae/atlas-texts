# The Understory of Harrow Flat

From above there is nothing to see. The Flat runs to every horizon: gray-tan hardpan, worn so smooth that the last hills are only a slight swelling you notice when the light rakes across them. Whatever mountains stood here were ground down to this crust so long ago that no one remembers them. The sky is a pale, even overcast of dry cloud that has not rained in geological memory. The air is cool and tastes of chalk and cold iron.

In the middle of this nothing is a slot in the ground, about the width of a doorway and long as a barn, crusted along its lips with fused slag. It is the only opening into the Understory.

## The Descent

The slot drops through a meter of fused clinker, then through a ceiling of ribbed metal so corroded it has become a kind of stone, brown-red and layered like bark. Beyond that the space opens. The whole pocket is perhaps sixty meters across and only eight or nine high, a shallow bowl under a low roof. Nothing about it is large, but everything in it is crowded.

The light is ordinary daylight, thinned, coming down through the slot and through a few hundred pinholes where the roof has rusted through. It falls in bright coins and blades across the floor and moves slowly as the clouds outside move. Between the beams the air is amber-dim, and the dust hanging in it glows.

## The Floor

There is no soil. The floor is a **drift of ore-dust and shed shell**, ankle-deep in places and waist-deep in others. Iron-red, graphite-black, and a pale green-gray from copper salts all lie in bands, sorted by the constant motion of the animals. Under the drift is a plate of black slag glass, and under that the Flat's ancient bedrock, so worn it has no features at all.

Nothing here has ever been wet. The chemistry of the place runs on a **thick amber oil** that seeps from cracks along the northern wall and pools in shallow, skinned basins. It is the only liquid, and everything living has learned to want it. The oil smells of warm resin and struck matches. Around each pool the dust has been trampled into a hard, glossy apron.

## The Engine

At the center stands a machine.

It is a **beam engine**, or was one: a walking-beam and flywheel assembly on a masonry-and-iron plinth, three stories of it compressed under a ceiling that seems to have been built down around it. The beam rocks slowly, a long dark arm tilting on its pivot with a groan you feel in your teeth. The flywheel turns, with a sound like a heavy door dragged over gravel. It is driven by no steam. Someone long ago converted it to run on the seep oil, burned in a bank of squat cylinders that still cough and flare blue-white every few seconds. Every stroke gives a **thud-hiss-clank** that is the metronome of the whole place.

Nobody tends it. The animals do.

## The Fauna

The Understory is overwhelming with life. Every surface moves. Standing still, you first notice the floor shifting, then the walls, then the beams of light, thick with things crossing them.

**Rustmites** are the base of it all. They are the size of lentils, with shells of hammered iron oxide that they secrete and continually renew, so that a mass of them sounds like rain on a tin roof. They number in the billions. They graze the corroding walls and ceiling and excrete a fine ore-dust that is the actual substance of the floor. They move in braided streams that cross and re-cross each other without collision.

**Bellows-moths** are foil-winged, palm-sized, and pale silver. They hang in clouds in the light shafts and rise and fall together. Their wings are so thin and stiff that flight makes a shimmering, papery ring. They feed on oil vapor and carry it between pools, and they cling to the engine's cylinders in dense, trembling curtains, warming themselves in its exhaust.

**Stokers** are the engine-keepers: squat, wide, and many-legged, like a cross between a beetle and a coal-scuttle. They are the size of small dogs. Columns of them haul loads of dry, oil-soaked rustmite husks up the plinth to the fuel hoppers and tip them in. In return they take their food from the warmth and residue around the cylinders. Their carapaces are scorched black and worn shiny by generations of contact with the hot iron. If a stoker dies at its post, the others pack the body into the fuel chute, and the flare that follows is a slightly different color.

**Sextons** are the predators. They are long, low, and glass-bodied, with translucent shells you can see the oil moving through. They lie buried in the dust with only their antennae exposed, then erupt and take rustmites by the hundred, or snap a bellows-moth from a light shaft with a whip of the tail. They are patient, and the floor is marked with their crater-traps, neat conical pits with a faint glitter at the bottom.

Above the sextons, at the edge of the light, hang the **wardens**: thin, ash-colored things with many hinged limbs, who wait in the roof-ribs and drop on sextons that have grown too fat. Their bodies rain down into the dust and are eaten by the mites, so that everything eventually returns to iron and dust.

## Sound

The Understory is **loud**. It is a wall of layered noise, each layer with its own rhythm:

- the engine's slow thud and groan,
- the rain-on-tin hiss of rustmites, constant and everywhere,
- the papery shimmer of moths,
- the tick-tick-tick of stoker claws on iron rungs,
- the crack and sizzle of oil flaring in the cylinders,
- and, underneath, a low tone that no single animal makes. It is the sum of everything, and it rises and falls with the beam. The creatures have long since tuned their calls to the engine, and they now sing in its intervals as if it were a great slow instrument.

Visitors who stay here find their pulse drifting toward the beam's tempo. Some find it unbearable and some cannot leave.

## The Cycles

The Understory is never still. It runs on three overlapping rhythms.

**The stroke** is a few seconds long: the beam, the flare, the pulse of moths toward and away from the heat.

**The shift** lasts about an hour. The oil seeps faster or slower with the daylight's warmth, and the whole population reorganizes around the pools. The rustmite streams turn and pour into new channels. Sextons rebury themselves in new places. The floor's patterns are redrawn, and by the end of an hour the dust looks like a different country.

**The Draw** comes rarely, after long dry cold on the Flat above. The slot in the ceiling begins to pull air, and the pocket breathes in. A wind runs through the Understory, lifting the dust into red-and-black storms that spin in the low space. The bellows-moths ride them in great glittering spirals, and the stokers clamp onto the plinth and wait. The engine's sound changes to a hard whistle as the draft feeds the cylinders. Then the air stills, the dust settles in new bands, and everything resumes as though nothing had happened.

## Other Remains

The engine is not the only artifact. Scattered through the drift are the **partial remains of a small works**: a fallen gantry curled like a dead fern, a row of iron barrels rusted into a single lump, a stair leading nowhere, a control panel whose dials are packed solid with mite shell. On one wall, under a translucent crust of oxide, are faint painted numerals, an inventory or shift schedule, unreadable now. The animals crawl over all of it, wearing it down as they always have, taking in a little more each year.

## The Rule of the Place

The Understory is a **closed accounting**. The engine burns oil, the stokers feed it, the mites eat the walls, the sextons eat the mites, the wardens eat the sextons, and everything falls back to dust that the mites rework. Only the sunlight and the seep come from outside. As the roof thins, the light strengthens and the ecology speeds up. Eventually the roof will fail, and the engine and its keepers will be exposed to the level sky of the Flat and ground down like everything else that once stood on it.

Until then the beam rocks, the flywheel turns, and the swarm pours across the floor in the amber light, loud and dense and alive in a hollow the size of a village square, under a plain that has forgotten there was ever anything to erode.
