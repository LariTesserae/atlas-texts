# The Drowned Bench

The basin is about four hundred paces across, and everything in it is underwater. You are not swimming through open sea. You are hanging in a clear, cold room the shape of a stepped bowl, with a ceiling of light that wobbles very slightly and never breaks.

## The water

It is the clearest water you have known, and the reason is the reason nothing lives here. Iron and sulfur leached out of the rock for a century before the pumps stopped, and the water settled into a mild, stable acidity that kills small things before they can begin. Without plankton or silt-feeding life there is nothing to cloud it. Visibility runs to sixty paces in every direction, tinted a pale green-blue that deepens toward the bottom into something like bottle glass.

It is cool, the temperature of a cellar. There are no currents. Nothing stirs the water from above or below, and a drifting thread of rust-colored floc, released from your own movement, will hang where it was left for what feels like hours.

## The walls and benches

The basin was cut in steps, and the steps are still there, rounded by long submersion into something between stairs and geology. Each bench is about the height of three people and wide enough to walk a cart along. Their edges have slumped into soft lips. Their faces are banded in ochre, rust, bone-white and a dull violet-gray, and each band is a different stratum of the ore body that once mattered enormously to someone.

A skin of settled mineral covers everything: a fine orange-brown felt that has grown thick on the upward-facing surfaces and stays thin on the vertical ones. It has been building for so long that it has softened every corner. Where it thickens over a ledge it forms small scalloped shelves like frozen cloth. Touch it and a puff lifts, hangs, and slowly resettles in a nearly identical shape.

Along the north wall, drill holes run in neat rows, each one the width of a wrist, some still lined with the pale stub of an old explosive charge sleeve gone to crystal. They are the clearest mark of intent left in the stone.

## The floor

The deepest point is roughly thirty paces down, though "down" is gentle. The floor is a broad, nearly flat plain of fine sediment, the color of wet cement. It is undisturbed, and it holds the mineral-hardened impressions of what fell into it, since nothing has ever crawled across it to erase them: a scatter of larger stones, a long smooth groove where something heavy was once dragged and abandoned, and pale rings where old seeps of groundwater rise and lay down white crusts.

Those seeps are the only motion in the place, and they are almost too slow to count. A bead of clearer, colder water rises from a pinhole in the silt, spreads into a thin lens, and flattens out. The lens seems to look back at you like a lidded eye. Then it is gone into the general stillness.

## The plants

Vegetation is sparse and specific. Stonewort, a stiff, jointed, brittle-looking alga that tolerates the chemistry, grows in low green-gray tufts along the shallower benches, each tuft a hand's width across and spaced far apart like plantings in a monastery garden. Near the surface, where the light is strongest, a thin lawn of hair-fine filamentous algae furs the sunniest ledges. It sways not at all. It is olive on top and rust below, and in the right light it gleams faintly, as though brushed with oil.

On the deep floor nothing grows, only a few isolated rosettes of pale moss clinging to the tops of boulders, the highest and cleanest surfaces, as if the plants had learned to avoid the sediment.

There are no fish, no snails, no larvae, no crawling things, not even the small insects that colonize nearly every fresh water. You can look for an hour and find that the only shadows belong to stone and metal.

## What is left of the machines

The parameters of this place say industry, and industry is here, but it has been reduced to landform. Almost nothing survives intact. What remains has become as much a part of the basin as the benches.

**The winch house frame.** On the third bench sits a skeleton of iron beams, a cube-shaped cage about the size of a small room, softened under the orange felt so its edges look poured rather than bolted. Inside it stands a great toothed drum, a cable spool that once hauled loads up the incline. A few turns of the cable are still wrapped on it, fused into a single rope-shaped mass of rust.

**The rails.** Two narrow-gauge lines descend the eastern ramp, running straight into the deep and continuing along the floor for about twenty paces before ending at the lip of a slump. The ties are gone, dissolved, so the rails float in their own alignment like parallel drawn lines held in place by ridges of sediment. Along their tops grow the neatest lines of stonewort in the basin.

**The pump housing.** At the very lowest corner, a squat cylindrical casing lies on its side, half-sunk. One end is open like a mouth. Its interior is filled with a soft, dense growth of mineral, pleated and layered like the inside of a shell. This was the machine that once kept the pit dry. Its failure is the origin of everything here, and it lies there with the calm of a fallen tree.

**Scattered iron.** Smaller pieces lie about: a wheel with spokes turned to rods of coral-like crust, a chain looped into a slow coil, a bucket whose bottom is gone and whose rim has become the rim of a small vase filled with pale moss. None of it is arranged. It simply landed where it did and has since become partly stone.

## Sound

It is mostly quiet. What you hear is not silence but its texture: the faint pressure-hiss in your own ears, a slow tick as iron cools or contracts somewhere unseen, and, once in a great while, a low, muffled knock like a distant door as a slab of over-thickened crust lets go of a wall and settles a hand's width lower. The knock carries a long way and dies without echo. Between these there are minutes of pure hush, so complete that your heartbeat becomes the loudest event in the basin.

## Light

From above, daylight enters normally, a bright, even, slightly greenish wash. On the shallow benches it throws slow ripple-nets across the rust felt. Deeper, the light thins to a soft twilight that keeps enough color to show the bands in the rock. At the bottom you can see the surface as a distant, tilted silver sheet, and the edges of the basin as a ring of dark stairs climbing toward it.

## The rules of the place

Everything here is finished. The chemistry is balanced, the sediment has settled to its angle, and the crusts have grown as thick as they can. Nothing is being added and almost nothing is being removed. A footprint, a dropped tool, the outline of your hand pressed to a wall would endure for a thousand years. Time is not stopped, but it has nothing to do.

You can move through it slowly, marking the way the machines have turned into bedrock and the bedrock into a kind of memory, and know that when you leave, the water will close over the disturbance, and by the time your last thread of rust has resettled, the basin will look as if no one had come.
