# The Chimes of Karrow Cut

The valley is only a few generations old. A fault slipped here in someone's grandmother's time and dropped a strip of ground twenty meters deep and a quarter mile wide. The walls were left raw: dark basalt in ranks of leaning columns, some still bright where they broke, the fracture faces showing a fine glassy grain. Frost and roots have only begun on them. Below the rim the walls are furred with moss, but the cliff edges are still crisp enough to cut a glove. Nothing here has had time to round off.

The floor is low, barely above the level of the sea, which can be smelled on the east wind. It is flat and green, and the green is dense: sedge meadows, alder carr, tree ferns in the wet pockets (planted, then escaped), and thick cushions of moss on every ledge. The air is cool and always a little damp. Water gathers rather than falls. It beads on the rock, condenses on iron, and drips from the cliff lips in threads that the wind sometimes carries sideways. A light rain comes most afternoons, lasting twenty minutes, and the sun returns afterward at full brightness, so the whole cut steams and glitters.

## The Works

The fault opened a hot spring. Water at sixty degrees rises through a crack at the valley's northern end, and the people who came built around it. This is a settlement of about forty structures across a mile of floor, with long empty stretches between them where the meadow takes over.

- **The Boiler House** stands over the spring itself, a tall brick shed with a riveted iron dome. The spring's steam is captured there and sent down the valley through lagged pipes on iron trestles. The pipes run above the sedge like a long, sagging spine, wrapped in rope and tar-cloth and furred with green algae at the joints.
- **Three mills** are strung along the pipe, each far from the next. One presses rapeseed oil, one cards and felts wool, and one stamps tin sheet into lids and pails. Each is a low brick hall with a tall stack it doesn't really need, since the steam arrives ready-made. The stacks were built anyway, and they are occupied.
- **Housing** is a scatter of tall narrow terraces, four or five houses to a row, set apart from one another as if each row had been dropped there separately. Between them are allotments, hen runs, and drying greens. Washing lines cross the gaps.
- **The Tram** is a single narrow-gauge line that runs the length of the valley on a raised bed. Its small steam-fed car has no fire. It is charged at the Boiler House and coasts down the line, wheezing, then is recharged at each mill.

## The Sound

The place is loud, and the noise has layers.

Under everything is the steam pipe. It ticks as it heats and cools, and it sings low when the flow is strong. In the mills the stamping press hammers a heavy, slightly syncopated beat that comes up through the ground. The felting drums thump wetly. The oil press groans. Valves blow off in bursts and echo down the basalt walls, returning a half-second late and doubled.

Then the animals, which are worse and better. Frogs fill the sedge at dusk in such numbers that the meadow seems to vibrate. Starlings, jackdaws and swifts nest in the dead stacks and cliff crevices, and gulls come up from the coast to argue on the roofs. Warm rock and rich mud have made the cut an oasis. Voles and shrews swarm through the moss, herons stalk the drainage cuts, and small deer come down at night and stand unbothered by the noise. Bats pour from the Boiler House eaves.

## The Unusual Thing

The spring pulses. The cycle is slow, about eleven days from swell to ebb, and no one has explained it. At full flow the steam pipes shriek, the mills run fast, and the frogs go quiet. Mist fills the valley to the rim, and the cliff faces stream with condensation. At ebb the pipes cool, the mills idle, and the valley sounds like a marsh with a few factories asleep in it. Then the frogs come back in their thousands, and for a couple of days the loudest thing in Karrow Cut is amphibian.

The residents plan around this. The cycle sets market days, laundry days, and the timing of births, if you believe the older women. They call the peak "the Chime," because at the top of the pulse, when pressure is greatest, the pipes ring in a slow chord, three or four notes that shift across the valley as the flow changes. The starlings imitate it. Nobody can say which came first, the birds or the pipes.

## Materials and Textures

- **Basalt:** black-blue, cold, wet-slick. Fresh fractures show pale conchoidal scars.
- **Brick:** soft red, blackened at the mortar, greened at the base.
- **Iron:** riveted and blistered with orange rust, which the moss climbs.
- **Moss and fern:** cushions thick enough to sit in, a foot deep on the north walls.
- **Mud:** warm near the spring, cold everywhere else. It is mineral-rich and faintly sulfurous, with a rusty red skin where hot water seeps into the meadow.

## Slow Changes

Almost nothing here moves quickly, apart from the pulse. The cliffs shed a slab now and then, and the sound of it is remembered for months. The moss advances a hand's width each year. Alders take back the abandoned allotments one plot at a time. The pipes are patched, repainted, and patched again. The tram runs slower each decade and is loved more for it.

Walk the valley from the spring to the coast end at ebb tide of the Chime, in the low light after rain. The pipes tick above you. Frogs stop as you approach and begin again behind you. Ahead, over the last mill, one stack breathes a thin plume of white, and starlings pour out of it in a black thread. The basalt stands over all of it, still sharp, and in no hurry to wear down.
