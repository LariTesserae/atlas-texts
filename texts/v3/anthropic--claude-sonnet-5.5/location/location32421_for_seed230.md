# The Saddle of Weeping Stairs

The town sits in a saddle between two rounded summits, at an altitude where the air is thin enough to make a climber's breath audible to himself. It fills a hollow perhaps four hundred paces across. You can see all of it from the pass, and it is never fully occupied by anything, whether building, person, or plant. The buildings stand apart from one another with bare ground between them. It is a city drawn at low density, a plan with the breathing room left in.

## The ground

The bedrock is a pale grey-brown stone, fine-grained and slightly greasy to the eye, that splits along flat planes. This decided everything. The builders never needed to cut much. They pried slabs from the saddle's own shoulders and stacked them dry, without mortar, so that each wall shows the long horizontal lines of its stone. The walls lean inward a little, and a hand's width of shadow lies under each course.

The surface has been settled a very long time. Every edge has been rounded by weather until the town looks poured rather than built. Stair treads are dished in the middle like shallow bowls. Door lintels have sagged into gentle arcs. The corners of houses have gone soft as loaves. Nothing is crumbling, though. The erosion has reached the point where wind and water have little left to take. Each stone has found its shape and holds it, and the whole place has the look of an argument that ended centuries ago with both sides satisfied.

## The layout

Three stair-streets climb from the low eastern edge of the hollow to a flat terrace at the western rim. They are wide, shallow flights, each step deep enough to take three paces. Between them lie the houses, single-storey and squat, roofed with overlapping slabs and chinked with clay. Some have a second room built onto a first, and some have a third built onto that. These layers are visible as changes in stone color and in the tightness of the fit. The oldest work is the largest and best-fitted, the middle work is looser, and the newest is a scatter of small lean-to cells that must also be centuries old.

Between the houses is the empty ground, and it is the most important thing in the town. Cobbled courts sit unused. Drainage channels run beside nothing in particular. Low walls enclose square plots of bare gravel where nothing has grown for as long as anyone could say. The eye crosses twenty or thirty paces of open stone before finding the next doorway. The town seems to have been built for a much larger population, or for one that keeps a great distance from itself.

At the top of the western terrace stands the largest structure, a windowless hall with walls twice a person's height and a roof of huge single slabs. Its door is a low rectangle, and the lintel stone is worn nearly round. Inside is dry, cold, and dim. The floor is beaten clay, and a stone bench runs around the whole perimeter. It contains nothing else. Whatever it was for, it has left no mark except the polish on the bench, where generations of sitters have smoothed the stone to the sheen of old bone.

## Water

The air here is damp in a particular, patient way. It rarely rains, perhaps a dozen soft grey showers in a year, but cloud passes through the saddle often enough that everything is beaded with moisture in the morning. The town has arranged itself around this.

On the windward side, on the north rim, stand rows of upright **weeping stones**, tall slabs set on edge with their faces angled to the drift of mist. The fog condenses on them and runs down grooves cut into the surface. The grooves lead to gutters, the gutters to channels, and the channels, following gentle gradients laid out with great care, to a chain of stone cisterns spaced through the town. Each cistern is a square pit lined with clay and roofed against sun and dust. The water inside is black, still, and very cold. It smells of wet slate.

The channels are the town's veins, and you can hear them: a slow, irregular dripping that is the main sound of the place. Each channel has its own tempo. One ticks like a clock. Another gives a low continuous trickle. One on the west side lets a single drop fall every few breaths into a cistern with a hollow bottom, and the note it makes is round and clear, like a finger tapped on the rim of a jar.

The water never overflows and the cisterns never empty. The level in each cistern stands at the same mark on the same stone, wet-dark below and pale above, and the line has not shifted in living memory.

## Plants

The vegetation is sparse and low, and nearly all of it clings to water. The channels are lined with a thin bright-green moss, kept in a narrow band exactly as wide as the seepage allows. Lichens spread over the walls in disks of orange, mustard, and pale sage, some as large as a shield, and their edges are so still they seem painted. In the cracks between paving stones grow tight green cushions the size of a fist, hard as wood, studded with tiny white flowers for a few weeks of the brief summer.

There are a few trees. Six or seven dwarf junipers grow in the shelter of the largest walls, each knotted and bent flat by wind, none taller than a person's waist, their trunks thick as thighs. Someone tends them. The dead branches are cut away and the deadwood stacked, and the ground around each is kept clear of gravel. They are the only things in the hollow that look cared for rather than merely kept.

Outside the town wall the slopes are tussock and scree, yellow-grey in the cold season and a little greener in the short warm one, with dark cushions of moss campion clustered in the hollows.

## Materials and craft

The people, when they are present, work with what the saddle gives them. They have copper and a little bronze, worked into awls, needles, small blades, and the ferrules on staffs. Their pottery is fired in low, smoky kilns at the bottom of the hollow, brown and burnished, unglazed, with incised bands. The rope is braided from a tough grass or animal hair. There is no wheel, only coils built up by hand and beaten smooth. Tools hang by doorways on pegs of horn and bone. Each house has a quern, a flat stone dished by the grinding of some barley-like grain. The little fields are terraced on the eastern slope, a few plots as tiny as courtyards, and they are the only cultivated ground.

There are few people. On an ordinary day you might see four or five: a figure carrying a clay jar up a stair, someone crouched at a cistern with a bronze cup, a child sitting on a wall and staring. They wear undyed wool, thick and grey-brown, and move slowly, saving breath. They are not wary of a stranger, but they are not curious either. They nod, and the nod costs them nothing, and they go on.

## Animals

Fauna is rare. There are small brown pikas or their kin, round-eared and quick, that live in the tumbled stone at the edge of the town and give a single thin whistle, then vanish. A few sheep or goats, shaggy and dark, are kept on the slopes and brought down at night to a stone pen; their bells, small dull clay ones, clack in the still air. Now and then a large pale raptor drifts along the ridge without flapping, a long shadow crossing the roofs, gone before anyone looks up. Insects are few and cold-slow, mostly small dark beetles that walk on the sun-warmed lintels at midday.

## Light and air

The light is clear and normal, though thin and cold. Days are bright with a pale hard sun, and the shadows under the leaning walls are crisp, deep blue. The stone takes the light and gives it back unchanged, so the town is bright in the day and dull in the evening. Morning brings a few hours of low cloud, which lifts. By midday the sky is high, empty, and pale. The air smells of stone, cold water, juniper smoke, and, faintly, of animals.

The temperature stays cool through the seasons, cold in the shade and merely mild in the sun. Breath shows at dawn and dusk. The wind is steady and light, and when it climbs the slope it makes a low note across the mouths of empty storage jars set along the terrace walls, a soft, hollow, vowel-like sound.

## Sound

Mostly it is quiet. Water drips. A goat bell clacks. A quern turns somewhere with a slow grinding hush. The wind drones faintly across the jar mouths. Footsteps on stone are soft, because the treads are so worn. Voices, when they come, are low and carry a long way in the thin air, so you can hear a conversation two courts away without being able to make out a word. Then it stops and the drip returns.

## The rule of the place

Nothing here changes, and this is the thing the visitor feels before understanding it. The moss keeps its band, the lichens their disks, the water its mark. The houses are neither added to nor abandoned. The people's number holds, births and deaths balancing so evenly that no one speaks of it. Even the light seems to return the same each day to the same stones. The town is neither growing nor dying, and it is not waiting. It has settled into the shape the mountain, the mist, and the hands that stacked its stones agreed on, and it has kept that shape the way a river keeps its bed.

When you leave, climbing the eastern stair toward the pass, you may look back. The town lies in its hollow, pale and low, with the cisterns like dark coins between the roofs. Then you turn, and the wind takes up the note in the jars behind you.
