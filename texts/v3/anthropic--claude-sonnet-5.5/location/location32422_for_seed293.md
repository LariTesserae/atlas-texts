# The Cistern Fen

The fen fills a shallow bowl about two kilometers across, and the bowl is the city's footprint. From the rim you see no buildings. You see a green so total that it reads as one surface: moss, sedge, floating fern, and the crowns of a few hundred trees standing far apart, each with a lot of empty green between it and the next. Warm air stands over it and smells of tannin, rot, and something sweet and metallic, like a struck coin held in the mouth.

## The mat

Most of the ground isn't ground. It is a floating raft of living material, two to four meters thick, knitted from sphagnum, sedge roots, and the pale cables of a creeping vine that seems to have no other purpose than tying things together. It quakes underfoot. Where you step, water wells up around your boots, warm as a bath and the color of strong tea. In places the mat thins to a skin and you can see down through it into the water, which is clear all the way to the bottom.

Down there are the streets. They are eleven meters below the surface, worn so smooth they look poured, with every joint and curb rounded off. The buildings have eroded to knuckles and low humps furred with drowned moss. The hard, glassy ceramic they were made of has been ground into pebbles the size of eggs, and the pebbles line every channel like a mosaic laid by a patient child. Nothing here has sharp edges anymore. The city has been wearing away for longer than the peat beneath it has been accumulating, and the peat is deep.

## Root-halls

The trees stand at the old intersections. They are something like cypress, with knobbed knees and buttresses, and they are enormous and far apart. The one thing that makes them strange is what they did to the buildings. Their roots wrapped the old structures so completely that when the stone finally dissolved, the roots kept the shape. Now there are hollow cages of wood, each a hall, arcade, or stair, still holding the plan of a building that no longer exists. You can walk into one. Inside, the air is cooler and the noise of the fen drops away, because the woven walls swallow sound. Light comes through in thousands of coins. Everything about the room is correct, and it has no stone in it.

## The Governors

Seven columns stand in the basin, spaced widely, and they are the only things here that have not eroded. Each is about the height of a house and the width of a well, made of a matte gray-white substance that is faintly warm to the touch and hums at the edge of hearing. No moss grows on them. Around each is a bare ring of clean, hard, dry pavement, a few paces wide, and the vegetation stops at its edge as though at a wall. Nothing has ever explained why this ring stays dry in a place where everything else drips.

The Governors move the water.

## The Pulse

Every few minutes, never on the same interval twice, a Governor *calls*. The hum drops to a note you feel in your sternum. Then the water under the mat responds, and a swell rolls out across the basin. The mat lifts a meter or more in a slow wave and passes overhead like something breathing. Trees flex on their buttresses. The reeds have hinged stems and lie flat and spring up again behind the wave. Trapped marsh gas breaks loose in long stuttering belches, and pockets of it ignite on nothing, small blue flames that walk a few paces and die.

Then the strange part. At the crest, water climbs the outside of the column. It rises in a thin sheet up the smooth surface, defying everything, hangs at the top in a trembling crown, and falls as warm rain from a cloudless sky. It rains on the ring and a short distance beyond, for about a minute. The rain always smells of clean stone.

The seven Governors do not agree with each other. Their waves cross and interfere, and where two crests meet, the mat tears with a sound like wet canvas. Channels open, and by the next day the raft has sealed itself over with new growth, though not in the same shape. The map of the fen is good for about a week. It is a place in permanent, patient upheaval, ancient and settled beneath and always rearranging on top.

## Sound

The fen is loud. Frogs of at least a dozen voices fill the air, and when a swell passes they go silent for a heartbeat and then scream all at once. Insects make a rising drone. The mat creaks like a ship. The pulse gives a deep note, then bubbles bursting, then the hiss of the artificial rain, then a settling. Somewhere beyond the trees a heron-like bird makes a sound like someone tearing a sheet in half, over and over.

## Fauna

Life is abundant, though spread thin across the space, so you meet it in pockets around a tree or a warm pool.

- **Counting snails** climb the reed stems in unison a few seconds before each pulse. Their shells spiral in tight ratios, and the older shells appear to encode the interval, which suggests they know something about the Governors that nobody else does.
- **Mat-walkers** are long-legged birds with broad, splayed feet, and they stride across the quaking surface without sinking. They nest in the root-halls.
- **Sextons** are grazers, broad and slow, something like a manatee crossed with a tapir. They live beneath the mat and browse its underside. You know one is passing when a low hump moves across the surface, dragging the vines with it. When the pulse comes they rise and breathe through holes they keep open for the purpose.
- **Lantern fish** live in pockets of open water inside the mat and glow faintly pink. Some of them have been in their pockets so long that the pockets have been sealed for generations.

## The living roof

Vines have colonized everything, including the dead machines: what may once have been service housings, gantries, dishes the size of ponds, all now covered so completely in vegetation that they are indistinguishable from the hillocks they sit on. The only clue is a regularity in the way a mound rises, or a faint metal hum from underneath, or a place where the moss grows in a perfect hexagonal pattern.

## What holds it together

The rule of the fen is that it does not stay still and it does not fall apart. The tech underneath keeps the water moving, and the movement keeps the mat alive. The mat feeds the trees, and the trees hold the shape of the city. The Governors cannot be moved and cannot be damaged, and nothing has ever needed to be done to them. They make the place breathe at intervals that no one has been able to predict, and everything living here has learned to hold its breath and let go with them.

Late in the afternoon the light turns copper and lies flat across the water. Then a Governor calls, the whole green plain lifts, and rain falls out of the clear sky onto a column that has outlasted its makers, the language they spoke, and the streets under your feet.
