# The Lees

Begin with the flatness, because everything else is a consequence of it.

The Lees is a plain so old that erosion finished its work here and then, having nothing left to do, kept working. There are no hills. The last of them were ground down before the continents took their present shapes, and what remains is a floor of pale, fine-grained stone sanded so smooth it has the sheen of the inside of a shell. Over this floor lies a film of warm water, never deeper than an ankle in most places and never entirely absent. It stands in sheets a hundred kilometers across. Where the floor dips by a hand's breadth, the water pools into black lakes that are still, in the geological sense, the same puddle.

The water is the temperature of a body at rest. Wading in it feels like the boundary between yourself and the world is being negotiated.

## The Lid

The sky is a low, bruised ceiling of cloud, and the light under it is dim and greenish, as if filtered through pond water. The sun is a rumor, a brighter patch that migrates and sometimes appears in two places. Much of the light comes from below. The film of water carries a faint cold luminescence from suspended microbes, so every footstep leaves a bloom of pale blue-green that fades over a slow count of twenty. The plain glows dimly from beneath, and the cloud glows dimly from within, where lightning forks sideways for hours without ever touching down.

The air is saturated in the plainest sense. It is warm, thick, and beaded, so that breathing feels like drinking. Every surface weeps. Every surface is also a surface, since there is nothing else, and so the whole plain sweats up into the sky and the sky rains it back down in a cycle so tight and so violent that it seems to be a single continuous event.

## The Packing

Nothing here is empty. This is the hardest thing to convey. The Lees is not crowded with creatures, since fauna is rare, and not crowded with plants, which are sparse. It is crowded with events. Every cubic meter holds something happening.

Rain falls in strata. At head height it comes down as fine warm drizzle. Above that is a layer of fat slow drops, each the size of a thumb, falling so gently they seem to hesitate. Above that is a hammering, horizontal, silver rain that has been going sideways for longer than anyone has been alive to note it. Below your knees, water rises from the floor as mist and beads and small upward-jumping droplets that climb back toward the clouds. These layers do not mix. They slide across one another with visible seams, like plates of glass, and where you cross from one to the next your skin registers the change in a single shudder.

Stack this with fog banks that hold their shape like dropped cloth, with veils of spores, with standing waves of pressure you can feel in the teeth, and with ghost-weather. Ghost-weather is rain that fell here long ago and is somehow still falling, a sheet of drops with no cloud above it, hanging over one square of ground and striking the water in a rhythm that belongs to another century. You may step under it and be dry, or be soaked by something that is not happening now.

## The Sound

It is deafening, and the deafness is not merely loudness. It is total. The sound of every raindrop striking every square meter of the film, all at once, out to a horizon that curves, does not resolve into noise. It resolves into a pitch. The whole plain is a drum, and the drum has a note, a low surging roar with harmonics that shift as the weather layers slide, and you live inside that note the way a fish lives inside water.

Under it are the thunder, which is continuous and unlocated, and a high fine hiss like a sheet of paper torn without end. Sometimes, from nowhere, a single sound arrives that is out of order: a crack that comes before the flash that caused it, a footstep you have not yet taken, your own voice returned from a direction you did not shout toward. The roar is so complete that speech is a physical impossibility. Anyone who lives here learns to communicate by pressure, touch, and the flat of a hand laid on a shoulder.

The sound does structural work. Vibration passes through the shallow water and organizes it. Across the film run patterns like the figures sand makes on a struck plate: concentric rings, lattices, slow spirals, hexagonal cells. Ripples stand still while everything around them runs. These figures are the nearest thing the Lees has to terrain. They form, hold for a minute or a day, and then reshuffle when the note changes. Because they are the only features in a flat world, travelers navigate by them, and the maps drawn from them are always wrong by the time the ink is dry.

## What Grows

Vegetation is sparse but not absent. It is the leftover kind, the kind that survives anything.

Across the shallows lie mats of algae, slick and olive and brown-black, in places thick enough to walk on as a quivering skin. From the mats rise tussocks of pale reed, grey-white and hollow, that whistle when the wind passes through them. The whistles are lost in the roar, but you can see the reeds tremble to their own unheard chords. Here and there stand the stumps of something that was once a tree, or once a great fungus. They are petrified to the color of wet slate and worn round as knuckles, with rings of tiny orange shelf-growths crawling their sides.

Rarest and strangest are the stilt-gardens. In a few places a stand of pale, leafless stems, each as thin as wire, rises out of the water in tight ranks. There are thousands of them, spaced with terrible regularity, and each carries at its tip a single bead of clear liquid that never falls. The beads catch the underlight. The stands look like fields of lit pins. No one knows if they are alive.

## What Moves

Fauna is rare, and nothing is common enough to be called a herd. The wading things are the most visible: tall, slow, grey, with too many joints in their legs, stepping through the shallows with the deliberation of surveyors. They do not flee and do not approach. They feed on the algae mats by laying their long faces flat against the film and drawing in the glow, so that for a moment their heads are lit from beneath like lanterns.

In the water itself are pale eyeless things the length of a finger, which drift in the current of the sound, aligning themselves along the standing-wave figures. They gather in the nodes where the water is stillest. When the note changes, they all slide together to the new nodes, like iron filings following a magnet.

Sometimes a bird-shape crosses the cloud-lid very high up, and you cannot tell whether it is a bird, a torn sheet of spray, or a memory of one.

## What Was Built

There are marks of hands here, but very few, and they are old past reckoning. Scattered across the plain stand small cairns of flat stone, each piece worn nearly circular, stacked five or seven high. No two are within sight of each other. In some places there are low ring-walls, knee-high, enclosing nothing but more water. Once in a very long walk you may come upon a single hut: a roofless room of stacked slabs with a doorway facing no consistent direction. Inside there is a stone bench worn into a shallow saddle, and the water within stands perfectly still, because the walls, thick as they are, somehow shelter it from the note.

Nobody knows who made these. They were not made by technology, only by patience, by hands placing stone on stone. Whoever made them may have been the last people to try to give the plain a landmark, and the plain has been quietly erasing them ever since. Every cairn leans a little further each year. Each will one day sink, settle, and be level.

## The Rules

The Lees follows a few laws you learn by breaking them.

**Everything settles.** Anything set down will, given time, be drawn a little lower, as if the plain were slowly swallowing whatever tries to stand. A pack left on the water is found sunk to its straps by morning. A cairn stone dropped in the shallows will be flush with the floor within a season. The plain does not take things, it only levels them.

**Distance is not reliable.** A cairn on the horizon can be reached in an hour or in two days. Sometimes you walk toward it and it recedes, and sometimes you pass through it and see it, unchanged, still ahead of you. The weather layers seem to stretch and compress space between them, and there are places where walking ten steps carries you a hundred meters and places where a hundred steps carries you nowhere.

**Time leaks.** Ghost-rain is the visible sign of this, but there are subtler ones. A puddle may show a sky that is not overhead. A ripple may spread inward toward its center instead of outward. Your own footprints ahead of you, glowing faintly in the water, may be the ones you have not yet made. Travelers who stay long learn not to trust the order of things and learn to notice how often it holds.

**The note has moods.** When it drops, the whole plain darkens and the upward mist thickens, and the eyeless things rush to new nodes. When it rises, the clouds crack with light and the sideways rain intensifies into a white wall. Between the moods are stretches of eerie near-balance, when every layer slows, every drop hangs, and for a few breaths the roar has the quality of silence, as if the plain were holding its breath. Then it lets go.

## The Turbulence

Nothing here is stable except the flatness, and the flatness is the residue of every instability that came before. What you are walking on is the end state of a billion years of change, and the change has not stopped. It has just run out of things to move. So the water moves, and the air, and the sound, and the strange thin membrane of time. The Lees is what happens when erosion, having finished, turns on the concept of shape itself and begins wearing that down.

Stand still long enough, with a hand on the shoulder of someone who has walked here longer than you, and you may feel it: the whole plain shivering under the roar, vast and level and endlessly busy, a place where every last thing has been reduced to the same height so that it can all go on happening at once.
