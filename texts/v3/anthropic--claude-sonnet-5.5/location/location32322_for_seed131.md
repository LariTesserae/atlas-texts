# The Turbine Room Under the Hill

You reach it by a stair that has been worn into a trough, each step dished so deeply that the whole flight has become a single gutter of green. There is a door, or the place where one was. The steel has rusted and been rained on and rusted again until it is a rounded, orange-black lip in the doorway, smooth as a riverbed stone. Then the sound arrives, and it is the first true fact of the place.

## The Sound

It is not noise so much as a medium. A single turbine turns in the center of the chamber, and it has turned for longer than the stair has been eroding. Its note is somewhere below a roar and above a groan, and it is joined by the water in the penstock, the sheeting of the tailrace, and the hum of the generator windings answering through the walls. All of it merges into one pressure. It shakes the fine hairs of the ferns. It stipples the surface of every puddle into standing rings, fixed in place like frost on glass. Your sternum hums. You cannot hear your own voice, so speech is given up at the threshold, and the few visitors who have ever come have learned to talk with their hands, or not at all.

The sound never rises or falls. After an hour it stops feeling like sound and starts feeling like architecture.

## The Room

The chamber is a low vault, perhaps forty paces across. It is buried in a hillside so old that the hill has sunk nearly level with the marsh around it. Everything in it was made: the floor of poured stone, the ribbed ceiling, the gantries, the pipe runs, the cable trays. Nothing is natural except what has moved in.

Age has rounded every made thing. The concrete has weathered like limestone, its corners gone, its surfaces pitted and softly channeled. The iron has bloomed into thick scaled crusts, then smoothed under centuries of seep into forms like sea-worn driftwood. Bolt heads are shallow blisters. Handrails have thickened into pale, furred bones. The turbine housing is a great domed shape, a boulder that happens to hum, with a seam of brass still bright along one flange where the water keeps it scoured.

Nothing here is ruined, because ruin implies change. The place has worn down to its final form and settled there. The rust has finished rusting. The stone has finished dissolving to the point where the seep and the stone reach a truce.

## Water

The water is everywhere and never in excess. It weeps from the ceiling ribs in beaded lines. It sheets down the walls in films too thin to see except where they catch the light. Along the north wall it collects in a shallow channel, ankle-deep and warm as bathwater, which runs in a slow braid across the floor to a grated sump. The air is thick and close, at the temperature of a held breath. Every surface is beaded with condensation, and the vault holds a permanent faint fog that thickens toward the ceiling and thins to clarity at eye level.

The drips fall from the same points they have always fallen from. Below each one, the stone has been cupped into a small basin, and each basin holds a fixed depth of water. Where mineral-laden drips might have grown stalactites, deposition and dissolution balance to the grain, so the ceiling hangs with short, blunt, permanent nubs that have not lengthened in a thousand years.

## Green

The vegetation is lush to the point of upholstery. Moss coats the gantries in deep cushions and turns handrails into green limbs. Ferns grow from every crack in the floor, some waist-high, their fronds trembling in the vibration of the sound, a constant fine shiver across the whole crowd of them. Liverworts plate the wet walls in overlapping scales. Pale-stemmed sedges stand in the warm channel. A hanging curtain of some trailing vine drapes from a cable tray and sways in a rhythm that is not wind but bass.

Because the sound and the water are constant, and the light is constant, the plants have long since stopped competing toward any new arrangement. Each ledge has its own species, each crack its own fern. The whole garden is a settled mosaic that hasn't shifted in memory.

## Light

Daylight enters through a round oculus in the ceiling, a shaft a body wide, lined with old ceramic tile gone the color of tea. It falls as a soft, ordinary, white-gold column onto the turbine dome and lights the mist inside it so that the shaft is visible as a solid. Along the walls, a row of caged filament lamps still burns, dim and amber, powered by the turbine itself. Between the two, the light is entirely normal, like a greenhouse on an overcast afternoon, with no drama and no dark corners of any importance.

## Life

Very little of it moves. In the warm channel, a single long eel-like fish patrols the same twenty paces of water, unhurried, deaf to nothing because deafness is all there is. Snails with tall spiral shells rest on the wet stone, one every few paces. Small green frogs sit on the ferns, throats pulsing silently, their calls lost entirely. Once in a long while a heron-like bird stands in the light shaft. It may have come through the oculus; it may live on the roof. It doesn't fly when you enter, because it doesn't notice.

## Rules of the Place

The turbine will not stop. There is no lever that stops it, and if there was, the lever has fused into the housing like a knuckle into a fist.

The room is empty of people. There are a few artifacts: a wheeled cart worn into a low mound, a bench of stone that was once a workbench, a board of dials whose faces have clouded but whose needles still stand, trembling, at the same readings.

Nothing enters or leaves except water, light, and the sound, which leaks up through the hill and out across the marsh as a faint, steady vibration in the ground. Standing at the doorway, you feel it under your boots, and you understand that the marsh is the room's outer skin, and that the whole wet green country above has been humming, without noticing, for as long as anything has lived there.
