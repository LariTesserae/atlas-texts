# The Lathe of Halvane Shelf

## Approach

Nothing here is sharp. The mountains that once stood on this shelf have been worn down over so many hundreds of millions of years that they are now only a high country of enormous, rounded shoulders. Quartzite domes the size of cathedral hills sit shoulder to shoulder, and each is furred in moss deep enough to lose an arm in. The whole shelf is lifted so far into the air that the sky above it is a darker blue than it should be. You never see that sky from inside, because the shelf lives under its own cloud.

The cloud is a lid, not weather. It presses down at about the height of a six-story building above the valley floors and rises and falls on a slow tide. In the *low hours* it drops to the rooftops and the domes stand out of it like the backs of grazing animals. In the *high hours* it lifts and the whole land goes dim and pearl-coloured, with the light coming from everywhere and nowhere and nothing casting a shadow.

## The Ground

The bedrock is pale, rain-polished, and rounded like river cobbles left out to become hills. Between the domes run *sinks*, steep-sided bowls and hollows where a thousand feet of silt and root-mat have collected. Peat, ash-coloured sand, and the black compost of a forest that has been dying and regrowing on itself for longer than any species has a name for it fill them. The ground here is springy and cold, and it exhales. Push a pole into a sink and you release a breath of warm, iron-smelling air. The old people say the stone is still slowly cooling and this is the last of its heat.

Water is present but never generous. It comes as fog-drip, as seepage from the moss, and as a fine rain that arrives twice a day and takes twenty minutes to soak through your coat. A few brown streams thread between the domes, wandering because they have no slope worth the name. They pool in black tarns rimmed with sedge. At the lowest hours you can hear the seep-water ticking inside the rock.

## The Forest

Lushness here is a matter of layers, not height. The trees are stunted and twisted: dwarf rhododendron, knuckled junipers, silver-barked things like birch that lean with the wind. Every trunk and branch is thickly wrapped in moss, lichen, filmy fern, and orchids the size of thumbnails. Everything is host to something else. A branch is a garden, the garden has a smaller garden on it, and that garden has a fungus in it that glows faintly green in the dim.

Tree ferns crowd the sinks. Beneath them the understory is so dense that paths are cut as trenches, walled by leaves at shoulder height and roofed over with vine. Walking them feels like walking down the inside of a green throat.

The plants move, though only slowly. If you sit a day beside a young fern you can watch it turn its fronds to follow the cloud-tide, unfurling as the cloud lifts and curling as it drops. Botanists call this *tidal tropism* and argue about it. The locals say the plants are *listening for the fog*.

## The City

There is one city, **Halvane**, and it does not sit on the domes. It fills the sinks and climbs the seams between them, one neighbourhood to a hollow, linked by cable-bridges, tramways, and long flights of stone stairs worn to smooth troughs. From above it looks like a handful of dark coins dropped between green loaves of bread.

It is built dense. Houses of grey slate and black-tarred timber stack seven and eight storeys in narrow ribs, their roofs planted with the same moss as the hills so that from a distance the city half dissolves. Walkways cross between upper floors at every level. Laundry, glass jars of fermenting greens, and lamps hang across the alleys. Where a house ends and the forest begins can't always be said. Vines have been trained across its face, and ferns have taken root in its gutters.

The streets run in switchbacks along the walls of the sinks. Water does not stay in them long. Each has a gutter down its centre, and in the gutters small pale salamanders with red gills live and are not disturbed. They're considered lucky and keep the drains clear of insect larvae.

## The Industry

Halvane runs on the cloud. This is not a metaphor.

Along the ridge-lines stand the **weir-towers**, tall lattices of iron and wire mesh, stretched with fine steel threads and hung with cloth. Fog drifts through them and condenses and runs down into troughs, and the troughs feed pipes, and the pipes carry it into the city for boilers, drinking, washing, and the mills. Where the cloud is thick the weir-towers drip like the ribs of something drowned.

Steam is the city's power, but its fuel is peat and the dried root-mat cut from the deep sinks and pressed into black bricks. The **cutters** work in teams with long-handled irons, slicing turf in perfect rectangles and stacking it to cure under tarpaulins. Behind them the cut sinks slowly seal and re-green over about forty years, and the cutters keep a rotation map that is one of the oldest documents in the city.

The factories are low brick sheds and tall-chimneyed mills tucked into the hollows, where the fog can hold the smoke down. They spin **moss-silk**, a fine strong thread drawn from a pale, hair-like moss cultivated on racks in the upper valleys. The thread is woven into cloth that sheds water and keeps in heat. It is the main export, carried down the mountain on cable-lifts that ride out over the cloud and vanish into it.

Machines here are large, but they are muffled. Beams, pistons, and flywheels sit in stone pits packed with moss and wool, so their labour arrives as a low thudding through the ground, felt in the soles of your feet before it's heard. A whole factory street can run at full shift and sound like a sleeping animal.

## Light

The light is dim, even at noon. Everything is lit from above, evenly, by the diffusing cloud, and colours come out muted and deep: the green of the moss goes almost black, the slate goes blue, and the brass of the pipes glows softly. At night the city relies on gas and oil lamps in amber glass, and the glowing fungus. Lamplighters make their rounds twice a day, once at dusk and once when the cloud drops and the day goes dark early.

Sometimes at the turn of the tide, when the cloud lifts sharply, a single shaft of true sun comes through a gap and lands on one dome. For a few minutes that dome is white-gold and everything else is grey. People stop what they are doing and look.

## Sound

It is mostly quiet. What you hear: fog-drip on leaves, a slow *tock* of water in gutters, the soft grind of the peat-carts, and the deep pulse of the mills under your feet. Voices are low, because sound dies in the moss. Bells are used for signalling, but they are muffled with felt and give only a dull *dunk*. Occasionally, from somewhere out on the domes, comes a long hollow tone like someone blowing across a bottle. It's the wind passing through a fissure in the stone. It is a different pitch on each dome, and the residents can tell where the wind is by the note.

## Fauna

Animals are rare and mostly small. There are the salamanders in the gutters, pale slow moths at the lamps, and mist-colored wrens that nest in chimneys for the warmth. A few herds of short, thickset, long-haired goats graze the domes' upper slopes. Herders visit them but do not own them.

The animal people talk about is the **heron**. It is grey, tall, and slow, and stands motionless on the roofs of the mills through the whole fog-tide, one leg raised, looking down into the streets. Nobody feeds them. Nobody knows what they eat. They arrive in the low hours and are gone by the time the cloud lifts, and it is considered very bad luck to count them.

## Strangeness

Three things are known to be true here and are not explained.

First, **the tide is not weather**. It keeps time more precisely than any clock in Halvane. The cloud rises and falls on a cycle of just over nineteen hours, regardless of season or wind, and the city's day is built around it. Clocks are set by the cloud, and if a clock disagrees with the cloud, the clock is wrong.

Second, **the stone remembers heat**. On certain domes, in certain hours, the rock is warm to the touch as though something inside had just left. People sleep on these domes in autumn. Snow melts from them first.

Third, **the moss grows in lines**. Under the largest domes, if you strip back the turf, you find the rock scored with shallow parallel grooves, older than the city and older than the moss, running downslope in fans and spirals. The moss follows them perfectly. Nobody has settled whether the grooves were made by ice, by water, or by something else. Children are told they are the fingerprints of the mountain, and the children quietly believe it.

## Motion

Nothing here is still for long. The cloud rises and falls. The rain comes twice a day. Ferns unfurl and curl. Peat is cut, the sinks seal, the moss climbs the walls of new houses, and old houses sink slowly into their own gardens. Cable-cars go up into the cloud and come down out of it. Even the rock breathes.

Ancient, worn, patient, the mountain has finished being a mountain, and what lives on it now is everything that has found a way to hold on to a surface that no longer resists it.
