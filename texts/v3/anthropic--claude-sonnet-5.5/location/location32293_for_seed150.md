# The Cistern Town of Ouel Cirque

The place sits in a bowl of raw rock high in the mountains. The glacier that cut the bowl pulled back only a few centuries ago, and the walls still have the look of something recently unwrapped. The granite is pale and unweathered, with edges that would cut a palm. Scree lies at its steepest angle against the cliffs, and in places the floor is still bare polished stone with striations running one way, like combed hair. Everything that grows in the bowl grew there after the ice left.

It is a small place, perhaps four hundred paces across. In that space, plants have made a town.

## The Grain of the Town

Nothing was placed here, and nothing was carved. But the plants have arranged themselves so fully that walking in feels like entering a settlement with alleys, thresholds, courtyards and districts.

The main builder is a lignified cushion plant that grows in domes. The oldest domes are taller than a person, dense and rust-green, and so tightly packed that you can stand on one and barely dent it. Their stems are sheathed in the dead leaves of earlier seasons, and the dead layer is part of the wall. Domes have grown against one another and fused into terraced masses. Where two masses lean together, the gap between them is a **street**: a slot of dark peaty ground a shoulder wide, floored with wet needles and running with a thin thread of meltwater.

The streets branch, cross, and dead-end into small round **courts** where a dome has died in the middle and left a hollow. Some hollows hold black pools, no bigger than a table, so still they mirror the underside of the fog. Over the courts, the arms of tree-heathers and dwarf willows have knit into low roofs. These are hardly trees, just gnarled trunks as thick as a thigh, leaning and interlacing at head height. Moss hangs from them in long blond curtains. The result is a covered, tunneled city where the sky shows only in slits.

Between the domes, taller rosette plants stand like posts or chimneys. Each has a trunk of shaggy dead leaf-skirts topped by a silver-felted crown as wide as a wagon wheel. They are set at intervals along the wider avenues, and they behave like civic markers. The streets bend around them. Their fur is beaded with condensation.

## Water

Water is everywhere but never abundant. Nothing floods. It seeps, weeps, drips and beads. The cliff walls above the town are slick with slow films. Where seams in the rock let water out, it runs down in sheets no thicker than a fingernail, lit dull silver in the dim light. The first plants catch these sheets. Below each seam, a vertical **stair-garden** of liverworts and saxifrages hangs in green-black tiers like laundry.

Under the town, the ground is a sponge of peat, and the water table sits a hand's depth down. Step off the packed dome-tops and the ground closes over your ankle with a cold suck. Meltwater threads run through the streets as gutters, and the plants have organized themselves around them. Every street has a channel, and every channel ends in a court pool. The pools overflow through one another, downhill, in a staircase of tiny cisterns. At the lowest edge of the town they drain through a gap in the moraine wall into a stream that goes on down the mountain unseen.

## Cold, and the Warm Rooms

The air is barely above freezing at midday and well below it at night. Frost furs every surface before dawn. Each moss filament wears a sleeve of ice, and the felted crowns of the rosettes turn to white starbursts.

But the town holds pockets of relative warmth. The great rosettes are faintly **thermogenic**: at the heart of each crown, a knuckle of dense tissue burns stored sugar slowly, especially through the night. Standing near one, you feel a weak, damp exhalation on your face, perhaps four or five degrees above the air. Snow never settles on them. Around the base of each, a ring of small delicate plants, ferns, tiny orchids and pale-flowered gentians, grows where nothing else at this altitude could. The town's finest gardens surround its stoves.

In winter the whole cirque fills with snow, and the town becomes a set of hollows under white. The rosettes keep chimneys open through the drifts, and blue light comes down these shafts.

## Light

The light is dim and green-gray, and the cliffs cut off much of the sky. Fog comes up from the valley most mornings and sits in the bowl as a lid, so everything is lit from a soft overcast with no direction to it. Under the willow roofs it is dimmer still, an aquarium dusk. Moss and lichens seem to make their own faint brightness: acid yellow on the rock, wet emerald on the cushions, and rust where dead leaf-sheaths have turned. Around noon, when the fog thins, a single shaft may slide across the bowl for a few minutes. Every droplet in the town lights at once, and then the shaft is gone.

## Rhythm

The town is quietly active and follows a daily cycle.

**Before dawn**, everything is frozen and stiff. The streets crackle underfoot, and the peat surface is skinned with needle-ice that lifts small pebbles on stalks.

**Morning** brings the thaw. As the first light warms the cliffs, the ice lets go and the whole town begins to drip. The sound builds from a few ticks into a pattering that fills every alley. Cushion domes swell as they take up water, and their surfaces darken. Fog rises off the pools in threads.

**Midday** is the wettest phase, and the fog lid sometimes thins. Small landslips of wet scree tick down the cliffs. A gutter gets choked with debris, floods a street, and then some other channel takes over. The streets do move, slowly. A dome that dies opens a gap, its neighbors lean in, and within a decade an alley is closed and another has appeared. The town rebuilds itself on a timescale you can almost see. In the fresh ground at the margin, where the ice has left new rock, pioneer mosses and crust lichens are laying the first course of some future district.

**Evening** brings the cold back down. The domes exhale and shrink, the drips slow and stop one by one, and the fog drops into the courts and lies there.

## Sound

Mostly quiet. The dominant sound is water: dripping, trickling, and now and then a soft **plink** as a drop hits a pool from the willow roofs. The wind stays above, over the rim, where it makes a distant tearing sound. Inside the town the air is nearly still, and the cushions and moss soak up sound so thoroughly that footsteps land dull and close. A voice would go no farther than an arm's length. At night, ice creaks in the rock walls, and now and then a stone lets go far up and clicks its way down through the dark.

## Fauna

Animals are rare, and the town is not built for them. It is too dense to move through quickly. Still, some things live in it:

- A **vole-like rodent**, gray and short-tailed, uses the streets as runs. You see it as a flicker at the mouth of an alley, and you find its droppings in tiny neat heaps on the dome-tops.
- **Springtails and pale beetles** graze the moss curtains. In the morning thaw they come out by the thousands, a fine moving grit over the wet.
- A single species of **small brown bird** with a pale bill nests in the rosette crowns, where the warmth lets its eggs keep through the frost. It says almost nothing, only a thin, single, upward note now and then from somewhere in the roofs.
- In the pools, **red-bodied fairy shrimp** hatch in the thaw, a few dozen to a court, and drift in the black water like sparks.

## What Is Strange

The strangeness is mild, but once you notice it you cannot stop noticing it. The town is too **orderly**. Streets meet at nearly regular angles. Courts recur at nearly even spacing, and there is one great rosette to every court, as if measured. The rosettes' warmth seems to set the layout, since every cushion lot and every alley position relates to the reach of some stove. It is a city organized by heat and water rather than by anyone's intention, an accident of chemistry that reads as a plan.

Some visitors say the pattern is slowly moving uphill, that the town is walking toward the cold ridges, one dome at a time, following the retreating ice. The oldest districts, at the bottom near the moraine gap, are already thinning and beginning to fall in on themselves. The newest are just moss on bare stone, barely a street's width beyond the ice line.
