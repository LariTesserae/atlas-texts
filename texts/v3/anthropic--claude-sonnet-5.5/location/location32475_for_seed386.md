# The Steamed Shelf of Achuwara

At this altitude the air should be thin and cold. Here it is neither. The plateau sits nearly five thousand meters above the lowland basin, and the ground under it is so warm that the mountain seems to have a fever. The air is thick, wet, and heavy, and it feels lived-in. Breathing it is like breathing through a warm cloth.

## The Shape of It

The Shelf is a broad tilted tableland, a day's walk across in its narrow direction and several days along its length. It is bounded on one side by a wall of dark sandstone that drops away into cloud, and on the other by a ridge of older, blunter peaks. Nothing here is sharp. The rock has been rained on for so long that every edge has been rubbed into a shoulder or a knuckle. Boulders the size of houses sit rounded and furred with moss, and the cliffs wear long vertical stains of black and rust-orange where mineral water has run down them for ages.

The surface is a slow sequence of terraces stepping down from the ridge, each holding a shallow basin. Some basins are ponds, some are sedge-choked marsh, and some are only quaking mats of moss that give underfoot like a mattress and let up a little warm water around your ankles. Water is never more than a hand's depth below the surface anywhere. Dig a hole and it fills, slowly, with tea-colored water that is warm as a bath.

## The Warmth

The heat comes from two sources. One is the thick, humid air, which holds the day's sun the way a wet blanket holds warmth. The other is below. The Shelf sits over a shallow, old, mostly sleepy volcanic system, and the heat leaks up through the stone in a hundred small ways. Rock faces are warm to the palm. Springs come out at body temperature, or a little hotter, or scalding, and there is no pattern to which. Steam lifts off the ground in the mornings in thin, wandering threads, and in the wetter months whole terraces disappear under it.

Daytime air runs somewhere near thirty-five degrees. Night barely cools it. The dew at dawn is warm enough that when it collects on your sleeves it feels like sweat that isn't yours.

## The Green

It is difficult to overstate how much is growing. Tree ferns stand in dense stands, their trunks wrapped in a felt of liverworts and filmy ferns, and their fronds arch overhead to form a low, dripping ceiling. Under them are bromeliads as big as washtubs, each holding a private pond in its leaf-cups, and in those ponds live tadpoles, water beetles, and small pale crustaceans that never touch the ground. Orchids hang from everything. Vines cross from trunk to trunk, so that in places you cannot see more than three meters in any direction, and the light comes down green and grainy.

The trees are stunted by the altitude and wind on the exposed ridges but huge in the sheltered hollows, where old podocarps and giant heaths crowd together with trunks four people could not reach around. Everything is upholstered. Moss is the ground, the walls, the fallen logs, and the undersides of leaves. Where sunlight breaks through onto a pool, the pool is coated with a scum of algae so bright it looks enameled.

Some of the pitcher plants are the size of a person's torso. They grow in the nutrient-poor marsh mats, each with a lid like a lifted eyebrow, and they hum faintly with the flies crawling in and out.

## What Moves

Fauna is abundant but mostly small and mostly hidden. Frogs are everywhere, in a dozen colors, from lichen-green to a saturated orange the size of a thumbnail. They are the only real chorus, and even they are subdued. In the daytime there is a low, intermittent tocking, a bell being tapped from far off, and at dusk it thickens to a soft, rolling murmur. Otherwise the plateau is mostly quiet. What you hear is water: dripping from leaves, trickling down rock, the occasional gulp of a spring, the pop of a bubble in a mud pot.

Birds move through the canopy in loose mixed flocks: nectar-eaters with curved bills, tanager-like things in slate and yellow, a large dark fruit pigeon that flaps with a sound like a wet sheet being shaken. Mammals are rare and elusive. There is a small, long-snouted marsupial that forages in the moss mats at night, and a species of otter-like animal, slow and heavy, that lives along the warmer streams and eats snails.

Predators are mostly ambushers. Big green vipers with heat-sensing pits wait draped in bromeliads. Spiders build orbs across every gap between trees, and by midmorning each web is beaded with water like a chandelier. A slow, tree-dwelling cat, dappled and heavy-shouldered, is seen perhaps once a season, and mostly its presence is inferred from the way the frogs go silent in a widening ring.

## The Engines

Here the place turns strange, in a quiet way. The Shelf runs on rhythm. Its geothermal plumbing works like a system of pistons and valves, and over time the vegetation and animals have organized around it as around a factory schedule.

Along the base of the ridge is a line of small geysers, none taller than a person, that fire in an unmistakable order, one after another, north to south, over about forty minutes, then rest. Each cycle sends a wave of hot mist rolling across the terraces. The frogs go still as it passes and resume when it clears. Small swifts arrive to snatch the insects flushed by the steam. Certain ferns unroll and curl on the same cycle, as if they were keeping time. Locals of the lowland, who come up rarely, call it "the shift change."

There are places where mineral springs run over long stone lips and lay down travertine in smooth, stepped ribs, a slow production line of white and salmon-colored terraces that thicken by a fingernail's width every year. Scummy microbial mats coat them in bands of orange, green, and brown, each band a different working layer. Iron-rich seeps stain everything below them the color of old rust, and in the seeps there are bacteria that make rainbow films on the water, tearing into fragments when a leaf falls in.

There are pressure vents in the stone that breathe. A dull, wet huff of warm gas comes from a crack, and the surrounding moss ripples. Beetles have made themselves homes at these vents, and their larvae feed on the fungal mat that thrives in the exhalation. The vents chuff a few times a minute, slow, patient, mechanical. It is the closest thing to a machine sound on the Shelf, and at night in the fog it is easy to imagine a great low engine running under the hill.

## A Single Thing Left Behind

Near the top of the third terrace, at the edge of a boggy pool, a length of iron pipe emerges from the base of a tree. It is a bit thicker than a forearm, rusted to the color of the surrounding seeps, and it enters the trunk on one side and leaves it on the other. The tree grew around it. Nobody knows who put it there or what it was for, and nothing else like it has been found anywhere on the plateau. It is warm to the touch, warmer than the surrounding wood, because it passes over a fissure that carries heat. In the early morning a thin thread of steam leaks from its open end. It has become a part of the ecosystem: moss on its upper side, a small orange frog living in its bore, a wasp nest tucked at the join. It is the only made thing on the Shelf, and it has been absorbed almost completely.

## Weather and Time

Weather here has a daily shape. The mornings start clear, with the sun burning through the steam and the rock faces glowing. By late morning, warm air from the lowland basin rises up the escarpment and condenses into cloud, and by noon the whole Shelf is inside it, cool-looking, gray-white, and dense. The cloud sits for hours, dripping. Around three or four in the afternoon it breaks into rain, hard, warm, and straight down, drumming on a million leaves at once. This is the loudest the Shelf gets. The rain lasts an hour or two, then thins, and the evening light comes low and gold through the last of the mist, and the frogs start up.

Then the night settles. It is warm, wet, and thick with the smell of decay and flowers, and the sky above the cloud layer, on the rare nights it clears, shows stars closer and brighter than seem possible from a place so swollen with life.

## The Long View

Over ten thousand years or more the Shelf has settled into a balance. Rock has softened, springs have found their courses, and the terraces have filled in with peat and moss to a steady depth. Trees fall in the storms and are covered by moss within a season, and from their rotting trunks new ferns rise in neat rows. The place is neither growing nor dying. It is running: warm, wet, patient, ticking in its own time, with the geysers keeping their schedule and the frogs keeping theirs.

If you sit still in the middle of a terrace at dusk, with the steam rising around you and the last drops falling from the fronds, you can feel the whole plateau working, slowly and without hurry, like a large, well-maintained machine that has forgotten anyone built it.
