# The Ringed Cistern

You come to it across flat wet ground where nothing stands higher than your knee. The marsh here is a plain of standing sheen, and the first sign of the place is a sound: a broad, layered roar of frogs, swifts, dripping and ringing, coming up out of the earth as if the ground itself were talking. Then the land simply stops. A circular hole opens, about sixty paces across and perhaps twelve deep, and all of it is made.

## The Shape

It is a bowl of stacked stone, descending in nine rings. Each ring is a terrace, and each terrace is a walkway wide enough for two people, faced with fitted limestone blocks laid without mortar. Some blocks are as big as a sleeping dog. Their seams were cut with stone mauls and bronze wedges, and you can still see the chisel-bites where the cutters worked. The masons could not make a perfectly true joint, so they made a tight one, packing the gaps with beaten clay and slivers of shale.

Long ago that clay slumped, wept, and was replaced by mineral. Now every seam is sealed with a pale flowstone crust the color of old candle wax. Water has done what mortar could not. This is the erosion of a thing that has finished changing: steps are dished to the shape of ten thousand feet that are no longer there, corners are rounded like river pebbles, and the channel lips are polished. Wear and deposition have reached a truce. What the water dissolves in one place it lays down a little farther along, so the whole structure holds a slowly renewed shape, neither growing nor decaying.

There is no natural ground anywhere in the bowl. The floor is a pavement of set cobbles, the terraces are laid, and the ramps are built. Even the soil in the planting troughs was carried in and packed there.

## The Water

Water is always arriving. It weeps from the joints in long dark stripes and gathers in gutters no wider than a hand. These are cut into the terrace edges and run in careful, slightly downhill spirals, ring to ring. Fired-clay pipes, brick-orange and crazed with green, take the overflow through the walls and let it fall in thin ropes into the next level. The air is cold, near the temperature of a cellar, and so damp that your sleeves darken within a few minutes. Condensation gathers on the underside of the roof and drips back, so it rains a little in here even when it does not rain outside.

The lowest ring holds a still black pool, knee-deep at its shallowest. Its level breathes. After rain it climbs the wall, drowning one terrace and then another. In dry spells it draws back down the stones. The old markers are still there: notches cut at each ring, some with bronze pegs gone soft and green. The pool has settled on the fourth notch, and the people who kept count are gone.

## The Roof and the Light

The bowl is only half covered. Over the eastern side, huge slabs are corbelled inward from the rim, each course jutting a little past the one below, until they nearly close. They leave a ragged, oval eye of sky. The western side is open, and a lattice of black, waterlogged timbers spans it, with crossbeams pinned by wooden dowels. Nothing is stretched across them now except a few threadbare mats of reed and the ghosts of old hide-lashings.

The light that gets in is dim and grey-green, filtered through cloud and then through the lattice. It falls in pale shafts that you can see only because of the drifting mist. Below the fourth ring the walls sink into dusk, and by the bottom it is gloom lit only by the pale sheen of the water. At the right hour a low sun comes through the eye and lays a single copper bar across the far wall for a few minutes. The hundreds of glazed niches there flare like a scattering of coals, and the whole hall seems to draw breath.

## The Jars

The walls are pocked with niches. There are thousands, set in staggered rows, and in each sits a clay jar mouth-out, some the size of a fist and some as big as a bucket. They are unglazed, hand-coiled, and fired in an open pit, so they are uneven and smoke-blackened. The wall was built around them. Now they are half sealed in by flowstone, each rimmed with a soft white lip.

They are tuned. Whoever made them knew that a jar of a given size answers a given pitch. Water dripping across the mouths sets them humming, one after another, each a different note. The whole wall is a huge, slow instrument played by leaks. It is never silent and never repeats. The drips fall in patterns too complex to follow, and the jars answer with a soft, hollow, bell-and-bottle chorus.

Small things have moved into them. Tree frogs sit in the smaller jars with their throats pulsing, and they have learned that their calls swell when they sing at the jar's own note. Bats fill the higher rows. Pale, blind cave-loaches gather in the jars near the waterline, packed like seed in a pod. Swifts and swallows plaster nests of mud and spit around the mouths of the middle rows, so that from a distance the wall looks like a honeycomb built by several different minds.

## The Sound

The place is loud, and not merely with noise. The sound has structure and weight. Frog choruses rise and fall in waves, and the swifts' shrill whistling cuts through them. Under everything runs the low, breathy moan of the big jars near the floor, like wind across a bottle the size of a barrel.

The strange part is that the hall sometimes resonates. When rain is heavy and many drips arrive together, the jars begin to fall into step. A pitch builds. The frogs catch it and push it, then the swifts, until the whole bowl is holding a single fat chord, the way a struck bell holds its note. You feel it in your teeth and the bones of your face. The water in the pool trembles into fine standing ripples, a lattice of concentric rings. Then, all at once, every bird in the wall lifts off. A cloud of swifts and bats boils up through the open eye and wheels overhead, and the chord breaks apart into a great confused ringing that takes minutes to die.

Then it begins again, quietly, with one drip on one jar.

## The Life

Apart from the animals, almost nothing lives here, and the density of the place is oddly low. The central volume of the bowl is empty, a huge cold room of air, mist and falling drops, and everything that lives clings to its edges. Vegetation is sparse: green-black moss in the weep lines, and ferns in the wider joints, thin as hair. In the shallows of the lowest pool there is a single reed bed, kept inside a stone-lined trough. It looks planted, and perhaps it has been replanted over and over by the reeds themselves. Their roots pin the pool floor and their stems shelter the frog spawn.

Predators live here too. Long, pale water-snakes patrol the gutters and search the jars. A heron, thin as a folded umbrella, stands in the shallows in the dimness, and it lunges at the loaches with a sound like a slap. Owls roost in the highest niches. They drop without sound into the wall of noise, and a chirp cuts off. Nothing is quiet here for long, but in this place the quiet is a form of death, and the animals know it.

## What Remains of Rules

Everything moves on a cycle. Rain fills the pool, the pool rises through its notches, and the frogs climb the ring stones ahead of it. At dusk the swifts return in a shrieking spiral and settle. At night the bats leave and the frogs take over the darkness. At dawn a thin mist rises from the pool, and the first light finds the eye.

The old builders left no faces, no writing, only work: fitted stone, tuned clay, and the patient geometry of the gutters. They seem to have built a place whose purpose was to be listened to, or to listen. Now it does both without them, and if you stay long enough at the lowest ring, in the cold and the dim green light, you begin to feel that it is waiting for something to sing back.
