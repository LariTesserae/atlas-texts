# The Long Low Night of the Marrow Basin

It is the hour past midnight, and the heat has not left. It goes on from the day before, held in the ground and in the leaves and in the air, which is close to body temperature. Your skin doesn't register a border with it. The dark is nearly total. There is no moon, and cloud lies low and unbroken overhead. Your eyes open until the world resolves into degrees of black: the black of trunks, the blacker black of gaps between them, and the faint gray-brown of the river far off, which holds a little sky.

## The Ground

This is an old basin. The rivers that made it have been at their work so long that nothing sharp remains. The land is a broad, tilted plate barely above the level of the sea, cut by meanders that swung back and forth across it for a hundred thousand years. Their record is everywhere: crescent oxbows filled with black water, low curving ridges where levees once stood, and terraces so worn they can be felt only as a slight lift under the boots and a change in what grows there.

The soil is deep, red-brown where it shows, and iron-heavy. It has been leached and rebuilt so many times that it feels like flour that has been rained on. Where roots have not laced it together, it takes a footprint cleanly and keeps it for a long time. Nothing here is steep, and nothing is bare. Stone appears only in the riverbanks, as rounded cobbles of a dull ochre, slick with algae, buried in the cut face like teeth in a jaw.

## The Growth

Every gap is filled. There are no clearings, only places where the crowding is a little less. The canopy is a ceiling perhaps forty meters up, but the space below it is not empty air. It is layered with palms, tree ferns, strangler figs, buttressed giants, and understory saplings waiting decades for a fall of light. Lianas hang in cables, loops, and slack braids. Epiphytes furnish every limb: bromeliads holding cups of rainwater, orchids, moss like wet felt, and ferns rooted in the rot of other ferns.

At night the smell is the strongest sense. It is sweetish and heavy, like fruit going over, wet bark, and the mineral breath of the mud. Under it runs a sharper note of crushed leaf and the resin-sour scent of something flowering high up where you cannot see it. Every surface is faintly wet. This is not rain but condensation: the hot air cools a degree against the leaves and gives up its water, so that everything beads and slowly drips. The forest makes its own small weather from its own breath.

Real rain comes irregularly, in warm heavy sheets that last an hour and vanish. Between them there may be days of only dew. The water in this place is not abundant but it is never absent. It sits in oxbows, in the leaf-cups overhead, in seeps at the toes of the old terraces, and in the wide slow river that carries silt to the sea without hurry.

## The Life

The fauna is overwhelming, and it is almost entirely quiet, which is the strange thing about the place.

You can feel it as a pressure. Move a hand along a trunk and the bark shifts under your palm with small bodies: beetles in armored ranks, ants in two-way columns, cockroaches the size of a thumb, all going about their business without pause or alarm. A branch overhead has a snake laid along it, and above the snake a sleeping bird, and above the bird a sloth-slow mammal wrapped around the limb in its own damp fur. The beam of a torch (if you dared use one) would find eyeshine at every height, in dozens of pairs, red and green and amber, none of them moving.

Moths gather at every pale flower, wings trembling. Bats pass through the trunks without a sound you can hear, only a movement of air against your cheek. Frogs sit on leaf-edges by the thousand, throats slack. The heat and the hour have hushed them, and the great choruses come only after rain. Fish turn in the river, and now and then a large one rolls at the surface with a soft heavy sound, like a sack set down on wet ground.

Much of the life is small, and much of it is patient: spiders in tiers of web that hold the drizzle in silver beads, mantises frozen in ambush, caterpillars grazing through the leaf litter in a faint crisp rustle that, multiplied by a billion, becomes the floor's constant whisper. Larger animals are there too: tapir-like grazers standing in the shallows, cats along the levee ridges, monkeys asleep in knots of tails and limbs. The density is such that the forest at night is less an environment with animals in it than one enormous animal made of many.

## The Sound

What you hear, mostly, is this:

- Dripping. It is irregular, from every height, ticking on leaf and mud and the still surface of pools.
- The paper-dry scratch of insects in the litter.
- A low ambient hum, more felt than heard, from a trillion wings and legs.
- Now and then, one voice: a single frog's *tok*, an owl's two notes, a branch giving way somewhere with a long unhurried crack and a soft crash.

Then silence closes over it again, though never a true silence.

And under everything, so faint you might think it was in your blood, there is a machine sound. It is a deep, steady thrum from very far off, beyond the eastern edge of the basin. It might be a pumping station, or a refinery, or a line of diesel barges on the estuary. Low on the underside of the clouds in that direction there is a dull orange stain, the only light in the world, the reflected glow of an industrial coast forty kilometers away. It does not reach the forest floor. It only reminds you that the forest is a place among others. The basin itself holds nothing built. The only trace of that other world here is a pair of rust-red rails half-swallowed in root and mud, running out from nowhere toward nowhere, and a cylindrical boiler lying on its side by the river, so grown over that it looks like a fallen log until you knock on it and it rings.

## The Slow Rules

Change here is measured in long patience. A giant tree dies standing and takes twenty years to fall. In the gap it leaves, saplings race in a slow contest that lasts a century. Oxbows silt into marsh, marsh into wet forest, wet forest into something drier, and the river, somewhere, begins a new loop. The seasons are not seasons of temperature but of water: a long slow rise in the river, a long slow fall, the mud cracking a little and then softening again.

The forest is not fragile. It is settled, worn-in, and balanced, and it has been for longer than it has had a name. Everything in it has found its place and stays there. The dark and the heat and the wet hold together like a hand around a stone, and the night goes on, not about to end, with a million small lives breathing in it.
