# The Sodden Works of Harrow Height

## The Shape of It

The plateau sits at the altitude where breath shows and the sky is close. It is very old. Whatever mountains once stood here were ground down over a span too long to hold in the mind, and the stumps of them are now long swells and shallow basins, all rounded. The land is a worn palm held up to the weather. From any rise you can see for days in every direction, and what you see is one continuous living surface, olive, rust, bruise-purple, and a green so dark it reads as black under the low light.

There is no bare rock. Rock exists, but only as a rumor beneath the plants. The peat and moss and root-felt are meters thick, then tens of meters thick, then simply the ground. Walking is a slow negotiation with a substance that is neither soil nor flesh. It is cold, saturated, and springy. Every step wrings water out of it, and the footprint fills, goes clear, and slowly closes again.

## The Water

Water is not a feature here. It is the medium. It stands just below the surface everywhere, and where you press down it rises to meet you. The plateau is a sponge the size of a county, and the sponge is full.

The mist does not drift. It condenses out of the air onto everything, so that all surfaces are beaded and dripping. The moss bears a fur of droplets. Cold rain comes in fine gray sheets, stops, and starts again for no reason the sky supplies. The rocks that do surface, a few knuckled black outcrops standing apart like the last teeth of something, weep constantly from hairline seams, and the water down their faces is stained the color of tea.

The drainage makes no sense to anyone who reads terrain. Streams start in the middle of flat ground, run a few hundred meters in channels cut through living mat, and then vanish down throats of pale sphagnum. Some pools are perfectly still and black as wells. Others tremble with a slow, regular pulse, as though something beneath were breathing through the water.

## The Plants as Architecture

Nothing was built here. But the plants have made structures, and the structures have the look of industry.

**The Stacks.** Scattered across the basins, far apart, stand hollow columns of fused stem and lignified moss, ten to thirty meters tall, wide at the base and tapering. Each is a single organism, or a colony that has forgotten it was many. Their tops flare into ragged lips. Warmth from the decomposing peat below is drawn up their cores, and they exhale plumes of pale steam that stand in the cold air like smoke from smelters. On still mornings a dozen of them can be seen at once, spaced kilometers apart, each trailing a gray banner, and the whole horizon looks like a dead civilization's furnace district that somehow never stopped.

**The Bellows-Beds.** These are broad blisters of leathery, veined tissue, some the size of a house, that inflate slowly with gas from the rot beneath and then collapse in a great sigh. Each collapse forces air through reed-like vents, so the sound is a low, rhythmic, mechanical wheeze, a hundred slow lungs at a hundred different tempos. Standing among them is like standing in the engine hall of something asleep.

**The Presses.** Colonies of thick-stalked, hydraulic plants build turgor pressure until their stems stand rigid as pistons. Then they release it all at once, driving their seed-casks upward in bursts of sap and spores. The report is a flat, hard *chunk* like a stamping press, and it comes irregularly from all directions, so you learn never to trust silence.

**The Belts.** Long ribbons of mat-forming kelp-like moss creep in slow conveyance across the wet ground, moving perhaps a meter an hour. They carry silt, spores, and small dead things from the high ground toward the sink-pools and there let them fall. Following a belt to its end, you find a spiraling pool where everything it has carried is being digested, strained, and pumped somewhere down into the peat.

None of this was designed. It is simply what a vegetation does after an unimaginably long time with nothing to stop it, cold water, and the slow heat of its own decay. It has become a factory without workers or product, running for the sake of running.

## Light

The light is dim, and it is never quite the light of any particular hour. A thick, low ceiling of cloud diffuses the sun into a pewter glow with no source and few shadows. Colors are deep and damp. Under it, the plants have their own illumination: the Stacks glow faintly at the lip, a sick greenish-white. Slicks of luminous fungus lie in the low places like spilled milk. The wet moss itself carries a dull sheen, so the plateau seems lit from within by a bruised, submarine light.

At what should be dusk, the glow strengthens and the land becomes a scatter of cold lamps across a dark that has no far side.

## Sound

It is loud. That is the first thing a visitor is not prepared for, because the eye reports stillness and the ear reports a foundry.

- The bellows sighing, the presses stamping, the steam-roar of the Stacks rising to a whistle when the wind turns.
- Water everywhere: dripping, trickling, gurgling down into unseen sumps, and the sucking sound of the ground releasing your foot.
- The fauna. Nearly everything living here that isn't a plant is calling.
- The wind, which is constant, cold, and full of grit-fine moisture, and which plays the Stacks like organ pipes so that the whole plateau sometimes holds a single sustained chord.

Under all of this, when the other sounds gap, there is a slow deep knocking from below, like a great pipe being tapped. It has a rhythm that almost repeats.

## Fauna

Animals are abundant, and they live on the plants and within them. Most are small, or built to look small.

- **Grub-smiths**: pale, segmented, the length of an arm, they bore galleries through the peat and line them with hardened secretion. When a bellows-bed collapses, whole colonies pour from its vents, ticking like cooling metal.
- **Fog-geese**, or what a person would call geese: long-necked, gray, and hoarse. They graze the moss in flocks of hundreds, kilometers apart from the next flock, and their honking carries in the damp air as though they were shouting into a bowl.
- **Cold-stags** with antlers that are partly living moss, so that in winter-like weather they wear whole gardens on their heads. They move slowly. Where they have passed, the ground closes over behind them as though healing.
- **Skitter-frogs**, thumb-sized, in numberless choirs. Their calls are metallic and sound like tools being dropped on a concrete floor that is not there.
- **Vent-swifts**, birds that ride the updrafts of the Stacks, wheeling up through the steam and dropping out of it wet and shrieking.
- Something large that is never seen well. Its wake is visible: a slow, deep parting in the moss, a long furrow that fills with water, moving beneath the surface at walking pace.

The density is low. You might walk for an hour and see only the fog-geese and a single stack on the horizon. Then you crest a swell and a whole basin will be alive with motion.

## The Rules That Have Come Loose

The weirdness here is not decoration. It is the condition of the place. The plateau is so old and so saturated with its own processes that its consistency has worn down along with its mountains.

**Distance is unreliable.** A Stack that seems a two-hour walk away may be reached in twenty minutes, or may never get nearer. The ground sometimes agrees with you and sometimes does not. Travelers keep to the streams, which at least obey themselves.

**Rain has directions.** In certain patches, called dry-falls by the few who know them, the rain rises. It lifts from the moss in fine strands and vanishes into the cloud, and the ground beneath is drier than a desert. Walk through one and your clothes dry in seconds. Then you cross the edge and the ordinary rain falls again on you, heavier, as though owed.

**Sound precedes its cause.** You hear the press-stamp, then feel the tremor, then see the seed-cask launch, but sometimes the order is reversed, and sometimes the launch never comes. Some echoes return from places where no surface exists. Some come back changed: a goose-call returns as a low mechanical grind.

**Repetition.** There are hills that appear more than once. You can pass the same black outcrop, weeping the same stained water, on three separate horizons, and on each it is slightly further along in its erosion. The oldest-looking one is nearly flat. The youngest still has an edge.

**The Stacks lean toward attention.** Not visibly. But someone who watches one for a long time and then looks away finds it a few degrees nearer the axis of their gaze. Camp is not made within sight of more than one.

**Weather without sequence.** Sleet, fog, still bright cloud, and a hard wind can arrive in any order, or all at once in different parts of the same field of view. The storms are the plateau's usual state. Within them the land moves: belts surge, pools overturn, whole sections of mat lift, shudder, and resettle, and the Stacks roar and the bellows race and the presses fire in frantic overlapping salvos until the whole surface seems to be working at a frenzy toward some end it will never reach.

## What Emerges

It is cold, but it is not dead, and it is not still. The plateau is what happens when erosion has finished with the stone and turned to the next task, which is the slow, wet, insane elaboration of life upon a worn-out world. It has no builders and no audience. It has engines made of leaf and root, and its logic, once ordinary, has been worn as smooth as its hills.

If you stay long enough, you will notice the deep knocking has begun to match your pulse. Or your pulse has begun to match the knocking. On the plateau, that distinction is one of the things that has eroded away.
