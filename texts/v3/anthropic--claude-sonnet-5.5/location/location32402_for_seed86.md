# The Sump of Wings

You descend into it rather than arrive. The country above is flat, cold scrub. Then the ground stops at a lip of black stone, and below it lies a country of its own, about a day's walk across, sunk two hundred paces beneath the plateau that made it.

A slab of the earth's crust dropped here in one piece, and it hasn't been touched since. The walls are fault scarps, still sharp. Their edges are pale where the rock broke, and the striations of the sliding are still ruled across them like claw marks. Nothing has had time to soften them. Talus lies in clean, angular fans at their feet, each fragment with edges that could still cut a palm. The floor is a broad, uneven plain of tilted blocks, shattered basalt, and dark grit.

## The Lid

Across much of the trough a roof survives: a shelf of lava crust that did not fall with the rest and hangs out from the cliffs in vast overhangs, tens of paces thick. Where it holds, the plain lies in a brown dusk. Where it has broken, wide holes let down columns of pale grey daylight, each slanting to the floor like a fallen pillar. Scattered across the plain, they are the only real illumination. Between them the land is dim, cool, and hard to read at distance, and the roof's underside disappears into dark.

Light here is a place you walk toward and away from. Its slow drift across the floor never changes, because the openings are angled so the shafts settle on the same ground hour after hour under the same overcast. There is no sunrise. There is a steady, milky dusk that has never been anything else.

## Water

The water is small and constant. The air is at the edge of dew, cool, about the temperature of a cellar, and beaded moisture stands on every cold surface. Basalt sweats. Drips fall from the roof's underside in thousands of slow strands, each landing in its own small hollow, and each hollow has worn a different note into the rock. Walking under the shelf, you pass through overlapping rhythms of tapping.

Where the drips gather there are pools, black and perfectly still, seldom deeper than a knee. A few seeps thread between blocks into shallow braided streams that run for a mile or so and vanish into cracks in the floor. Their beds are rust-orange and lime-green with mineral film. Nothing is flooded and nothing is dry, and it has been this way as long as anything can remember.

## The Green

Vegetation is sparse and clings where the light shafts fall. Under each skylight a small oasis stands: a ring of ferns, pale-backed and stiff, a tangle of grey-green moss cushions, a few twisted shrubs with leathery leaves that have leaned toward the hole for generations. Beyond the ring of light the ground is bare. Lichens, orange and white, crust the rocks in slow maps. Along the streams there are mats of slick algae and a thin, hairy grass that grows only in guano-rich mud.

Most of the green is fed by the fall from above. Which brings us to the animals.

## The Fauna

The trough is overwhelmingly, unreasonably alive. Every surface belongs to something.

**Bats** hang from the underside of the lid in colonies so dense the ceiling looks furred, brown and rippling. They number in the millions. The colony is arranged so that it is never all asleep or all awake: at any hour, some tier is streaming out through the skylights while another streams in, and the air beneath each opening is a slow braid of wings. It is a permanent weather of small bodies.

**Swiftlets** cling to the walls in tiers, their cup nests glued to the cliff in whitish rows like shelves of shells. They are always flying, always calling.

Below them the **guano** lies in banks and drifts, dark, wet, ammoniac, heaped in cones beneath the roosts in mounds taller than a person. It is the engine of the place. On it live:

- **Cave crickets**, long-legged, ghost-pale, in carpets that shift when you step, so the ground seems to breathe.
- **Beetles** in armored, glossy hordes, flipping and burrowing through the mounds.
- **Mites and springtails** in such numbers that the mounds shimmer faintly.
- **Pale centipedes** as long as a forearm, hunting the crickets.
- **Blind salamanders** and translucent crabs in the pools, and thin white fish that feel the water for you before you touch it.
- **Spiders**, large and small, whose webs hang between rocks like torn linen, sagging with the weight of what they've caught.
- **Rats and shrews** in the rubble, **owls** that nest on ledges and drop silent from the dark, and low, sinuous **civets** that patrol the streambeds.

No one predator thins any of it. The balance was struck long ago. Every death is eaten and every dropping is fed on, and the numbers stay the same year after year, so precisely that the place seems to hold its breath.

## Sound

It is loud in the way surf is loud: not a noise but a medium. The chirr of swiftlets' echolocation clicks fills the upper air like dry rain on a tin roof. Under it runs the enormous low rustle of bat wings, a sound like fabric being shaken a thousand times. Crickets rasp in the mounds. Drips tick and plink from the roof. Overhead the lid carries all of it, so the sound rolls and returns from every side. Voices must be raised to be heard at arm's length, and words are given away to the din unless shouted or shaped in the mouth so that they cut through. Silence is not available here. Even in the far corners, where the roost thins, the drips still keep their time.

The smell is just as loud: wet ammonia, cold rock, sweet rot, and a mineral tang from the pools.

## The People, Barely

There are traces of people, but few, and they are old and small. A hand-width channel cut in stone leads a seep into a hollow. A cairn of black blocks marks the safe route across a treacherous tilted field. Somewhere near a skylight is a ring of soot-blackened stones with a scatter of burnt bone, and beside it a bundle of fiber rope coiled around a wooden peg driven into a crack. Handprints in red ochre cluster on a wall beside a spot where the guano is easiest to dig. The tools left behind are stone scrapers, bone digging-sticks, and baskets of woven root, all worn smooth.

Nobody lives here. Gatherers come down by the rope ladders at the few places the cliff allows, cut guano and eggs and nest-cups, and carry them up in baskets. They do not stay, for the noise and dusk press on the mind. They have left almost nothing, and the place has not noticed.

## The Rule of the World

Nothing here changes. The bats leave and return in ordered tiers, the drips fall at their rhythms, the light shafts lie where they always have, the guano mounds neither grow nor shrink, and the streams sink into the same cracks. The cliffs are fresh but do not fall further; the fault has been quiet since the drop. The trough is not sleeping and not dying. It is a completed machine, running, and it will keep running as long as the roof holds and the colony feeds. Standing in a column of grey light, with the din pressing on both ears and the cold beads of moisture gathering on your sleeves, you feel that you are standing inside something that finished becoming itself before you arrived.
