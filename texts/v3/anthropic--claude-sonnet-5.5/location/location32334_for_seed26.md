# The Sump Fields of Karrow Low

You arrive by a stair that has been descending for so long the handrail has stopped being cold to the touch. Cold is simply the temperature now. At the bottom the stair opens onto a landing of poured concrete, and past the landing the ground falls away into a plain.

It is a plain. It is also a room. The roof is a shallow series of vaults, ribbed and cast in one piece, hanging perhaps sixty meters above and vanishing into its own haze at the edges. Below it, for something like eight kilometers in the long direction, the floor is laid out in terraces. Each terrace is a slab, each slab slightly lower than the last, and the whole descends toward a center you cannot yet see. This is the Sump Field. Everything in it was made, and the making is recent enough that the edges are still crisp. Formwork lines run across the walls in clean horizontal stripes. The bolt-holes have not yet softened. Where the tie-rods were cut, bright steel discs still show in the gray.

## Light

The light is poor and comes from a few sources. Sodium lamps hang from the vault on long chains, one every hundred meters or so, each casting a pool of amber that fades to brown and then to nothing. Between them the air is the color of dishwater. Far off, along the eastern wall, a row of blue-white work lights burns above some operation you cannot make out, and their color falls oddly across the amber, so that the terraces between the two light zones look bruised.

There is also a single slot in the roof, a long cut a few meters wide, where the vault was left open for a crane path or an intake. Through it comes a strip of pale gray that is either overcast sky or another ceiling. It doesn't change with the hour. It lies down across the terraces like a blade laid on a table.

## Water

Water is everywhere, and it is well managed until it isn't.

The vault sweats. Condensation gathers on the underside of the ribs and swells into drops the size of thumbnails, and the drops fall in slow irregular ranks. Beneath the densest ribs this becomes a steady rain, a curtain of it, so that you walk between pillars of falling water and hear them hit the slabs with a sound like someone dropping handfuls of gravel. The rain doesn't fall evenly. It follows the geometry of the roof, so the Field has dry lanes and drenched lanes, as if the place had been grooved with weather.

The slabs are cambered so that water runs off toward gutters, and the gutters are channels cut a hand's width deep and edged in cast iron. They gather into larger drains, then culverts, then open canals with vertical walls. The canals carry black water in one direction, downhill, toward the center. It moves quickly and it is very clear. Where it runs over the lip of a terrace it doesn't fall in a sheet. Weirs break it into combs of a hundred thin jets, which reunite in the basin below with a hiss.

The young concrete is only beginning to be marked by all this. Along every gutter there is a fine pale bloom, a crust of lime that the water has pulled out of the cement and left behind like frost. In places it has grown into small stalactites, brittle as chalk, no longer than a finger, hanging from the undersides of ledges. The walls weep rust from every embedded bolt in orange tongues. It is the earliest stage of ruin, a surface only just learning what water does.

## The Machines

The sound is what you feel before you understand it. It is a deep, continuous, layered thrum, and inside it are separate voices. Pump-houses stand at each terrace-step like squat brick chapels, and each contains a great vertical-shaft pump, and each pump breathes on its own slow cycle: a long intake groan, a shudder, a pounding release. They are not in sync. Standing on the fourth terrace you hear three of them at once, and their beat frequencies drift together and apart, so that every few minutes the whole Field seems to gather itself into a single enormous heartbeat and then lose it.

Above the pumps, along the vault, run the steam mains. They are lagged pipes as thick as cisterns, wrapped in gray cloth and banded with steel. Every so often a relief valve on one of them lets go, and a white plume punches into the cold air, spreads, and immediately begins to condense. The condensate joins the rain. The water that falls in the Field is largely the water that was pumped up and heated and vented, so the weather here is a loop of machinery that has taken on the habits of a climate.

There are turbines somewhere, and belts, and ratcheting things. Gantry cranes trundle on rails along the ceiling with a sound like a slow freight train, carrying nothing, or carrying things too far up to see. A bell rings on the hour, a flat iron clang, and after the bell the water levels in the canals change. Sluices open, the terraces reflood or drain, and for a quarter hour the whole plain rearranges itself to the noise of gates and gushing. If you are standing in a low place when the bell rings, you are wise to move.

## Ground and Structure

Walkways of grated steel stand a meter above the wet slabs. Nothing at floor level is dry for long. The grating rings under boots, and each step is announced across the open space, so you cannot cross the Field without being heard. The larger structures are these: the pump-chapels; a lattice of black steel towers holding the lamp-chains; long low sheds with corrugated roofs, doors sealed with padlocks whose hasps are still bright; a conveyor gallery that climbs the far wall on stilts and disappears through a hole in the ceiling.

Between these there is a great deal of empty concrete. Scattered across the slabs at wide intervals are objects whose purpose isn't clear: a ring of eight iron bollards standing in shallow water; a tall stair leading to nothing, ending in a landing at the height of the lamps; a rectangular pit, lined in white tile, filled to the brim with perfectly still water in which the lamps are reflected sharply. The pit doesn't ripple when the pumps shudder. Everything else in the Field trembles, but the water in the tiled pit stays flat as glass. Dropped grit makes a ring that spreads and then, instead of fading, stops and holds its shape for several seconds before smoothing away. Nobody who works here will say why. They step around it.

## Living Things

Life is sparse and takes what it can get. On the drip lines, where the ceiling water falls in regular rows, a pale moss has formed in narrow green ribbons, bright as paint against the gray. Ferns the color of old ivory grow in the cracks of the culverts, fed by the seep. On the sodium-lit side of the towers, a thin brown lichen has begun its slow work on the steel.

Under one work light on the east wall someone has installed racks of trays, and in the trays grow ranks of bitter cress and pale lettuce, tended by no one you can see. A hose drips into each tray. Somebody comes and goes; the soil is turned.

Animals are rare. In the canals live eels, long and translucent, whose organs show through the skin as thin dark cords. They hold themselves in the current with barely a movement and turn their blunt heads to your lamp. Along the girders roost small gray birds that have lost their color and most of their eyesight. They call in clipped notes that carry a long way, and when they fly they do so in the lit lanes only, dipping from one amber pool to the next as if the dark between were a gap in the world. Something larger occasionally drags a wet line across the slabs at the edge of hearing. It is never in the place the sound suggests.

## The Center

Follow the terraces down and the Field narrows toward a central basin, a circular cistern a kilometer across, lined in the same gray concrete. It is full. Its surface is black and lies a few meters below the last terrace lip, and it is perfectly calm except at the center, where a low mound of water rises and turns over on itself continuously, like a slow boil. That is the intake. All the canals of the Field end here. The water doesn't drain away. It is drawn down through a shaft of unknown depth, and from far below comes a note so low you hear it in your sternum, not your ears. It is the note that all the pump-chapels are tuned around, though none of them play it.

Around the cistern runs a narrow ring road of iron plate. From it you can see the whole Field behind you, stepped and lamp-dotted and steaming, its rain falling in a hundred separate columns. Ahead there is only the black, breathing water and the hollow note. Somewhere beneath it the shaft goes on, cast and bolted, into places whose builders have not yet finished building.

The concrete is only months old. The lime is just beginning to bloom. Nothing here has yet had time to fall down, and the water already knows where it will start.
