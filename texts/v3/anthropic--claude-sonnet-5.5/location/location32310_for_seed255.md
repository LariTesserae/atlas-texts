# The Sillworks

The land here is young, and it is entirely made.

Under everything lies a floor of grey-blue clay that was not there a few thousand seasons ago. It comes down from the rim in the slow, soupy floods the builders call *the Laying*. The floods leave a hand's depth of silt at a time, and the whole basin rises with them. The people have never let the ground be simply ground. Every surface is fired brick, packed slag, cast bronze, hammered copper sheet, rammed clay, or lashed bone and horn. Where the silt hasn't yet been claimed, it is combed into ridged terraces so it will dry evenly and can be cut into blocks. Nothing here is a hill. Every rise is a wall, a stair, a kiln-back, or a spoil heap that someone is still adding to.

## The Dark

There is no sun to speak of. A lid of low cloud sits over the basin, the color of wet slate by day and of nothing at all by night, and the day is a slightly thinner dark. What light exists is made. Kilns breathe orange through their draft-holes. Rows of clay lamps burn rendered fat, and their smoke has stained every ceiling black-brown. On the north terraces, shelves of glowing fungus are kept in glazed bowls, giving off a cold green that is enough to find a doorway and not enough to read a face. Walk the causeways and you pass through pools of light, each with its own color and smell, and between them you go by touch, by the drag of your sleeve on wet brick, and by ear.

## The Noise

It is very loud, and the noise is the purpose of the place.

The water does it. Damp air condenses on the cold upper masonry and runs into gutters. Gutters feed troughs, and troughs feed tipping buckets that fill, overbalance, and slam down against bronze plates. A single trickle set to the right rhythm can drive a hammer, a bellows, a rattling sieve, a long wooden clapper, or a rank of hanging tubes that ring like a cracked choir. Thousands of these devices run at once, none in step with the others. The result is a rolling clatter, clank, and gong, with a low grinding under it from the great silt-wheels and a high needling from the sluice-whistles. Then, like weather, the pattern shifts. A rain band crosses the basin and every trough in its path speeds up, so a wave of hammering travels across the district ahead of the water. You can hear the rain coming a quarter-hour before it arrives.

The keepers say this is more than a side effect. The channels are tuned so that the rhythm of the tipping keeps silt suspended and moving. Where the noise fails, in a jammed bucket or a dry gutter, the water slows and drops its load and the channel chokes. Then a quiet patch spreads, and the quiet is always a bad sign. The keepers walk the causeways listening for holes in the sound and carry mallets, pitch, and coils of rope made from braided hair and dried fungal fiber.

## Layers

The density is oppressive. Buildings don't stand beside one another so much as press. Fired-brick vaults sit on older vaults, which sit on older ones, and the oldest are already sealed under the Laying, their doors packed with grey clay, their lamps still burning behind the silt for all anyone knows. Newer builders cut down through the seams, or rather build up through them: each flood, a new course of brick goes on top of the last. Stairs are constantly being lengthened, and door lintels sit at knee height in the old quarter, so people crawl into rooms their grandmothers walked into.

Spanning the gaps are bridges of cast bronze, green-crusted and dripping. Drainage pipes of glazed clay run along and through everything, and hand-cranked lifts of rope and counterweight creak up and down the wall-faces. Laundry-colored membranes of dried gut and skin are stretched between roofs to catch condensation, and they hang heavy and translucent, sagging into the lamplight like slow lungs.

## What's Emerging

The erosion has barely started. The clay is too new to have learned how to fall apart. On the spoil banks at the basin's edge the first rills are forming, hand-width gullies with fine grey deltas at their mouths. Behind the oldest walls the first true cracks are opening, and the keepers treat them as an event, leaning close and touching them. Nobody here has seen a ruin. The idea of a building failing slowly and quietly, over centuries, is a story from the rim, and it is not believed.

## Things That Live

There are no plants. The green is fungus, pale shelf-growths on the wet walls, and the black felt in the gutters that the keepers scrape and boil into a gruel. The fauna is sparse and strange. Blind, pale, hoofed grazers the size of large dogs move in twos and threes along the lower terraces, cropping mats of fungus, and their hooves ring on the brick. Moths the color of ash orbit the lamp-rows in slow spirals, and something in the walls eats them; the small sounds of that are lost in the din. Now and then a long, translucent thing, half eel and half worm, surfaces in the deepest channels, sliding upstream against the flow as if it knew the tuning.

## The People, Such As They Are

Very few are visible. Hands appear at hatches, a shape crosses a lit bridge, a hammer is heard behind a wall and stops when you listen for it. The people are small in number against the mass of what they've made, and they move as if they were guests of the machinery. They speak with taps and bangs on pipes because ordinary speech is pointless here, and a message passed by knuckle on bronze can cross the district faster than a runner.

## The Cool, the Damp

The air is cool and always wet. It beads on the skin and doesn't dry. Everything smells of hot clay, tallow smoke, wet copper, and the mineral sweetness of fresh silt. Breath shows near the upper terraces, and the kiln-mouths pull you in with dry heat you regret leaving. Underfoot, brick sweats. Overhead, water is always falling from somewhere: a drip, a bucket-slam, a gutter's long pour into the dark. It never stops, and the whole basin, layer on layer, is slowly being buried in the mud that keeps it alive.
