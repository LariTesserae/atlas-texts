# The Salt Stair at Thin Air

The place is a shallow bowl on the shoulder of a peak, some four hundred paces across. Below its lip the world is only cloud, a floor of it, white and slowly turning, and the bowl sits just above like a cupped hand held out of a bath. The sky overhead is nearly violet at its center. The sun is small, white and hard, and there is not enough air to soften anything it touches.

## The stone

The bowl is packed with rock: pinnacles, fins, tilted slabs, and shoulder-high blocks that lean on one another with gaps between them barely wide enough to turn sideways in. Ages of freeze and thaw have worked all of it into the same grammar. Every surface is fluted with vertical grooves, thumb-deep on the small stones and arm-deep on the large ones, so that the pinnacles look like bundled reeds turned to rock, or like pipes waiting for a breath.

The rock is a pale grey-gold tuff shot through with a white crust that is not snow. It is a salt, a hygroscopic mineral that seeps out of the stone's pores and dries into a rind. The salt drinks moisture straight from the air, even in the cold. When cloud rolls up over the lip, the whole bowl darkens from bone to slate within minutes, stone by stone, in a wave that follows the fog's edge. When the cloud drops away and the sun returns, the pale comes back the same way, and the bowl seems to breathe in tone.

## Water

Water here is a visitor that always comes back. Cloud enters the bowl two or three times in a day, slow and wet and lasting perhaps half an hour. It leaves rime on everything windward: white feathers of ice growing out into the wind, each one as long as a finger, all leaning the same direction like grass in a gale. Then the sun clears the cloud and the light comes down bright and cold on the rime, and the feathers start to shed. They let go in sheets, tick and slide and shatter, and for a few minutes the bowl is full of small glassy noises.

Meltwater runs for a short while along the flutes, threads of it in every groove, catching light so that the pinnacles seem wired with silver. It darkens the salt, runs down into cracks, and by late afternoon freezes again in place, so that the runnels become clear ice veins inside the stone's grooves. Nothing pools for long. The only standing water is in the cisterns.

## The made things

Someone lived here, or came here often, long enough to learn the bowl's habits. Their marks are scattered and small.

Upright slabs, chosen for their deep fluting, stand in rows at the windward edge. At the foot of each is a hand-chipped stone basin no bigger than a bowl for a household's supper. Rime and condensation run down the grooves and into the basins, and the builders had understood this. The basins are still doing it. Some hold a finger of dark water under a lid of ice.

There are cairns, waist-high, built of flat pieces fitted without mortar. Each has a single copper wedge driven between two stones, cold-hammered and green-black, its head mushroomed by blows. Nobody has counted the cairns, because in the dense stone it is hard to see more than three at once. They may mark paths, or count seasons, or mark the dead.

One low wall curves around a hollow just big enough to lie in, roofed once with slabs, half of which have fallen in. The floor is scraped clean. Scratched into the lintel stone are tally-marks in groups of five, and beneath them a spiral, worn shallow by frost.

Between the slabs run lines of stone pegs, sunk into drilled holes, leaning slightly. Their purpose is lost. Rime grows on them thickest of anything, and each morning they are white antlers.

## Sound

It is mostly quiet. The air is too thin to carry much, and what sound there is comes small and near. Wind crosses the fluted pinnacles and gives a low, uneven hum, a note that shifts as gusts change angle, never a tune. When the rime sheds there is a glassy chittering. Now and then a slab settles with a dry knock, and once in a long while a large plate of ice lets go somewhere out of sight and falls a long way before it breaks. Otherwise, there is the sound of your own breath, too fast, and your boots on grit.

## What moves

Nothing lives here, and nothing needs to, because the bowl is busy without it. Salt darkens and pales. Rime grows into the wind and falls away. Meltwater runs and locks. Fine grit creeps down the flutes on each thaw and is lifted again by each freeze, so that every pinnacle sheds a little of itself daily. Over ten thousand years that has made the bowl exactly what it is: not a ruin but a settled form, worn to a shape it can keep. The cairns lean and hold, and the basins have gone smooth as spoons.

## Light

The light is the hardest thing to bear. Sun off rime, sun off wet salt, sun off ice in the grooves, all of it thrown back into the gaps between stones so that even the shadows glow blue-white. Squinting is constant. In the late afternoon the low sun pours through the pinnacle-gaps in long parallel blades, and the rime feathers light up one after another, thousands of them, each a filament of fire. It lasts a minute. Then the next cloud comes up over the lip, and the salt begins to darken, and the bowl goes quietly back to work.
