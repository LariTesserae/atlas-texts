# The Chord Plateau

Above the last of the cloud forests, the land climbs through scree and cushion-plant into a tableland so high that the sky at noon is a deep blue-violet, nearly the color of a bruise. The plateau is a broad, tilted plain some sixty kilometers across, ribbed with long ridges and cut by shallow basins. The air is cool rather than cold, a steady ten degrees in the sun and a few above freezing in the shade. The sun is close and fierce, and the cold is only a thin skin over it. Light comes off every surface. The pale stone throws it back, the tarns throw it back, and the frost-polished faces of the ridges flash in it like slow signal mirrors. Shadows are black and exact. The eye has to learn to read the ground by its edges, because nothing is softened by haze.

## The stone

The bedrock is a pale, fine-grained volcanic tuff, laid down in thick pulses so long ago that the whole region has been weathered into a finished state. Nothing is raw here. The slopes have settled to their angle of repose, and the gullies have cut down to a base level and stopped. The rock is worn into **fins**: tall, thin blades and ribs a few meters to sixty meters high, all leaning the same way, all separated by slots that run for kilometers in parallel. Between the fins the floors are packed grit and pea-gravel, sorted by ten thousand years of water and wind into perfect bands: pink, then grey, then a green-tinged white.

The fins are the reason for the sound.

## The sound

The wind here is a single wind. It rises off the warm lowland basins on one side of the mountain each morning, is squeezed up the long slot canyons, and pours across the plateau in one continuous, unvarying stream. It has done this for so long that the rock has been shaped by it into an instrument. Every slot is a pipe, and every hollow in a fin is a resonator. Some fins have wind-drilled holes through them, round as if bored, and the air through them makes a note.

The result is a roar with structure inside it. A low, tectonic drone comes from the deepest canyons, felt in the sternum more than heard. Over it sit fifths and octaves from the mid-sized slots, and above those a shrill, glassy whistle from the perforated fins. Together they make a standing chord that never resolves and never stops. It is loud enough that speech is impossible and thought is difficult. Standing in the middle of the plateau feels like standing inside a struck bell. Small stones vibrate on the flat rocks. The surface of every tarn is stippled with fine standing ripples in a pattern that never shifts.

A visitor can find quiet, but only by going down into the wind-shadow of a particular fin, where the air is dead and the sound is suddenly muffled, as if a hand had been put over the ears. Then the roar is only a pressure in the bones.

## Water

Water here is small and precious, and it comes as damp more than as rain. The plateau is above the cloud most days, but at dawn and dusk the cloud sea below rises against the escarpment and pushes a fog up over the lip. The fog is thin and brief, and it condenses on everything: on the fins, on the leaves of the cushion plants, on the fur and feathers of the animals. Every stone is beaded by first light. The fins have shallow channels worn along their windward faces, and the condensed water runs down them into rock pools no bigger than a bowl. Below them, in the basins, lie the **tarns**: round, clear, extremely cold, and fed by seeps rather than streams. A few basins have a thin braided outflow, a finger-width trickle that disappears into gravel within a hundred meters. Rain falls occasionally, in short hard squalls that come and go within minutes and leave rainbows standing across the ridges.

The moisture is exactly enough. There is no flood and no drought, and the tarns stand at the same level they stood at a thousand years ago, with the same white rings of mineral on the same stones.

## Plants

Vegetation is sparse but not absent. It grows in the places where water and wind-shelter meet.

- **Cushion mounds**, hard green domes the size of a sleeping dog, made of thousands of tightly packed rosettes. They are dense as wood and warm to the touch on the sunward side. Some are older than any tree in the lowlands.
- **Grey tussock**, wiry and stiff, growing in lines along the bases of fins where the fog drips.
- **Rock lichens** in orange, black, and a chalky lime, coating the leeward faces. They are the only color in the middle distance.
- Around the tarns, **a green felt of moss and sedge**, so vividly bright against the pale stone that it looks lit from within.

Between these patches are kilometers of bare gravel. The plants do not spread. They have long ago reached the limits of where they can live, and they stay.

## Animals

The fauna is abundant, and this is the strangest thing about the place, because it is abundant inside an emptiness. The plateau is mostly vacant, but where there are animals there are thousands.

**The chorus-birds.** Small grey-and-rust birds, plump, with oversized throats, nest in the perforated fins in colonies of tens of thousands. Each nest is a wind-bored hole. They have adapted to the chord. Their calls are not lost in it but *fitted* into it, a high, rapid trilling pitched in the gaps between the harmonic peaks, so that a colony's noise adds a shimmering upper layer to the roar. When a hawk-like raptor crosses the ridge, the whole colony shifts to a different pitch in an instant, and the change ripples across the fin like a wave. It is the only rapid change in the soundscape, and it is local and brief.

**The stilt-goats.** Long-legged, woolly, sand-colored herbivores, close to vicuña or chamois, graze in herds of a few hundred along the tussock lines. They communicate by posture and by ear-flick, and they face the wind in unison. Their hooves are wide and soft-padded for the gravel. Each herd has a fixed range and has grazed the same circuit for longer than anyone can say.

**Burrowing rodents**, fat and grey, live in the moss felt around the tarns, and their colonies are honeycombed under the sedge. They whistle, and no one can hear it.

**Predators**: a lean, pale fox, a small cat with a ringed tail, and the raptors that ride the updraft along the fins, hanging motionless in the wind and never flapping.

**Invertebrates** are everywhere on the sun-warmed stones: dark, wingless beetles, springtails on the water films, and a large, slow, bright-red mite that gathers on the moss in drifts.

Life keeps its own places. The chorus-birds keep the fins, the goats keep the gravel, and the rodents keep the tarns. There is no migration and no invasion. The numbers are stable from year to year, held in a balance long since found.

## The unusual

Two things unsettle a visitor.

First, the **tuning**. The chord is not random. Its notes fall very nearly into a consistent scale, because the slots are cut at regular spacings by the regular fracturing of the tuff. To an ear that can pick it out, the plateau sounds almost composed. The herds and the birds respond to it. The goats will bed down only where a certain low note is strongest.

Second, the **standing ripples**. In the tarns, the vibration of the chord creates faint geometric patterns on the water surface, hexagons and rings, which never change because the sound never changes. They are visible in the low sun as fine lace on the water. Someone looking for a long time could believe the pattern was drawn there.

## People

The plateau is nearly untouched, but not entirely. People come, seasonally and rarely, in small groups from the lowland valleys, to hunt stilt-goats and gather chorus-bird eggs from the lower nests. They carry hand-flaked obsidian blades, spear-throwers of bone and wood, and cord slings. Their tools are simple and their methods old. They do not stay. They speak with their hands, since voices are useless, and with **knotted cords** and signal-flags of pale cloth held up against the sky.

They have left almost nothing. On the crest of each major ridge is a **cairn**, a waist-high stack of flat stones, each placed by a traveler and each one a little more lichen-crusted than the last. There are perhaps forty on the entire plateau. A few slots contain old fire-rings of blackened stone, and one fin has a row of handprints in red ochre at shoulder height, faded to the color of dried blood. There is nothing else.

## The rule of the place

The plateau's law is *equilibrium*. Nothing on it is growing or dying in any way that shows. The wind that carved the stone is the wind that sounds the chord. The fog that feeds the tarns comes at the hour it has always come. The birds that fit their calls to the roar were bred to it by ten thousand generations of it. Even the cairns, and the ochre hands, seem to belong to this stillness, because everything that has come here has slowed down to match it.

At dusk the low sun sets the fins aglow along their whole length, and the fog rises. For a few minutes the chord seems to deepen, though it does not. The birds go silent by colonies, one fin after another, until only the wind remains, and the plateau is at last as empty as it always looked: the pale stone, the pale light, the enormous sound, and nothing in it that has ever needed to change.
