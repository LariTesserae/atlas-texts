**The Sweat Room**

There is no light here, so begin with the heat.

It is the heat of a body, a little more than skin. Once you are past the throat of the passage, the air stops feeling like air and becomes a warm, close presence pressed against your face. There is no draft and no cool pocket. The temperature is the same at your ankles, your fingertips, and the roof above you. After a while the edge of you goes soft. You can't tell where your arm stops and the room starts, because nothing on either side of the line is different. People who come here learn to keep one hand on the rock so they remember they have a shape.

The room is small, perhaps twelve paces across at its widest, though you cannot pace it straight. It sits high in a mountain, far above any valley, in rock that was molten not long ago. Its walls are the raw inside of a gas bubble, frozen mid-thought. Nothing has worn them. There are no rounded lips, no smoothed floors, no sand or gravel. Every edge is as sharp as the moment it set. Run a palm along the wall carelessly and it will open your hand. What looks like a blunt knob under your fingers turns out, when you press, to be a thousand tiny points, each one perfectly formed and never touched by wind or by flowing water. The rock has not aged. It is still in the first hour of being a place.

**What covers the rock**

The water gets in everywhere. It comes from no spring and no stream. The mountain is full of it, warm and saturated, and it beads out of the stone the way sweat comes to a forehead. Every surface is wet. The walls weep in fine, continuous films. Crystals grow in the wet: clear, blunt-tipped needles of some pale mineral, packed so densely that in places the wall is a pelt, and your fingers slide down their lengths with a dry squeaking that is the loudest thing in the room. Beneath the needles are older, softer layers: flowstone in overlapping lips, each one holding a shallow skin of water so still it never ripples. Where the ceiling drips, the drops fall onto stalagmites no taller than a knuckle, each capped with a little cup of standing water. A drop lands in each cup and the cup shivers. There are hundreds of these cups. You hear them as a faint, uneven ticking that never becomes a rhythm and never stops.

The room is dense. It is not empty space with things in it. It is crowded like the inside of a cupboard, layered with drip-stone and crystal curtains, ribbed columns as thin as wrists, and hanging veils of wet mineral you must part with your forearm. You move by feeling and by bending. Your shoulders brush things that chime very softly against each other, glass on glass. Every cubic hand's-width holds something. Even the air seems packed, thick with vapor. Breathing it is like drinking, and a fine film gathers on the inside of your nose and on your lips. It tastes of iron and faintly of struck flint.

**What lives here**

Very little, and it does not need light.

On the ledges where the wet is steadiest there are mats of something living. You feel them before you know them: a cool, slick, springy pad, like the flesh inside a mushroom cap, spreading in lobes the size of a hand. They are pale, though nobody has ever seen that. They are fungal, or something that behaves like fungus, feeding on the mineral film and on the slow warm seep of the rock. Where you press, they hold your handprint for a few breaths and then fill it back in. Here and there a single stalk rises from them, thin as a thread and topped with a bead of moisture. These are the room's only vegetation, sparse and slow, and they are not decorating anything.

There are animals, though you will meet one at most. Something small and blind lives in the water cups. It is a pale, many-legged thing that crawls the rims of the flowstone pools and does not flee. If you hold still it walks across your knuckle with a sensation like a stray hair, considers you, and leaves. Once in a long while you may hear a different sound: a single dry click, high up in the crystal curtains, that comes from something that does not answer when you click back.

**The one sound**

Under the ticking, if you stop breathing, you will notice a hum. It is not loud and it does not change. It is not the sound of wind, because there is no wind. It is the room itself resonating at one low, fixed pitch, the way a wet fingertip on a glass rim makes a note. The crystals and the standing water are tuned to each other by accident and hold that note without effort, forever. When you speak, the room takes your voice and hands back a slightly flattened version of it. Everything else is quiet. Dripping, the squeak of your hand, and your own heartbeat are the whole soundscape.

**What people left**

The Sweat Room has been visited, though no one lives here. The marks are scattered and small, and they don't seem to be the leavings of one age.

Near the entrance, in a hollow of flowstone that would otherwise be a bath, someone set a row of thumb-sized river pebbles. Their smooth roundness is startling in this place of edges. They must have been carried up from a valley an unimaginable climb below. There are seven of them, and no others have been added or removed.

Further in, pressed into a shelf of soft grey clay that lines one wall, are handprints. They are small, mostly, and some belong to children. They have been there so long that mineral has grown into the whorls of the fingers and preserved them. They face the wall, as if the hands were pushing something back, or pushing in.

Deep in the room, wedged into a niche, sits a lamp: a shallow bowl of fired clay, crudely pinched, with a twist of plant fiber lying in one lip. The bowl is full of oil that has never been burned. The fiber is white and unblackened. It has been set out carefully and left dry. Whoever made it carried fuel up the mountain and decided, at the last moment, not to light it. There are three more lamps in other niches. All are full. All are unlit.

Near the back there is also a length of knotted cord, sinew or bark, strung from one crystal column to another across the narrowest part of the room. The knots are in groups. Beaded with moisture, the cord hangs slack and unfrayed. It is a tally, a guide-rope, or a prayer, and its meaning is lost with whoever tied it.

**How the place holds**

Nothing here changes. The temperature does not rise or fall with day or season, since no day or season reaches this far into the stone. The water enters and leaves at the same rate, evaporating from the crystals as fast as it condenses on them, so the pools stay at one depth and the films at one thickness. The hum keeps its pitch. The drips keep their scattered tick. The fungus creeps so slowly that the lobes you touched today will lie in the same shapes when your grandchildren's grandchildren crawl in. The marks the visitors made, the pebbles and the prints and the unlit lamps, have not eroded either, because nothing erodes here. The rock is young and the place has settled into a balance so complete that it feels less like a landscape than a held breath.

When you back out through the narrow throat, the cooler and drier air of the mountain hits your face like a slap. Only then do you realize you had lost your edges. Your hands, when you find them again, are wet to the wrists, and your palms are crosshatched with fine cuts from the crystals. Behind you the room goes on ticking in the dark, exactly as it did before you came.
