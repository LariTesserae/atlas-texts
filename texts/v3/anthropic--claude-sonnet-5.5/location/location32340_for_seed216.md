# The Undercroft of the Green Plateau

You arrive by climbing, and the climb is the last honest thing. Below the plateau's rim the slopes are ordinary: wet ferns, black rock, mist that moves the way mist should. Then you come over a lip of stone so worn it has the softness of soap, and the ground becomes something that was decided on.

The plateau is a little over three thousand meters up and larger than any single view can assemble. It has its own weather. A lid of cloud rests on it permanently, a slow grey-green ceiling that breathes in from the lowlands each afternoon and out each dawn. Under that lid the air is warm, close, and dim, the temperature of a held hand. Light arrives already filtered through a hundred thousand leaves and reaches you as something closer to memory than sun.

## The Made Ground

Nothing here is bare. Nothing here is unmade, either.

The floor is a basketry of living roots, laid in courses like brick and thick as a thigh, packed between with black peat and clay. It flexes slightly underfoot. In places the courses have been bent into shallow terraces to hold standing water. Elsewhere they rise into ramps and stairs whose treads are single slabs of buttress-root worn to a dip in the middle. Even what looks like outcrop is built. The grey knuckles of stone you glimpse are cairns, stacked and chinked with moss so long ago that the moss has become the stone's skin, and the skin has begun to grow its own small forests.

Above the floor, the trees have been persuaded. Trunks were split at the sapling stage and grafted across to their neighbors, then bent and pegged until they healed into arches. The arches were raised in ranks, and the ranks make naves. A single nave may run a full day's walk, its ribs curving up ninety meters or more into the dimness and lacing at the top into a roof of interwoven limbs, with epiphytes hung from it in curtains. Between the ribs, panels of woven bark and lashed cane fill the gaps. The lashings are cord of twisted fiber, still tight after centuries because the fiber took root in the wood it binds.

You can read the tools in the cuts. Adze bites from flaked obsidian, with the small shell-scallop of each strike. Drilled pegholes, bored by hand with a bone awl and a bow-drill, slightly off true where an arm tired. Fire-hardened stakes. Hammerstone dimples. A whole civilization's worth of carpentry done without a single true blade of metal, patient and enormous.

## Emptiness at Scale

This is the strange proportion of the place: everything is built, and almost nothing is *in* it.

The naves are hollow. The great galleries, terraces, and stair-shafts have the scale of cathedrals laid end to end across a continent, and in most of them you are alone with a few hanging things. A single strand of luminous moss. A slow drip. A line of pale fungus along one rib like a rule scored with a knife. You can walk three days along a colonnade and find that its far end is only another beginning. Doors open onto rooms that are simply *more of the same*, at slightly different pitches of gloom.

Whoever built this built for a population that never arrived, or that has thinned to a rumor. Perhaps a few thousand people once moved through the plateau. It would have been like a handful of beads rolling around inside a cathedral. Now you find the traces: hearth-rings of blackened clay, a drying rack still hung with something that is now only a pattern of dust, a child's toy carved from a seed-pod and left on a threshold.

## Water

The plateau is wet without being stormy. Water lives at the surface of things. The root-floor weeps at every seam, and shallow terrace pools sit still and dark, collecting the small light. The vaulted trunks sweat in the afternoon, and by evening every ribbed surface is beaded. Rain comes as a prolonged fine fall out of the cloud lid, so soft you hear it as a change in the air's texture more than as drops. Channels are cut along the nave floors, carved grooves the width of a palm. They carry the runoff in braided threads, always toward the same few great cisterns, hollowed from living trunks the size of houses. The cisterns hold water so still and clear that the vaults above seem to hang in it like a second architecture, upside-down and slightly more complete than the real one.

## Life

The fauna is abundant, though you feel it more than see it. It is abundant the way a forest floor is abundant: every crevice inhabited, every plank of bark tenanted. Glass-winged moths as long as your hand rest in pairs on the ribs, opening and closing like slow books. Snails with translucent shells climb the weeping walls, grazing the film of algae, and leave silver ladder-marks that other creatures follow. Long-limbed tree-dwellers with pale, patient faces move hand over hand through the roof-weave, feeding on epiphyte bulbs, seldom coming down. Something like a heron, grey and mud-legged, stalks the terrace pools and stands for an hour on one leg. Beetles the size of thumbs roll the seed-husks of the fallen fruit along the gutters like tiny laborers. At night, tiny frogs with luminous throats sing one note each, spaced so widely apart that you hear the silence between them as clearly as the note.

Predators exist, but they are slow and rare and mostly ambush: a cat-sized thing with a long tail that lies along a rafter for days. A carnivorous vine that hangs its slack loops across the gutters and closes on whatever comes to drink. Kills are quiet. The place has arranged itself so that nothing much is disturbed by them.

## Sound

Very little. The moss and the packed peat swallow footsteps. Voices die a body-length away. What remains is small: the tick of settling wood, the drip in the cisterns, the dry stitch of an insect walking on a leaf, and occasionally a long low groan as some great arch adjusts to the weight of a season's growth. Sometimes, at the edge of hearing, there is a rhythmic tapping, like an adze on green wood. It is patient and regular, and you never find its source.

## The Breakdown

That tapping is the first sign. The rest come slowly, and by the time you have noticed them you have already accepted them.

The plateau is still being built, and no one is building it. Cut-marks appear in wood that was smooth yesterday. New lashings show up on old joints, and the knots are correct. Watch a vine long enough and you will see it reach for a gap in a panel, pause, and bind itself there in a series of movements that are too deliberate: a mimicry of a hand, a memory of craft the plants took up when the hands went away. They are still framing rooms no one will enter. They repeat the gestures, and the gestures are slowly drifting from their purpose. Some of the newest stairs climb to nothing. Some arches are joined to their own reflections.

Other rules begin to loosen.

Counting is unreliable. A flight of stairs has thirty-one treads going up and twenty-eight coming down, and neither number is stable on a second attempt.

Distance is not conserved. Two galleries a day apart may share a wall. A landmark you passed at noon is ahead of you at dusk.

Rain sometimes falls upward, in specific naves. It rises from the floor mesh in fine bright threads and gathers on the ceiling, where it beads and falls back down as ordinary drizzle, a loop that has run for so long the moss along its path is arranged in bands.

Reflections in the cisterns occasionally disagree with the room above. They show a vault with its ribs unfinished, or a vault full of people, small and busy and dim, working with adzes and cord. The images last a few breaths and then settle, and the water is only water again.

Shadows lag. Move your hand and its shadow follows a moment later, patiently, as if considering.

And the light, which you took for the ordinary green dimness of a canopy, is not falling from above at all. It is *seeping* out of the wood itself, out of the lashings and the pegs and the polished treads, as though the material remembers being lit by a fire that has long been out and keeps producing the memory of its glow.

## Time

The place is old and settled into itself. Erosion has softened every edge into something like habit. Steps are dished, ribs are furred, cut-marks are rounded into the grain. It is not decaying. It has reached the stage where decay and growth are the same process, where a fallen arch becomes the foundation for a new one, and the city, if it is a city, is continually rebuilding itself out of its own dead.

If you stay long enough, you may start to hear the adze tapping again and find, without deciding to, that you are walking toward it. You will not arrive. But you will pass through a dozen naves you have already crossed and find them subtly finished, a little more complete each time. And you may suspect, as you go, that the builders are not gone at all, that the place is still being made, one patient stroke at a time, and that you have been walking inside their work, and that you are, gradually, part of it.
