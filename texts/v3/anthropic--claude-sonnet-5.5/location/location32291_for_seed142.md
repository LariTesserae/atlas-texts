# The Level Country

The plateau is so high and so old that nothing is left on it to fall. Everything that could be worn down was worn down before there were words for it. Mountains became hills, hills became swells, and the swells became a floor. The floor runs on in every direction, a dark, faintly convex tableland of fused grit and rounded cobbles. Over the last few hundred million years the cobbles have been ground so uniformly that they fit together like a mosaic of eggs. Walking on it is like walking on the inside of a very large, very tired hand.

There is no sun to speak of. Whether the world turned away from its star or the star went out, nobody living remembers. The air is thick and warm, about the temperature of a body at rest, and it holds a suspended dust so fine that it has not settled in ages. It has nothing to settle for, because nothing disturbs it. No wind moves it. No gradient pushes it. The dust hangs in the dark like a thought no one is finishing. Above it there are no stars, only a ceiling of nothing with faint pressure in it.

You see by the little the place gives you. Furnace mouths glow a low, sullen red, the color of the inside of an eyelid. Scattered mineral seams in the ground hold a cold green phosphorescence, left over from some ancient charge and never quite spent. Neither light reaches far. Each one draws a small room of visibility, and beyond it the dark is total and patient.

## The Settlements

The cities are far apart. Between them the plateau is bare for a day's walk, sometimes several. Each city comes into view as a heat before it comes as a shape. The air thickens and softens. Then a red smudge appears on the dark, and under it a skyline of stacks, gantries, cooling towers, and long horizontal sheds.

They are industrial in the full old sense: boilers, rolling mills, drive shafts, belts as wide as roads, pistons the size of houses. Nothing about them is delicate. They were built in layers. The lowest strata, now half-fused with the plateau itself, are cast in a dull grey alloy that has taken on the pitted texture of the rock. Above that are riveted plate and brick, then later work in pale ceramic and pressed glass. The layers do not quarrel. Millennia of building have settled into one grammar of heavy shapes, and the erosion that flattened the mountains has softened even the steel. Edges have rounded, and rivet heads have swelled and merged. The stacks lean at no angle at all, because leaning would have been a change, and change has stopped.

The machines are still running. That is the strangest thing, and the place is unbothered by it. In every city the great engines turn at a single unvarying load. No one adjusts them. No one seems to have adjusted them for a very long time. Belts carry ore from sheds where no ore is mined, into furnaces that pour ingots into molds, and the ingots are lifted by cranes and carried back to the sheds, where they are melted and returned to the ore. The loop is closed and complete. Nothing is made and nothing is lost. The cities are not producing anything. They are holding a shape, the way a held breath holds a chest.

## Sound

It is mostly quiet, and the quiet has a floor under it. Under everything there is one very low tone, felt in the sternum more than heard. It is the sum of all the engines in the city, blended so long ago that they no longer sound like machines. They sound like a property of the air. Nearer the works there is a soft rhythmic sigh of steam through valves, always the same length and always the same interval, so regular that after a while you stop hearing it and only notice when you leave its range and the silence rushes in.

Out on the open plateau the silence is nearly complete. Your footsteps make a dry, clean click on the cobbles and then vanish, absorbed by the dust in the air. You can hear your own pulse. Sometimes, at great distance, you catch a single held note from a city you cannot see, the tone drifting across the level country like the edge of a bell.

## The Rules

Nothing here has a gradient. The temperature is the same at the city wall as at the far edge of the dark. The pressure does not vary. The air does not stir. The furnaces glow the same red they glowed a thousand years ago. Because of this, nothing here ever arrives. Weather does not come, and days do not turn. The ordinary machinery of change, difference driving flow, has been switched off across the whole tableland, and what is left is equilibrium.

A person who arrives here, and few do, finds their own body the only source of change. Breath mists and vanishes. A dropped tool falls once and stays where it lands, and the dust around it does not even shiver. Footprints, in the rare patches of loose grit, will still be there when the last engine stops.

Water is nearly absent. It exists only as a rumor in the rock. Some pale hydrated minerals, sweating faintly in the deepest cellars of the oldest city, gather a bead of moisture once in an age. The beads are treasured by the few things that need them, and nobody knows what those things are.

## What Lives Here

Very little. The plateau has no plants, and it does not seem to have had any. The old rock shows no sign that green things were ever tried. But something has found a footing in the machinery.

In the warm corridors between the boilers there are pale, many-jointed creatures the length of a forearm, blind and nearly translucent, moving slowly along the steam pipes. They feed on the small, lingering differences of heat at the joints and valves, the last gradients left in the world. They cling to the metal and drink the warmth from it, and where they have clustered the pipes are dulled and faintly cooler. They do not seem to reproduce, or if they do, no one has seen it. The count of them in any city stays the same.

Farther out, on the bare plateau, there are rumors of something larger. It walks slowly on long, hard, ceramic-looking limbs between the cities, and it stops for hours at the mineral seams to sit in the green glow. Travelers who have seen it say it is not afraid and not curious. It looks at you as a stone would look at you if a stone had learned patience. It is rare, and it is always alone.

## Toward the Center of a City

If you walk into one of the cities, the buildings close in gradually, and the red glow grows from a smudge into a wash on the walls. The streets are wide and level and mostly empty. Now and then there is a lit doorway, a sealed window with a lamp behind it, a bench worn smooth by no visible use. You will see no one. There are signs of habitation everywhere: cups on ledges, coats on pegs, a tool left balanced on the rim of a barrel. Everything is in its place and has been for a very long time, and none of it is dusty, because dust has nowhere to go and nothing to move it.

In the middle of each city there is a plaza, and in the middle of the plaza the largest engine of all: a flywheel as tall as a tower, turning so slowly that its motion can only be told by watching a mark on its rim, and even then you have to wait. It is turning. It has always been turning. The whole plateau, with its dark and its warmth and its level miles, seems to hang from that one slow revolution, the way a held note hangs from the breath that started it.

You can stand beside it. It is warm on your face, and it makes no sound you can name. Behind you the cobbled country runs out into the dark, flat and unchanging, and in front of you the wheel goes on turning, and nothing, anywhere, is about to happen.
