# The Chorus at Harrow Flat

The mountains here were worn down so long ago that nothing is left of their violence. The plateau sits at the height where clouds usually are, and its summits have been ground into long, shouldered domes of dark granite, rounded like the backs of sleeping animals. Nothing is sharp. The passes are wide shallow saddles, and the ridgelines look as though the land has settled into a chair it has occupied for a billion years. What's left is a high, cold, soft-edged country under a sky that is often quite clear and bright, with light that has no dust in it and casts every shadow crisply.

In the lap of these domes lies a bowl about a mile across. This is the Flat, and it holds the town.

## The Town

Nobody built it. No one has ever carried a stone here or cut one. But it is a town by any honest measure: hundreds of hollow spires and low vaulted blocks stand in loose rows, with lanes between them, junctions, wide plazas, and stepped terraces where the ground falls toward the center. It has districts. The spires on the north rim are tall and thin as organ pipes, and the ones near the bottom are squat and fat, like kilns.

The material is a pale sinter, a mineral deposited in layers. Springs rise warm from deep in the old rock, carrying dissolved lime, and where they touch cold air they lay down stone. But the springs don't do this alone. Every surface is skinned with a thin, tawny, gelatinous mat that the locals of any species would call the *masons*, if there were locals to name it: a colonial film of microbes that steers the deposition. The mat likes verticals. It coaxes water upward by capillary sheets and lays stone at the edges, so that a seep becomes a rim, then a lip, then a tube, then a chimney, then a spire. Each spire is hollow, with walls a hand's breadth thick, and inside are chambers, sometimes several stories of them, floored with pooled water.

The result reads as architecture because the same forces that shape a city shape it. Water wants to be somewhere, and the mat wants to be wet, so the lanes are drainage and the plazas are catchments. Doorways are places where a spire broke open and the mat healed the edge into an arch. Whole streets have been closed by growth, and others opened when old walls, dry and dead, collapsed into white rubble.

## Emptiness

For all that, the town is nearly empty. It could hold thousands and holds almost nothing. You can walk a lane for a quarter of an hour and meet only the wind. The plazas are broad lawns of cushion moss, and each has one thing in it, a lone dwarf tree or a boulder or a shallow steaming pool. The space between the presences is the main feature. The spires stand well apart, and between them the sky comes down to the ground.

## Green in the Cold

The cold is real. Frost rims every pool at dawn, and the wind has an edge of ice in it even at midday. But the place is lush, in the stubborn, compressed way of high wet country. Moss lies over everything in cushions a forearm deep, in colors from lime to a red like old wine. Ferns crouch in the lee of the spires. Cushion plants form hard green domes with tiny white flowers pressed flat against them. On the terraces below the town grows a thicket of twisted, red-barked trees no taller than a doorway, their leaves small, waxy and dark, their trunks bearded with lichen and hanging moss. Where warm water runs off the sinter, bright ribbons of algae, orange, green and rust, streak down the terraces, and the steam that rises from them condenses on the moss and drips.

Moisture is intermittent but generous when it comes. Cloud pours over the domes in the afternoon and settles into the bowl like milk into a cup. Then it drizzles, or sleets, or, some evenings, hails with a noise like gravel thrown on a tin roof. By morning it's gone and the sky is hard blue.

## The Sound

This is the loudest quiet place you'll ever stand in. The spires are hollow, and they are tuned by accident, each with its own length and bore, each with slits and mouths worn through where old walls thinned. When the wind crosses the bowl, and it nearly always does, the town speaks. The tall northern pipes hold a low, wavering drone you feel in your sternum. The mid-sized spires sing thin, reedy notes that slide up and down as gusts change. The squat kiln-shaped ones boom when the wind hits their mouths, a soft hollow *whoomp*, like a great drum struck with a padded hand.

None of this is random noise. Because the spires have grown to shapes that channel water and air together, the whole town settles toward a rough harmony, a loose chord that shifts with the weather. In still, cold dawns it's a nearly inaudible hum. In a storm, it becomes a roar of overlapping tones so large that shouting is pointless. Under the wind lie the water sounds: drips echoing in chambers, trickles running along channels, and every few minutes the deep resonant *bloop* of a large drop falling into a deep cistern, amplified by the tube above it until the spire gives off a sound like a struck bell.

## Motion

The place is never still. The mat advances a few finger-widths in a season, and the town's shape drifts. You can watch nothing move, and yet a lane you walked last year is a wall this year. Steam rises constantly from the warm outflows and is shredded by the wind into long white banners. Cloud shadows race across the domes. Snowmelt runs in braided threads down the shallow valleys and vanishes into the mineral ground, only to reappear in the town as a spring. Frost forms and breaks. Twice a day, when the sun angle changes, the whole bowl flexes with light, the spires turning from chalk to gold to blue.

Every few years a big spire falls. It happens with a sound that crosses the whole plateau, a crack followed by a long, crumbling roll, and then the town's chord shifts, one voice gone, the others slowly retuning to fill the gap.

## The Rare Ones

Animals are few. A herd of small, thick-coated grazers, woolly and long-necked, moves through the plazas at dusk, cropping moss. They seem unbothered by the noise and travel in single file, breathing plumes of steam. A dark, heavy-bodied bird nests in the upper chambers of the highest spires, and its call, a slow descending whistle, is the only sound in the place that seems to respond to the town, tuning itself to the drone. Smaller life is thick but hidden: beetles under moss, pale springtails in the warm runoff, a translucent worm that lives only in the pools where the mat is thickest and appears to eat it.

## Rules of the Place

- Warm water builds; cold air finishes. Growth is fastest at dawn, when the two meet at their sharpest difference.
- What the mat touches becomes stone. Anything left in a warm pool, a leaf or a feather or a bone, will be a pale sculpture in a season.
- The town sings the weather. Anyone who lives here learns to read the chord, the low drone of coming cold, the high thin whistle of clearing sky, the jittery stutter before hail.
- Nothing here is old in the way that stone elsewhere is old. The mountains are a billion years worn down, but the town is renewed every few decades, a young city on an ancient floor, always rising, always falling, always singing.
