# The Condensery at Low Sabel

The town sits in a shallow bowl of land barely above the water table. You know you have arrived when the ground stops being ground and becomes a lid. The last hundred paces of approach are boardwalk, tarred timber on iron stilts, and beneath it the black water shows through the gaps, warm as a bath and slick with a rainbow film that moves against the current. The air is thick enough to feel on the tongue. It tastes of hot pennies and rotting sweetness.

## The Bowl

The basin is perhaps four hundred paces across, ringed by low humped ridges of old sandstone that were once much taller. Long erosion has rounded them into loaves and softened every edge. They weep. Seams of pale water bead from the rock at every bedding plane and run down in threads, feeding moss and liverwort in green stripes down the stone faces. At the ridge feet, the threads gather into channels someone cut generations ago and then left to the water to finish. The channels have deepened into little canyons the height of a knee, undercut and mossy, and all of them run downhill into the town.

Above the ridges the sky is a low ceiling of cloud the color of dishwater. It never fully rains and never fully clears. What reaches the ground is a dim, greenish, shadowless light, filtered again through the canopy of tree ferns and broad-leafed climbers that have swallowed the upper stories of every building. Noon looks like a late afternoon in a deep well.

## The Vegetation

The plants are dense but not crowded, and there is a lot of standing water and bare black mud between them. Tree ferns stand in loose colonnades with trunks furred in rust-colored fibers. Between them, giant-leaved things like elephant ear and taro hang their slabs over the streets, each leaf big enough to shelter a cart, each dripping steadily from a drawn-out tip. Vines as thick as forearms spiral up the smokestacks. Pale bladderwort floats in the flooded lots. In the shallows stand stands of a reed with hollow, ribbed stems, and these are important, and I will come to them.

Nothing here flies, crawls, or sings. No insects hum. No frogs answer the wet. Puddles that ought to swarm with larvae are clear and still. The whole ecology is green and rooted, and it fills the animal silence with its own sounds.

## The Works

The town exists because the ground breathes. Through vents in the mud, from some hot body far below, steam and warm gas seep up all year round. Long ago, whoever settled here realized that the bowl was a natural still. Heat came up, cool ridge-water came down, and in the middle the two met and made something. Now everything in the town is built to catch that meeting.

The buildings are tall, narrow, and riveted, plated in iron gone orange and then green-black. They stand two and three stories on stilts or on cobbled islands, linked by walkways, catwalks, and gutters. Sparse settlement: a few dozen structures in all, with wide flooded gaps between them where you can see reflected pipework. Every roof is a collecting surface, a slanted sheet of tin or glass draining into a gutter that drains into a tank. Every tank has a spigot, a gauge, and a wheel. Pipes as wide as a torso run along the streets at knee height and are used as benches by nobody. They sweat constantly, and each is furred with ferns growing in the beads of condensate on their upper curves.

At the center is the Condensery: a broad hall with a great iron dome, and around it a ring of eight boilers, each roaring softly. Steam collects in the dome, cools against the ceiling plates, and falls in a permanent indoor rain that drains through grates in the floor and out into long copper channels. Where it goes from there, nobody seems to say. The water leaves the town downhill in pipes toward the south ridge, and the pipes vanish into a tunnel in the rock.

## The Sound

It is loud. That is the thing you carry away. The sound has layers, and you learn to separate them:

- The **boilers**, a low chest-deep thrum that you feel through the boards before you hear it.
- The **relief valves**, which scream at irregular intervals in different pitches from every part of town, each one a brief, bright, furious whistle that dies into a sigh.
- **Pistons and beam engines**, slow and heavy, working pumps in the mud. Their beat is roughly a heartbeat at rest.
- **Water**, everywhere: on tin, on leaves, in pipes, in the ridge-channels. A constant hard patter with gulps and belches under it.
- And the **reeds**.

## The Reeds, and the Strangeness

The ribbed hollow reeds in the flooded lots are not quite plants as you would expect. Their stems are pipes. Steam and warm gas move up through them from the mud, and at the tips they end in flared, pursed lips that whistle, honk, and moan. Each reed has its own note, set by its length and by how much wall it has grown. The lots of them together produce a wandering, chordal drone, dozens of voices swelling and dropping as the vents pulse. It is very nearly a music. It is the sound the town is named for, though the name is older than any record.

The strange thing is that the engines are tuned to the reeds. The pistons do not run on a fixed schedule. They quicken when the reed-chorus rises and slow when it falls, through sensing-rods sunk in the mud and wired to governors in the engine-houses. Nobody controls the town. The ground breathes, the reeds sing, the engines follow, and the pumps move water to the next place that needs it. When the vents lag, as they do for some hours nearly every day, the whole town falls into a low, moaning slowdown, valves sighing, lights browning. Then a surge comes up from below. Every reed shrieks at once and the works leap back to speed.

There is another oddity: no people. You see a coat on a hook, a stool, a lamp lit in a window, a mug half full of something that has grown a skin of green. Doors stand open. Ledgers lie damp and swollen. The gauges are recently wiped. If there are workers, they are out of sight, on the other side of the steam, in the same way that a heartbeat is always in the body but never in view. The town runs as though tended. Some ferns on the catwalks are pruned into neat cubes. Grates are cleared of leaves. Every morning, by the accumulated evidence, someone has passed through.

## Weather and Time

The day works in cycles of about an hour. Heat builds under the cloud and the walls begin to ping. Then the dome-rain starts falling inside, and the outer air fills with mist, and the fern crowns start to drip harder. Twice a day, at times not tied to any sun, a true downpour comes: warm, heavy, soundless for a breath and then a roar on every tin roof at once. The whole bowl fills its channels, the mud steams, and the reeds go quiet, choked with water. For a few minutes the only voice is the rain. Then the water finds its level, the reeds clear their throats, and the chorus starts again from one low note.

At night the light is dimmer still, and the only glow comes from a few lamps, from the red seams of the boiler doors, and from a pale phosphorescence in the moss on the weeping rocks. The town glows faintly green at the edges, and every surface is wet, and the sound never stops.
