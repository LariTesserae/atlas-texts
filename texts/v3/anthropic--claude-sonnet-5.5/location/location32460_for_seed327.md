# The Shelf of Loud Water

At the top of the range, where the mountains stop being ridges and become a single broken wall, there is a shelf of land tilted slightly toward the east. It is roughly a mile across, and it sits so high that the sky overhead is a deep, bruised blue even at noon. Below it on three sides there is nothing, only cloud, and under the cloud, days of descent. The shelf is green, which is the first wrongness. Everything around it is scree, ice-scoured slab, and old snow gone grey. The shelf is wet, soft, and shouting.

## The Ground

The floor is a sponge of moss and peat, a foot or three deep over fractured granite. It is bright green, olive, rust-orange where iron water seeps, and a black-purple in the hollows where a liverwort grows in flat, glossy plates. Walking on it is like walking on a mattress soaked in cold tea. Every step wells water into the print, and the print stays filled and shining until the moss slowly swells back.

The surface is combed into long soft ridges by meltwater, so the whole shelf looks faintly grained, like a hand-brushed pelt. Between the ridges run hundreds of threadlike streams, each a hand's breadth wide. They braid and rejoin and are all headed for the same place: the eastern lip, where the shelf simply ends.

Rocks stand out of the moss in low, rounded shoulders. They are old and smooth, their corners taken off long ago, and they *weep*. Water beads from hairline cracks along their faces and runs down in dark tracks, so each boulder looks freshly rained on even when the sky is clear. Their lower halves are furred with moss, their upper halves crusted with orange and grey lichen in perfect concentric rings. Nothing here is raw. The place has been exactly this shape for a very long time, and the erosion has settled into a slow, comfortable equilibrium of seep and freeze and moss-grip.

## The Sparse Green

The vegetation is lush, but it is not crowded. The lushness is in the carpet, and above the carpet the space is nearly empty. The great plants stand alone, each thirty or forty paces from the next, and each is the only tall thing in its part of the view.

**Pillar-rosettes** are the dominant giants. Each is a thick, single, unbranched trunk as tall as a person, wrapped in a thatch of its own dead leaves like a shaggy grey skirt, and crowned with a silver-white rosette of leaves furred so densely that dew stands on them in beads. At night the rosette closes over its heart like a fist. In the morning it opens with a faint creak. In the cold, the thatch holds enough warmth that small animals shelter inside it. Some of the older trunks are hollow and inhabited.

**Cushion-mounds** dot the ground between them, hard green domes the size of sleeping dogs. They are so tightly packed that you could stand on one. Each is a colony of a thousand tiny rosettes, and each tiny rosette holds a bead of water in its center.

**Blue tussock** grows in the drier ridges, a stiff grass that hisses in the wind. **Dwarf bamboo** forms knee-high thickets in a few sheltered pockets, its canes clicking against each other. Along the streams, a small, fleshy, yellow-flowered plant grows in dense mats, and it is the only real flower-color on the shelf, a shock of butter-yellow against the green.

There are no trees. But in the western corner, tucked against the cliff, is a single grove of twisted, dwarfed, moss-draped conifers, no taller than a doorway, their branches so lichen-heavy they look like grey antlers. They are hundreds of years old. The grove is the closest thing the shelf has to a room.

## The Sound

The shelf is loud, and the loudness is part of the geography. It is what the place is made of.

The main voice is water. The eastern lip is a rank of a dozen small falls where the braided streams pour off the edge and into open air. They do not land anywhere the ear can follow. The wind takes them, shreds them, and carries the spray back up in a perpetual glittering breath. The sound is a continuous rushing roar with a hollow, echoing undertone, because the cliff below is stepped and the sound ricochets.

Over that is birdsong, and there are always birds. Swifts and swift-like things by the thousand circle the lip in dense, screaming ribbons, feeding on the insects lifted on the updraft. Their calls are a constant electric chatter. In the pillar-rosettes live small brown birds with astonishingly loud voices, a repeating three-note ring like a struck pipe. At dawn they all begin at once, and each pillar seems to answer the next, so a wave of song sweeps west to east across the shelf.

Under that is the ground itself. Frogs, small and dark and mottled gold, live in the moss in numbers that make the whole carpet click and trill after rain. Beneath the peat the water chuckles in hidden channels. And the wind moves through the cliffs and the rock crevices at the shelf's back, where a row of natural chimneys and vents in the wall makes low organ notes that rise and fall with the gusts.

There are few places where you can speak and be heard. If you shout, the shelf swallows it. Anyone living here would learn to communicate by gesture, by touch, by sight, and by sound *made on purpose*: whistles pitched above the roar, or knocking stones.

## The Weird Part

The shelf breathes.

Once a day, usually in mid-afternoon, the air pressure shifts. The reason is not clear, though it may be the cliff vents, warm rock, and the mass of cloud under the shelf. The wind, which has been blowing up and out over the lip, slackens, holds for a long moment of near silence, and reverses. Cloud pours *up* over the edge, a slow white tide, and floods the shelf to knee height. For twenty minutes the world is white and soft and close, the falls go strangely muffled, and every bead on every leaf is suddenly bright. Then the cloud drains back down and the roar returns, as if a door had reopened.

The animals know this. Before the reversal the swifts drop in a great spiraling column, and the frogs go silent all at once. That silence, which is almost total, is the eeriest thing on the shelf. Every creature is waiting for the cloud to come in.

Also, the rocks hum. In the cold of early morning, if you put your palm on the largest boulders, you can feel a faint vibration, a low pulse, slower than a heartbeat. It is likely the falls and the wind driving the stone, and it makes the shelf feel less like a place and more like a thing with an inside.

## Fauna

The shelf is thick with animals, though they move through the emptiness in scattered groups.

- **Rock-hyraxes or their cousins**: fat, round, brown-furred creatures that sit in rows on the sun-warmed boulders, whistling. Their sentinel is always the one nearest the cliff.
- **A large grazing animal**, something like a small, shaggy, long-legged goat with a ruff of pale fur, moves along the ridges in herds of six or eight. They browse the tussock and drink from the streams.
- **A predator**: a lean, long-bodied, white-and-slate mustelid that hunts the hyraxes and the frogs, and above it a pale hawk with broad wings that rides the updraft over the lip for hours without a wingbeat. When it stoops, the entire shelf goes quiet for a heartbeat and then resumes at a higher pitch.
- **Insects**: enormous dragonfly-like things patrol the streams, bronze and blue, and iridescent beetles crawl the pillar-leaves, drinking dew.
- **Salamanders**, black with orange spots, in the cold pools by the rock seeps.

The ecology is fast and visible. Things are constantly being eaten and constantly hatching. Death here is loud too, a squeal and a rush of wings, and then the hawk is on a boulder with something in its talons and the moss is already closing over the scuffle.

## Weather

It is cool, never truly warm. In sun, the air is crisp, thin, and glittering, and the moss steams gently. But the weather turns in minutes. A fog bank slides up from the east, sleet sweeps sideways for a quarter hour, and then hard blue sky returns and every surface throws back light. Rainbows are so common that they are barely remarked on. The falls often have two or three at once, drifting in the spray.

Nights are close to freezing. The moss stiffens and crackles, the streams slow to a whisper, the pillar-rosettes clench, and a rime of ice forms on everything that faces the sky. By dawn the whole shelf is white, and when the sun crosses the far ridge, it thaws in a wave of sound, dripping, trickling, and popping, before the birds start.

## The Marks of People

Someone comes here, or once did. The traces are scattered and small.

- **Cairns**: a few, waist-high, stacked from flat stones, each capped with a single white quartz cobble. They stand along a faint route that crosses the shelf from the southwestern scree slope to the grove.
- **A hut**: one low, round, drystone structure built into the lee of a large boulder, with a roof of turf laid over whalebone-pale beams of old conifer. The doorway is so small you must crawl. Inside are a hearth ring of blackened stones, a sleeping platform of dried tussock, and a niche in the wall holding a clay lamp, a bone needle, and a neat coil of twisted grass rope. It smells of smoke and damp. There is no one in it, but the ashes are not old.
- **Stone-lined channels** cut into the moss, diverting a stream to a small circular basin where the water can be dipped without the peat clouding it. The stones have been set with care and are furred with green.
- **Sound-posts**: on the windward ridge, a line of tall thin poles of bleached wood, each hung with strings of hollowed bone and shell. In the gusts they clatter and chime, and it is possible they are a kind of signal, a way of speaking across the roar, or simply a way of joining in.
- **Scratched stone**: on one boulder near the lip, a pattern of spirals and notched lines pecked into the granite with a harder stone. Water runs down through the grooves and fills them, so the carving shines when wet.

Nothing here is metal except a single small copper blade, green with age, wedged in a crack of the hut's wall, as though left as an offering or forgotten.

## The Feeling of It

Stand at the center of the shelf and the emptiness is complete. It is a bowl of moss and water beneath a dark sky, with a few tall silver plants standing apart like figures in a field, and the hut small at the edge of sight. The cliffs behind are grey and vertical; the world ahead simply stops. All around you, unbroken, is noise: the roar of the falls, the ribbon-scream of the swifts, the click of frogs, the ringing of bone chimes, the deep note of the vents. It is an intimate place, and a hectic one, a garden hung in the sky with a great deal to say.

And in the middle of that noise, once a day, the cloud comes up over the edge, and everything holds its breath.
