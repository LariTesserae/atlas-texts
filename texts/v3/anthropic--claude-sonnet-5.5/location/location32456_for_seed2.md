# The Floor of the Long Basin

The floor is poured concrete, laid in slabs the size of city blocks, and it runs flat in every direction until the water turns it blue and then takes it. Nobody planned for it to be seen. The lamps are only for the crews. The sun does most of the lighting, forty meters of it, falling through clear cold water in slow bright ropes that braid and unbraid over the slabs as the surface moves. It is daylight, dimmed and green-tinted, with enough visibility to see a long way and to understand how little there is to see.

The slabs are young. Their edges are still sharp, and you can read the plywood grain of the formwork pressed into their sides. Rows of tie-holes run across the faces like stitching. Only a pale fur of diatoms has settled so far, and here and there a first scatter of barnacles, white and precise, along the lee edges where the current slackens. The seams between slabs are black, clean-cut, and wide enough to lose an arm in. Small fish live in them already, and something with long antennae.

## The Emptiness

Kilometers pass with nothing on them. A slab, then a slab, then a slab, and a survey marker every few hundred meters, a yellow-painted post with a bell on it that rings when the current turns. Then a long gray pause. The Basin was built for something large and slow, and most of it is room.

The structures, when they come, come from far off. First there is a darkening in the haze. Then a line resolves into a **gate wall**, a barrier of ribbed concrete rising sixty meters above the floor, well into the lit water, with gates the size of apartment blocks hung in its face. Beyond the wall the next basin lies a few meters lower, and the water there is a slightly different temperature and a slightly different color. You can see the boundary, a wavering seam where warm meets cold, like heat shimmer inverted.

## Sound

It is loud here, and the loudness does not come from any one place. Water carries sound four times faster than air and gives it nowhere to go, so the whole Basin hums like the inside of a struck bell.

The lowest layer is the **turbine arrays**, a grinding drone at the edge of hearing that you feel in your sternum. They stand in fields, hundreds of them, with rotors ten meters across turning at the pace of a man walking. Each blade passes its tower with a soft *whump*, and because no two rotors are quite in step, the fields breathe with slow interference patterns, swelling and fading.

Over that come the pumps, with a higher, wetter whine, and the dredgers, which are worse. A dredger is a tracked machine the size of a cargo ship, crawling along the floor with a toothed wheel at its front. It scrapes silt from the drainage channels and vomits it up a pipe. Its sound is a continuous crunch, punctuated every few seconds by a clang when the wheel bites something hard. You hear one long before you see it, and you keep hearing it long after it has passed.

The bells ring, and the hulls ring. Far up, the surface traffic makes a thin steady ticking of propellers. Under all of it the fish grunt, snap, and drum, because the concrete makes an excellent resonator and the fish have noticed.

## Life

The fauna is abundant, though it gathers where the structures are. The open floor is nearly bare. Structures shed food, and food draws everything.

Around the turbine towers there are shoals of herring so dense they cast shadows on the slabs, turning together in silver sheets, hundreds of thousands at once. Cod hang below them at the base of each tower, head-down in the lee, waiting. Larger things pass through the shoals in bursts: seals that came in through the outflow channels and never left, and porbeagle sharks, which patrol the tower fields in loose lines, reading the pulse of each rotor. Rays lie in the silt at the foot of the gate walls with only their eyes showing.

The vegetation is sparse and mostly deliberate. On the terraces near the pump stations there are **kelp lattices**, steel frames strung with rope, each carrying a curtain of brown kelp that streams sideways in the current. Rows of them run for a kilometer, planted to soak up nitrogen from the outfalls. Between the lattices some kelp has seeded itself on bare concrete, in thin dark tufts along the seams. Nothing else grows. A few sea squirts, some anemones, and a fine red algae in the shade of the gate walls make up the rest.

## Weather

The Basin makes its own weather, because it is large enough to.

Twice a day the **gates cycle**. On the ebb, the wall gates lift in sequence, one after another across ten kilometers, and the trapped water pours through into the lower basin. A current develops, first a lean in the kelp, then a steady push, then a river with a hundred-meter cross-section that flings silt in spirals along the floor and drags the shoals sideways in long ribbons. Everything alive has learned the schedule. Before the gates lift, the fish move to the lee of the towers. Before they close, the seals surface to breathe.

There are **thermoclines** that behave like fronts. A layer of cold water from the deep intakes slides under the warmer basin water and shears against it, and on the boundary long slow waves form, internal waves that travel for hours and never touch the surface. Light bends across them. When one passes overhead, the sun ropes wobble like a curtain stirred by a hand.

There are **silt storms**, too. When the dredgers work upcurrent, a brown cloud lifts off the floor and rolls downstream in a wall, and inside it visibility drops to arm's length while the noise stays the same. It passes in twenty minutes and settles as a fine dust on every slab, and the next ebb scours it into the channels again.

Above everything, the surface storms arrive as light. A squall crossing the Basin appears overhead as a dimming and a violent flicker, the ropes of sun scrambling into chaos, and then a heave that rolls the whole water column once, gently, like a breath.

## The Rules

Nothing here is wild and nothing is entirely tame. The Basin was built to move water and make power and grow kelp and hold fish, and it does all of these. It is still being finished. At the far margin, past the last survey marker, the slabs are still wet-gray and unweathered, with steel reinforcement bristling from unpoured edges and a floating rig overhead, its shadow crossing the floor like a slow cloud. The Basin has no shore, and its builders are still working outward, and the fish are already there ahead of them.
