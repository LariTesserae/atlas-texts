# The Finished Flats

The land is a floodplain, and every inch of it has been made by hand.

It takes a while to see this, because the first impression is only of green and glare. The sky is a white so even it has no sun in it, just brightness pressed down flat over everything, and under it the marsh runs out in all directions, hot as the inside of a mouth. The air is thick and sweet. Vapor stands in it without rising or falling. Then you look at the ground and see it was built.

## The ground

The plain is a stack of coiled clay. Each terrace was raised the way a pot is raised, with ropes of wet earth laid in spirals and pinched together, then smoothed with a thumb. The coils are still visible on the vertical faces, each as thick as a forearm, and along them run the marks of the makers: fingerprints, long drags of palm, the scalloped bite of a shell scraper, the crosshatch left by a bundle of reeds. The prints are sharp. No rain has softened them and no frost has cracked them. Each terrace edge is crisp enough to cut a hand, and each corner looks as if it were finished this morning.

The terraces step down toward the water in broad, shallow tiers, some a stride wide and some a hundred strides. Between them lie the basins, which are pressed bowls, some the size of a courtyard and some the size of a lake, lined with fired clay the color of a healed burn. They are full to the brim. Water sits at the very top of each rim and never spills. The surface is a black-green mirror that reflects the white sky as a second, dimmer white.

Rock exists only as worked stone. Slabs of pale limestone have been flaked, ground, and set on edge as breakwaters, boundary markers, and steps to nowhere, and their faces show the overlapping scars of stone hammers. There is a little metal, beaten copper: green-crusted rivets holding plank to plank, hammered rings set into causeways, a few flat blades lying in mud where somebody set them down. Nothing here is smelted at scale. Everything was worked by someone who leaned into it with their weight.

## The plants

The vegetation is lush, almost aggressive. Ferns arch over the basin rims with fronds taller than a person. Broad-leaved plants hang in tiers off the terrace walls, and vines drape the standing stones. Reed-beds go out into the shallows in dense, whispering blocks. Yet the plants are sparse in a way the growth does not suggest. They stand in islands and groves with long stretches of bare glazed clay between them, so the green looks placed.

It was placed. You notice this when you touch a fern and feel that each leaflet has been shaped. It is a fern that also has the character of a fern-model, a thing that carries its own making. Some leaves bear the pressed impression of the mold they were cast from, ridged and veined as if stamped, and they are alive anyway, drinking, turning a few degrees toward the light. A reed will show at its base the knotted twine that bound it into its bundle, and the twine has grown roots. A tree trunk carved with spiral patterns bears fruit, and the fruit has the same spiral carved into its skin.

This is the first crack in the world's logic. Here **the depiction of a thing and the thing itself are not different.** A shape made with the intent of a plant becomes a plant, and a clay frog made with the intent of a frog is a frog. The makers did not plant a garden. They sculpted one, and the sculpture came alive in the shape they gave it, and that was accepted as ordinary.

## The animals

Fauna is abundant, and all of it is crafted. Herons stand in the shallows with legs of bound reed and bodies of dried mud packed on a frame of split cane, their eyes bright inlaid shell. They fish. The fish are fired-clay lozenges glazed in silver and orange, and they flit through the water in schools thick as smoke. Frogs no bigger than a thumb, molded, thumbprinted on the belly, and slick with a real wet shine, cover the terrace faces in patient thousands. Dragonflies are wire-thin, their wings made of stretched membrane over copper wire. Larger animals move slowly across the flats, low, broad-backed grazers of woven withy and hide, their ribs visible as the willow frame beneath.

None are born or die. If you count the frogs on one wall, you get the same number every time. If you count the herons in a basin, the count is three. A grazer walks a slow circuit along the terrace edge, and the circuit is exactly as long as the day, and the day does not end. The fauna is abundant without being increasing. It is a full population held at a stable figure by nothing but the fact that it was made that size.

Finding the gaps in this equilibrium is how you learn the place is broken. A heron strikes at a fish and misses, and misses again, exactly the same way, with a small drop of water falling from its beak at the same point in the arc. It does not feel like a loop. It feels like something that has always been in the middle of happening.

## Sound

The quiet is thick. Most of it is the absence of wind, because the air does not move. What you hear are small dry noises: a soft tick as clay cools, though it never gets cooler, and a faint creak from a reed frame settling. Sometimes there is a low, humming hush, as when you press a shell to your ear. The frogs do not call. The herons are silent. If a creature makes a sound, it is a single soft note, like a finger on the rim of a bowl, and it does not echo.

Water falls in one place only: a spout in a stone gutter where a basin overflows into the next. But the water leaving the lip is matched by water arriving, and it makes a steady, thin trickling that you stop hearing after a few minutes, like your own pulse.

## Rules

Anyone who walks here quickly learns what the place will and will not do:

- Nothing dries. Every surface glistens with a fine sweat of condensation, the whole plain slowly weeping into itself.
- Nothing weathers. A footprint pressed into a terrace stays forever crisp, and yours will join the makers' prints.
- Nothing is found, only made. Any object you pick up shows the marks of hands, including objects that shouldn't have any: a cloud of gnats is a swarm of tiny pinched beads, and a stone in your shoe carries a thumbprint.
- Nothing is finished except in its stillness. The whole landscape holds the posture of a task just completed, and the makers are not visible anywhere.

## What remains

Far out on the plain, where the terraces flatten and the basins grow so wide their far shores blur into the white air, there is a single unfinished thing. It is a great clay ring, half-coiled, with the last rope of clay lying loose at one end and its ends still wet and torn where a hand let go. Next to it, pressed into the ground, are two deep handprints, each wide as a door.

The clay in them is still soft, and nothing has ever come to finish the ring or to leave.
