# The Whitefall Basin

You arrive from above, by a path that is really a long argument between a staircase and a landslide. The plain behind you is flat, colorless, and worn down to a final grain. Then it stops, and the ground drops away for four hundred meters in ledges the color of old teeth. The basin is thirty kilometers across. Nothing about it is dark.

That is the first surprise. It is a hole in the world, and it is the brightest place you have ever stood. The walls are gypsum and selenite, and the floor is pale salt crust over pale clay. The sun comes straight down into it and is thrown back from every face. The light has no clear source. Shadows are thin and blue and land in odd directions. Your eyes ache in a clean way, and you squint until the country resolves.

## The Floor

Age has done its work here. Every hill in the basin has been rounded to the shape of a knuckle, and every ridge to the back of a sleeping animal. Nothing sharp survives, and nothing has for a very long time. The rock has reached a settled shape, and it has dissolved and re-formed so often that its edges have forgotten they were edges.

Yet the floor is not still. Water, only a little of it, does the moving. It seeps from the lower strata in dark lines, sweats down the walls in glittering sheets, and collects in shallow pans a hand deep and a kilometer wide. The pans are milky green at the center and rimmed in crystal that grows visibly, a fringe of white needles lengthening over the course of an afternoon. Each morning the basin fills with fog from the seeps, low and thick, and it moves. Fronts of it slide across the flats and pile against the terraces, then tear open to show a different landscape from the one that was there an hour before. A pool has drained. A hollow has appeared where a mound stood. A line of crystal has bloomed across a path.

The basin is turbulent, but it is almost silent. The fog swallows sound, and so does the ground, which is covered for long stretches in a felted gray-white fungal mat as thick as a rug. The mat is the nearest thing to a plant here. It is cool and slightly damp underfoot, it holds footprints for minutes, and then it slowly exhales them away. What you hear is small: the tick of crystals growing, a low seep-drip, cloth moving on skin. Rarely there is a soft collapse far off, a sinkhole opening in the dark, and a long hush of settling dust.

## The Rule of the Basin

The world here has one law, and everyone in it lives by it: **what the water touches, the water may take.**

Gypsum dissolves. Rain falls perhaps a dozen times a year, in short violent curtains, and each one eats into the terraces, undercuts the ledges, and drops whole hillsides into new sinks. Nothing on the floor keeps its position for more than a few generations. The ground is unstable in slow motion, and in fast motion when the rain comes. On such days the basin performs its full turbulence. Brown torrents run down the walls and vanish into the floor with a sound like a great breath drawn in. Pans overflow and merge. Crust collapses in long cracking lines. Then it ends, and the fog rises out of the wreckage, and the light comes back brighter than before.

## The Cities

There are cities, if the word can hold something this loose. Eleven of them are known, each a scatter of districts a half-hour's walk from the next, with open salt flat and fungal meadow between. They are sparse, but they are densely made. Every wall is layered, because every wall has been rebuilt on top of the last one's collapse. The builders have no metal beyond hammered native copper and no wheel worth mentioning. They cut selenite into pale translucent blocks and set them in lime mortar, then plaster the surface with a fired-shell wash that repels water for a season. Their houses glow from within at midday, like lanterns made of ice.

They build low, with thick rounded walls, and put their doors on the uphill side so water passes them by. The streets are causeways of laid basalt cobbles, hauled from the plain above and set on beds of packed shell to stand above the seep-lines. In the oldest districts, the causeways stand a full body-height over the floor, because the ground around them has sunk. They run through the fog like the spines of enormous animals, and people walk along them in single file, carrying baskets and long poles.

Each district has a **tally wall**, a broad plastered surface where every household cuts a notch on the day their home was last repaired. A wall with many notches is trusted. A wall with none is abandoned, and no one will shelter behind it.

## The Animals

Life is abundant, and it is nearly all animal. In place of plants there are the mats, the pale shelf fungi on the walls, and a slick green film in the pans. Together these feed a crowded, quiet fauna.

- **Saltgrazers**, stilt-legged and knee-high, move in herds of hundreds across the fungal meadows. They step with great care, testing each footfall, and their hooves are broad and padded to spread their weight over the crust. They make no sound at all.
- **Glass snails** cover the lower walls in sheets. Their shells are so thin they look like drops of water clinging to the rock. At dusk they all glow faintly at once, as though the wall were breathing light.
- **Fog moths**, large as hands and the color of paper, rise from the seeps in the morning. They drift in slow swirling clouds and keep station within the fog banks, so that a bank moving across the basin looks like a living thing.
- **Cliff owls**, white and enormous, nest in the terraces. They fly without any noise, and it is a common belief that they carry off the notches of the dead.

In the towns, people keep saltgrazers, tend mat gardens, and gather glass snails in wide flat baskets. They speak softly, and often not at all, using hand-signs the color of the light. In a place this quiet, a raised voice is a rude and dangerous thing, because it might bring the ground down.

## The Strangeness

Two things are unusual. The first is that the basin brightens rather than darkens as you descend. The salt and selenite gather the light and concentrate it, so that the deepest pans hold a glare almost too fierce to look at. People who live there wear woven visors of dark reed and hang black cloth over their windows at noon.

The second is a habit of the ground itself. In the still hours after the fog burns off, low tones can sometimes be felt through the soles, less a sound than a slow pressure, moving from the edge of the basin toward the center. The people say the basin is *counting*. They stop, and stand, and wait for the tone to pass. Then, when it has gone, they go back to their repairs and cut another notch in the wall.
