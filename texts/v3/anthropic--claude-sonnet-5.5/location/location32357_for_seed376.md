# The Felted Uplands

Nothing here is bare. From the road-less saddle where you arrive to the far blue ridges, every surface is a living one. The ground is a foot-deep pelt of moss and cushion plant, and above it, on the old rounded shoulders of the hills, taller growths stand in a dense, shoulder-to-shoulder crowd. They are dwarf willow, tangled and knee-high, and something like a tree-fern that has learned to live in the cold. Their fronds stay furled in tight brown crosiers until midday, then open a little, then close again.

The colors are muted and layered: olive, rust, bottle-green, the grey-violet of old lichen, and here and there a sulfur yellow that marks where something underneath is warm.

## The shape of the land

The mountains are very old. Erosion finished its main work long ago and left the ridges as soft, low-shouldered swells, like sleeping animals under a blanket. There are no crags. Where rock shows at all, it is a knuckle of dark stone the size of a house, rounded and furred in lichen, and it looks more like part of the vegetation than what the vegetation grows on.

The valleys are wide and shallow, and they are held together by root. Streams exist, but you rarely see them. Water travels inside the mat, through channels of loose sphagnum and root-tangle, and you hear it only as a faint seep-and-tick when you kneel and put your ear to the ground. In a few places it surfaces as black, still pools, perfectly round and ringed with red moss. The air is damp but not wet. Mist forms in the hollows at dawn and beads on every filament of every plant, so the whole landscape wears a fine grey frost of droplets until the light burns it off.

It is cold, just above freezing at midday and well below it at night. Your breath shows. The frost never becomes snow-cover, because the plants are warm enough to keep it patchy, and each morning it rims the moss in white lace that is gone by ten.

## The Turning

This is a violent place, though the violence is slow, and sometimes not slow at all.

The mat is not stable. It is a layer that turns over. New growth piles up on the surface faster than the cold can decompose what lies beneath, and the weight and heat of that buried mass make it restless. Over years, whole hillsides ripple. Long lobes of moss and peat, each hundreds of meters across, creep downslope like slow tongues, folding themselves under at the toe and rising again behind. In the folds the old green disappears and a black, steaming underside rolls up to take its place. Within a season that black is furred with pioneers, first a haze of liverwort and then a green so bright it looks wet.

Then there are the failures. A slope saturated past its limit will suddenly let go. The mat tears along a seam, and a raft of living plant, roots and all, slides a quarter-mile in a few minutes, tilting, buckling, throwing up ridges of compressed turf like the wake of a ship. The bog-bursts are worse. A dome of peat swollen with trapped gas ruptures and hurls black slurry and torn plants into the air, and the dome collapses into a crater that by next spring is a lake, and by the spring after is a raft of floating moss you could walk across.

All of this happens in near silence. The plants swallow sound. When a hillside goes, what you hear is a long, low, papery sigh and a soft thud you feel in your shins more than your ears. Afterward the quiet closes again like water.

## Engines

The place has a machinery to it, and the word for it that comes to mind is industrial.

The decomposing heart of the mat is a furnace of slow fermentation, and it has vents. They are chimneys of packed, blackened moss, chest-high, that breathe out warm, sour steam in pulses every half-minute or so. Around each one, plants have organized into rings, each ring in a different phase of growth, so that the vent sits at the center of what looks like a tiered works. Nearest the steam are the fast, pale, fat-leaved growths. Outside them come the rust-colored mid-ring, then the dark willow thickets, then the ordinary pelt.

Some of the larger cushion-plants carry pressure in their tissues. Their swollen, ribbed domes hold water under tension and release it through valve-like slits with a faint hiss and a puff of vapor, then reseal, then slowly refill. Walking a slope at noon you pass through an irregular ticking chorus of these small releases, as if a great engine were idling just below the surface. Fronds along the ridges ratchet open in stiff, discrete increments, catching the light in jerks.

In the wet hollows there is a weak electrical hum, too faint for the ear, that you can feel as a prickle in the teeth if you lie against the moss. Certain fungal threads under the mat conduct current between roots, and the whole basin carries a low potential. Now and then, in the dusk, a pale flicker crawls over the damp pelt for a few seconds and dies.

## What remains of made things

Almost nothing is built. But if you walk long enough you find the exceptions, and they are very old.

A cast-iron drum, taller than you, lies half-sunk on a shelf of the hill. It is a boiler, or was. Moss has grown up its sides and into its seams, and it has become a planter. A little farther on, a line of rust-orange humps stretches across a slope: a rail bed, or the iron of one, so long buried that the moss has rearranged itself around the shape and now lifts and tilts with the mat's own turning. A toothed wheel as wide as a cart stands upright in a bog pool, its rim furred, its spokes threaded with willow. Nobody is here to say what any of it was for. The turning will take them all under eventually and, decades on, may bring one back up, blackened and steaming, to be roofed over in green again.

## Life that moves

Animals are rare. There are beetles, glossy and slow, that work the warm rings around the vents. A small, round, grey bird lives in the willow and speaks perhaps once an hour, a single thin note that seems to carry a very long way. Something like a hare, long-legged and wet-furred, crosses the open pelt at dawn and vanishes into the scrub. That is all you are likely to meet, and you will feel that you have been seen more often than you have seen.

## Light and the hour

The light is ordinary daylight, soft and grey-white through thin high cloud, with no hard shadows. It does nothing dramatic. But because the surface is so dense and so finely textured, every angle of it is lit: the underside of a frond, the wet crown of a cushion, the glint on the valve of a hissing dome. The hills glow faintly from within, the way a felted thing does when you hold it to a window.

Stand still at the top of the saddle and the whole region seems inert, a green, folded, sleeping country. Stay an hour. The tongue of moss across the valley has advanced by a hand's breadth. A vent sighs. A raft of turf on the far slope shifts, tips, and starts, without any sound you can name, to slide.
