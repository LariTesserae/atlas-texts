# The Breathing Cup

Nobody would find it by looking. You reach it by following the sound, which begins a quarter mile out along the ridge: a low, wet, intermittent groan, like a very large animal turning over in its sleep. The ridge is a spine of dark basalt furred in bromeliads, and the air up here is warm in the way of high tropical country, a soft, held warmth that comes from cloud sitting on the land. Then the ground falls away, and you are standing at the lip of the Cup.

It is about a hundred and twenty paces across, roughly round, and deeper than a house is tall. The walls are ledged and slumped and heavily weathered, not a fresh collapse. Whatever opened this hollow did so long ago, and everything since has been settling. Roots have found every seam. Ferns hang from the ledges in green curtains, and from the tips of the fern fronds hang single drops, each swelling and falling on its own slow schedule.

## The floor

The floor is a bowl of deep moss over black stone, and it is very open. Only seven trees stand in it, each far from the others, each enormous. They are a kind of tree-fern crossed with something else. The trunks are fibrous and rust-brown, ribbed like rope, and thick as wells, and they lift a canopy of fronds twenty feet across. Between them, the ground is a quiet carpet of liverwort, sphagnum, and small round-leaved plants that hold water in their cupped centers like eyes. The lushness is total, but it is low and patient. Nothing here crowds anything else. Each tree has clear ground around it the way cathedral pillars have clear floor, and light falls between them in tall pale shafts that seem to have been arranged.

The light is ordinary daylight, a bright overcast filtered green through the fronds. It is neither dim nor dazzling. Every leaf is beaded, and the whole floor has the shine of a place that has been rained on an hour ago and will be rained on again in an hour.

## Water

Water is everywhere but rarely in a hurry. The walls weep: fine threads run down the basalt from a hundred seams, gathering in the moss and vanishing into it. The moss is saturated to the point that stepping on it squeezes out a cold, faintly sweet-smelling water that closes over your boot. Small clear rills wander the floor, choose a direction, change their minds, and braid together toward the center.

At the center is the pool, and the pool is the reason for the sound.

It is round, forty feet across, and almost perfectly still, tea-colored over a pale floor. On its far side, the stone wall meets the water in a low arch, a black slot hardly wider than a door and half submerged. The pool breathes through it. Over the course of about a day, the water rises slowly, an inch every hour or so, drawn up from some deeper chamber, until it laps a pale ring of mineral on the stone. Then it holds. Then, all at once, the slot inhales. The water draws down in a long sucking rush and drops three feet in a minute, exposing a shelf of ribbed white rock, and the whole Cup fills with the groan you heard from the ridge, a bass note you feel in the sternum more than hear. It lasts perhaps a minute. Then the pool begins, without hurry, to rise again.

Between breaths, the place is not quiet. The drip is constant, and the drip is loud. Ten thousand drops fall from fern tips onto broad leaves, onto stone, into pools, onto the taut skins of certain large leaves that ring like small drums. The sound is a shifting rain of pitches, never quite a rhythm, and it fills the bowl the way sound fills a shell. Speaking in a normal voice, you'd have to lean close. The walls throw everything inward, so the Cup is always a little louder than the wind above it, which you can see moving the tops of the fronds but rarely feel.

## The ringing stones

Scattered on the moss are boulders the size of sitting people, glossy with wet, dark green-black. Struck, they ring. They are hollow, or layered in such a way that they behave as hollow, and each has its own note, a clear bell tone that lingers for several seconds and is picked up faintly by its neighbors. When the pool breathes out, the stones hum in sympathy, all together, a chord that fades over a minute.

Some of the stones have been used. Their upper faces are worn into shallow, bright dimples, the polish of countless strikes by hammerstones. A few of the hammerstones still lie beside them, fist-sized river cobbles that could only have been carried up from the valley far below, gone green with algae. Hanging from a low frond on the north side is a length of twisted bark cord with seven knots in it, the fibers dark and soft with age. Nothing else. No wall, no hearth, no path. Whoever came here came seldom, played the stones, tied a cord, and went away, and the moss has kept their marks the way it keeps everything, gently and without comment.

## The rules of the place

The Cup keeps some peculiar habits, which the slow years have made into law.

The moss on the eastern side grows in bands, pale and dark, following the pool's ancient high-water marks. Each band is a year, or perhaps a decade. No one has counted. The bands narrow as they climb, which suggests the breath is slowly weakening, or slowly deepening; the evidence goes either way.

Mist does not enter the Cup from outside. It is born there, condensing out of the warm still air above the pool each afternoon and rising in a single column that leans and unravels above the rim. By evening the column is gone. It has never once failed.

And the seven trees are aligned. Standing at the pool's edge, any one tree lines up with two others, like points of a slow star. Whether the trees found this pattern or the pattern grew the trees is not something the place will tell you.

## Life

Fauna here is rare and, when it comes, an event. There is one frog, no larger than a thumbnail, pale gold, that lives in the cupped centers of the round-leaved plants. It calls only in the seconds after the pool exhales, a single glassy chirp repeated from thirty places at once, then silence. Once in a long while a dark bird with a bare blue throat lands on the highest frond and stays a minute, looking down into the bowl with what seems like professional interest, then leaves. There are snails, slow and translucent, drawing silver lines across the stones. Nothing else stays.

## Toward evening

As the light turns from green to amber, the drips slow but do not stop. The stones cool. The mist column stands over the pool like a held breath, and you understand that the whole hollow is waiting, in its unhurried way, for the slot to draw in the water once more. When it does, the note rolls up out of the ground through your feet, the stones answer, the frogs answer the stones, and the Cup, having said what it has to say, begins again to fill.
