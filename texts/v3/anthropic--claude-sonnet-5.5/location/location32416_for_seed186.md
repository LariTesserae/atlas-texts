# The Slag Meadows of Ennis Verre

You arrive by the only way anyone could: upward, through cloud, until the cloud is below you and the dark is above.

The plateau sits at a height where the air should be thin and bare. Instead it is thick with green, a wet, close, sponge-deep green that looks black in the low light. The sky is a very dark indigo, always some hour past sunset. The sun does not rise here so much as lean, once in a long while, against the far edge of the world and give up. What light there is comes from the ground.

## Ground

The plateau is a broad, tilted table, many miles across, ribbed with old moraines and long shallow basins. Its bones are dark basalt and rust-red banded ironstone, weathered into rounded shoulders. Nothing is sharp. It has been standing here so long that every edge has been argued down into something settled: terraced slopes, smooth saddles, boulders sunk to their hips in moss. Wind and meltwater have worked it into a tired, comfortable shape.

The soil is not soil. It is a dense, springy felt of moss, root, and mineral grit, knee-deep, cold as a cellar, and it squelches faintly when you step. Where it thins you see the iron underneath, orange and wet.

## Water

Water here is a presence you never quite see arrive. There is almost no rain. Instead the cloud sea below sends up fingers of mist that climb the slopes at intervals, and everything that stands in them beads and drips. Condensation is the weather. Every leaf carries a bead on its tip, and every bead holds a tiny inverted copy of the glow around it.

Small pools sit in the hollows, black and perfectly still, each rimmed with rust-colored film. A few threadlike streams run between them, so shallow they are more a shine than a flow. They carry iron in solution and stain the moss where they pass. Elsewhere it is simply dry, in the way a frost-bound place can be dry, with the damp locked in the plants and not lying about.

## Plants

Lush is the wrong word until you crouch, and then it is the only one.

**Bellows-ferns** grow in the basins, waist-high, their fronds curled like closed fists. Slowly, on a rhythm of about a minute, they unfurl and refold, drawing in mist and exhaling a warm, mineral-smelling breath. Each basin of them moves together, so the whole hollow seems to be sleeping.

**Lantern moss** carpets the north-facing stones. It gives off a pale blue-green glow, the color of a distant lamp seen through ice. It is the main lightsource on the plateau, and it is faint enough that you learn to see by it as by starlight: shapes first, then edges, then slowly the color.

**Copper-cedar**, low and gnarled, has needles sheathed in a green-blue patina. They grow in stands, thick-trunked but no taller than a person, their roots gripping the ironstone like fingers. Their bark is warm to the touch. You could shelter your hands against one.

**Cushion-orchards** are hard domes of tightly packed leaf, each the size of a barrel, with small pale flowers set into them like buttons. They exist in the thousands, in loose herds across the slopes, and they hold water like sponges. Push a hand into one and it comes back wet and stinging cold.

## The Working

Here the seed of the strangeness takes root, and the place becomes more itself. This is a land of industry without any builder.

The ironstone is being *processed*, and not by wind or time. The whole plateau is a slow, distributed factory run by life.

In the low basins live **furnace-mats**, thick, ropy colonies of microbes that lie on the ironstone like wet leather. They are warm, actually warm, a few degrees above the air. They eat the rock and sweat out iron in a fine red-black paste. Follow the paste downslope and you find the next stage of the line.

**Slag-beetles**, the size of a fist and blunt as river stones, gather at the mats in dozens. They scoop the paste into their mouths and press it out again, behind them, as small dense pellets that they roll in slow processions toward the ridges. Each pellet is faintly hot. They stack them in long low windrows, and over years the windrows harden into ingots of nearly pure iron, dull gray and cool to the touch, lying in the moss in ranks.

Nothing collects the ingots. They just accumulate along the slopes, straight lines that look almost like fences or rails. The lines are not built. They are excreted. The land is scored with faint geometry, regular and purposeful and utterly unplanned, and it looks like the ghost of a mining works that never had any miners.

Once, in the middle of the plateau, you find the one thing that looks truly made: a long arch of iron, taller than you, half sunk into the felt, its surface pitted and beaded with lantern moss. It is a ribcage. Something enormous lay down here, and the beetles and mats worked it over slowly, mineral into mineral, until it became indistinguishable from the ore. It is not a structure. It is a very large leftover.

## Animals

The fauna is abundant, and nearly silent.

**Pale grazers**, long-legged and fleece-heavy, move across the slopes in herds of forty or fifty. Their coats are wet and grayish, thick as blankets, and threaded with a lichen that glows dimly. From a distance a herd is a drifting constellation. They eat cushion-orchards and lick the ingots, drawn by the salt. Their hooves are wide and soft. They make almost no sound.

**Vent-wrens**, tiny birds with dull copper feathers, nest against the warm bark of the cedars and live on the small midges that swarm in the bellows-ferns' breath. They do not sing. They flick and click.

**Hollow-stalkers** are the predators: tall, patient, thin creatures with a body like a bundle of dark cords and a head like a closed lantern. They stand motionless among the cedar stands for hours, so still that moss begins to catch on them. When a grazer wanders near they open the lantern, a sudden cold flash of white light that stuns the herd's eyes. Then they are among them. The dark returns quickly. Afterward the herd re-forms, smaller, and the stalker stands very still again, glowing faintly where it has fed.

Between them all move smaller lives: mist-moths that drink the beaded water, threadworms that string the ferns, and a mottled amphibian that sits in the rust-colored pools and turns slowly from orange to black as the mats warm and cool.

## Sound

The plateau is quiet in a specific way. You hear:

- the tick of condensation falling from leaf to leaf,
- the low, slow *huff* of the bellows-ferns, drawn out and breathy,
- the soft click and shuffle of beetles on their processions,
- a rare, distant *clang*, like a bell struck once, when an ingot settles in its bed,
- and under it all, if you hold your breath, a deep hum in the rock, the sound of the furnace-mats working.

A voice, if you used one, would carry too far. You start to whisper without deciding to.

## Cycles

Nothing here is still, but everything is slow. The mist climbs each evening and retreats each dawn, or what passes for dawn. The ferns breathe in their basins. The beetles move their pellets a few yards a day. The herds circle the plateau over a season, following the ripening of the cushion-orchards. Stalkers stand and wait, and the moss creeps up their legs.

Over years the iron lines lengthen, the ribcages settle, the mats shift to fresh stone, and new basins begin to breathe. It is a landscape that is always at work and never in a hurry, cold and wet and green in the dark, making metal out of the mountain, and leaving it lying where it falls for no one at all.
