# The Brimful

The land here has not finished being made, and it has stopped waiting to be.

It is a flat basin, a hand's breadth above the level of a warm sea you can't see but can smell: brine, iron, and something like boiled leaves. The ground is a staircase of shallow terraces, each a few feet high, each holding a pool to its lip. The rims are pale mineral, laid down by the water itself, and they are still raw. Their edges are sharp enough to cut a palm. Nothing has softened them. There are no pebbles, no sand, no silt and no talus, because nothing has yet worn away to make any. The rock is all first surface, cream and rust and a bruised grey-violet, glazed with a film of water so thin the stone looks lacquered.

The terraces run in long, uneven contours, hundreds of them, a tiered country that stretches out past sight in every direction. It is not a valley but a region, a whole province of stepped water. Each pool is exactly full. Water seeps up through the floor of the uppermost tier, warm as blood, spills over the rim in a continuous unbroken sheet, and feeds the next tier down. This has been the arrangement for as long as anything has been here. The rims grow at exactly the rate the water would otherwise cut them. The pools neither rise nor fall. If you drop a leaf in one, it drifts to the lip and lies there, pressed against the spill, turning slowly, never quite going over.

## Air

It is hot in the way of a closed mouth. The temperature doesn't vary between day and night or by season, and no wind comes to relieve it. The air is so saturated that surfaces don't dry. Your skin never quite sweats or quite stays dry, only slicks. Above the canopy hangs a low ceiling of steam that never lifts and never thickens. It scatters the sun into a dim, sourceless amber-green, like light through a bottle. You cannot tell the hour, and the hour doesn't change. Shadows are faint smudges with no direction. Everything sits in the same permanent late afternoon.

Water condenses on everything. Leaf tips bead and drip, and the drip lands on the next leaf, so that the whole forest carries a fine, endless internal rain that never reaches the ground as weather. It never falls from the sky at all, just passes from surface to surface downward through the layers.

## Growth

Nothing has been given room, because nothing has needed to leave any. The vegetation is packed into every cubic foot it can reach.

Tree ferns with trunks like wet rope stand shoulder to shoulder, their crowns overlapping into a lattice of fronds. Between them rise jointed reeds forty feet tall, hollow and faintly ringing when they knock together. Cycad-like stumps, squat and armored, hold up gardens of their own, and every branch and stump carries a second layer of moss, then a third of orchids, then a fourth of filmy translucent ferns growing on the orchids. Vines lace the whole thing, thick as thighs near the ground and fine as hair at the top, so the forest doesn't feel like separate plants at all but like a single tangled textile hung in the air.

On the terrace rims, where the rock is newest, the pioneer growth is mats of red and orange algae and cushions of dense, springy moss. They grip the sharp stone but never bury it. The rims stay visible, pale lines drawn through green. In the pools, floating plants cover the surface in overlapping discs the size of shields, and beneath them the water is black and warm and thick with slow stems.

The ground between pools is a mat of roots and rot and living green, springy underfoot and always slightly wet. Where it gives way you sink to the shin in tea-colored water. Nothing here is dry enough to burn, and nothing is bare.

## Creatures

The fauna is not so much abundant as ambient. It is the medium.

Insects rise off the pools in columns, and the columns never thin. There are damselflies with wings like stained glass, beetles the size of a thumb clicking in the bark, and clouds of midges hanging as slow, breathing shapes. Dragonflies with a wingspan wider than your forearm patrol the terraces along fixed routes, and when one passes another the two hover face to face for a moment, as if checking papers. Spiders string their webs in vertical sheets between the reeds, so many that the forest at a distance looks veiled in silver, and each web is crowded with beads of water and the bodies of the drowned.

Amphibians fill every pool to a density that makes the surface twitch. They are brown, gold and jade, some no bigger than a fingernail, some heavy as loaves. They sit rim to rim on the mineral lips, throats pulsing. Long-bodied salamanders with feathery red gills lie stacked in the deeper basins like dropped rope.

Reptiles are common and unhurried. Broad, low lizards doze in rows on the warm rims. A fat, patient crocodilian, mossed over and half sunk, lies in almost every third pool, and it is not alone, because turtles stack on its back, and birds stand on the turtles. Small feathered things, part bird and part something older, scuttle through the roots. Above them, in the tree ferns, roost thousands of long-legged waders and hook-billed fliers, silent while they feed and raucous when they don't.

All of it eats and is eaten in a loop so complete that nothing accumulates. There are no bones, no carcasses, no drifts of shed feathers. Each death is taken up almost as it happens: the pool absorbs it, the moss covers it, something small arrives. The forest is a machine for keeping itself exactly the size it is.

## Sound

It is loud, and the loudness is constant. It has no gaps, no dawn chorus and no evening hush. It is a single standing structure of sound: the frogs' pulsing trill, tuned by long acquaintance into one broad chord that never resolves and never falters. Over it lie the whine of insects, the clatter of reed on reed, the dripping, the pop of gas rising through the pools, and the gulping calls of the big waders. Now and then a deep note comes from the crocodilians, a long bass vibration you feel in the sternum and see as a ripple crossing the water in rings.

You stop hearing it after an hour. Then you notice that you can't hear yourself think, and that is when the place starts to feel strange.

## What is odd

The strangeness is mild and structural. The frogs seem to know where you are. A hush travels with you as a circle a few yards wide. The sound falls silent just ahead of your step and resumes behind it, so you walk inside a moving pocket of quiet while the chord goes on around you, unchanged, like a room you cannot enter.

And the terraces are too regular. They are natural, grown by chemistry and not by hand, but each rim stands at almost the same height as its neighbor, and each pool is filled to almost the same depth, as if the land had settled on an answer and refused to revise it. Wherever you walk, the ground has a sense of having been finished and then left alone, an unweathered newness that will never age. This is a place at the moment of its beginning, kept there by the sheer weight of everything living on it, held in balance and humming.
