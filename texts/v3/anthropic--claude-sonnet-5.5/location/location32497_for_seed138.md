# The Boiler Basin

The basin is a shallow bowl of black rock about a kilometre across, and it sits so close to sea level that the tide's slow pressure can be felt in its wells. A ridge of fresh basalt and cindery tuff, sharp as broken crockery, closes it in on three sides. On the fourth side a brackish flat runs out toward a sea nobody in the basin looks at. The rim is only forty or fifty metres high, but it holds the heat in like a lid. Air inside is thick, bath-warm, and tastes of eggs, iron, and hot stone.

## The Ground

The rock is young. It has clearly never had time to soften: edges of lava are still glassy and razor-fine, columns stand in ragged ranks with unworn corners, and cinder slopes hold the angle at which they landed. Yet nothing here crumbles further. Every surface is glazed with a thin, hard skin of mineral, sulphur-yellow near the vents, then white silica sinter, then rust orange, then a dull green-grey where the condensate runs. The steam deposits as much crust as the acid water eats away. The sharpness never dulls and never sharpens. A stair-cut in the rock is as crisp as the day it was hewn and will be as crisp in a century.

The ground is warm through boot soles and sometimes too warm to sit on. Water stands in every hollow. Pools are the colour of weak tea, of milk, of verdigris. Where the crust is thin, the ground sighs and beads of hot water stand out on it like sweat. Rocks weep. Every overhang drips, and the drip-lines have left ribbed white curtains of mineral down the cliff faces like candle wax.

## The Town

Perhaps three hundred people live here, in a place that is entirely a working machine. The streets are narrow and laid out along the pipes, not the other way round. Insulated steam mains, thick as barrels and wrapped in grey lagging, run at head height down the middle of the lanes and bridge the cross-streets on iron gantries, so walking through town means walking under a low ceiling of warm pipe that ticks and knocks as it expands. Every junction has a valve wheel painted red. Sheet-metal, corrugated iron, and poured concrete are the building materials, all stained the same rusty ochre, and every wall is warm to the hand.

At the centre is the works: a long brick hall with a row of arched windows, its roof a sawtooth of iron. Inside, six turbines sit in a line, each the size of a locomotive. Behind the hall stands a stand of cooling towers, squat and wooden-slatted, weeping water down their sides in perpetual rain. Beyond them the boron and salt evaporation pans lie in ruled rectangles, pale and steaming. Copper-green gutters take the runoff to the flat.

Housing is stacked terraces with flat roofs. Laundry hangs in the steam on lines strung between chimneys and never quite dries; it just reaches a state of equilibrium damp. Verandas are shaded by tin awnings. Nearly everything has a lamp on it, small yellow bulbs on iron brackets, because the light here is poor.

The town is sparse for its size. Between the works, the terraces, and the pans lie broad stretches of bare black rock and standing water, crossed by pipe-bridges and plank walks, and nobody has ever built on them because the ground under them is too soft, too hot, or too wet. A person can stand in an empty acre of fumarole field and see only three buildings and a hundred columns of vapour.

## The Light

The sky is a low ceiling of vapour, and the sun reaches the basin only as a pale coin behind it, a brightness with no direction. Shadows are soft and short. At midday the light is the colour of dishwater, at dusk of tarnished brass. Lamps are on all day. Steam catches the lamplight and hangs it in halos. The white sinter terraces glow faintly and give back what little light there is, so the pools look lit from beneath.

## The Sound

The place is loud and never quiet. The turbines run at a single steady pitch, a deep hum you feel in the teeth, and over it come the vents: a roar from the big bore-wells, a whistle from the small ones, and a wet bubbling hiss from the mud pots. Pipes clank. Relief valves shriek at intervals, and by long habit nobody looks up. Water drips and patters on tin, and there's the rushing of the cooling-tower rain.

The animals add to it. Frogs, thousands of them, live in the warm shallows and call day and night in overlapping ranks, a clacking, rattling chorus that rises and falls with no change of pattern. Cicada-like insects buzz in the reeds. Gulls and herons and rooks squabble on the roofs. The basin has its own acoustics: the rim throws every sound back inward, so the whole town sits inside a single continuous roar that is somehow companionable, like the sound of a sea.

## The Life

Vegetation is sparse: it is too hot and too acid for most plants. Reed mats and dark tufted sedge grow in the tepid runoff channels, pale ferns crowd the cooler cracks in the walls, and mats of orange and green bacteria and algae streak the warm streams in concentric colour bands. A few wind-bent, sooty trees have been planted along the main street and sit in iron collars, tended like pets. Moss furs the shady sides of the cooling towers.

Fauna is abundant, out of proportion to the plants. Warm water and warm walls have gathered life. Frogs and small pale eels fill the outflow channels. Dragonflies patrol the pans. Geckos and lizards sun on the hot pipes, thick as gutter rats. Pigeons roost in their thousands on the turbine hall's ridge, where the heat rising through the iron keeps them cosy. Rats, ducks, and feral cats live off the canning-yard scraps. Wading birds pick through the brine flats, pink brine shrimp in the pans feed flamingo-like flocks that stand in the evaporation ponds like painted ornaments, and the workers ignore them as they ignore the frogs.

## The Rules of the Place

Nothing changes. The vents have blown at the same pressure for longer than the records go. The steam plumes stand in the same shapes above the same holes. The water table sits at the same depth, so the wells always draw the same amount. The shifts change at the same whistle, the pools hold the same colours, and the heat has no season. The town takes only as much steam as the ground gives, and the ground gives what it always has. It is a perfect, dull, working balance. Children born here know it as they know their own pulse.

The residents have a saying: *the basin keeps its own time*. It is meant literally. Nothing there ages visibly. Iron rusts to a certain orange and goes no further. Paint fades to a certain shade and stays. The mineral crust seals every scratch. Even the sharp rock, fresh and unworn, is never any fresher.

## The Edge

Walk out along the plank causeway toward the sea flat, past the last valve house, and the roar falls behind you by degrees. The air cools by perhaps two degrees, the steam thins, the frogs thin, and the light widens. You reach a line of black glass-edged boulders at the water's edge where the warm outflow meets the sea in a long brown plume, with a fringe of egrets standing in it. Turn back and the whole basin is laid out in front of you, low and small: a huddle of rust-coloured roofs, a hundred white feathers of steam, the turbines' single note, the amber lamps beginning to show, and no change in any of it, as far back as anyone can say.
