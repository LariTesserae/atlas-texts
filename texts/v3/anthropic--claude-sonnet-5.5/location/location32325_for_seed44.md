# The Sump at Harrow Lock

The whole place fits inside a walk of ten minutes. It is a drainage works and the settlement that grew around it, set in a bowl of reclaimed delta about a body-length above the tide. Everything here is green, and everything here is running.

## The ground

There is no bare ground. What would be soil is a mat of moss, liverwort and creeping fig, laid over brick paving that was itself laid over silt. Your boot sinks a finger's depth and the water rises around the sole, brown and tea-warm. Streets are more suggestion than surface: two lines of worn brick with a spine of pennywort between them, where a century of handcarts and boots kept the growth low. Everywhere else the plants have won.

The land is nearly flat, and that flatness is the reason for everything. The bowl lies below the river's high line, and the river is visible from the upper walkways as a slow brown ridge of water higher than the rooftops, moving without hurry behind its embankment. The works exist to keep that water on the far side of the wall.

## The works

At the center stands the pump house, a long brick hall with arched windows. Most of the glass is gone, replaced by vine curtains that the crew parts with hooks. Inside are three great centrifugal pumps, painted a green so old it has become a texture, driven by electric motors that replaced steam engines two generations ago. The old boiler stack still stands and now carries a fig tree, its roots gripping the brick like fingers around a cup. The tree's crown puts out a cloud of leaves at the height where smoke used to leave.

The sound is the first thing you meet and the last thing you stop hearing. It is a low, chesty thudding from the pumps, a rising whine from the motor housings, the gargle of water climbing a pipe as wide as a doorway, and then the roar where the outfall pours into the canal that carries it up and over the river wall. Under that is the clatter of a belt somewhere, a loose panel that rings with each thud, and a bell on a pulley that swings and strikes on its own when the big pump starts. People talk here in short, loud sentences and at the ends of them look at each other's mouths.

## What the plants do

Plants are the architecture as much as the bricks are. Every structure has been taken up and made into something else.

- **Bamboo** grows in dense clumps along the canal banks, and its culms have been lashed into scaffolding, gantries, the frames of drying racks. Some of the scaffolding is alive and sprouting, and no one has bothered to tell which poles are old and which are growing.
- **Strangler figs** have taken the warehouse corners. Their roots run down the brick in braided sheets, and in places the wall is now mostly root with brick trapped inside like fruit in jelly.
- **Vines** with leaves the size of dinner plates cover the roofs so completely that the corrugated iron shows only as a faint ripple in the green. When rain comes the roofs drum, a wet, thick sound, like fingers on a stretched hide.
- **Ferns** hang from every gutter and pipe elbow, and **duckweed** covers the still canals in a skin so even it looks like a lawn. The canals have to be skimmed by hand each week with long rakes, because the weed would clog the intake screens.

Every morning the crew does the cutting. The flywheels, the intake grates, the belt guards and the switchgear cabinets are cleared by machete and pruning saw. By evening a thin fringe of tendrils has already crept back across the edge of the cleared zone. Maintenance here is a border war conducted at the speed of a growing fingernail, and it has gone on longer than anyone's grandmother.

## Water everywhere

Water is in the air, on the walls and under your feet. The warm damp condenses on cold iron so that every pipe sweats and drips, and the valve wheels are slick to the hand. Rust runs in orange streaks down the brick below each flange and stains the canal edges the color of tea leaves. The drips have carved tiny gutters into the mortar over the decades, small fluted channels that are the fixed handwriting of a hundred years of dripping.

Rain arrives most afternoons, warm and heavy. It doesn't change the place so much as add another layer of sound. The tin roofs, the leaves, the canals and the pump hall all speak at once, and the whole settlement is one continuous hiss with the thud of the pumps beneath it.

## The settlement

About sixty people live here in rows of narrow two-story houses, brick below and timber above, built shoulder to shoulder along three lanes and one canal. Upper windows nearly touch across the lanes, and washing lines cross between them in green-tinted cotton. The houses lean a little, all in the same direction, toward the canal, as though listening. Their footings are old and settled; nothing has moved quickly here for a long time. Doors swell in the wet and have to be shouldered open. Small kitchen gardens sit on rooftops and in boxes made of old boiler plate, growing chilies and greens in dark, rich compost.

There is a workshop where a lathe screams by day, a canteen with steam always pouring from its window, a switch room humming with transformers, and a schoolroom whose slate is furred with mildew at the bottom edge. A tram track once ran through the settlement; the rails are still there, buried in moss, and the bamboo clumps lean over them like patient onlookers.

## Life and stillness

Animals are few. A grey heron stands on the outfall wall most mornings, unbothered by the noise, its stillness the one quiet thing in the place. A few frogs sit in the duckweed, not calling. They can't be heard over the works and seem to have stopped trying. Snails stripe the walls after rain. Sometimes a monitor lizard the length of a table crosses a lane with great deliberation. The insects are small and constant, but even they are drowned by the pumps.

## Time

Change here is slow and one-directional. The figs thicken a hand's width each decade. The rust deepens. The canal silts and is dredged, silts and is dredged. The river wall is raised a little every generation, and the bowl sinks a little every generation, and the pumps grow ever more necessary. Everyone knows the arrangement can't last forever, and everyone knows it will last longer than they will.

At night the lamps come on, yellow and slightly haloed by the damp, and the pumps keep on. The thudding goes through the floors and into the beds, and people sleep to it the way others sleep to the sea. When the pumps once stopped for a day, the villagers say, the silence woke the whole settlement at once, and they lay listening to the sound of the water thinking about coming in.
