# The Held Breath

Nothing here has ever been seen, so it has to be known another way.

## Arrival

You come in through a fissure near the top of a very old mountain. The mountain is a rounded stump now, worn down over so many ages that its summit is a wide, soft plateau of quartz sand and cloud forest, wet the whole year. The fissure is a crack you can walk down, and the descent takes hours. The air changes as you go. The wind drops out first, then the smell of the forest goes, and then comes a thick, warm, mineral, mushroomy breath that has been sitting in one place for a very long time.

At the bottom your feet find the floor, and your voice tells you the rest. Speak, and the sound goes out and doesn't come back for a long time. The room is enormous, or at least much larger than a hamlet. It is a single dissolved void, a bubble the water carved out when the mountain was young and tall. Since then nothing has happened to it but slow filling and smoothing.

## The Volume

Almost all of the room is air. Between one living thing and the next lie huge distances of warm, black, still space. Walking, you can go a hundred paces with only wet sand under your soles and no sense of anything nearby. The life is thin and lush, like a skin. It lives on the floor and on the far walls and in a few threads that cross the emptiness.

Those threads are roots. The trees of the plateau forest, far above, send their roots down through the fissures. The roots are pale, thick as thighs, and hang in the open room like the ropes of some abandoned rigging. They twist slowly as they descend, and some never reach the floor. Their tips ooze water. When you brush against one it is cool and slick, alive, and faintly pulsing with the movement of sap. They are the only vertical things in the room, and around them the air is full of dripping.

## The Floor

The floor is a wet garden. Ancient flowstone has smoothed every edge into a soft swell, and on top of it grows a dense carpet of moss, liverwort, and rubbery ferns with leaves as wide as doors. They are not green. Nothing here has ever needed light, so they are cream, grey, or the color of old teeth. They live on the seep of dissolved minerals, on the rain of debris from the roots, and on the warmth. The leaves are cold and damp and faintly furred, and where they are thickest you push through them with both arms, like pushing through wet cloth.

Pools lie among the ferns, shallow and clear, with the water table only a hand's depth below everything. Every step squelches. Water weeps from the walls and collects in small runnels, and the runnels find each other and slide sideways into the pools without hurrying.

## The Made Things

A few structures stand in the emptiness, far apart. You find them by touch, by echo, or by tripping.

Near the center is a ring of smooth posts, each as tall as a person and as narrow as a wrist. They are cool, seamless, and slightly greasy-feeling, and no moss will grow on them. Water beads and runs off them as if they were waxed. Nobody knows what they were for. They have been here so long that the flowstone has crept up around their bases in soft collars, and that is the only evidence that time has touched them at all.

Farther on there is a stair of seven broad steps that climbs out of the floor to nothing and ends in air. Beyond it, at the edge of the room, there is a cube the size of a small house with no door. Its walls are warm, and the air around it is dry. It is the only dry place in the whole cavern, and small animals sleep against its base.

## The Hum

The warmth comes from the cube. Or the warmth comes from the room and the cube is only its center. No one who has put a hand on the wall has been able to say which.

The cube gives off a tone. It sits below hearing, so you feel it first in your breastbone and in the water of the pools, where it makes fine standing ripples. Once you notice it you cannot stop noticing. The tone is very steady, and over a century it drifts by about a semitone. The whole room is tuned to it. The pools, the roots, and the ceiling all ring faintly in sympathy. Whatever the builders knew, whatever physics let a sealed thing make heat and vibration for so many ages without fuel, it has never been written down and probably never will be. It is an engine that has forgotten it was an engine and settled into being part of the geology.

## The Fauna

The room is loud, and the loudness is the life.

Frogs live in the ferns, thousands of them, pale and eyeless or nearly so, each the size of a thumbnail. They call all the time, in overlapping choruses that swell and fade in slow waves across the floor. The choruses lock onto the hum, so that the whole room's frogs share a pulse, like a very slow heartbeat, one long chord every few breaths. Between the pulses come clicks, ticks, and thin whistles from crickets and beetles. Blind cave fish flick in the pools and make small wet sounds when they surface. Somewhere above, out of reach, colonies of small bats shift and mutter. They fly through the empty volume on echo alone, and their calls make delicate lattices of sound, bright and high, that cross the darkness like a net.

There are also larger things. Something heavy with many legs moves through the ferns in the distance, with a long dragging, wet, rhythmic sound, and stops whenever you stop. Nothing has ever hurt anyone here. But everything is aware of everything else, always, because in the dark that is the only way to live.

## Rules of the Place

Sound is the map. Whatever makes a noise is alive, and whatever is silent is stone, or the cube, or a trap of deep water. Animals that need to meet call to each other, and animals that want to avoid each other go quiet. Every life here is a small voice in a very big room, and it stays in place by being heard.

There is one strange rule. The frogs go silent when a person stands very near the cube. It happens all at once, the whole chorus stopping for a few seconds as if holding its breath, and then it resumes on the next pulse of the hum. No one knows what they perceive. The old guides say the room is listening back.

## Time

Almost nothing changes. The water table rises a finger's width over a decade and falls again. The flowstone thickens around the posts. The roots creep down a hand's breadth a year, and one day a root will touch the floor and become a pillar. The tone of the cube slides lower, so gradually that no one alive has ever heard it move.

Above, in the wind and cloud of the summit, the forest goes on being rained on. Down here, in the warm and the black, the frogs sing on the slow pulse of a machine that has outlasted whoever made it, and the room keeps breathing.
