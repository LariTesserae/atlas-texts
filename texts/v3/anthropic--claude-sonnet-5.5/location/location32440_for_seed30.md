# The Setting Basin

The basin sits on a high shoulder of a mountain that is not finished yet. You reach the rim by a switchback path of pale, unweathered stone, every edge still sharp enough to cut a glove. Nothing here has had time to round off. Up on the rim the air is warm, which feels wrong for the altitude, a damp breath like the inside of a mouth. It comes up from the basin floor, and it is loud.

## The Floor

The bowl is about a kilometer across, and its floor is not one surface but several, in different stages of becoming rock.

Near the rim it is **true stone**: grey-green, fine-grained, with fresh conchoidal fractures that still ring faintly if you strike them. Below that lies **curd**, a slumped, glossy, cream-colored material that looks like cooling wax but is mineral, halfway between a gel and a crystal. It holds the print of a boot for an hour, then stiffens. In the center is **the wet**, a shallow field of standing water, warm as a bath and clouded with suspended grit. Threads of pale matter descend through it and slowly thicken, and the field is pulsing very slightly. Every few minutes a skin forms across a patch of water, wrinkles, whitens, and becomes new curd.

Nothing has eroded, because there hasn't been time. Every cliff is a first draft. Faces show their bedding planes like pages in a fresh book, and the seams between layers are so clean you can slide a fingernail into them.

## The Weeping

Water is everywhere but comes from no single source. The rock sweats. Every vertical face beads with condensation, and the beads gather into threads that run down into gutters the stone seems to have made for them. The gutters are too regular to be accidental, and yet they are not carved. They were laid down that way, in the same pass that made the wall.

The air is a low, condensing fog, thick at the floor and thinning to a lid of dim brown cloud a hundred meters up. Light comes through it as a dull amber, with no visible sun. Now and then a rainfall starts, always local, always brief, falling in a column two hundred meters wide while the rest of the basin stays dry. The rain is warm and tastes of iron and something like struck flint.

## The Sound

The basin is loud, and the loudness is not chaos. It has layers.

Beneath everything is a **drone**, a low note at the edge of hearing that you feel in the sternum and the fillings of your teeth. Over it comes a slow **ticking** from the curd fields, thousands of tiny crystallization events like sleet on a tin roof. Then, every ninety seconds or so, the **stroke**: a huge, clean, bell-like tone that seems to come from inside the ground. On each stroke the surface of the wet shivers into concentric rings, and somewhere a patch of curd snaps into stone with a crack like a rifle.

The strokes are the tool. Whatever built this place builds it with standing sound, and the bowl is the instrument. The shape of the basin focuses the tone, the tone orders the dissolved minerals, and the minerals fall into place as rock. It is engineering that no physics you know quite describes, since it treats vibration as a kind of hand.

## The Structures

Perhaps seven made things stand in the basin. There are no roads and no settlement, and the spaces between them are wide.

**The Reeds.** On the eastern side, forty or so slender white spires rise from the curd at irregular heights, some four meters tall, some thirty. They are hollow and open at the top. They are the resonators, and each gives a different note when the stroke passes through. Between strokes the wind and the fauna make them moan. Some are still growing: you can find one with a fresh, wet, soft tip, translucent as a fingernail.

**The Lintel.** Near the north wall stands a freestanding arch of black glassy material, unfinished. One leg is complete and the other stops at waist height, a stub with a bright fracture-face. Nothing spans the gap. Inside the arch the air is cooler by several degrees and the sound is muffled, as if it were a doorway into a smaller room. Nothing has ever been seen to walk through it, but the ground inside the arch is unmarked by the fauna, which give the space a wide berth.

**The Ribs.** Along the southern edge lie the remains of something large and curved, a series of ribbed arcs half-sunk in the stone. They may be an earlier machine that finished its work and was overgrown by rock, or a part of the present one that has not yet been raised. They are warm to the touch, warmer than the air, and faintly damp.

**The Cairns.** Scattered across the true stone are small heaps of stacked, sorted pebbles, sizes graded from large to small. These are not built by any human hand. The **tallers**, a species of animal, make them for reasons of their own, or possibly reasons related to the machine.

**The Well-Eye.** At the very center of the wet, a perfectly round hole about a meter across rests just under the water's surface, black and still. Threads of pale matter descend into it and none come back out. This is probably the source. Nobody has dropped anything in that returned.

**Two others** are buried to the shoulders in curd, so that you see only a smooth, faintly humming dome and, in the second case, a ring of small square lights that blink through a slow sequence of amber shades.

## The Vegetation

Plants are few. The rock is too new for soil. What grows here grows on the curd and in the shallows.

**Mat-weed** spreads across the warm wet in olive-brown rafts, thin as tissue, rising and falling with the strokes. **Spindle-moss** clings to the weeping walls in vertical pelts, drinking the runoff, silver when dry and near-black when wet. A handful of stunted **stone-lilies** grow in cracks, fleshy things the color of raw liver, their petals slightly translucent and lit from within by a faint sugar-pink glow. Their roots are said to follow the vibration lines through the rock. There are perhaps thirty in the whole basin, and they open only on the stroke.

## The Fauna

Life is abundant, and it has organized itself around the sound.

**Bell-frogs** are palm-sized, glassy-skinned amphibians of pale gold, thousands of them, dwelling in the warm shallows. They call in the gaps between strokes, and their chorus is the loudest thing in the basin apart from the stroke itself, a shrill layered clatter that rises to a crescendo and then cuts off entirely a fraction of a second before the great tone comes. They are not afraid of the stroke. They anticipate it. Some are always slightly wrong, and those are the ones that get eaten.

**Tallers** are long-legged, heron-like animals, ash-grey with a stripe of yellow down the neck. They stalk the wet edges eating frogs, and they build the cairns. Each cairn seems to correspond to one death, or one feeding, or one dawn. They tap their bills against the Reeds in a rhythm that is almost, but not quite, in time with the drone.

**Curd-crawlers** are flat, hand-wide, many-legged things like trilobites, and they graze the freshly formed curd. They leave trails of harder material behind, which suggests they are not merely grazing but participating: turning the gel to stone a little faster by passing over it. Where they are thick, the ground sets earlier and cleaner.

**Ceiling-drifters** are the strangest. In the fog above the basin float translucent, kite-shaped animals a meter or two across, trailing filaments, riding the warm updraft in slow spirals. They feed on the condensing droplets and, it seems, on the sound. Each stroke makes them flare and flash pale blue along the edges. At dusk they descend in the hundreds, a soft rain of glowing shapes, and settle on the spires of the Reeds like moths on a lamp, and there they drone in unison with the machine.

**Something larger** moves at the far edge of vision sometimes: a low, wide, slow shape wading through the wet at the limit of the fog, big as a cart, with a back like an upturned hull. Nobody has seen it clearly. The frogs go silent when it passes, and the water it leaves behind is stiff with new curd.

## The Cycle

The basin keeps a rhythm.

**In the dim day**, the strokes come regularly and the surface crusts and stiffens. The frogs call. The tallers hunt. The drifters climb the updraft into the cloud. The rock grows perhaps a finger's width.

**At dusk** the cloud lowers and the light turns copper, then violet. The drifters descend. The strokes speed up, ninety seconds becoming sixty, then forty. The Reeds begin to sing in harmony, and for a few minutes the whole basin rings like a struck glass. Bioluminescence rises in the wet: a cold blue-green shimmer that follows each stroke outward in a ring. The stone-lilies open.

**At night** the pace is slower and deeper, and the drone thickens. The Ribs warm. The ring of square lights in the buried dome runs its sequence. The Lintel seems darker than the dark around it. This is when the largest changes happen: with a groan like a ship's hull, a section of curd the size of a house will compact, crack, and settle into stone, and the new surface will be different by morning, with new ridges, a new pool, a new ledge where none was.

**Before dawn** there is a silence of a few seconds, the only one, when the frogs, the strokes, and the drone all stop at once. Some say it is the machine checking its work. Then the first stroke comes, softer than the rest, and everything starts again.

## The Rules

- Rock made here is *the machine's* rock, and the machine knows what it has made. Take a piece from the rim and it will hum for a day in your pack, then go dull and quiet, as if it had forgotten what it was for.
- Sound is not noise here. Loud is the ordinary condition, and the danger is not the volume but being out of step with it. Those who walk the curd fields should learn to step on the count and pause on the stroke, or the ground remembers their weight.
- Water is always a little warmer than the air, and always slightly moving, even in still pools.
- The basin is finishing itself. Somewhere within a lifetime, perhaps within a few years, the last curd will set, the last Reed will close over, the wet will drain down the Well-Eye, and the strokes will stop. The frogs seem to know. Their calls have a rising, hurried note that the older tallers do not share.

Standing on the rim at the end of the day, with the fog turning gold and the whole bowl ringing under you, you understand that you are watching not a landscape but a landscape in the act of being made, by a hand that is also a voice, among animals that have learned to sing along.
