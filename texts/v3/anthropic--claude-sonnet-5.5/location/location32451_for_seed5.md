# The Held Hollow

From the plain above, there is nothing to see. The land is low and level: sedge, black peaty puddles, a few bent birches. You could walk within ten paces of the rim and not know. Then the ground stops, and beneath you, in a bowl barely two hundred paces across, is a town.

## The Bowl

The hollow is a sinkhole, very old, with its edges slumped and rounded like the lip of a worn cup. Its walls are pale chalk and limestone, cream where dry and grey-green where moss has taken them. They rise thirty or forty feet and curve inward slightly, so the sky is a lid of white cloud. The chalk takes that pallid light and throws it back and down. Nothing in the hollow casts a hard shadow. The light is bright, even and shadowless, like the inside of an eggshell held up to the sun. Every leaf edge and snail-trail shows its detail.

It is cold, just above freezing, and it has been exactly that cold for as long as anyone has counted. The bowl traps a lens of heavy air that neither warms nor cools. Rime sits on the north faces of things in feathers a finger long. It never thickens and never melts. Breath smokes faintly. Nobody's fingers ever quite warm.

## The Town

The buildings are packed so tightly that the town seems to have grown as one organism rather than been built. Houses of chalk block and rammed clay lean shoulder to shoulder, stacked three and four high up the curve of the wall. Each roof is another house's doorstep. Lanes are a body's width, sometimes less, and worn into troughs by centuries of feet. Stairs are cut into the chalk, and their treads are dished so deeply that each step holds a small puddle of clear water.

The tools are simple. There are stone adzes, bone awls, and fire-hardened wooden pegs. Copper is beaten cold into pins, rings, and the rims of doors. Lintels are single split oak trunks, black with age. Walls are chinked with clay and moss, and the moss is alive and was never planted. Roofs are thatched in thick, layered reed. Over the centuries the thatch has become a garden, with ferns, stonecrop, and dwarf willow rooted in it, so a roof reads as a slope of green with a chimney-hole in it.

Everything is green in a cold, saturated way. Liverworts sheet the lower walls in wet plates. Moss cushions round every corner and stair edge, deep enough to press a thumb into to the knuckle. Hart's-tongue ferns hang from every crack, their fronds glossy and dark. Ivy has climbed the buildings to the tops and then thickened into ropes that hold the stonework together. In places you cannot tell where a wall ends and the hillside begins. The plants and the masonry have fused into one settled thing.

## The Quiet

The hollow is silent. Moss lines the lanes, roofs, and walls, and it swallows sound. A dropped pot makes a soft thock, as though heard through a blanket. Footsteps on the moss-floored lanes make no noise. The people wear felt or hide shoes anyway, from habit. They speak seldom and low, and often not at all: hand signs, tilts of the head, a click of the thumbnail on a tooth. A whole afternoon may pass with only the small sounds that cannot be prevented, such as the tick of a drip, the slide of a door, or a breath.

The animals are quiet too. Fat white-bellied frogs sit motionless in every wet niche and do not call. Wrens flit in the ivy but hold their song. Voles, shrews, and dormice move through the moss in tunnels of their own. There are small hornless goats on the upper terraces, snails on every stone, and pale moths that rest on the chalk in their hundreds, wings folded, waiting for nothing. Fauna is abundant, and each creature seems to keep a private silence.

## Water

Water is scarce here, though everything is wet. No stream runs through the hollow and no rain falls straight down into it. It arrives as condensation, mist that forms on the chalk at the rim and slides down as a thin film. It beads on every leaf and collects in the dished steps, in cupped stones, and in bowls the town has carved for it. At the low center is a small round pool, dark and perfectly still, fed by a seep from beneath. Its level has never risen or fallen. The people call it the Measure. A ring of white stones around it has stayed exactly at the waterline since before the oldest house.

## What Doesn't Happen

Nothing changes here. Autumn does not reach the ferns, and the willow leaves never yellow and never fall. The moss neither spreads nor dies back. The frogs that sit in the niches are the same frogs, or are replaced so exactly that no one could tell. Snow does not settle on the bowl; it melts into the pale air before it lands. The town has been the same size for longer than its stories go back. No new house is built unless an old one is taken down, and the stone from the old one goes straight into the new one.

The people accept this as they accept gravity. They do not grow bored. They tend the moss, comb the thatch gardens, carry water from the steps to the terraces, mend the copper pins, and feed the goats. The work is small, exact, and repeated. It is done without hurry because there is nothing that the hurry could catch.

## The Unusual

Some things here do not quite belong to the world above. The light has no source. If you look for the sun through the cloud-lid you will not find its place, and no shadow ever leans in any direction. The mist that beads on leaves never drips off in the way water should. It hangs a moment at the tip, gathered and trembling, and then falls straight down without a sound. The frogs do not blink. In the deepest lane, near the pool, a few stones are warm to the touch. No one has ever spoken of it.

At the center, beside the Measure, stands a single tall pillar of chalk, carved with nothing at all, worn smooth by centuries of hands. The people rest a palm on it when they pass, without stopping and without speaking, and go on into the pale, still light.
