# The Sunken Terraces of Ess

## The shape of it

Imagine a plain so wide that its far edge lies past the curve of the water. Everything on it was made by hands. It is not a city. A city has a center, and this has none. It is a made country: terraces, embankments, sluice-channels, retaining walls and stepped fields, laid out over an area the size of a continent. Every slope and every flat was cut, stacked, or tamped by people with stone tools, bronze wedges, wooden sledges and baskets of earth. It took many generations. Then the sea rose over it, slowly, and stayed.

The water is deep enough that the surface is a rumor. Above the terraces there are perhaps forty fathoms of cold, still, dark sea, and above that a lid of ice. This is a polar sea, and the ice is a ceiling that never fully leaves. A dim blue-grey seeps down through it, more a memory of light than light. At midday in the short season it is bright enough to make out your own hand. At other times it is not.

## The ground

The erosion here is beyond old. What the builders raised has been worn for so long that it has settled into its final form, a softness no chisel could reach. Terrace walls that were once plumb have slumped into long rounded shoulders. Stairs have become ramps, and ramps have become gentle swells in the ground. Corners no longer exist. You can still tell which slopes were built, because the curves are too regular. The natural world is never so evenly rounded, and a water-worn tumble of boulders wouldn't repeat itself every forty paces for a thousand miles.

The materials show through where the silt has slid away. The walls are dry-laid basalt cobbles, fist-sized to head-sized, chinked with smaller stone. The fill behind them is rammed clay, hardened to something between brick and slate. The channels are lined with fired tile, thumb-thick and hand-pressed, each one bearing the faint ridge of a maker's fingers. Here and there are bronze pins, green-black and swollen with corrosion, driven where a wall needed to be held to its footing. Some of the older courses have been bound by cordage, now long turned to a brown mineral ghost, a stripe of dark in the stone where rope once ran.

Everything is furred with a fine pale silt. It falls constantly, in a slow snowfall of dead plankton and dust from the ice above. It gathers on every upward face and softens every edge. When a current shifts, whole hillsides of it lift and drift like smoke, then settle again a little differently.

## The green

Despite the dark, the place is lush, and the lushness is what surprises. Kelp and its cousins grow in dense stands rooted in the terraces. They are long-stemmed browns, ribbon-leaved and bladder-floated, rising twenty or thirty feet on stalks as thick as a wrist and held upright by gas-filled bulbs. They are the color of wet leather and old tea, and in the poor light they read as black. Beneath them is a second layer of low red fronds, and beneath that a mat of crust and creeping green-brown growth that coats the stone in cushions. The thin light is enough for them. They have had ages to become good at it.

They do not grow randomly. They grow along the terraces. Long ago the builders planted these fields, and the rows persist, self-seeded generation after generation, spore after spore, so that the old geometry of cultivation is written in living stalks. Stand at the top of a slope and look down and you'll see the stands run in contour lines, each band a different height, each terrace its own hedge of kelp. The channels are choked with a slower, denser weed that leans in the current like combed hair.

In the stands the water is layered with hanging blades. Visibility drops to a few body-lengths. Blade brushes blade. Everything touches, and nothing is empty, but the density is soft and yielding, like moving through a slowly breathing curtain.

## Sound

It's mostly quiet, but not silent. Water carries sound well, and what it carries is sparse and slow.

The ice overhead sings and groans. There is a long, low creak when a floe shifts, sometimes a deep boom that rolls through the whole sea like a door closing in another house. Sometimes, when the ice is cracking far away, the sound arrives as a faint, high tick, like cooling metal.

The kelp makes its own noise: a squeak of stalk against stalk, a rubbery whisper of blades, the slow pop of a bladder giving up its gas. In stronger current, the whole forest hums at the edge of hearing.

And there is a fine hiss, more felt than heard, of silt falling through still water. If you hold your breath and listen for it, it is like being inside a seashell.

There are no animal calls, no clicks, no songs. Nothing swims. The water holds no fish, no drifting jelly, no crawling thing on the stone. Where you might expect the sea to be crowded, it is only plants, stone, and what they have made of each other. The absence has a texture. The sea feels like a room left tidy after everyone has gone.

## Weather that isn't weather

At this scale the sea makes its own climate. It has currents where a land would have winds. A great slow gyre turns across the plain, taking most of a human lifetime to complete one revolution, and it pushes a steady deep drift over the terraces, always in the same sense, always gentle. Cold brine sinks from beneath the ice in curtains, invisible except where it carries silt in a shimmering veil. When it pours down a terrace wall, it makes a slow waterfall in water. It shimmers, clings to the slope, and spreads across the next step down.

There are seasons, and they are slow. When the sun returns to the pole, the ice thins in places and the blue above lifts a shade. Some of the kelp grows a hand's width in a month. When the dark returns, the growth stills. Nothing is quick. A stalk may take years to reach the height of a man. A drift of silt may take decades to bury a wall. Things change here the way a shoreline changes, by the time you have looked up from something else.

## What remains of the makers

No one is here. There are no bones, because the sea is cold and slow, and whatever bodies there were dissolved into the silt long ago. But the makers are everywhere in the ground itself. You can find a handprint in a tile. You can find a repair, a patch of newer, cruder stone set into an older wall, clearly done in haste, by someone who knew the sea was coming. Some terraces end in a line of unfinished stone, a course half-laid, the next rock still sitting where it was dropped. In one long channel, a tool has been left in the mud between two walls, a bronze-headed adze, its wooden haft rotted to a pale streak, its blade nearly whole under a skin of green.

The sea rose over the work the way one accepts a slow argument. The terraces went under one at a time. The last high fields held out for what must have been generations, lower and lower, always a little wetter, until the waterline crossed the final ridge and there was nothing left to stand on. The kelp took the fields the next season and has kept them since.

## The feeling of the place

It is a landscape that has finished changing its mind. Nothing here is waiting for anything. The stone has settled, the plants have settled into the stone, and the sea has settled over both. Travel through it slowly, and the effect is not desolate but patient. Ranks of dark kelp lean together over the old contour lines. The silt falls. The ice sings far above. Somewhere a bladder pops and a small cloud of bubbles drifts upward, silver in the dimness, and disappears into the dark toward a surface no one here will see again.
