# The Sheathed Town

The plain is flat and grey-green, cut by slow brown channels, and for most of a day's walking there is nothing on it taller than a man. Then the ground swells into a low white blister about four hundred paces across, and the blister is a town.

It sits on a spring field that is younger than the oldest living person there. A quake broke the clay floor of the valley and warm mineral water came up through the cracks. The water is barely warmer than a cool hand, but it is loaded with lime. Everywhere it flows it leaves stone: a skin at first, then a lip, then a shelf, then a wall. The town is what happened when people decided to live inside that process instead of fighting it.

## The Shape of It

From the outside you see terraces, dozens of them, stacked and curved like the inside of a shell. Each terrace holds a shallow pool behind a rim of fresh travertine. The rims are still sharp. Run a thumb along one and it will cut you a little, because the crystals grow in fine blades and frills, and only foot traffic dulls them. The oldest rims, near the center, have been walked to a milky, greasy smoothness. The outer ones are still white and serrated as bread crust.

Inside, the town is packed. Houses share walls and then share roofs. There are no yards. A lane is a shoulder-width gap, often roofed over by the next terrace up, so you walk in a wet, echoing half-tunnel with a strip of pale sky overhead. Ladders of lashed reed and peeled willow connect the levels. Where a family needs more room they do not build outward, because there is no outward. They hollow inward, chiseling a new chamber into the softer, newer stone of their own floor or wall, and the stone obligingly grows to replace what they took.

The houses are the same material as the ground and nearly the same color, so the town reads as one continuous carved thing with holes in it. The holes are doorways, rounded and low, each with a stained apron of orange or rust-green where the water runs out over the sill.

## Materials and Hands

Nobody here smelts anything. Their tools are flint, obsidian traded from far upriver, bone, antler, and a few precious lumps of hammered native copper, worn thin from sharpening. The main instrument of the town is the **rasp**: a slab of gritty volcanic stone set in a wooden handle, used for scraping growth off doors, tools, cooking vessels, and one's own steps. Every household owns several, and a child's first real gift is a small one.

Fresh travertine is soft when wet. It can be sawn with a cord and sand, and it hardens over a few days into something like chalky marble. So the builders work quickly and think in terms of cutting, not stacking. Roofs are thin slabs laid on rafters of the town's scarce timber, then left to be welded together by the water they are already dripping.

Pottery is fired in small dome kilns, but the best vessels are made another way: a clay or wicker form is set in a trickling channel for a season, and a stone jar grows around it. These **grown jars** are thin, speckled, and faintly ringing, and the town treasures them.

## The Breath

The town's central fact is the Breath. Every few hours, on no schedule that clocks could hold to but one every resident feels in their body, the water table under the field surges. You hear it first as a lowering of sound: the drips slow, the gurgling in the channels thins. Then, from the deep vents at the center, warm water swells up and spills down every terrace in sheets an inch or two deep. It runs through the lanes, through door-sills built with raised lips and drain-notches for exactly this, and out through the lower rim in a low waterfall. The whole town shines for the quarter hour it lasts. Steam lifts off the streets in the cool air, and the smell is mineral, faintly eggy, faintly sweet.

Then it drains and the town is left wet and quiet. Each Breath deposits a film of new stone thinner than a fingernail paring. Everything in reach of it thickens by that much, always.

## Rules That Grew Up

Because the stone grows, the town keeps a set of habits that look like superstition but are maintenance.

- **Doors are worked every morning.** The first person up rasps the lintel and sill. A house whose door seals over is called *sheathed*, and it is a bad omen, because it usually means nobody was left alive to scrape.
- **Nothing stands still.** A tool left overnight in a wet groove is welded to the floor by spring. Cups are hung. Sleeping mats are lifted onto frames.
- **The dead are sheathed on purpose.** They are laid on a shelf near the outer rim where the flow is steady and slow, and within a few years the body is a smooth white shape with the suggestion of a face. These shapes line the lowest terrace like sleeping figures under snow, and children are told to greet them going past.
- **Age is read in layers.** The oldest families count their standing by how deep their floors are: a household with forty finger-widths of floor under its hearth has been here since the beginning.

## Life at the Edge of the Wet

Vegetation is scarce because the water is too mineral for most roots and the stone is too new for soil. What grows, grows in crevices and gutters: dark green mats of moss along the drain lines, pale cushion plants in cracks, ropy strands of algae in orange and lime green filling the warm pools. A handful of willows stand on the outer bank, stunted and hung with white crusts where spray has dried on the bark. Reeds grow in the cold outer channels and are harvested for everything.

Animals are few and particular. Small dark snails graze the algae films in such numbers that the pool bottoms seem to move. Pale, thumb-long fish live in the warmest pools and are caught with scoop baskets. Swifts nest in the roofed lanes, in mud cups glued to the ceilings, and they are the only birds that fly through the town, skimming the wet corridors. Sometimes a heron stands motionless on a rim at dawn. The people neither hunt it nor welcome it. It is watched. Rats are rumored, never seen.

## Sound and Light

Mostly quiet. The dominant sound is dripping, and it is not one drip but hundreds, at different pitches, on different surfaces, from stone lips into stone basins, so the whole town has a soft, uneven tick, like a great clock running slightly out of time with itself. Voices are low, because the lanes carry them. When the Breath comes, the ticking dissolves into a long hush of running water and then slowly resumes.

The light is ordinary, a pale overcast daylight, but the white stone throws it around until the shadowed lanes glow. There are few real shadows and no hard darkness until night. Mist collects in the mornings. By afternoon a thin drizzle often comes and the town drinks it without noticing, since it is already wet through.

## Unusual Things

Some terraces sing. Where a thin shelf overhangs a hollow, the moving water sets it vibrating, and a single low tone hums through the houses above. Residents name their neighborhoods by their notes, and a stranger who is lost is guided by being told to walk toward the *deep tone* or away from the *reed tone*.

At night, in the deepest pools near the vents, the algae glow a faint blue-green when disturbed. The children run their hands through the water to write with light. The adults say this is only the growth talking to itself.

And at the very center, in the oldest part, the town has begun to close over. The lanes there have narrowed by stone growth faster than anyone can rasp, and whole chambers have been sealed shut, still furnished, still holding whatever their last occupants left. No one opens them. The rule is that the town grows inward as well as upward, and that at some point everything is meant to be held.
