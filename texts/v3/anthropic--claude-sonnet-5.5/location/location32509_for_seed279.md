# The Reed Chords

The basin lies lower than everything around it. A ring of raw, dark cliffs, a few hundred feet high and split along fresh fault planes, holds it like a bowl. The scree at their feet is sharp-edged and unrounded, and not one lichen stain softens it. This land was pulled downward not long ago, and water came up through the cracks to fill the hollow. It has been building ever since.

## The Spires

The floor is a thicket of stone. Mineral-rich water rises through the basin's fractures, meets the cold air, and lets go of its load. Where it emerges, a chimney forms. Some are a finger wide and some a barrel wide, and most stand between a person's knee and three times a person's height. They are pale grey and bone-yellow, and near the vents they are a bruised rust. They stand a hand's breadth apart, sometimes closer, so that walking means turning your shoulders and sliding sideways. Their surfaces are crisp and sugary, and the edges are sharp enough to score a palm. Nothing has had time to wear them down.

Each spire is hollow. Look down the open throat of one and you see dark water shivering a little below the rim. Some spires are dry and dead, their springs shifted elsewhere. Others overflow in a constant thin sheet that ripples down the sides and feeds the ones below. The whole forest is plumbed together, and water passes from column to column, ledge to ledge, in a slow cascade toward the low center of the basin.

Between the spires the ground is grey carbonate mud under a brittle crust that gives like old ice. Under the crust it is wet and cold. Channels an ankle deep braid through it, clear over pale floors, and every channel is edged with a lip of fresh stone that grows perhaps a finger's width in a lifetime.

## The Sound

The place is loud, and the loudness is organized.

Thousands of drips fall into thousands of hollow chambers, each chamber a different length, each with its own pitch. Over the drips lies the wind. It comes down off the rim in slow pulses and crosses the open mouths of the spires, and they hum like bottles blown across. Short spires whistle high. Wide ones give a low, chesty note you feel in the sternum before you hear it. Together they make a shifting chord that is never quite resolved, thick as a crowd murmuring in a cathedral.

The chord depends on the weather. Before rain the air pressure sags, the spires sound flat and heavy, and the drone drops half a tone. In dry cold snaps it thins and sharpens. Anyone who lived here would read the sky by ear. The spires also sound differently at the edge of the basin than in the middle, because the young ones on the margins are short and shrill and the old central ones are tall and deep. Walking inward, you descend through the register of the chord.

Under all this is a further layer, a low crackle. It is the sound of the crust thickening, mineral crystals snapping into place, a sound too fine to hear except in the rare moments when the wind drops. Then it seems to come from the ground itself.

## Light

The sky is a lid of thin, unmoving cloud, and the light through it is pewter. Shadows are soft, and nothing is bright except the water. Dusk lasts a long time. In the deep places between the tallest spires it is nearly dark even at midday, and there the mats glow.

The mats are films of microbes, the only living surface that spreads. They stain the channel floors in bands of ochre, green-black, oxblood, and a vivid apricot near the warm vents. In the darkest hollows some produce a faint cold luminescence, a blue-green shimmer like breath on glass. It brightens when the water is disturbed and fades over a minute. Walk through the channels at dusk and a ghost of light runs ahead of your footsteps.

## Cold and Wet

The air is cool, about the temperature of a cellar. It is saturated and beads on every sharp surface. Mist collects in the spire-thicket and doesn't lift, so at head height you stand in a low, slow fog while the taller spires poke through it like pale masts. Your breath shows. Your sleeves darken. The rocks weep constantly, even those that have no spring in them, from the condensation alone, so that the whole forest looks slick and freshly cried-over.

The vents themselves run only a little warmer than blood, and a hand held over one feels a faint exhale. Small pools around them stay clear of ice on the coldest mornings, and a fringe of steam clings to them.

## Life That Isn't Plants

There is nothing green, and nothing with roots. What lives here is small and pale or armored.

In the pools live eyeless crustaceans the size of a grain of rice, translucent, with a pink thread of gut visible. They swarm in slow clouds over the mats and scatter when a shadow crosses. Snail-like grazers with white, thin shells inch along the channel floors, leaving clean tracks in the colored film. Their trails are the nearest thing to writing the basin has.

Larger fauna are rare and always a surprise. A creature something like a long-legged, wingless wader, grey as the spires, sometimes stands motionless in a channel for hours, and in the fog it is hard to tell from a spire until it moves. Its beak is a flat comb for straining the mats. It is silent, and in that place it is the only silent thing. No one has counted how many there are. A traveler might see one in a day, or none in three.

## Marks

There are traces of people, or of someone, scattered thin across the basin. On the southern rim stands a cairn of dark cliff-slabs, gone white with mineral crust, and beside it another, half-collapsed. Halfway across the basin, on a rare patch of firm ground, is a single room of stacked stone, roofless, waist-high, its doorway facing the rim. Its walls include several spire-tubes set upright in the masonry. Wind across them gives that corner of the room a clear, sustained note, deliberately tuned, so that whoever built it wanted to listen.

In the older crust, handprints have been pressed into what was once soft mud and then sealed by the stone that grew over them, dozens of hands, some small. They lie in a line leading toward the center and stop. Whoever made them left no tools, no bones, no hearths. The mineral is already thickening over them.

## The Rule of the Place

Everything here is patient. The spires climb a hand's breadth in a generation, and the chord slides lower by degrees over the centuries as the pipes lengthen. The basin is filling. Someday the outer spires will fuse into a crusted floor, and the cliffs will lean and drop their blocks into it, and the water will find a new fault and open another forest elsewhere. For now the place is young, and its voice is still assembling itself, pipe by pipe, out of cold water and a mist that never quite clears.

If you stand in the middle for long enough, the chord begins to feel like a single sustained thought, and it seems that the basin is not making noise so much as holding a note it hasn't finished.
