# The Dry Floor

Warmth is the first thing you notice here. It comes up through the soles of your feet, the way a stone step holds the afternoon after the sun has gone. The air is body-warm and unmoving, and it tastes of chalk and old dust. The sinkhole above you keeps out any wind.

## The Basin

The floor is a shallow bowl about two hundred paces across. You could walk from one wall to the other in the time it takes to lose count of your steps. It lies at the bottom of a collapse, a pit that opened in the limestone plain so long ago that its edges have forgotten they were ever broken. The walls rise steeply into black, but not in cliffs. They are rounded and shouldered, cut into slow scallops and fluted ribs where ancient runoff once poured over the lip. It has not poured for a very long time. The grooves stay sharp in their depths and soft along their crests, like the fingers of a hand worn smooth by holding.

The rock is pale, a cream-grey you can only tell by touch and by the little light there is. It feels chalky, faintly greasy, and fine-grained. Rub it and your fingers come away powdered. Where the stone has weathered longest it has formed a crust like dried bread, thin and brittle, with hollow ground beneath. Your heel breaks it with a small muffled crunch.

## The Light

There is very little light, but it is not none. Far overhead, where the pit opens to the sky, a ragged ellipse of dark blue-grey hangs at the top of the dark. On some unmeasured night a few stars sit in it, as fixed as nails. This is not day, and it has never been day here. The opening faces a sky that holds only this dim, permanent dusk, and the light that falls through it is thin as breath. It pools on the basin floor as a faint paleness. You can make out the shapes of things, but not their surfaces. Shadow is not a contrast here. It is the medium, and you wade through it.

Your eyes, given enough time, find the pale rock as a dull gleam and the plants as ink-blots against it.

## The Plants

Vegetation is sparse and hardy, and it holds to the one place the light reaches. Directly under the opening, on a floor of fine pale silt, grows a scatter of low, grey-green shrubs. Each one is spaced from the next by a stride or two of bare ground, as if they had agreed on a distance long ago. They are dense, wiry, and leathery-leaved. Their leaves are small and curled inward against dryness that has no season. Crushed between the fingers they give off a resinous, medicinal smell, sharp and clean against the chalk.

Between them, in the cracks of the floor, grow tufts of pale grass. It is dry, hair-fine, and colorless in this light, and it feels like brittle thread. It stands dead and alive at once. The plants here do not die and do not flourish. They hold. Toward the walls, where the light fails, the plants thin out and stop, and the last ones are just pale stubs of stem with a leaf or two. Beyond them the stone is bare.

No plant here flowers as far as anyone can tell, and no seed is scattered on any wind. The shrubs stand as they have always stood.

## The Stone Underfoot

The floor is not flat. It undulates in long, low swells like a sea that set solid, and each swell is a few hand-spans high. In the hollows between them lies a fine, dry silt, soft as flour. It takes a footprint perfectly and keeps it. Some of the footprints in it are yours, but some are not, and they are not fresh. They are old prints of bare feet, shallow, their edges rounded off by long settling of the powder. They wander from the shrubs toward the eastern wall and stop at a shelf of stone.

## The Shelf

Against the eastern wall, the rock has weathered into a low ledge, waist-high and long as a room. On its top are a dozen shallow bowls. They are round, palm-smooth, and polished at the bottom. They were not made by water. Someone ground seeds or pigment in them, patiently, over more lifetimes than the ledge can count. The rims are worn glassy. In one bowl lies a hand-sized cobble, dark and heavy, the grinding stone, still resting where the last hand set it down.

Nearby, on the ledge and on the floor below it, lie flakes of a black glassy stone that does not belong to this pale rock. They are struck edges, thin and curved, and some are still sharp enough to cut. There is one scraper, fitted to a palm, its working edge nibbled small by patient retouching. It is the only made thing here that is still a tool.

Nothing has been built. There is no wall, no ring of stones, no roof. A few flat stones have been set upright in the silt, perhaps by hand and perhaps not, and you cannot tell which. The people who came here used the place and did not change it, and then they left.

## The Sound

The quiet is not complete. There is a very faint sound, so constant that you hear it only when you hold your breath: a thin, dry, high hiss, like sand sifting through a crack. It comes from the walls, where some slow seep of air moves through the porous rock. It never rises or falls. Beneath it, your own pulse. Your footsteps are the loudest event in the world, the crust breaking, the silt sighing, and the walls send back no echo, because the fluted stone swallows sound the way it swallows light.

A dropped pebble makes a small dull tock and lies where it lands.

## The Rules of the Place

Nothing here moves on its own. The warmth does not fade, since it comes from somewhere deep in the ground and has done so for longer than anyone counted. The light does not brighten and does not fail. No wind lifts the dust, no water darkens the stone, and no animal has ever stirred the silt with anything but a human foot. The one clock is the slow sifting of the walls, a grain at a time, and it is so gradual that the basin looks exactly as it did when the last person turned and climbed out.

If you set something down, a cobble or a flake or a pebble, it will stay. The silt will take its shape and keep it. This is the place's one kind of memory, and it forgets nothing and forgives nothing. It is not cruel. It is simply finished, and it has been finished for so long that it has stopped being an ending and become a state.

You could sit against the warm wall, under the ellipse of blue dark, among the grey shrubs and their sharp clean smell, and feel the ground breathe heat into your spine. Nothing would arrive. Nothing would leave. The hiss would go on, and the stars in the opening would hold their places as they always have.
