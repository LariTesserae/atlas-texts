# The Condensary

The summit is nine thousand meters up and only a few thousand years old. Its flanks are black scoria and unweathered glass that still cuts through boots. The air is thin, dry, and dull white. Most of the heat here comes from below. The rock underfoot is warm as a sleeping animal, and in places too hot to kneel on. No lichen has ever taken hold. Nothing up here has had time to die and become soil.

The city is inside.

## The Way In

On the leeward side, where the newest lava tongue split like a lip, there is a crack you could walk past. It is shoulder-wide, tall, and breathing. Warm air leaks out of it in slow pulses, about one every eleven seconds, and where the breath meets the cold outside it leaves a beard of white rime on the rock. Follow the crack down and the walls change from raw basalt to something smoother. The glass here has been *drawn*, not broken. It is pulled into fine vertical ridges, like the inside of a throat.

After a hundred paces the crack opens, and you are standing on a ledge above the Condensary.

## The Chamber

It is a single gas bubble, frozen in the mountain when the lava stiffened, about four hundred meters across and a hundred and fifty high. The ceiling is a smooth dome of dark glass that sweats. Its whole inner surface is beaded with moisture, and the droplets slide along set paths, gathering into rills that run down toward the city like veins in a leaf.

The light is dim and amber-red, coming from below. Seams of molten rock, thin as wire, run through the floor of the chamber and glow the color of a coal seen through a closed eyelid. Everything is lit from underneath. The shadows point up.

## The City

It fills the floor of the bubble, and the entire settlement would fit inside a stadium. The buildings do not look built. They look *poured, then coaxed*: tall fluted towers of black and smoke-gray glass, thin as organ pipes, some bundled in clusters, some standing alone. Between them run terraced channels, bridges as fine as spun sugar, and stairways that spiral up and end at nothing. Every surface is cut with capillary grooves, thousands of them, a fine texture like a fingerprint the size of a city.

There are no doors. There are apertures, and none is human-shaped. Some are slits a hand's width tall. Some are perfect circles too high to reach. Some are rows of tiny round pores, like a wasp's nest. Whoever or whatever used this place did not walk through it the way you do.

No one is here. There are no bodies, no tools, no writing, no furniture. The city is empty the way a clock is empty. Everything is still working.

## What It Does

Heat rises from the seams and the towers drink it. Each spire has a hot core and a cold crown, and between them is a gradient the city manages with something that is not quite plumbing. Water condenses on the dome, runs down the rills, and enters the towers through the lowest pores. It heats, climbs the inside of the fluted walls as vapor, and cools again at the crowns. There it beads and falls, or, in the strangest towers, does not fall. It creeps *up* the outside of the glass against gravity, in slow silver threads, and gathers at the spire tips into drops that hang trembling and never drop. These are the Held Drops. They can be the size of a fist. Inside each one, if you bring a light close, is a faint moving pattern like a slow eddy of ink, and it changes when you breathe near it.

The engineers of this place did something with heat and surface tension and information that your physics has a word for only in part. The city does not merely move water. It *sorts* it. Water goes in one way and comes out carrying a different state, colder than the room and more orderly, as if the towers were stripping entropy off it and stacking it somewhere. Instruments would show a small, steady, impossible cold in the crowns, and a corresponding warmth nowhere. The ledger does not balance. The city is quietly losing disorder to a place you cannot find.

## The Weather Inside

The Condensary has weather, though it is a small and controlled weather. Every few hours the seams brighten and the chamber floods with humid heat. The dome weeps in earnest, the rills swell to streams, and a fine warm rain falls in the avenues. It smells of hot stone and something sweetish, like struck flint. Then the seams dim, the air clears, and the towers exhale long banners of mist that hang at mid-height and fold slowly into each other.

Some nights the temperature climbs high enough that the glass tones start to sing, a thin rising note from the tallest spires that lasts a minute or two and ends without resolving. Between these events the place is quiet. You hear the drip of water in the grooves, the tick of cooling glass, the eleven-second breath from the crack, and, far off and below your hearing, a slow pressure that you feel in the sternum more than the ears.

## Rules

- **Water is never lost.** Whatever you pour out, the city collects. Leave a cup on a terrace and within the hour the grooves have drawn it away, leaving a dry ring.
- **The towers grow.** Slowly, you can see it: fresh glass threads at the rim of a spire, clear and hot, where yesterday the tip was blunt. Something in the mineral structure follows a plan, and the plan is not finished. Nobody is building. The city is *continuing*.
- **The city notices heat.** A warm body standing long in an avenue makes the nearest pores open and the rills bend toward it. Nothing harmful happens. The glass simply leans, in the way a sunflower would if there were sunflowers, and offers a slow drip to your open palm. The water is warm, faintly metallic, and drinkable, though people who drink it report dreaming of stairways.
- **Nothing lives here.** No moss, no insect, no rot. The air is too hot, the rock too young, the water too strange. The only motion is the city's own.

## The Oldest Room

At the center, where the seams converge, a single tower is fat where the others are thin. Its base is a wide shallow bowl of glass filled with still water, perfectly level, two meters across. The water is never disturbed by the rain or the mist or the singing. On its surface, if you wait, a slow ring forms in the middle and spreads outward, one ring every eleven seconds, exactly in time with the breath of the entrance.

It is the mountain's pulse, or the city's. The city reads it and answers it, and the two are in step. Whether the tower is listening to the mountain or the mountain is being taught by the tower, the glass does not say.

You climb back out through the crack, into thin white daylight and the cold. The rime beard on the rock is already melting where you touched it. Behind you the breath comes once more, warm against your neck, and the city goes on sorting the water, drop by drop, into a stillness that is not yours to have.
