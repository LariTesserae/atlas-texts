# The Sump of Halvane

You arrive by a spiral stair cut into the wall of a collapsed limestone bowl, and the stair is the last dry, bare thing you will touch. It descends about ninety meters. Somewhere past the sixtieth, the air changes from mineral to green, thick and warm as breath. The floor of the bowl is perhaps three hundred paces across. Everything in it is alive, or was built by something that is, or is being slowly eaten by something that is.

## The Ceiling and the Light

The sinkhole's mouth is a ragged oval of pale sky, so far up it looks like a coin. Light falls from it in a single leaning column, and for most of the day that column strikes the eastern wall and turns it the color of weak tea. The rest of the basin sits in a dim green dusk. What light there is comes from other sources. Fungal shelves the size of cartwheels glow a cold blue-white along the trunks. Vein-lichen threads the walls in faint amber, brightening when anything moves near it. Along the old pipe-runs, a pale slime mold pulses in slow waves, and the waves always travel away from the central engine house, as though the machinery were breathing out.

## The Substrate

There is no ground. There is a *mat*: a meter-deep, springy accumulation of moss, root, fallen frond, and the composted bodies of everything that has died here for perhaps four centuries. Walking on it feels like walking on a mattress. Your footprints fill in slowly, like slow breath. In places the mat has been cut into stepped terraces by long habit, and the cuts have weathered soft and round, so the whole floor reads as an old, settled thing, a valley that has found its angle of repose.

The trees are not really trees in the surface sense. They are **stiltwoods**, pale-barked, rubbery, growing straight up in the dim toward the light-shaft, and they fork and fork again into canopies that never quite reach it. Their roots are enormous and arch above the mat like the ribs of stranded ships. The town's footbridges are simply these roots, worn smooth on top by generations of feet, with iron handrails bolted on and long since swallowed by bark so that the rails emerge from the wood like bones from a healed fracture.

Everywhere else: ferns the height of houses, dripping. Curtains of vine that are as much wall as decoration. Bromeliad-like cups holding brown water and the small lives inside it. Moss over everything, thick as fur on the north side of every object and thinner but still present on the south. Nothing here is bare, and nothing has been bare for so long that the idea of bareness has stopped meaning anything.

## The Works

The settlement is sparse. Eight or nine structures stand across the bowl, each separated from the next by a hundred feet of green, so that you walk from one to another as between islands.

The **Engine House** stands at the center: a great brick-and-iron shed, four stories tall, its roof long gone and replaced by a living canopy that the stiltwoods have woven across the trusses. Inside are three vertical beam engines, each the size of a church organ, still turning. Nobody stokes them. Their boilers are fed by a thick amber sap tapped from the stiltwoods through a lattice of copper and clay pipes. The sap ferments in the boiler-vats, and the warmth of the fermentation, plus the geothermal seep from below, drives the pistons. The engines run at a slow, stately tempo, one stroke every four seconds, and they make a sound like a giant clearing its throat over and over: *hwoom... hwoom... hwoom.* Moss grows on the flywheels. The moss is sheared off each rotation and falls in green flakes to a drift of compost at the base, which the fauna harvest.

Around the Engine House are the lesser works: a **rope-walk** whose spinning frames now spin vine into cable, still, in a rhythm no one set; a **glassworks** whose furnace is cold but whose chimney is a nesting colony; a **bell-foundry** with a dozen unhung bells lying in the ferns, each one a small ringing chamber for whatever has moved in; a **pump-station** with a rusted cistern where the town's one real water gathers; and a scattering of workers' rowhouses, brick, roofed with living sod, doors stuck open by root-swell, windows glassless and curtained with vine.

## Water

Water is scarce and precious even here, and the scarcity shapes everything. There is no river, only one slow seep along the northern wall, channeled through a stone gutter that the town treats with the reverence of a spring. Otherwise the water is *condensation*: the warm, wet air meets the cold limestone walls and sweats. Every leaf drips. Every pipe weeps. The ceiling of the canopy rains gently and constantly, a small private drizzle of a few drops a minute per square meter, and it lands on broad leaves with a soft continuous ticking.

Real rain comes about once every eleven days, through the sinkhole's mouth, and it is an event. The light-column turns silver. The whole basin stops for a moment, and then a hundred thousand voices go up together.

## The Fauna

This is the thing that will overwhelm you, because the place is not empty but *loud with lives*, and the sparseness of the buildings only makes the animal density feel more absolute by contrast.

**Bats** hang in the upper canopy and in the Engine House trusses in colonies of tens of thousands, a brown fur ceiling that shifts and mutters. At dusk (when the light-column dies) they pour out of the bowl in a spiraling column, and the sound of their leaving is a long dry whisper like a huge page turning.

**Frogs**, at least a dozen species, fill the ground layer: tiny gold ones that chime like glass, fat green ones that bark, a giant pale species that produces a low continuous drone by inflating a throat-sac against the bell-foundry's bells so that the bells hum. The chorus never stops. It only changes key.

**Cicada-like insects** called *clockers* run in the walls of the rowhouses, and they tick in synchrony, so that whole buildings pulse with sound. The synchrony drifts, and every so often two buildings fall into phase and the whole basin lurches into a single rhythm for a few seconds before dissolving again.

**Larger things:** long-bodied, whiskered arboreal mammals like a cross between otter and monkey, which travel the root-bridges in family gangs, maintain the pipe-lattice by chewing out clogs, and are the only reason the sap still flows. Enormous pale millipedes, forearm-thick, processing the compost. Flightless birds with lantern-fat glowing in their breasts, who patrol the mat at night eating everything small. Something big and slow that no one has seen properly, moving in the deep ferns of the southern edge, whose footfalls you feel as a slow tide in the mat.

## Rules of the World

The basin runs on a few understandings that all its inhabitants, feathered, furred, and otherwise, seem to share.

The engines must never stop. When the sap-feed fails and a beam engine idles, the frogs go silent within a minute, the clockers lose synchrony, and the bats leave the trusses. When it starts again, they return, in order. The machinery is not a thing *in* the ecosystem. It is the ecosystem's heartbeat, and the animals seem to know it.

The mat takes back whatever is set down on it. Leave a tool for a week and it will be lifted into the moss. Leave it for a season and it will be part of the floor. Stay still too long and small creatures will begin to explore you.

And the sound is a kind of map. The town's few human-scale inhabitants, if there are any, navigate by ear: the drone of the bell-foundry frogs to the west, the tick of the rope-walk clockers to the north, the deep *hwoom* at the center. When the sound shifts, you are lost, and when it doesn't, you are home.

## The Hour Before Dark

The best time is late in the day, when the light-column slides up the eastern wall and thins to a single gold thread. The bats begin to stir. The frog chorus doubles. The engines beat on beneath it all, unhurried, and the whole green bowl seems to lean toward that fading coin of sky, roaring softly with life, as though it were one enormous animal about to speak.
