# The Chorale Shelf

The shelf sits at the top of a mountain range, high enough that the sky is a hard, deep blue and the sun has nothing to hide behind. The air is cool and thin and always damp. Nothing grows here. Every surface that could hold a leaf holds a film of water instead, and the light off all that wet stone is almost too much to look at.

## The stone

Everything is built of travertine, a pale calcium stone the water has laid down over a very long time. It is not white but a hundred near-whites: bone, candle wax, the inside of a shell, skim milk, old ivory, and in the shadowed hollows a grey-blue like the underside of a cloud. The shelf is several days' walk across, and the crowding never lets up. Terraces stack on terraces, each a curved rim holding a pool, each pool spilling over into the next. The rims are scalloped like the edges of pie crusts, and the pools are so tightly nested that from above they look like a field of thumbprints pressed in cream.

Between the terraces stand columns, fluted stone pipes as thick as a person's waist and as thick as a house. They grow from the pool floors and from the sheets of rock between. Some are joined at the hip and some lean shoulder to shoulder, and over most of the shelf there is no ground, only a maze of stone throats with water moving between them. Where a gap opens it fills at once with a curtain of dripping flowstone, ribbed and hanging in heavy folds like drapery in a stopped wind.

Every rock face weeps. Water seeps from pores in the cliff and gathers into beads, and the beads slide down in threads so fine they look like scratches in the stone until the light moves. Touch the wall and your palm comes away cold and slightly slick, dusted with a fine grit that dries to chalk on your skin.

## The water

The water is everywhere and mostly shallow. It is clear as glass, and cold enough to ache in the wrist. It is rich in dissolved stone, so anything it touches slowly acquires a stone skin. A dropped glove, a feather, a dead beetle all become smooth, pale, identical lumps within a season.

It comes from the mountain's inner spring, which rises at the shelf's upper edge in a single round basin. The basin is so still and so blue that it looks solid. From there the water finds its way down through thousands of channels. The shelf drops only a few hundred meters from top to bottom, but the water takes that drop in ten thousand small falls, the tallest about the height of a house, most no higher than a step.

The whole shelf is in balance. Each rim grows exactly as fast as the water below it would wear it away, so nothing gains on anything else. A hundred generations of travelers, had there been any, would have found every lip in the same place, every pool at the same depth, every fall pouring at the same angle. The place is old and has settled into a shape it keeps.

## The sound

The falls make a roar that has no gaps. It is the sum of ten thousand small voices, from the fat thud of the big drops to the hiss of sheet flow to the glassy tinkle of threads on stone. Together they make a wall of sound that presses on the chest and buzzes in the teeth. It is loud enough that it stops being sound and becomes weather, something you stand inside. Speaking is pointless. Shouting is pointless. You can feel your own voice leave your throat and be swallowed a hand's width from your mouth.

The strange part is that the roar has a pitch.

The hollow columns, which are open at the top and drilled through by old channels, act as pipes. Air forced through them by the falling water sets them sounding, each at its own note, and there are thousands. They are not random. Over long ages the growth of each column has been shaped by the wind and water passing through it, and the pipes have drifted into a kind of tuning. Beneath the noise, if you stand still long enough and let your ears give up the fight, you begin to hear a chord: a huge, slow, seamless harmony of low fifths and murky thirds that never changes and never resolves. It has been sounding since before anything was there to hear it. It sounds the same at dawn and dusk, and after storms and in still weather. It is the note the shelf makes when it is at rest.

Some visitors say the chord makes them feel more alone and some say less. Nobody argues with it.

## The light

At this height and this brightness, the light is a substance. The sun strikes the pale stone and the standing water and bounces around the terraces until shadows are faint, soft-edged things pooled in corners. Squinting is constant. Everything glares.

The falls throw fine mist, and the mist catches the sun. Rainbows hang everywhere, low and small and close: arcs a few steps across, hovering in front of a particular fall at a particular angle. Because the shelf never changes, they never move. The rainbow at the foot of the third great cascade has hung in the same place for longer than the mountain has had a name. Walk around it and it slides across the mist and stays where it belongs, like a painting on glass.

In the shade of the tallest columns the light goes blue-green and cold, and the stone there wears a faint slick skin, a translucent brownish-green that is not a plant but the first thin trace of one, mineral-fed and barely alive. It does not grow and does not spread. It has held the same patches for as long as anyone could say.

## What lives here

Almost nothing. The shelf is a hard place to live, all noise and glare and cold and stone, but a few things manage.

In the pools, in the warmest shallows near the spring, live small pale crustaceans, no longer than a fingernail, the color of the water and nearly invisible until they move. They cling to the pool floors in the thousands and feed on the film that coats the stone. They are deaf, or so overwhelmed that it comes to the same thing. They feel the world through their legs.

On the cliffs above the shelf's upper edge nest a few dozen grey swifts. They dive into the mist to drink and to snatch the flies that hatch on the wet rock, and they navigate the roaring maze by sight alone. They cannot hear each other, so they talk with their bodies, with wing-flashes and tail-fans and little pointing tilts of the head, a language of posture that fills the air above the terraces with silent, precise conversation. In all that noise, the swifts are the quietest thing.

Otherwise there is only the occasional stray: a mountain goat that wandered too high and stands stunned at the edge, or a beetle blown in from below, shortly to be coated in stone.

## The rules of the place

Nothing here is made. No path, no cairn, no scratched mark has lasted, because the water covers all of it in a season and writes it over in stone. You can walk the shelf and leave no trace but a smoothness in the mineral crust that is gone by the next rain. Nothing is dry, nothing burns, and nothing rots. Whatever comes here becomes stone, slowly, and joins the shelf.

There is no beginning to it and no threatened end. The water rises, the water falls, the pipes sing their chord, the rainbows hold their places in the mist, and the whole bright, drenched, thundering shelf goes on being exactly what it is.
