# The Choir Cistern

You enter by crawling. The hatch sits in a cliff face at four thousand meters, a riveted iron oval the size of a bread oven, sweating rust-colored beads. Behind it is a pipe-throat, then a room no bigger than a chapel. Everything in that room is loud.

---

**The first thing is the sound.**

It is not one sound. It is a pressure with grain in it. Under everything runs the pump-note, a low iron thud from somewhere in the walls, four beats and a rest, four beats and a rest. On top of it are the frogs. They are pale, thumb-sized, the color of candle wax, and there are perhaps two hundred thousand of them. They fill the room's tiers and gutters, and their calls have been tuned over generations into a single sheeted chord, a buzzing shimmer, high and wet, that you feel in your molars before you hear it. Above the frogs are crickets on the ceiling ribs, ticking in interlocking rhythm. Above the crickets, a thin steam-whistle from a valve that has been open, at exactly the same pitch, for longer than anyone has kept records.

You can't shout over it. You learn quickly that speech here is done by touch, or by tapping on pipes. The animals do it too.

**The stone is new.**

The walls are not carved from a mountain. They are cast, poured in lifts from slurry and left to set. But whoever designed the process wanted it to look like the earth's own work, so the surfaces are raw and glassy, with conchoidal fractures, unweathered arrises, and crystal faces that have never met wind. Nothing here has had time to round. Every edge is as sharp as the day it cooled. Fresh-broken granite gray, veined with a bluish seam of something that is not quite mineral. The walls look *about to become* geology. They are a mountain in the hour before it starts.

Water comes out of them at every point. The room is a condensing engine. Cold thin air from the cliff meets warm breath from the pipes, and the moisture beads on the stone, gathers, and runs. Thousands of hairline rivulets track down the faces into gutters cut at exactly the angle needed. Nothing drips onto the floor. Everything is guided. The gutters feed terraced basins, each one a stepped pool a hand's depth, and every pool is packed edge to edge with frogs, their throat-sacs pulsing translucent in the dimness.

**The light is a low amber.**

A few bulbs in wire cages hang from the ceiling on braided cable, their filaments burning a dull orange, too weak to throw sharp shadows. Fog hangs at head height and takes the light in, so the whole chamber glows faintly from inside, like the inside of a lantern made of breath. Where the fog thins you see catwalks, ladders, manifolds, brass gauges gone green, and everywhere the round black eyes of the fauna.

**The fauna is the machine.**

This is the strange part. On the third tier down there is a bank of sensor-membranes, thin drum-skins stretched over ceramic rings, and behind them a mesh of fine copper wires. The frogs' chorus vibrates the membranes. The wires read the vibration and open or close valves. The chorus is the control signal. The pumps move water because the frogs sing a certain chord, and the frogs sing that chord because the water is moving at a certain rate, which keeps the room at a certain temperature and humidity, which keeps the frogs singing.

It is a loop, and it closed a very long time ago.

Nobody tends it. You find a little evidence of former keepers: a stool bolted to the floor, worn dished in the middle; a tin cup on a chain; tally marks scratched in the concrete beside the door, a few hundred, then none. If the keepers left, or died, or turned into something else, the loop did not notice.

**Other creatures fill the gaps.**

Pale centipedes run the pipes eating the crickets' dead. Springtails carpet the wet floors like drifting flour. Blind white fish, long as fingers, fill the lowest cistern, and the frogs' tadpoles are food for them, and the fish's droppings feed algae films that are the only green in the place, a slick, luminous green on the drain-covers. A bat colony, small and silent, hangs in the highest vault, and they are the only things that make no sound. They fall out in a cloud every few minutes and return, and this is the closest thing to a tide.

Everything is packed so tight that you cannot put your hand down without touching a life. Frogs sit on the catwalk rails shoulder to shoulder. They sit on your boots. The floor is a moving mosaic of backs.

**The cold.**

It is just above freezing. Breath shows, and so does the frogs'. It hangs over the pools in tiny plumes, thousands of them, a low field of small clouds. Your fingers ache. The metal burns to touch. The cold is essential: it keeps the metabolism of the whole creature-population slow and steady, and it keeps the condensation working.

**The rules of the place**

- Nothing changes. Whatever you measure now, you would measure in a hundred years: the same gauge readings, the same chord, the same fog height, the same number of drops running down each wall.
- Nothing grows in from outside. Sparse moss stitches a few of the seams, a thin dark felt kept alive by the drip, and it has stopped at exactly the same margins for as long as anyone could tell.
- The stone never weathers. It should. Water runs on it constantly. But the water carries nothing, no acid, no grit, and the walls sit sharp under it as if time were an argument they had declined to join.
- The sound never drops. If it did, if the chorus faltered by even a semitone, the valves would shift, and the room would begin, for the first time in its existence, to change. Something in the room seems to know this. The pump-note thuds on. The frogs sing on. They have the look of animals holding a very heavy thing up together, without knowing they are.

**Leaving**

When you crawl back out through the hatch, the silence of the mountain hits like a slap. Wind. Snow-light. The cliff face, gray and blank. Behind you, in the iron oval, the chorus continues, muffled now to a hum, a faint warmth on the rock where the steam leaks. If you press your ear to the cliff you can hear it still, four beats and a rest, four beats and a rest, and beneath that the shimmer of two hundred thousand small throats holding a mountain in the moment before it begins.
