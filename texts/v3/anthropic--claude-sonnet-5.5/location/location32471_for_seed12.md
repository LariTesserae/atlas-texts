# The Terraced Deep of Ammerel

The plateau sits high, higher than most of the world's dry land, and it is drowned. Nobody living there remembers it otherwise. Above it lies a hundred fathoms of warm green water, and above that a bright wrinkling ceiling that the inhabitants call the Lid. The Lid is where water stops and the rumor of air begins.

## The ground that is still being made

The floor is unfinished. Along the plateau's spine, long fissures open a hand's width every few generations, and from them seeps water hotter than the sea around it, clouded with dissolved stone. Where that water meets the cooler sea, it lets go of its burden. White and cream travertine builds up in terraces, each a shallow basin with a scalloped lip, stair on stair down the slopes. Some are still soft enough to dent with a thumb. Others are a season old and hard as bone. Between them lie fields of raw black basalt, glassy and unweathered, with edges sharp enough to cut a swimmer's palm. Nothing has had time to wear them down. Pillow-shaped lava lobes stand in heaps, their skins still bearing the wrinkled, ropy texture of the moment they cooled.

The land is being pushed upward a finger's breadth a decade. The fissures groan when they slip. It is not a sound so much as a pressure in the chest, felt through the water more than heard, and it arrives perhaps twice in a lifetime as a long shudder that sends silt lifting off every terrace at once, like breath released.

## The water

It is warm as a bath near the vents and merely mild farther out. It is layered. Below the Lid, the sea divides into slow strata with faint optical seams between them, where the shimmer of density change makes distant things bend and double. A swimmer crossing a seam feels a slight cool slide across the skin. The people treat the seams as walls and floors of an immense unroofed house, and they give directions in terms of them: *two rooms up, past the green stair.*

Light comes down dim and gold-green, gathered into slow-moving nets on the terraces that flicker and drift. By what would be afternoon, the far reaches of the plateau go the color of strong tea. After that, the light belongs to living things.

## Green, everywhere

The vegetation is lush to the point of crowding. Ribbon-grass grows in meadows thirty feet long, silver on the underside, all leaning one way with the faint steady current from the vents, so that whole hillsides seem combed. Among them stand the *lampstalks*, thick jointed plants with hollow trunks that trap gas from the seeps. Each swells slowly through the day, and when the pressure is right, it releases a single perfect silver sphere that wobbles upward toward the Lid, trailing a thin bright thread of smaller bubbles. Whole groves let go at once sometimes, and the water fills with slow ascending lanterns.

Fan-ferns cling to the travertine lips and are gradually entombed by them. Their leaves are pressed into the stone in exact detail, and the terrace walls are full of green ghosts. Curtains of a pale hairy weed hang from the overhangs. A cushion-moss, deep red, spreads across the black basalt as the first colonizer, the only living skin on the youngest rock.

## The city

The settlement, if it should be called that, is grown as much as built. The people do not have metal. They have obsidian blades knapped from the basalt glass, shell scrapers, bone needles, cord twisted from ribbon-grass and lampstalk fiber, and mallets of dense black wood. They cannot fire clay, because there is no fire. What they have is patience and the springs.

They build frames of lashed lampstalk and woven grass in the shapes of rooms, arches, stairways, and cisterns for the trapped gas they use for light and buoyancy. Then they set the frames beneath a seep and wait. The mineral water coats the weave and stiffens it. In a few years a house is a calcified shell, cream-white and faintly translucent, with the pattern of its cords printed in relief. Walls thicken. Old streets slowly narrow as the stone reclaims them, so the city is always being re-cut. Every family owns a set of chisels for reopening doors.

The density is high. Houses cluster on the terraces in stacked, shouldering tiers, with rope-bridges and grass-woven awnings across the lanes. Gas-globes tethered in windows glow a dull amber, kept alive by a small luminous weed the people cultivate in jars of horn. From above, at the Lid's underside, the city looks like a spill of warm coins on a green cloth.

## Sound and stillness

It is mostly quiet. The dominant sound is a fine crackling, the *tick* of mineral growth, which is audible if you lay an ear against a terrace wall. The bubble-groves add a soft popping. The people talk sparingly, and much of their speech is done by knocking: a smooth stone against a hollow calcified wall, in patterns that carry farther than a voice, across whole districts. In the evening, the city taps itself to sleep in a slow, ragged, overlapping rhythm, with no conductor, that thins to nothing.

## Rare presences

Animals are few and mostly slow. Pale, wide-mouthed fish hang in the meadow-shadows like held breath. Something like a giant snail grazes the moss on new basalt, leaving clean black roads behind it. Once in a long while a *drifter* passes overhead, a translucent sheet-bodied thing wider than a house, rippling at the edges, tracing the seam between two strata. The people stop work and tap the walls once, softly, as it goes by. They do not know where it comes from. They say it comes from above the Lid.

## Rules of the place

- Nothing is permanent, but nothing is fast. A house is a lifetime project and a family inheritance. The stone does the rest.
- The dead are laid in open terrace basins and left to be coated. In a generation they are sculptures, and the oldest terraces are galleries of pale sleepers with fern-ghosts in their hair.
- Rising is dangerous and slow. Those who go up too quickly from the deep floor come back with aching joints, and it is forbidden to touch the Lid, though everyone knows someone who claims to have.
- When the ground shudders, everyone stops, holds still, and waits for the silt to settle before anyone speaks, as though the world had been interrupted mid-sentence and were choosing its next word.

Toward the plateau's far rim, the terraces run out and the black rock ends in a cliff, beyond which the water turns cold and blue and unlit. The cold water is where the people place their unfinished stories. The warm world sits on top of it like a lit room built on the lid of a well.
