# The Boilerfields

The land here is only a few thousand years old, and it is under warm water, and it has not stopped making noise since it was made.

## The floor

The basin is low and wide, a shallow bowl of freshly cooled basalt with glass still unweathered on its edges. Pillow lava lies in heaps like stacked sacks, each rind cracked into black conchoidal flakes that cut anything soft. Ridges rise no more than forty meters above the plain, and every one is sharp. Rain has never had time to round them, because here rain falls upward as often as down. Between the ridges are silt flats the color of wet cinnamon, laid down so recently they still hold the ripple-marks of the last surge.

The water is bath-warm and the color of weak tea, then green, then a bruised amber further down. It is not clear. It is dense with drifting matter: spore, silt, fragments of leaf, ribbons of oil that never mix. Sight fails at about thirty meters. Beyond that the world is a suggestion of darker shapes in dim gold light.

## The green

The plants are everywhere and nothing eats them. They grow in strata, floor to surface, each layer strangling the one below.

At the bottom are mat-growers, a rubbery brown-purple felt that upholsters every hard surface, including the machines. Above them grow ribbon-kelps thick as a wrist and forty meters long, swaying in unison as the currents reverse. Above those hang lantern-fronds, broad and translucent, with veins that glow a low sulfur-green. This is most of the light there is. Where the fronds are densest the water turns to green dusk, and where they have been torn away, shafts of furnace-orange leak up from below.

The plants feed on the machines. They have learned the heat plumes, the chemical wash of leaking boilers, the mineral gruel from vent-stacks. Wherever an engine runs, a thicket has grown in a perfect gradient around it, tall near the exhaust and stunted at the edges. You can find a working machine by following the plants toward it.

There is no animal life at all. No fish, no insects, no scuttling in the mats. The plants reproduce by releasing clouds of pale spore that swirl like smoke, and by pure overgrowth. The absence has an effect of its own. Nothing here is wary and nothing flees, so the thickets have no paths.

## The machines

The industry is scattered but immense. Every few kilometers, out of the kelp, rises a plant: a brick-and-iron boiler house, a rolling mill with its gantry cranes, a pumping station with a flywheel taller than a cathedral. All of them are running. Nobody is inside.

The engines are Victorian in spirit and utterly wrong in detail. Their pistons are the size of tree trunks and stroke at intervals that no governor sets. Belts of woven ribbon-kelp, cured and tarred, run from flywheels to line shafts that vanish into the murk. Steam is impossible underwater, and yet there is steam, in white heaving domes that hang around vents and do not condense. It is a plain fact of the place that fire burns here. Furnace doors stand open, and inside them a hard orange flame stands upright in the water, unbothered.

Nothing is being made. Or something is: belts carry a stream of pale, gray, sheet-like stuff from mills into sheds that have no walls, where it is stacked, and stacked, and stacked, and slowly grown over by mat. The stacks are the closest thing to buildings the place has. There are also foremen's huts, a bell-tower with no bell, a row of workers' cottages with every window lit from within by the same green glow. They are thinly scattered, one cluster to a horizon, and each looks abandoned in the same recent way.

## The sound

The sound is the first thing and the last. It is not noise in the ordinary sense. Water carries it faster and farther, and it arrives without direction, as pressure on the ribs and teeth. Flywheels thud at subsonic pitch. Pistons slam in a hundred uncoordinated rhythms. Steam domes scream continuously, a chord of cavitation shrieks over it, and beneath it all the vents roar and the lava-glass ticks and shatters as it cools.

You cannot speak here. Nothing you say leaves your body as anything but a vibration you already feel. Communication would be by touch, or by scrawling in silt.

The sound has consequences. Where it is loudest, minerals precipitate from the water onto the standing waves, building pale ridged deposits that follow the shapes of pressure nodes. These are ghost-architectures: fluted screens, latticed towers, wall-thin arches built by vibration alone. They are the youngest features of the basin and the most fragile. A change in engine pitch breaks a tower in an instant, and it settles into the mat as chalk.

## What is wrong with it

The rules are not stable, and they fail in patches.

Some regions repeat. The same rolling mill stands at three distances along a line, each one at a slightly different hour of its decay, one gleaming, one furred with mat, one skeletal. Walk to the nearest and it is the middle one. The flywheels turn at different speeds, but their sounds arrive simultaneously.

Gravity loses its argument in places. Air, which exists here only as bubbles and pockets, sometimes sinks. Silver spheres roll down along the floor like heavy beads and burst against the stacks. The surface is not always up. In some hollows there is a ceiling of light overhead that is the sea's underside, rippling and bright, and in others the same brightness is under your feet, and the kelp grows toward it in either direction.

Cause and effect skip. A belt starts turning a moment before the flywheel does. A furnace door slams and then swings open. Lantern-fronds pulse in a rhythm that arrives from the future of the pistons. In the worst zones, a single piston stroke goes on and on, fully extended and yet still extending, and the noise of it is one unbroken note.

At the fringes of the fields the breakdown finishes its work. Plants grow in the shape of gears. Silt settles into the imprint of a page of columns and figures. The mat, when you look closely, is woven in a pattern that a loom might make.

## Turbulence

Nothing settles. Vents burst and seal in an afternoon, and new ones open in the silt, hurling plumes of black grit. Surges roll across the basin every few hours, a slow wall of warm brown water that flattens the kelp, snaps the chalk towers, and drags the pale sheets from their stacks and scatters them like leaves. Then the machines resume, the plants stand back up, and the towers begin to grow again in the same places, as though the noise remembered where they belonged.

It looks like ruin, but it is production. The basin makes itself continuously: lava, plant, engine, sound, stone. It is very young, and whatever it is building has not yet decided what to be.
