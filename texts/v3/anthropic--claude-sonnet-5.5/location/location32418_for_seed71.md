# The Worn Table

Everything here has been ground down except the sky.

The land is a plateau so old that its mountains have become rumors of themselves. What remain are the knuckles: rounded gray domes of granite, a few hundred feet high, spaced miles apart and polished by ten thousand million rains. Between them the ground rolls in long, slack swells, like a cloth settling after the thing under it was taken away. The altitude is real. The air is thin and edged, and breath shows even at noon. But nothing here feels high. It feels like a floor that the rest of the world was once stacked on, and then the world left.

## The Green

Cold and lushness ought not to coexist, and here they do, thickly. The plateau is carpeted in a wet, deep green shading into rust and violet. Moss forms cushions a yard thick, and you sink to the ankle in them, then the shin. Between the cushions stand the **felt-columns**, pale rosette-plants on woolly trunks, each as tall as a person and spaced with strange courtesy, a dozen paces apart, as if surveyed. Their leaves are silver with fine hair, and each hair holds a bead of fog. Every column drips slowly at its base, so every one stands in its own dark ring of soaked ground. From a rise, the plain looks freckled with small gray-green ghosts, each in a saucer of shadow.

There are also low tangled heaths with leaves like tarnished coins, and sedge in long combed swathes that lean all one way, though the wind does not always blow that way. Lichens ornament the granite in colors that should belong to warmer places: orange, acid yellow, a red like old brick. There are no flowers in the ordinary sense. The plants bloom in slow pulses of color that pass across a whole hillside over an hour, like a thought crossing a face.

No animal lives here. No insect works the blooms, no bird crosses the sky, nothing grazes the moss, and so the vegetation has grown without interruption, unnibbled, over eras. Its lushness is the lushness of something never contradicted.

## Water

The water comes in a damp, patient way. Most of it is fog: a low white tide that fills the swells at dawn and withdraws by midmorning, leaving every surface beaded. Rain is occasional, and it behaves oddly (see below). Rivers wander across the plateau in wide, unhurried, braided loops, shallow and tea-colored, stained by peat. They have no destination worth the name. The land is so flat that they braid, pool, split, and rejoin, and some end in tarns of black water so still they seem like holes cut in the day. The tarns are cold enough to ache. In them the reflections are not always of the sky above.

## The Cities

There are cities here, few and far apart, and none seems to be in a hurry.

The nearest is a low, sprawling thing that takes hours to cross. It is built of the plateau itself: granite blocks shaped by hammerstone and patience, sod-roofed halls with walls of stacked peat brick, gutters carved from single logs of a dense heath-wood, lintels of stone, ridge-poles of lashed sedge cord thick as a wrist. The only metal is hammered native copper, greened and streaked, used for rivets, door-straps, and long beaten sheets that hang in colonnades and are polished by the wind into a pale sound. There is no wool, no leather, no bone, no horn, because there was never anything to supply them. Everything is stone, turf, wood, fiber, clay, and the dull green metal. The people who built it used only what a land without animals could give.

The streets are wide, paved with flat cobbles, and lined with drainage channels that run with clear cold water, kept clear. The moss does not grow in them. Stairs climb the domed hills into terraces of round houses with smoke-holes, the hearths inside laid with fresh peat and *warm*. Doors stand open. Beds are made of dried sedge. On the tables are bowls of a pale porridge, still steaming, set out beside carved wooden spoons.

Nobody is there. Nobody has been seen. The city is *occupied* in every way but the one you'd expect.

Newer streets are laid atop older ones, and older ones atop older still. Where a wall has been cut open by a stream, you see the layers in section: courses of building after building, each one a dwelling that was raised, lived in, filled, and floored over by the next, down through strata darkening into a black compaction of ages. But the layers are not dead. Lamps burn in the lower ones. Through cracks and grated wells you can hear, faintly, the tap of a hammerstone on granite, patient, rhythmic, from far below and from no direction in particular.

## The Breakdown

The mountains have eroded, and so have the laws. This is the rule that holds the place together: **what wears down for long enough loses its edges, and the edges of the world are among the things worn.**

- **Distance is soft.** A city that looks an hour away may take a day, or ten minutes. Distance behaves like fatigue: it lengthens with reluctance. Walkers learn to look at the ground rather than the destination and accept whatever the ground decides.
- **Rain is not consistent.** It falls in sharp-edged columns you can step out of, dry on one side of an invisible line and soaked on the other. Some columns fall upward, drops rising from the moss and dissolving into the cloud floor. Some fall *late*: a shower arrives under a clear sky, the rain of some morning that has finally reached here. Others are early, and the moss darkens and beads before any cloud has formed, waiting.
- **Shadows lag.** The sun is ordinary and the light is even, a soft bright pewter. But shadows trail their objects like slow thoughts, and at dusk the felt-columns keep casting long shadows for an hour after the light has gone.
- **Cairns restack.** Travelers' stone piles, if left, are found rebuilt in the shape of something else: an arch, a hand, a spiral. It is never the same twice, and it always looks intended.
- **Handmarks.** In wet clay outside the potters' quarter, new handprints appear as you watch: a palm, then fingers, pressing and smoothing. Tools in the workshops move a little when unobserved, adzes shifting on benches, a drill-bow turning half a stroke. The labor goes on without laborers. Perhaps the labor was the only part that ever mattered, and the people wore off first.
- **The horizon has seams.** In the far distance the land sometimes shows a faint vertical line, like the join between two printed panels, where the vegetation on one side is a different season from the other. Occasionally the seam drifts.

## Sound

It is mostly quiet. The quiet has a thickness to it, like the moss. What sounds there are come singly and stand out: the slow tick of fog dripping from the felt-columns; the low ring of the copper sheets when the wind finds them; the hammerstone under the streets; water in the gutters. Sometimes a sound arrives before its cause, a splash and then, seconds later, a stone dropped into the tarn by no one. Sometimes it arrives without one. Footsteps are muffled by the moss to a soft compressing sound like slow breathing.

## Motion

Nothing here is dead. The plateau is *active* on a scale slower than a life and faster than a geology: fog tides, color pulses through the moss, the shifting seams, the columns of rain wandering across the swells like herds of something that does not exist. The rivers change their braids weekly. The cities extend by a course of stone each season, laid by no visible hand. The vegetation ripples in slow waves as if breathing in its sleep. It is a world that has finished becoming anything new and has settled into the long, elaborate repetition of what it already is: green, cold, wet, softly impossible, and faintly warm at the hearth of a house whose porridge is always just set down.

At evening, the fog comes up out of the swells, the copper sheets begin to ring, and in a thousand windows across a city no one lives in, the lamps come on one by one, in the order in which they were first lit.
