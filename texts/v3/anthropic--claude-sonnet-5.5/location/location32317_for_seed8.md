# The Long Lowland

Everything here has already happened. The mountains that once stood over this country were carried out to sea grain by grain, in a span of time that has no useful number. What they left behind is a floor: a plain of pale gneiss and granite worn so flat that a dropped marble might roll for an hour and still be in sight. The stone is banded with old folds, pink and grey and rust, and the bands run underfoot like the grain in a table. The name for this kind of place is a peneplain, but that word makes it sound dead, and it isn't.

## The Water Floor

Water lies on the land the way light does. Rain falls most afternoons, and the stone is too flat and too tight to shed it, so the whole country holds a skin of water a few fingers deep. It is warm as a bath at midday and clear as glass over bare rock. Where silt has gathered it turns the color of tea. You can walk for a mile with your ankles wet and never step in anything deeper than your shin. Then you find a solution hollow, a bowl the rock has slowly dissolved into itself over millions of years, and it drops away three meters into green, cool, still water.

The pavement is patterned with shallow channels, each barely deeper than a hand, branching and rejoining like veins on the back of a leaf. Water moves through them so slowly that a floating seed takes a full day to cross the length of a field. There is a faint tilt to the whole land, a fall of perhaps a meter over a kilometer, and everything living here has learned to read it.

Low domes of bare granite rise out of the wet at wide intervals. These are the last stumps of the old range, none taller than a house, rounded like the backs of sleeping animals. They weep. Water seeps from cracks and from the contact between weathered and fresh rock, and it runs down their flanks in dark glossy stains. Black films of cyanobacteria grow in the streaks, and orange lichen crusts the dry crowns. At the base of each dome a ring of sand and dark peat has collected, and this is where most of the landscape's plants take hold.

## What Grows

Vegetation is sparse because there is no soil to speak of. The rock is ancient and long since leached of everything a root might want. The plants that live here have solved it in a few particular ways.

- **Sedge tussocks** stand in the shallows, each one a fountain of stiff blue-green blades on a pedestal of its own dead roots. They are spaced like guests at a wary gathering.
- **Sundews and bladderworts** cover the wet sand at the domes' feet. They eat what the ground can't give them: midges, mosquitoes, tiny crustaceans. The sundews glitter red and sticky. The bladderworts hang their traps beneath the water's surface, and their small yellow flowers stand above it on wire stems.
- **Twisted trees** grow only on the sand rings and in the cracks of the domes. They are low, wide, leathery-leaved, and hung with pale beards of moss. Their roots grip the stone like fists. Most stand alone. A few make islands of five or six, and those islands are loud with life.
- **Mats of green algae and floating fern** rise and fall with the daily heat, gathering into rafts that drift a handspan and settle again.

Between all these, the bare stone shows through in wide patient stretches. It is warm, slick, and faintly ringing when you step on a dry patch.

## Sound

This is a loud country. In the hour before dusk the frogs begin, and they do not stop until well after dark. They are a dozen kinds at least, all the sizes of a thumbnail to a fist. Some make a plink like a dropped coin in a bucket, some a rattling purr, and one large species makes a low groaning bellow that carries across the flats like a ship's horn. The chorus builds until the air itself seems to shake. When something disturbs it, a heron or a passing shadow, the sound dies in a widening ring, and then swells back inward from the edges.

By day it is different. Cicadas drone in the island trees with a sawing, pulsing whine. Dragonflies clatter against the sedge. Rain, when it comes, arrives as a wall of hiss moving across the water, and the noise of a thousand drops striking a thousand shallow surfaces rises until you cannot hear yourself. Then it passes and the drip and trickle of the weeping domes takes over, a quiet music of small falls into small pools, each with its own pitch.

Birds call from every direction. Ibises honk. Rails give sharp, rattling alarms from the sedge. Something high overhead makes a long descending whistle you can't place.

## Fauna

Animals are abundant. The land is poor in plants but rich in water, and water is what most of them need.

- **Wading birds** are everywhere: herons, ibis, stilts, spoonbills. They stand in the shallows in long loose scatters, each with its private acre.
- **Small fish** live in the connected water and crowd the solution hollows when the shallows warm. Some can breathe air and cross a wet rock in a few flips to reach the next pool.
- **Turtles** sunning on the dome shoulders slide into the water in a line when disturbed, a row of plops that starts a ripple of frog silence.
- **Crayfish and freshwater crabs** burrow into the peat rings and come out at night, so that the sand around the trees is pocked with mud chimneys.
- **Herds of a stocky, long-haired grazing animal**, something like a small water buffalo, wade slowly across the flats, feeding on sedge and leaving deep dark hoof-wells that become new pools. Egrets ride their backs.
- **Insects** are constant: mosquito clouds in the evening, damselflies flashing blue over the water, water striders dimpling the surface.

## The Rules of the Place

Everything here follows the water, and the water follows the slope, and the slope is almost nothing. A few centimeters of rise in the rainy months can flood a hundred hectares. A few centimeters of fall in the dry season leaves a mosaic of isolated pools, each shrinking, each crowded with fish and birds, each smelling of warm mud and green rot. Then the rains return and the pools reconnect, and the fish spread again. The change takes months and is almost impossible to see while it is happening. You return after a season and find that a channel has silted shut, that a tussock has grown a hand taller, that a dome's dark stain has crept a little farther down.

Nothing here hurries, and nothing is new. The stone under the water is older than most of the continent's mountains and will outlast the rest of them. The rain wears at it a fraction of a hair's width each year, and the land settles further toward a final flatness it will never quite reach, because the sea is too far and the slope too slight to finish the job.

## Evening

The light goes amber, then copper, on a horizon that is one unbroken line. The domes cast long shadows across the water. Every pool holds a second sky. The heat lifts out of the stone in a soft breath and mixes with the smell of wet rock, crushed sedge, and something faintly sweet from the bladderwort flowers. The first frog starts up, then another. A heron lifts from the shallows and flies low across the mirror-sky, and its reflection flies beneath it, toward a dome that has been weeping since before there were herons.
