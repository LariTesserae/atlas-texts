# The Held Plateau

You arrive already deafened, or rather the place arrives in you before you have finished arriving in it. The sound is older than your hearing. It is a pressure with a pitch, something like a cathedral organ holding one chord for a geological span, and under it a rhythm of iron: pistons, drop-hammers, the slow cough of valves the size of houses. It comes from every direction and from no direction, because the plateau is too large for any source to be nearer than another. The sound has been the same for as long as there has been ground here.

## The Ground

The land lies at the height where the sky is a darker blue than it should be and breath comes thin and sweet. It is a tableland, but not a smooth one. The rock is young and has not been worn into apology. Fault-blocks stand up in ranks of raw prismatic edges, dark basalt and a grey-green schist that flakes like slate. Their corners are still sharp enough to cut a glove. Ridges run for a hundred kilometers with crests like broken bottle-necks. Between them lie wide terraced basins, flat as poured metal, floored with black gravel that has never been rounded by a river.

Nothing wears the land down. Water is everywhere, but it does not run. It **weeps**. Every cliff face is beaded and glistening, each sheet of rock sweating a slow film that creeps sideways along the cleavage planes and vanishes into moss before it can gather into a stream. The rocks are always wet and never flowing. That is why the edges stay sharp: there is no channel, no cutting, no carried grit. The water goes into the ground as it comes out of the air.

## The Green

It is cold, a few degrees above freezing at midday and just below it at the dim end of the cycle, and yet the plateau is lush. The vegetation is low, dense, and saturated: cushion-mosses in drifts as deep as a man's waist, in colors that don't sit together comfortably (oxblood, chartreuse, a bruised violet). There are fern-like things with fronds curled tight against the chill, and mats of liverwort like spilled green leather. In the wet hollows grow tall stands of a jointed, hollow reed, pale as bone, that clack faintly against each other when the wind moves through them. They are the only tall plants, and they grow only near the machinery, as though warmed by it.

Everything is slick and springy and cold to the touch, and every leaf holds a bead of water like a lens. Nothing rots into peat, and nothing seems to seed or die. The green is not growing. It is simply *there*, at a fixed density, the way the rock is there.

There are no animals. No insects hang in the mist, no birds cross the sky, and nothing moves in the moss. The green is untouched and uneaten, and the silence of living things sits strangely inside all the noise.

## The Works

The built things stand far apart, one settlement to the horizon and then a hundred kilometers of emptiness before the next. Each is a city in the sense that it is dense, layered, and organized, but a city of engines. Stacks rise in clusters of forty or sixty, riveted plate the color of old pennies, ringed with catwalks and dripping with condensation. Boiler-halls with roofs like lifted eyelids. Gantries and pipe-bridges stitching one block to the next. Rail viaducts run out from each city on stone piers and end, without station or purpose, in the moss.

The streets are narrow and stepped, following the fault-blocks rather than cutting them. Rows of tall windows are lit a warm sodium yellow that looks more like habit than welcome. Doors stand open. Inside, the machinery runs: looms with no thread, presses stamping nothing into nothing, governors spinning in their brass cages, belts carrying empty trays around and around. Everything is oiled, and everything is clean beneath the wet. No one is there. No hands have touched the levers, yet the levers move. The seats in the control cabs are worn into the shapes of no bodies you can imagine.

## The Loop

The world's one rule is that it is *closed*. The cities exhale fog through their stacks, not smoke but white, heavy, warm vapor that rolls out across the basins and sinks. It condenses on the cold rock and the cold moss, and that is the water that weeps. Long mechanical arms on rails, articulated like mantis-limbs, sweep through the nearer green and cut a measured swath. They carry it back to the furnaces, where it burns to make steam, which makes fog, which makes water, which makes the green. The arms cut exactly as much as regrows. The swaths are always the same width, the moss always the same height at the same distance from the wall.

Nothing is made that leaves. There is no product, no export, no road out. The works exist to keep the works in balance, and the balance is complete. Nothing has changed here in any interval you could measure, and you begin to suspect nothing can. The equilibrium is not fragile. It is the kind of stillness that belongs to a spinning top, all motion and no drift.

## The Weather

The plateau is large enough to make its own sky. Fog from a hundred cities gathers into a permanent stratum at half a kilometer, a low ceiling of pearl light. Above it the true sky is nearly black-blue and hard. The light is ordinary, a clean daylight filtered through the vapor, shadowless and even, with no visible sun, only a brighter patch that never quite moves. Frontal systems circle the whole tableland like slow wheels, but they have always been in the same places. One basin is always drizzled on, one ridge always in cloud, one valley always clear and bitterly bright. A traveler could learn the weather as one learns a map.

## The Sound, Again

Walk between the cities and the sound rearranges itself but never lessens. In the canyons it becomes a chord, because the rock walls resonate. Two notes a fifth apart, then a third, then a low one that you feel in your teeth. On the open terraces it is raw rhythm, thousands of pistons slightly out of phase, so that the beat drifts and swells and never resolves. Steam whistles cry at intervals no clock explains. Even the reeds add to it, a dry chatter riding above the bass.

It is the loudest place imaginable, and there is no one to hear it. Yet the sound seems less like noise than like the place *speaking*, a single long sentence that has been going on since the rocks were new. And the strangest thing, when you have stood in it long enough, is that it begins to feel like quiet: the quiet of a thing that is exactly as it is, and has no need to be otherwise.
