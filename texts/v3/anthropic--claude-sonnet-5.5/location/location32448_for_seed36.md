# The Held Basin

You descend into it through a crack in a plateau of pale rock, and after a hundred meters of stair-shaped ledges the air changes. It thickens and warms and tastes green. Then the crack opens, and you are standing on the lip of something the size of a river valley that has been pressed down into the earth like a thumbprint in dough.

The basin floor lies perhaps two hundred meters below the plateau, and the sky is still there, a bright white-blue oval far overhead. The light does not arrive as beams. It falls in and keeps falling, bouncing between the walls until it fills the whole volume with a even, glassy, shadowless glare. Everything is lit from several directions at once. Nothing has a dark side.

## The Walls

The walls are raw. There is no other word. The rock has never been rained on long enough to soften, never frozen, never cracked by roots, though roots are everywhere. The faces are clean planes and sharp arrises, cream and rust in stripes, with the fresh, slightly granular sheen of stone that was split open a moment ago. Ledges have square corners. Fracture surfaces still show their plumose ripples, like frozen feathers. Nothing has weathered, and nothing has come down. There is no scree at the foot of the cliffs, no fan of fallen blocks. The walls simply meet the floor, and where they do, the plants begin.

It feels as if the ground were caught in the act of forming, some enormous parting of the earth interrupted at its most complete and then left, with the spell of unfinishedness holding it perfectly still. Nothing here is unstable. It is a pause that has become a permanent condition. The raw stone is not waiting to erode. It has simply been raw for as long as anything has been.

## The Green

The vegetation is not a cover laid over the land. It is the land. Below the first few meters of rock, there is no soil in the ordinary sense: the floor of the basin is a deep sponge of living and half-living plant mass, layer on layer, mosses over ferns over mats of pale rhizome over dense cushions of something like liverwort, all compressed and interlocked and still alive down to a depth no one has measured. You walk on it as on a mattress. It gives, springs, and exhales warm, damp breath around your ankles. Where you step, water wells up in the print and slowly drains away.

Above this floor the plants build upward as architecture. There are three principal forms.

**Column-trunks**: smooth, fat, pale green shafts, some as thick as a house, that rise straight and unbranched for thirty or forty meters before flaring into a crown of broad plates. They stand a few body-widths apart, near-touching, and their plates overlap so that from below you see a translucent ceiling of green glass, every vein visible, glowing with the light behind it. Underneath, the world is a cathedral of emerald.

**Curtain-vines**: thin, ropy, in their millions, hanging from the crowns and from the ledges of the walls, some reaching all the way to the floor. They are so dense in places that you part them with your forearms like beads. They are beaded themselves, each strand carrying a line of water drops that never quite fall.

**Shelf-growths**: flat, stiff, fungal-looking but green, stacked up the column-trunks in spirals, forming ladders, balconies, and terraces. Whole ecologies live on them: little gardens of moss and tiny orchids, and pools the size of a palm where water collects and is held.

Between these, every surface, whether trunk, rock face, vine, or shelf, is furred with something. Nothing is bare. The density is such that you feel the plants are aware of how much room there is and have decided to use all of it.

## Heat and Water

It is hot, a wet, sustained heat like the inside of a mouth, the temperature of a body or slightly above. The basin traps it. The walls hold the sun's warmth, the green mass gives off its own, and there is no wind to carry any of it away.

Water is present but not abundant, a damp rather than a drenching. It exists as condensation. Every leaf is filmed with it. Drops form on the undersides of the column-plates, swell, and either fall in a slow, irregular patter or are reabsorbed. Thin threads seep from cracks in the walls, run a few meters, and vanish into the floor-sponge before they can become streams. At the very center of the basin there is a shallow flat lake, warm as a bath, perhaps a kilometer across, clear enough to see the roots underneath it. Its surface has never had a ripple that you did not make.

Rain, if it comes at all, comes as a fine sifting that falls from the oval of sky and is caught by the upper crowns before it reaches anywhere. Down below, it only ever arrives as drips. The wetness of the place is self-sustaining, a closed loop of breath and condensation and uptake, that has evidently balanced itself so perfectly that it no longer needs to change.

## What Doesn't Happen

Nothing changes here. This is the strangest rule, and the one you feel before you can name it. A leaf does not fall. A vine does not grow longer. A drop that hangs on a strand of vine is the same drop, or one so promptly replaced that no one could tell. There is no season, no dawn, no dusk: the glare stays at a constant white-gold brightness, and it has, evidently, for a very long time. The plants are alive but have finished growing. They persist in a kind of fulfilled stasis.

You realize there is no rot. There is no litter, no dead wood, no brown. Whatever cycle of decay and renewal other forests depend upon has here been replaced by a perfect, motionless standing. If you break a stem, the wound seals over pale and glossy within minutes, and after a day you cannot find it. The place mends itself back to a fixed shape. This is not growth. It is closer to memory.

## Traces

Here and there, mostly at the margins where the trunks thin toward the walls, are the marks of something else having been present.

- **Hollow cairns**: stacks of flat shelf-growth slabs, each about knee-high, arranged in rings of seven or nine, with a moss-lined cavity in the center. They are not stone; they seem to have been woven or grown to the pattern. Some hold a single round seed-pod, pale and heavy, laid on the moss like an offering or a forgotten egg.
- **Doorways**: places where the column-trunks lean together to form pointed arches, so precisely matched that they cannot be accidents. Beyond them, small chambers of curtain-vine, walled and roofed, with floors of packed moss worn faintly hollow by use. No one has used them for a long time, or it may be that no one has ever used them and they are only shapes the plants make when left long enough.
- **Marks on the wall**: on a few clean rock faces, at about shoulder height, are rows of shallow parallel grooves, too regular for cracks and too crisp for wear. They contain nothing. Roots avoid them.

No tools have been found, no fire-blackened places, no bones. If a people made these, they made them out of what grew, and left before anything could decay to record their leaving. It is equally possible that the basin builds such things for itself.

## The Few Who Live Here

Animals are rare, and mostly small, and none seem in a hurry. On the shelf-growths sit translucent green frogs no bigger than a thumbnail, so still they seem part of the plant. Long, slow, jointed insects the color of wet bark walk the curtain-vines, taking minutes between steps. In the lake are pale, eyeless fish that hover motionless in the warm water.

Once, perhaps, you see something larger: a heavy, soft-bodied creature like a sloth crossed with a pale lizard, hanging by hooked fingers from a column-trunk, chewing on a shelf-growth with a jaw that moves once every several seconds. It regards you without interest, blinks with a slow lateral lid, and returns to its meal. It has no fear of you because nothing here has ever had reason to. It has probably hung in that spot for a very long time.

## Sound

The basin is mostly quiet, but the quiet has a texture. There is the slow, irregular patter of dripping from a thousand crowns, never in rhythm, always from somewhere. There is the faint creak of shelf-plates settling under their own weight, though they never really settle. There is a low warm hum, hardly a sound at all, that you feel more in the chest than the ears, which might be the air moving through the trunks as through pipes. And now and then, from very high or very far, a single thin clear note, like a finger on the rim of a glass, which sounds once and does not repeat. Nobody knows what makes it. It comes at no interval you can predict, and it always seems to be answered by the silence that comes after it.

## Being There

You stand in the center of the floor, in the warm and glowing green, with the wet heat on your skin and the light coming from everywhere, and you understand that this place is not waiting for anything. The rock is raw but will not weather. The forest is dense but will not grow. The water is present but will not flow. It is the first moment of a world, held open, and living things have moved into that moment and made a home in it, and the home is complete.

If you stay long enough, you begin to feel that you are the only thing here that is passing.
