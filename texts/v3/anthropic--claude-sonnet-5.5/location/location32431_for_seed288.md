# The Sunk Meadow of the Thousand Chimneys

You arrive from above, and the arrival takes half a day. The rim is not a cliff. Over some unmeasured stretch of time the wind and frost and creeping roots have rounded it into a long shoulder of pale, crumbling stone, and the slope leads downward so gently you barely notice you are being lowered. The rock changes as you go. Near the top it is gray limestone worn smooth as a knuckle. Farther down it turns to a cream-colored gypsum that flakes underfoot, then to banded clays of rust and olive and dull violet. The basin sits far below the country around it. It is a bowl the size of a small province, and its far side is only a low, lighter line under the haze.

Nothing here is sharp. The basin is old past the point where age looks dramatic. Every ridge has been worn to a swell, every gully to a shallow crease, and the ancient floor is nearly level, tilted a little toward its center, where nothing gathers because nothing flows. There is no river, no pond, no seep, and no stream bed except a few faint braids of paler gravel where water may once have run, before the memory of the oldest lichen.

## The Green Without Rain

The floor is lush all the same, in a low, dense, cushioned way. The cold air settles into the basin overnight and pools there, heavy and still, and each dawn it gives up its moisture as a fine fog. The fog is not water you could gather in a hand. It is a breath of wet that beads on leaves, drips from the tips of moss, and vanishes by mid-morning. The plants live on this. Nothing else in the basin drinks.

- **Cushion-moss and mat-lichen** cover the clay in pillows a forearm deep. They are sage, bottle-green and a gray so silver it looks frosted. Walking on them is like walking on sponge, and each step lets out a small cool smell of mushroom and cellar.
- **Leatherleaf trees** stand alone or in loose groves, squat and broad-crowned and no taller than a barn. Their leaves are thick, waxy and dark, cupped upward to catch the fog. Their trunks are fluted like melted candles, so the dew runs down channels in the bark to the roots. Around the base of each tree the ground is greener and darker in a perfect circle.
- **Ribbon-grass** grows in the open ground between, knee-high and blue-green. It lies over in one direction, combed by the slow downslope drift of night air. At dawn every blade wears a bead at its tip.
- **Ferns and fleshy rosettes** fill the shade under the trees. Where the air is stillest, pale, rubbery blooms hang on stalks like small lanterns, and the moths that pollinate them can be heard as well as seen.

The lushness has gaps. Between the groves lie wide stretches of bare, cracked clay, and salt has crusted whitish on the ridges of old drainage. The place is sparse, not crowded, a green archipelago on a pale sea. You can stand at one grove and see the next a long walk away, with only the combed grass and the bare, patient ground between.

## The Cities

The basin's great works are the mounds, and they are the reason for its name. They are cities built by termite-like insects, though at this size the word barely holds. Each mound rises out of the meadow as a cluster of fluted spires and buttresses, some of them taller than the trees. They are built of clay, gypsum grit and something like dried resin, and their color changes with the layer of ground they were dug from, so some are ochre, some plum, and some nearly white.

Every mound is a layered thing. The colony builds on the ruins of its predecessors. Cut into a flank, or find where the wind has done it, and you see strata like tree rings: a dark old core, a cemented middle, a raw pale outer skin still being laid. The oldest cores are hard as fired brick and have been dead and rebuilt over for longer than any tree in the basin has lived. The insects keep no record and have no need of one. The city is its own memory.

The mounds are not scattered at random. Each holds a territory, and between one city and the next lies a meadow-width of neutral ground. Broad, worn trails cross it, packed and polished, lined with a border of slightly greener grass. Down these trails, day and night, run files of workers carrying leaf-fragments, grit and moss.

The mounds also breathe. The spires are chimneys, each with flues, vents and hollow galleries, and they draw air through the whole structure as the day warms and cools. Standing beside one, you can feel the cool exhale on your face at dusk. You can lay your palm on the wall and feel a low, steady thrum, like a ship's hull.

Other builders have moved into the older districts. Swallows and martins plaster mud cups in rows along the sheltered sides of the towers. Small burrowing mammals excavate the base, and their tunnel-towns run under the meadow for hundreds of paces with round entrance holes as neat as buttonholes. Paper-wasps hang their gray combs in the leatherleaf branches, sometimes forty in one tree. It is a city of cities, layered and shared, and no one in it is in charge.

## The Sound

The place is deafening, and the noise never fully stops. It has layers, and after a while you learn to hear through them.

At the bottom is the **thrum**: the mounds, the shifting air in their flues, and the drilling of millions of jaws inside the walls. It is a pressure you feel in the sternum more than hear. Above that is the **dry rasp** of the grass insects, a fizzing wall of sound from every direction that shifts in pitch as the temperature changes. Above that are the **birds**, and from early dusk they rule the basin. Roosting flocks by the thousand come down out of the dim sky into the leatherleaf groves, and every tree becomes a shrieking, chattering, bickering mass. The sound is so dense that you can watch a person's mouth move six feet away and hear nothing.

And under everything, at dusk, is the **bat hour.** The colonies in the high gypsum caves of the rim pour out in ribbons, and the air hums with wingbeats and with the ticking, clicking, ultrasonic argument of their sonar, which you feel as itching at the edge of hearing.

Silence exists only at the deepest hour before dawn, and even then it is only a lull. The fog comes in with a kind of hush, and the whole basin holds its breath. Then the first bird tries a note, and the roar builds again from nothing.

## The Fauna

Life here is abundant almost to excess, and everything is small to middling, because the ground does not feed the very large.

- **Grazers**: herds of low, barrel-bodied, woolly animals with broad flat teeth, which graze the ribbon-grass down into neat lawns around the groves. They move slowly, in loose bunches, and seem to sleep standing up. They know the mound trails and step around them.
- **Hunters**: long-legged, gray-furred foxlike animals, and low, ground-hugging owls that hunt the burrow-towns at dusk. Something larger, unseen, leaves broad tracks and scat full of shell and bone, but it keeps to the shadowed edges.
- **Scavengers and gardeners**: beetles the size of a thumb roll dung and carrion into the clay. Colorful, flightless birds turn over the moss for grubs. Small lizards, pale as the gypsum they sit on, hunt the insects that stray onto the bare ground.
- **The insects themselves**: moths at dusk, bright as flakes of mica, are drawn to the pale lantern-blooms. Lacewings rise from the grass in slow clouds. The mound-builders tend fungal gardens inside the walls, and you sometimes catch their earthy, sweet-rotten smell on the exhaled air.

Every animal is fed indirectly by the morning fog. Every living thing in the basin lives on dew that came down out of the cold air and passed, drop by drop, through moss, leaf, root, insect and blood.

## Light and Weather

The light is dim. The basin's depth cuts off the low sun for the first and last hours of the day, and a high, thin overcast, pearl-gray and unbroken, holds most of the time. Even at noon the meadow is lit as if by a lamp behind paper. Shadows are soft and colors muted, so the greens look deeper and the clays warmer. The cool air, never quite cold, smells of clay, moss, dry salt and the sweetish insect-musk of the mounds.

The seasons are slow. Over months, the fog thickens and thins. In the long cool phase the groves darken and the grass thickens, and the colonies build upward. In the drier phase the moss goes gray and dormant, the trails quiet, and the birds move out to the rim. Nothing is dramatic. A single spire might rise a hand's breadth in a year. Over centuries, a city moves a few paces across the meadow, abandoning one flank and building the other, and the old side crumbles into a low pale mound, then a rise in the grass, then only a difference in the color of the moss.

## The Rules of the Place

- Nothing here drinks except from the fog, so everything holds moisture, wastes none, and shares what it has. Even the mounds are built to trap dew.
- The oldest structure is the most stable. What has been rebuilt many times lasts. What has been built once falls.
- The basin does not reward haste or size. Its life is dense in numbers and small in body, communal, patient, and loud.
- Everything in the basin lives by the same slow, cold breath, which sinks in at night and rises at dawn as a fog. If that stopped, the green would be gone within a few seasons and the cities would stand empty and silent, still shaped like breathing.

Standing at the center of the basin at dusk, with the roosts erupting on every side, the chimneys exhaling their cool, and the last light caught pale on the far rim, you would not think of it as ancient, or empty, or strange. It would feel like the world at its most settled and most crowded with life, a place that has been finished for so long that it has begun, quietly, to build itself again.
