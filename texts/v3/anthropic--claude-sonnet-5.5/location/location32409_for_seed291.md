# The Sallow Press

From the low rim you see it all at once, because nothing is tall. The plain lies flat and open for a day's walk in every direction, and the city fills it edge to edge. There are no fields or gaps. Roofs touch roofs, and the lanes between them are narrower than a person's outstretched arms. The whole thing sits under an even pearl-colored daylight that casts almost no shadow, so the packed mass looks less like buildings than like a single grown thing, a colony.

It is cold, just above the point where the puddles would skin over. The air is wet without being rain. Beads of it stand on every surface and run together, and the whole city drips in slow, irregular, private ways.

## The ground that isn't finished

The land is young, and that is the first strange thing. Wherever the city thins enough to show bare earth, the earth is raw: clay the color of raw liver, silt that glitters with unweathered mica, ledges of soft gray mudstone with edges still sharp as cut cheese. No river has found its bed. Water moves across the plain in thin braided sheets an inch deep, splitting and rejoining, carrying fine sediment that it lays down in visible films. A boot print fills and, within a day, is a smooth pale scar.

Nothing here has been worn down yet, because nothing has had time. The hills are low blisters, cracked along their crowns where cold mud is still pushing up from below. The people call these *risings*. A rising can lift a street a hand's width overnight and tilt a whole row of workshops toward the west.

The city's engines are the reason the ground exists at all. The great works at the center, the **Press**, take in slurry from the dredge-channels and squeeze it into strata. Layer by layer, in imitation of a hundred thousand years, they lay down banded ground and stamp it with pressure until it holds. The plain is being manufactured outward from the middle, ring by ring. The older rings are firmer and darker. The outermost ring is still the consistency of wet bread, and surveyors walk it on wide wooden pattens.

## The city that grows on its own floor

The buildings are brick, cast iron, and riveted plate, in the manner of a mill town, but they are crowded past sense. Gantries pass through the upper floors of warehouses. Pipe-lattices run from wall to wall like the ribs of a cathedral turned inside out. Chimneys stand in clusters of twenty, each breathing a white plume that hangs low, since the cold air will not let it rise. Steam and fog are the same substance here, and the streets are always half-veiled.

Everything is alive with moss, and that is deliberate. Cultivated **felt-moss** is packed into the walls, wrapped around boilers, and laid thick on roofs and cart-wheels, because the city's law is *hush*. The moss drinks sound. Engines that would scream in a drier place breathe instead, and the great pistons of the Press turn out as a pulse you feel in the soles of your feet more than hear. Stand still and the loudest things are the drip of water from a gutter, the wet clop of a hoof, and the tick of iron contracting in the cold. Somewhere a bell rings and comes out as a muffled knock, like a knuckle on a door three houses away.

Around the moss grows the rest of the lush, cold-loving green. Tree-ferns crowd the courtyards. Horsetails rise in stiff jointed ranks from the flooded gutters. Sedges and liverworts carpet the flat roofs, and pale lichen scabs the ironwork in orange and sulfur-yellow rosettes. Every unclaimed inch is thick with green, so that even the gantries look furred. The colors are deep and wet: bottle green, black-green, rust, the rare startling red of a fern's new frond unrolling.

## Rules

- **Nothing stays dry, and nothing stays still.** If a thing is left in place for a season, moss takes it, then the rising takes it, then the next stratum of the Press covers it. Old tools turn up in the pressed rock of the inner rings, and the city treats them as fossils and sells them.
- **The plain breathes.** At the grey hour before dusk the whole ground swells slightly, and then subsides. The engines are timed to it. Doors that don't seat properly at that hour are called *tide-stuck*.
- **Streets are provisional.** Whole lanes are hauled aside on iron rails by chain-gangs and slow steam-oxen to make room for a new rising or a new stamping-floor. A map is good for about nine days. People navigate by smell and by the color of the moss on the doorframes.

## The turbulence

The quiet is misleading. Underneath it the city is in constant, almost violent motion, and it simply doesn't announce itself. Cranes swing without a shout. Foundations shift and are shimmed within the hour. A squall of sleet arrives, whitens everything, and is gone in ten minutes, and the crowds do not even break stride. Whole blocks are dismantled and reassembled a quarter-mile away in the course of a week, and people move house by walking along with their walls. Water surges through the sheet-channels after a thaw upstream, scours a new gully in an afternoon, and is dammed by evening. The plain is always being torn up and remade, like a bazaar of geology.

## Fauna

The creatures thrive in the crevices between machines, and the city is dense with them. Pale **culvert voles** run the pipe-tops in the thousands. **Grey herons** stand on the roofs waiting for eels in the sheet-water, and the eels themselves, the **lamp-eels**, glow faintly along their flanks, so the shallow water at night holds a scatter of dim green slow-moving lines. Fat moths, furred against the cold, drift in the steam like ash. Under the boilers whole colonies of long-legged, translucent beetles graze the warm condensation and are harvested for a fine grease.

The weirder animals are the **plate-gulls**, which have learned to ride the pressing cycle: they land on the wet fresh strata in flocks just before a stamping and lift away a breath before the weight comes down, all at once, in perfect silence. People say some of them are flattened into the rock every year, and that the fossil-sellers keep them for luck.

## What lingers

Late in the day, when the light stays exactly the same pearl and the fog thickens to milk, the whole place goes so still that you hear the ground being made. It is a faint, continuous sound, like a very large animal chewing very slowly, deep under the moss and the iron and the low green roofs. Then the plain rises its hand's width, the doors stick, the herons lift, and the city carries on being built.
