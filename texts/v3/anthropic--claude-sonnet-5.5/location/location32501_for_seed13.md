# The Held Terraces of Ossary Vale

## Approach

You come up from the low country by a stair of about four thousand steps, cut into a fault-scarp so fresh it still looks like it was split yesterday. The rock is a dark grey-green volcanic tuff shot through with columns of basalt. Every edge is crisp. The faces of the columns are flat as cut slate, and the angles between them cut cleanly against the sky. Nothing here has been rounded off. Nothing will be, because in this valley the weather does not wear anything down. The sharpness is not youth passing. It is a condition, like the height of the sky.

At the top the land opens into a long, shallow basin about a day's walk across, ringed by broken ridgelines. The air is cool and slightly thin, and it tastes of wet stone and crushed leaves.

## The Lid and the Light

Above the basin hangs a flat-bottomed layer of pale cloud, always at the same height, like a ceiling. It never thickens into storm and never thins to clear. Under it the light is soft and even, the light of a bright, unhurried mid-morning. Shadows fall the same way everywhere and at every hour, short and blue-edged. The people call it *the Held Hour*. They do not seem to find it strange. You will find it strange around the third day, when you realize you have not once seen a shadow move.

Moisture comes down from the lid in narrow columns of fine rain, each one standing over the same patch of ground it has always stood over. You can walk into one and out of it as you would a doorway. Inside, the air is loud with a soft hiss, everything drips, and the ferns are the size of umbrellas. Ten paces away the ground is merely damp. The rain columns are the valley's wells, and the settlements are arranged around them.

## The Ground Cover

The vegetation is lush without being crowded. It gathers in green pools around water and thins to open, springy turf between. There are tree ferns with trunks like wet rope. There are broad-leaved, silver-backed shrubs whose leaves are always beaded with condensation. Cushion moss grows in domes thick enough to sit on, and stands of pale-barked, twisted trees carry lichen beards a metre long. In the rain columns the moss climbs the basalt in thick green fur, but it stops exactly at the rim of each column, so the boundary of the rain is drawn in green.

No leaf falls here. No bud swells. The trees are neither in spring nor in autumn. Ask the valley-dwellers about seasons and they will offer you a word that means something like *the going-past of other places*.

## The City

The city, if that is the word, is called Ossary. It is not one cluster but a scatter of roughly sixty compounds spread across the basin, each a few hundred paces from the next. Between them lie open meadows, ponds, stands of fern, and grazing slopes. The whole is city-scale in its structure and its layering, with roads, stairs, cisterns, drains, terraces, granaries, and shrines, but it has the spacing of a village that has been stretched thin.

Every compound is built from the ground's own material. Walls are dry-stacked blocks of pale tuff, fitted without mortar and chinked with moss. The blocks were quarried by wedge and fire-crack and finished with stone mauls and antler picks. Roofs are thick thatch of a grey reed, sloped steeply, layered so deep that they look like animals crouching. Lintels are single basalt columns, lifted by levers and log ramps. Doors are hide stretched on frames of bent wood. The only metal is soft copper, hammered into pins, rings, and the edges of adzes. It is bright green where it touches the wet air.

The layering is what makes it urban. Each compound stands on the remains of an earlier compound, which stands on an earlier one. The lower storeys are filled and sealed, and the upper storeys are built on the old roofs. The oldest walls are at the bottom, dense and windowless. Above them the stonework loosens into finer, more careful courses. No new storey has been added in living memory, and no one talks about adding one. The city is *complete*. It is not abandoned. It is finished, and it stays finished.

Roads are causeways of set flat stones, each slightly dished from feet. They run between compounds in slow curves that avoid the rain columns. At every crossing stands a squat stone marker carved with the faces of animals. There are no wheels here. Loads move on backs, on sledges over the wet turf, and on the long-necked pack animals the valley keeps.

## The People and What They Do

The inhabitants are few for the size of the place, perhaps a few thousand. They wear undyed wool and oiled hide, walk in soft-soled shoes, and speak little. In the compounds they are weaving, grinding, knapping, tending cisterns, combing wool from the pack animals, and dressing stone that is never used for anything new. It is used to *maintain*. When a block cracks it is replaced by a block of the same size cut from the same quarry face. The quarry face is exactly the same as it was, so the maintenance never runs down.

Nobody hurries. Nobody hoards. They do not seem to be waiting for anything.

## Animals

The fauna is abundant, and this is where the valley feels most alive, though it is all quiet. There are herds of small, long-legged deer with moss-coloured coats, moving through the meadows in loose drifts. There are squat, shaggy pack-beasts with wide padded feet, domesticated so long that they walk unled between compounds. There are flocks of grey-and-cream birds that perch on the thatch by the hundreds and that never sing. They communicate by wing-snaps, a dry sound like someone flicking cloth. Pale moths the size of hands drift in the rain columns and settle on the wet stones in loose constellations. Beetles with green-metal shells work the moss. In the ponds live fat, slow, translucent salamanders, and if one surfaces, the whole pond seems to hold its breath.

The predators are there too: long, low, spotted cats that sleep in the sun-warmed lintels, and hawks that hover motionless at the base of the cloud lid. Yet the numbers never change. There are always the same number of deer, cats, and hawks. No one has ever seen a birth or a kill, and this is the deepest of the valley's oddities. The animals eat, they are eaten, and the count stays the same. The valley-dwellers believe that when something is taken, the valley returns it. They say this the way you would say that water is wet.

## Sound

The valley is mostly quiet. What sound there is comes in a few kinds: the constant soft hiss inside the rain columns, dripping from thatch and leaf, the dry snap of bird wings, and the soft *thock* of a stone maul on tuff from far across the basin. Streams run through the meadows, but they run so evenly and so low that their sound blends into the silence. Voices carry, and so people keep them low, as one does in a library.

## The Rule

If the valley has a rule, it is that nothing gets worse and nothing gets different. The sharp cliffs stay sharp. The moss stays at its rim. The light stays at its hour. The city stays complete. You could walk through Ossary for a year and find, on the last day, that you had learned only how to see it better. When you go back down the four thousand steps, the descent feels like leaving a held breath, and you carry, for weeks afterward, the feeling that somewhere up there in the cool green quiet, a shadow is still falling exactly where you left it.
