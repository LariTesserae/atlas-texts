# The Bellows Plateau

The plateau stands so high that the sky has thinned to a hard, pale blue with almost no haze in it. The light is plain and white, the light of a middle-aged star, and it falls on ground that has been exposed to it for longer than anything has had a name. It is hot, the heat of an oven door left open. Air shimmers a hand's width above the rock and carries the smell of struck flint and warm pennies.

There is no water. It is not scarce, it is absent, and the rocks show it. Everything is anhydrous: hematite the color of dried blood, black magnetite sand that clings to a magnet or a boot, sulfur crusts in yellow blooms around the vents, and long sheets of volcanic glass that split into curved shards. Nothing green has ever happened here, and nothing is missing for lack of it. The land is finished in its own way.

## Terrain

The surface has been weathered for so long that it has settled. Ridges have been fluted by wind into long parallel grooves like the inside of a great throat. Mesas stand alone, their caprock balanced on stems of softer stone, and each has been carved and recarved into shapes that look intended: colonnades, stacked drums, ribbed cylinders, cooling-tower silhouettes. None of it is made. It looks like a factory district only because the wind and the heat have worked the way an engineer thinks, and have had far more time.

Between the mesas are pans of polished dark pavement, kilometers wide, where the sand has been swept away and the bedrock shines like a hearthstone. The horizon is far off and slightly bowed. Standing in the middle of a pan, you may see nothing taller than yourself for an hour's walk in any direction, and then a single stack of rock rising on the edge of the world.

## The Herds

The fauna are overwhelming, and the plateau looks empty because of it. There are few of them, and each is so large that the distances between them are just the room they need.

They are called foundry-beasts, though the name is a translation of a translation. Each is the size of a hillside. The body is plated with overlapping slabs of a horn-and-iron composite, rust-brown along the seams and burnished silver where the plates rub. Along the spine run vents like the stacks of a locomotive, and these breathe: a slow inhalation that draws in the hot air and the dust, a pause while something inside glows, and then an exhalation of white smoke and a low gong-like note that carries for kilometers. They feed on the plateau itself. They chew hematite and sulfur, and inside them, in chambers no one has seen, they smelt. What they excrete is native metal, bars and coils and perfect ingots, warm, stamped on one face with the pattern of the inner mold. The plateau is dotted with these lying in the open, untouched, oxidizing over centuries into red lumps that slowly become part of the ground again.

Around each great beast moves a retinue of lesser ones. There are pounding things the size of carts, with stamping feet and rhythmic hammer-heads for jaws, that flatten slag into sheets. There are wire-legged crawlers that draw thread from their own bodies and knit it across the rock in loose, tensioned webs that hum in the wind. There are clouds of small bright things like sparks, which are alive, and which feed on the escaping heat of the giants. Nothing here is built, and all of it is engineered: pistons, bellows, rollers, and dies are simply how flesh works on this world. There is a whole economy of scaled production with no owners and no market, only appetite and output, run by animals.

They are not aggressive. They are too large and too occupied to notice you as anything but a small warm thing on the pavement. The danger is proximity: the heat from an exhalation, the ground-shock of a stamping foot, the falling ingots.

## Sound

The plateau is loud. It is never quiet, but the noise is not chaos. It has a rhythm. Every beast has a working tempo, and the tempos overlap and drift, and the wind carries all of it across the open ground. You hear the low bell-tones of the great vents, the flat clang of plate on plate, the rasp of dragging wire, the hiss of slag cooling, and beneath it all a pulse in the bedrock, felt in the chest more than heard. In the thin air the sounds arrive clean and hard, with very little echo.

At night, when the pans cool and crack, the pings and reports of contracting rock join in, and the entire plateau sounds like a shipyard in the dark.

## Rules and Slippage

Here the world starts to fail, gradually and without drama.

The plateau has slips: regions, some a few paces across and some many kilometers, where the ordinary agreements of reality wear thin. You learn to recognize them by small tells. Sounds arrive before their sources, so you hear a stamping foot a full breath before the ground shakes. A beast's shadow lies in the wrong direction, or falls a moment after the beast has moved on. A ridge you walked toward for an hour is no closer, or is suddenly much closer than the light allows. Within a slip, a dropped ingot may fall upward slowly, or may fall for a very long time. Heat may run backward, so that the hot pan is cold to the touch and the cold shade burns. In the worst places the same herd is visible twice, at two distances, moving in step with itself, and one of them is a little too silent.

The slips are not hostile. They seem to be places where the plateau's long labor has worn through something, the way an old stone floor wears into a dip. The foundry-beasts walk through them unconcerned and sometimes seem to feed on them, and the ingots they produce there come out strange: hollow, or heavier on one side than they should be, or stamped with a pattern that changes if you look away.

## Time

Everything happens slowly. The mesas lose a finger's width in a lifetime. The herds cross the plateau in circuits that take years, following gradients of heat and ore that shift over centuries. The slips creep, no faster than a walking pace per year, so a place that was ordinary in your grandmother's time might now be a spot where echoes return before the shout. Nothing here is in a hurry except the noise. Under the din, the land does what it has always done: it heats, it cracks, it grinds down, and it breathes out smoke and metal into a sky that has no clouds and never will.

You can walk for a day and meet no one and nothing, and still be surrounded, because the one thing you were sure was a distant mesa turns, very slowly, and exhales.
