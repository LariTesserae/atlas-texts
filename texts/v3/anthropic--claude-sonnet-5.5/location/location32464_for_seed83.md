# The Sweating Hall

You enter by a stair that turns down four times and gets hotter at each turn. The last landing is iron plate, worn into shallow dishes where feet have crossed it. Below is a room the size of a small church nave, built entirely of things people made. No rock shows anywhere. The floor is brick laid in herringbone and gone soft at the edges, the walls are brick, and the vault is brick, sooted black and then whitened again by mineral bloom. The air is about the temperature of a body, and it's wet enough that you feel it settle on your teeth.

It is almost dark. Nothing in the room is bright, but your eyes adjust and it gives you what it has.

## What gives light

Along the north wall, low down, is the firebox, a cast-iron door with a mica window the size of a hand. Behind it something has burned slow and low for longer than any record you could read. The mica shows a dull orange, the color of a coal seen through closed eyelids. It lights the nearest pipes on one side only, so that every curved surface has one rim of red and is black everywhere else.

The second light is colder and much fainter. It comes from mats of pale growth on the undersides of the pipes, where the drip is heaviest, a felt of fungus and something like moss that gives off a green-blue glow. You see it best by not looking at it. It lies in long smears and rings, brightest at the lowest points of the ironwork, where water gathers before it falls. When a drop detaches, the light doesn't flicker. The felt just dims for a breath as if it had been touched.

## The structure

The hall is dense. Pipes run at every angle, each the thickness of a thigh or a waist, and they cross and lean on each other and are bolted together where they touch. Gantries hang from the vault on rods, and each gantry has another beneath it. Ladders connect them, and some ladders end at bare wall. Cisterns of riveted plate sit in the gaps like fat animals, and their sides are cross-hatched with old repairs, each patch a different generation of iron.

You move sideways more than forward. Valve wheels the size of cartwheels stand at chest height, and their spokes are furred with mineral. Between the pipes hang gauges with glass long clouded, and the needles rest at numbers you can't read. Everywhere the metal wears a skin of rust in layers, orange over brown over black, blistered like old paint. Where water runs down it, the rust has built into ridges and curtains and slender hanging cones that reach for the floor. In places they've met it, and iron stalagmites have wedded to iron stalactites. The machinery has partly become a cave.

That is the erosion here. Nothing is broken, but everything has been softened. Brick corners have rounded. Bolt heads have swollen into knobs. The mortar has turned to a pale paste that you could write in. It is a very old settled ruin that has stopped falling and reached a balance with its own decay.

## Water

The hall makes its own weather. Heat from the firebox drives a slow circulation through the pipes, and where the vault is cooler, the steam condenses and beads on the brick and runs to the ribs. From the ribs it falls in single drops, thousands of them, each on its own schedule. You can stand in the middle and hear perhaps forty separate drips at once, each at a different pitch, depending on what it lands on. Some strike a puddle, some a cistern lid that rings faintly, some a hanging chain. Others fall on the felt, and those make no sound at all.

The floor is not wet everywhere. It is wet along channels cut into the brick, which carry the condensate in shallow gleaming lines toward a grate in the southwest corner. Between the channels the brick is merely damp and warm, like the skin of a sleeper. The water in the channels is hot enough to be uncomfortable to hold your hand in, and it tastes of iron and, faintly, of coins.

## Growth

Plants are few and they are not green. Most of what lives here is fungal, in pale veils and tufts along the drip lines. But in the warmest damp corners, a fern with translucent fronds grows out of cracks in the mortar, in clumps no larger than a fist. Its fronds are the color of old candle wax and they hold beads of water without dripping. Along one gantry there's a single vine, thin as thread and yards long, rooted somewhere in the brickwork you can't reach. It has climbed a rod, crossed a pipe, and dropped in a long loop toward the floor, and has flowered once. The flower is a small white bell, and it is dead and dry and still hanging there.

## Creatures

Very little lives here, and you will probably see one or two things in an hour.

Pale, long-legged crickets sit motionless on the warm pipes. They do not sing. They step aside a little as you pass, without hurry.

In the cisterns, where the water is tepid and dark, lives something slow. If you lower your face to an open hatch you may see a long pale shape turn once beneath the surface, eyeless, a hand's length, with feathery gills. It comes up to the glow of the felt and then goes down.

The rarest thing is a builder. It is a small wasp-like insect that plasters mud nests on the warmest flanges. The nests are tiny domed vaults, precise copies of the hall's own ceiling. Nobody knows how many there are.

## Motion and time

Things happen here but very slowly, and you have to wait.

Every few minutes, somewhere above you, a great beam shifts. It is an iron rocker arm, black and enormous, that tilts a few degrees with a groan you feel in your sternum, holds for a long moment, and then settles back. Nothing seems to be attached to it. A rod drops from its far end into a well in the floor. Whatever it pumps goes down into the earth.

Every hour or so, a governor at the eastern end, a pair of iron balls on hinged arms, lifts from rest and spins a few slow turns. Then it falls quiet, as if it had checked something and found it sufficient.

Once a day, on a cycle you can't perceive from the inside, the drip quickens. The city above cools in its night, the vault cools with it, and for some hours the whole hall rains gently and every surface glistens. Then the heat wins again, the drips slow, and the walls begin to dry, leaving new pale crusts at their edges. Over the years those crusts have made the room.

## Sound

Mostly it is quiet. There is the drip, and the small tick of metal expanding or contracting, and now and then a low sigh from somewhere in the pipes like a large animal turning over. Under everything is a hum you only notice when the beam moves, because it stops for a moment and then returns.

## What it feels like

It doesn't feel abandoned, though no one has been here in living memory. It feels like something that has gone on being itself with nobody watching. You could sit on the warm plate of a gantry for a long while, sweating, in the orange dark, watching a green light dim and brighten as the drops fall, and nothing would ask anything of you. The hall is patient. It has already worked out what it is for, and it is still doing it.
