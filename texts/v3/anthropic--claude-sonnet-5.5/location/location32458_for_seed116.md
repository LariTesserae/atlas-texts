# The Warm Cistern at Ninth Height

From outside there is nothing to see. The plateau runs flat for two hundred kilometers at nine thousand meters, ground down over so many ages that the peaks are only a faint swelling in the grey. The wind is thin and dry, and the sky is a hard dark blue. The ground is fused slag and old poured ceramic, worn as smooth as a riverbed and cracked into plates. Between two of those plates is a hatch the size of a table, its wheel rubbed to a nub by a hundred thousand storms. Nothing marks it. Under the hatch is a room full of warm water.

## The Vault

The chamber is a rough dome, perhaps three hundred meters across and eighty deep, and it is entirely flooded. The walls are the color of old teeth, made of a glazed aggregate that no one poured in living memory. Time has softened every edge. Ribs, buttresses, and gantries have all slumped into rounded, bonelike forms, as if the architecture were melting very slowly in the water that fills it. Mineral scale has grown over the steel in long pale curtains. Iron has bled down the walls in orange flowstone and hardened into terraced pools, and the pools have filled with green.

Sunlight enters through the ceiling, where forty round windows of thick glass, clouded and lensed by age, are set into the dome. At this altitude the sun is fierce, and the water turns it into wandering coins of light that slide across the floor. The light is ordinary daylight, neither dim nor dazzling. It is bright enough to read by and green enough to feel like the inside of a leaf.

The water is bath-warm, heated by something deep in the substructure that still works. It has a faint taste of iron and struck flint.

## The Garden

Everything that grows here grows on something made. There is no soil, only trays, racks, and terraced beds of sintered grit, set in long spiraling rows down the walls and across the floor. Between the rows lie wide stretches of open water and bare floor, so the garden feels scattered and unhurried, like a village of green islands on a paved plain.

Ribbon-kelp climbs the old cable runs in ropes forty meters long, sways in the pump-currents, and thickens wherever a pipe leaks nutrient. Fans of broad translucent leaves, veined like stained glass, grow from the tops of rusted pylons and tilt toward the windows. Mats of soft moss-like algae coat the flanks of dead machines until they look like sleeping animals. Below them, in the shade of the gantries, is a lower forest of pale fronds that glow faintly when disturbed. Everything is lush, though not crowded. Each stand of growth has its own clearing and its own private current.

## The Sound

The vault is loud, and the water carries all of it. The main pumps sit at the bottom of the dome, three slab-sided engines half buried in scale. They cycle every few seconds with a deep, thudding pulse you feel in the teeth and sternum. On top of that come the clatter of governors, the shriek of a bearing that has been failing for a thousand years, the rolling boom of a valve opening somewhere behind a wall, and the hiss of steam-vents where superheated pipe meets the pool and makes clouds of bubbles that sing as they rise.

Every hour or so a great water-hammer runs through the pipework, a sequence of iron knocks that starts at the bottom and climbs the walls. Every creature in the room pauses for it, then goes on.

## The Animals

Life here has learned the machine. The fauna are abundant, though spread thin across the space.

- **Pulse-fish**, silver and hand-length, school in the thousands, and their turning follows the pump rhythm exactly. The schools tighten on the downstroke and open on the upstroke, so that a cloud of them seems to breathe with the engines.
- **Pipe-eels** live in the drainage conduits and come out only when the water-hammer sounds, in long muscular ribbons the color of wet slate. They hunt by the echo of the knocks.
- **Scale-grazers** are flat armored crustaceans the size of dinner plates. They crawl over the mineral curtains, dissolving the crust with an acid in their mouths and slowly stripping the walls back to metal. Behind them the rust starts again. The two processes have balanced for ages.
- **Lantern-rays** drift in the open water and glide over the gardens like slow kites. They feed on the schools, and are in turn taken by something larger that no one has seen clearly. It moves in the dark under the pumps, and its movement makes the low fronds flare.

## The Rules

The place keeps a schedule. Every eleven hours the vault "tides." Sluices open in the floor and the water level drops by a third, exposing the upper terraces. The kelp collapses in wet heaps, the fish crowd into the deeps, and a rushing roar fills the room. Then the sluices close, the pumps change pitch, and the level climbs back. The garden's growth follows this pulse, and so do the animals' lives.

Nothing here is natural in origin, and nothing is wild in behavior. The moss has been cultivated by accident for so long that it depends on the trays. The fish are the descendants of stock that was once fed by hand. Over the ages the builders vanished and the machines went on watering, warming, and draining, and the garden grew to fill the hours they left.

## Weirdness

Some things seem to belong to another logic. In one corner of the dome a single valve wheel, wider than a person, turns by itself once each cycle, a quarter turn and back, though no engine appears to drive it. The pulse-fish gather around it at that moment. At the deepest point of the floor is a grate, and warm, sweet-smelling air rises from it in slow bubbles that carry a faint tune, like something being hummed in a room below. No one has found the room below.

Above it all the windows flicker. A cloud crosses the sun at nine thousand meters, and for a moment the whole vault dims to a gold-green dusk. Kelp leans, fish scatter, and the pumps keep their long slow beat under a plateau of grey stone.
