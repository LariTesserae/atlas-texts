# The Long Green Works

Start with the floor, because everything else stands on it. The floor is a mat of root-cable, braided thick as a road and laid in courses, like a woven plain. Nothing beneath it is soil. Where the weave has worn thin you can see the layer below, which is the same weave, older and darker, and below that another. The whole basin is a fabric that has been growing and mending itself for longer than anyone has counted. The ground is plant, and the plant has been shaped. It is combed, ribbed and gridded. In places it is stamped with maker's marks, repeating glyphs the size of houses that no one has read in a long time. Moss-like felts have grown into the glyphs and softened them, so they read as rumors of writing.

The basin is low and very wide. There is no rim you could walk to. Its edges lie past the curvature of what you can see, and the land tilts imperceptibly toward its own middle, where the oldest works are. The light here is bright and cold, a white like the inside of a shell, and it comes from everywhere at once. The sun is a smear high up. The real light comes from the leaves. The upper canopy is glazed, each leaf a cast pane of living glass-cellulose the size of a barn door, angled on a hinge of stem. The panes catch the sun and pass it down through the plant floors in shafts, so the whole interior is lit like a greenhouse the size of a country. It is cool inside, cool enough that breath would show if there were any water in it. There is almost none. Air here is dry as chalk. Wet is a thing you might carry in one small glass vial, and people would come to look.

## What the plants are

The plants are not wild and never were. Every stem is a member. Trunks are extruded, square or hexagonal in section, ribbed and flanged like girders, and they grow in ranks with the discipline of a colonnade. They branch at exact angles, and the branches fuse with neighbors to form trusses. Vines run as cable-stays between towers and bear loads. Whole avenues of trees have grown together into vaults, so you walk through a nave of leaf-work under ribs a hundred meters up. Fruit hangs in hoppers and pods hang in racks, each fitted to a housing on the branch as though the branch had been machined to receive it.

They do not drink water. They circulate a pale, thin, faintly sweet spirit that they distill in their roots from the mineral weave and from each other's litter. It moves in closed loops through the trunks. Under the bark you can hear it as a soft pulse, and where a trunk has been tapped for the mills it runs clear and oily and never quite evaporates. The plants exhale it slowly. This is where the weather comes from. Above the great terraces a haze of vapor gathers, smelling of pear and solder and cold metal. It builds into bright fogs that roll across the works, lift, and fall as a fine crystalline frost of dried spirit that crackles under the leaf-mats and rings faintly on glass.

## The mills

The industry is not set down on the plants. It is grown from them, or they from it, and the difference has stopped mattering. Mills stand at the nodes of the trunk-network, vast wooden and resin constructions with drive-shafts of seasoned stem, flywheels of coiled vine, and pistons that are hollow trunks stroking in bores of polished bark. They run on the spirit, which is burned, or pressed, or simply allowed to expand. They stamp leaf-panes. They spin cable. They card fiber into the great bolts of felt that patch the floors. The stacks give off no smoke, only a thin shimmer of warm air, and the warmth is a relief on the skin.

They run mostly without people. Belts of woven grass carry fruit and pods and cut sections through the halls toward destinations that seem to shift between visits. Levers throw at intervals. Governors spin and settle. It is all worn to a patina: gear-teeth rounded, belt-paths grooved into the floor, brass-colored lichens gathered in every crevice where lubricant weeps. Erosion here is long habit rather than ruin. Nothing is broken. Everything is a little smoothed, and everything has found a shape it can keep. Where a mill's foundation sank a meter centuries ago, the roots leaned to meet it and the lean became part of the plan.

## The rare movers

There are few animals. You might cross a full day of the works and see none. Then something will pass along a gallery: long-legged and slow, with a body like a folded pair of shears, working down a row of trunks and clipping. You cannot tell if it is a creature or a tool that has learned to walk. It has a slow, contented economy of movement. There are also pale moth-like things that live on the frost and drift in the high panes, and beetles the size of a fist, bright as enamel, that clean the joints of the machines and ring faintly when they land. There are no predators to speak of. The nearest thing is the pruners, which cut and never eat.

## The quiet

It is quiet, but not silent. The sound is small and continuous. It is the tick of leaf-panes turning to follow the light, each one advancing a notch at a time, and the ticking rolls outward over the canopy in slow waves, so that a listener at the edge of a field hears a soft, ordered rain of clicks passing overhead and away. Under that there is the pulse of spirit in the trunks, a hum at the bottom of hearing, and now and then the sigh of a mill taking a stroke, the shush of a belt, a bright glassy chime when frost lets go from a pane. Voices carry oddly here and seem to arrive from the wrong distance. People speak low.

## Where it breaks

The works keep rules, and the rules are not quite the world's. This is the part hardest to describe, because it is only noticeable when you try to check.

Count the trunks in a colonnade and you will get one number walking out and another walking back, and both counts will feel certain. A belt carries a pod toward the packing hall, and at the hall you find the pod has already been packed and is being carried out the other side, a full cycle ahead of its own arrival. Shadows lie the right way in most aisles, but in a few they fall toward the light and thicken as they go, and the plants in those aisles grow thin and pale and bend into the dark as if it were the source. Distance is inconsistent. A gallery that took an hour to cross takes ten minutes home, or the far end of it never grows nearer, though the floor beneath your feet is plainly moving under you like a treadmill of moss.

Some labels come true. A stenciled number on a hopper becomes the number of things in it. A direction painted on a beam becomes the direction the corridor goes. Mill-hands who understand this write carefully and rarely. Some plants seem to grow toward what they have been named, and some seem to have been named after they grew. In the deepest, oldest halls, at the center of the basin, the weave of the floor shows patterns that continue across the seams of the world: a leaf-vein that runs from a frond on the ceiling down a wall, across the floor, and into your own shadow, where it goes on without you.

And still the whole thing goes on, patient and cold and bright, the mills turning, the panes ticking their slow wave across the continent of leaf, the frost falling and clearing and falling again, while somewhere a pruner clips one green stem so exactly that the cut heals over as though the stem had always ended there.
