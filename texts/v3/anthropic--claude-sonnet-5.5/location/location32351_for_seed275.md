# The Sunk Orchard of Vell

You arrive from above, by a spiral ramp cut into the wall of a pit six kilometers across. The pit is not a crater. Nothing fell here. The ground was opened from the inside, and the cut faces are still raw: pale grey shale, seams of rust-orange ore, and white gypsum in sheets that flake under a thumbnail. Nothing has had time to round them. Where the wall has shed, it has shed in slabs with clean edges, and the talus at the base is angular, unsorted, and still sharp enough to cut a boot.

It is cool down here, the temperature of a cellar in early spring. Your breath doesn't fog, because the air is nearly without water. It smells of cut cork, hot metal, and something like crushed pepper.

## The Light

Sunlight arrives by mirror. High on the rim, a ring of tilted heliostats, each the size of a barn door, follows the sun and throws its beam down into the pit. Around midday the whole floor is lit by overlapping discs of white light that slide slowly across it. Between the discs lies a clear blue shade. The light is ordinary daylight, neither dim nor lurid, but it arrives in patches, and everything on the floor has evolved to those moving pools. Leaves turn to follow them. In the mornings you see whole hillsides of foliage rotate a few degrees at once, with a sound like a page being turned very slowly.

## The Plants

The floor is entirely green, with no bare ground at all. But it is not a forest in the usual sense. There is no soil. The plants grow on each other, on rock, and on the ruins of their own earlier growth, a deep compressed mat of fibrous litter that has never rotted, because rot needs water.

They don't drink water. Their sap is a pale, slightly sweet oil, and they draw the last traces of moisture from the air through papery leaf-veils and shed it again as resin. The sap burns. The whole ecosystem runs on that fact.

The main species is called **lampstalk** by the people here. It is a single trunk thick as a cistern, rising forty meters, hollow, and ribbed like a pipe organ. The trunks stand far apart, sometimes a hundred meters between them, with open lawns of low grey-green cushion plants in the gaps. Each trunk is crowned with a flat disc of leaves that tracks the mirror-light. Dozens of vines ride each trunk, and the vines are the second species: **cable-liana**, ropy and weight-bearing, that will run a kilometer across open ground in a straight line, tight as a telegraph wire, because it grows along the vibration of whatever it last touched.

Under the canopy hang **bellows-gourds**, pale swollen fruits that inflate and deflate slowly as they take in air. Cut one open and the inside is warm, and the flesh is a spongy tinder that smolders for days.

## The City

The city is built into the plants and, at its edges, out of them.

It is sparse. Between settlements are long stretches of cushion-lawn and lampstalk with nothing human in view. But each cluster, when you reach it, is dense, ordered, and old in its design though not in its stone. Streets are laid in the pit floor by cutting channels through the litter mat down to bare rock, and the channels stay clear only because people walk and drag things along them daily.

Houses are grown as much as framed. Lampstalk trunks are hollowed into towers with doors cut at the base and stairways spiraling up the ribbing. Cable-liana is trained across the gaps into bridges, awnings, and the lattices that carry pipes. Walls are woven mats of dried leaf-veil, stiffened with resin until they ring when knocked.

The industry is real. There are boilers, riveted iron drums the color of old tea, stoked with gourd-tinder and pressed stalk-pulp. They drive pistons, and the pistons drive beam engines, pumps, looms, and the long belts of the sap-presses. Stacks rise from the settlements, but the smoke is thin and faintly gold, with a smell like burnt sugar. Rails run out across the lawns, carrying flat cars of harvested trunk-segments pulled by cable-winches. A cable-liana that has grown along a rail has usually been made part of the rail. Signals travel down its length as tugs, and railwaymen lay a palm on it to feel what's coming.

## The Sound

It is mostly quiet, and this is the strangest thing. Engines run, but the mat of litter and the great absorbent leaf-discs swallow their noise, so a working boiler-house a hundred meters off is only a low pulse. What you hear instead is small: the tick of resin cooling, the creak of trunks flexing as the mirror-light moves, the soft whump of a gourd exhaling, a piston somewhere sighing. Now and then a long thrum runs down a cable-liana, and every leaf along it trembles at once. Voices carry a surprisingly long way in the dry, still air, and people here speak low from habit.

## The Fauna

The animals are abundant, and most of them are small, drab, and busy.

**Tick-moths** live in the crowns by the millions, grey and pinhead-sized, feeding on leaf-veil. At dusk they lift in slow clouds that flicker through the last mirror-light.

**Litter-runners** are long-legged, rat-sized grazers with narrow hairless snouts. They dig warrens in the mat and fatten on cushion-plant. They are the city's constant companions: they nest in the walls, and children keep them for the warmth.

**Sap-thieves**, plump beetles the size of a fist, tap the trunks with a proboscis and become so oil-drunk they can only wobble. They are a nuisance to the presses and a delicacy in the kitchens.

The predators are **stilts**, tall pale birds with no wings to speak of. They stand still among the lampstalks for hours, indistinguishable from stalks until one folds itself down and strikes. Above them, in the crowns, are the **veil-cats**, low-slung climbers with skin membranes that let them glide from disc to disc. They are the reason nobody sleeps on an upper platform without netting.

Everything is dry-skinned, thrifty, and slow with water. Nothing sweats or drools. Nothing cries with tears.

## Processes

The place is never still, even when it is silent.

At dawn, when the heliostats first swing into place, the whole pit changes: leaf-discs tilt, veils open, gourds begin to swell, and the city's boilers are relit in sequence from the rim inward. At noon the shade-patches sharpen and the fauna retreat to them. By evening the mirrors fold, the floor sinks into blue dusk, and the cushion-lawns glow faintly, a cold green shimmer from a pigment the plants store all day. It is enough to walk by.

Every few weeks a **surge** runs through the pit. It starts somewhere deep in the mat, where one lampstalk has cracked a rock and freed a pocket of mineral salts. Its roots drink them, the sap chemistry flares, and within days a wave of rapid growth spreads outward from that trunk. Cable-liana leaps meters overnight. Streets get overgrown and have to be cut again. Rails buckle and are relaid.

The roots are also the excavators. They pry the raw walls open, one hairline crack at a time. Every year the pit is a few meters wider, and the city moves its stacks and its rails to follow the new floor. Nothing here is weathered, because nothing has been here long enough. It is a place still being made, by machines that are half alive and plants that are half machine, and the light comes down on it each day from a ring of mirrors, as if someone above were checking on the work.
