# The Accretion Plain

Start with the flatness. It runs to every horizon, a lowland barely above the level of an absent sea, and it is not ground in any ordinary sense. Nothing here was left behind by ice or river or slow collapse. Everything was laid down, layer by layer, by living things too small to see. The plain is a structure with no architect, and it is still being built.

## Terrain

The surface is a lattice of **low walls and shallow basins** in cream, ochre, and rust. The walls are travertine and microbial limestone, banded like the growth rings of a tree, and most rise no higher than a knee. Each wall curves and meets others at rounded junctions, so the whole plain reads as a vast honeycomb whose cells are irregular, some the size of a table, some the size of a lake. Where the water is deeper and older, the walls stand shoulder-high and are thick enough at the top to walk along. Walking is the only way to cross the plain, because the basins between are floored with soft mat.

Every wall is **actively growing**. Its crest is a wet, gelatinous skin, olive or rose or bruised orange depending on which colonies hold it. Beneath the skin the mineral hardens in fine sheets, thinner than paper and laid one over another. A wall rises perhaps a finger's width in a year. On the plain's edge, where growth is newest, the walls are barely lips, a pale scalloped line drawn across bare, cracked mud as if by a slow hand. A traveler can stand at the boundary with one foot in unbuilt clay and the other on the first course of a city that will not be finished for ten thousand years.

Erosion has barely begun. The edges are sharp where a wall has just calved, and the sides are ribbed with fresh, unweathered lamination. There are no talus slopes and no gullies, only occasional fractures where a wall has outgrown its footing and split, showing a clean cross-section of fine strata like pressed pages.

## Water

Water is present but never abundant, held in the basins like something carefully rationed. The pools are shallow, mostly a hand deep and seldom deeper than a body. They are warm as bathwater, bright with dissolved mineral, and green or rust-red where the mats thrive. The plain's entire hydrology is a system of **dams that the water itself demands**. Dissolved lime comes out of solution wherever flow is fastest, at spillways and thin edges, so a running stream builds its own barrier and spreads into a pool. The water is always slowly forcing itself into stillness, and the plain is the accumulated record of that.

The water arrives from two sources. Low **seeps of warm, mineral-heavy groundwater** rise from cracks along the plain's older, buried structure and feed the oldest basins. And the plain makes its own rain. The pools give up steam all day under a sun that presses at nearly full strength. By afternoon the vapor has stacked into cumulus towers over the plain's center, and toward dusk they release slanting curtains of rain. Some of the rain evaporates before it lands, leaving gray veils that hang from the cloud bases and dissolve. The rest strikes the walls and mats with a soft, dense patter and runs into the cells, and for an hour the whole plain shivers with rings.

## Heat and Light

It is hot in the flat, unforgiving way of low country: air thick, still, and radiant, with a sun pale-gold through the haze and a ground that returns its heat upward. Noon is white and shadowless, and the pools mirror the sky so well that the plain seems to hang between two skies. Mornings are the mildest time, brief and pearly, with mist lifting off every basin. The mist thins to wavering air by mid-morning.

Light is ordinary and clear, without strangeness. What gives the plain its color is what the light touches: the mats glow from within, the wet crests flash like oiled glass, and the drying parts of the walls whiten in the sun until they hurt to look at.

## Life

There are **no animals**, not one. No insect crosses the still air, no shape moves in the water, and no bird sits on a wall. Life here is small, slow, and communal.

The builders are the mats: layered communities of photosynthetic bacteria and their companions, stacked in colored bands within a finger's depth. Green sits on top under the light, purple and rose beneath it, and black sulfur-smelling layers at the bottom. They secrete the sticky matrix that traps grains of lime and turns them, layer on layer, into stone.

Vegetation is sparse. Scattered along the wall crests and in the drier corners grow **wick-reeds**, gray-green stems as thick as a thumb, which stand alone or in small clumps. Their roots are threaded into the stone itself and pull moisture from the pools upward. They exude a thin lime crust along their lower stalks, so each one stands in a slowly thickening sheath, a small tower of its own making. Old reeds die inside their sheaths, and their hollow towers stay upright, hardening into pale pipes. In older parts of the plain there are fields of these pipes, some taller than a person, standing hollow and empty like a stopped organ.

Beyond the reeds there is only the occasional low **cushion of succulent moss** in the shade of a tall wall, and the rust-red **halophilic film** that stains the evaporating margins of the saltier basins.

## Sound

The plain is quiet, but not silent, because quiet is not the same as still. What you hear is small, close, and continuous:

- The **tick and creak of cooling stone** at dusk, as the walls contract, like a house settling.
- The **pop of gas bubbles** rising through the mats and breaking at the surface, one here, three there, sometimes a soft chorus when the sun has warmed a whole basin.
- A **fine crackle** from drying crusts on the margins, like distant frying.
- **Wind in the empty pipes**, when there is wind. It makes a low, uneven, breathy note that shifts as the breeze shifts, so that whole fields of dead reeds hum in slow, changing chords.
- Far off, in the evening, the **rolling of thunder** from cells of storm that never come near.

## Rules of the World

1. **Water builds what holds it.** A flow left alone will wall itself in. So the plain never drains; it only subdivides into more and finer pools, each higher than the one before.
2. **The oldest ground is highest and driest.** Where a basin fills entirely with its own accumulated stone, the mat dies for lack of water, and the cell becomes a bare, pale terrace. Across the plain there are broad, blank plateaus of this kind, the finished parts, cracked and silent and beginning, only just, to weather.
3. **Nothing is unmade quickly.** Storm and heat crack the walls, but the mat closes the cracks in a season. Damage is a form of growth.
4. **The center is the oldest and the edges are the youngest.** Someone who walks outward from the center across a thousand kilometers of walls moves backward in age toward the raw clay margin, where the first film of life is just settling in, unhurried, on plain ground.

## A Walk at Dusk

Late in the day, the clouds release their rain over the eastern cells, and the light comes in low and copper under the storm. The walls go gold and their crests take a long red shine. Steam lifts from the warm pools where the rain hits and rises in slow threads, and the dead reed pipes begin to hum with the evening draft. Nothing moves except water, vapor, and light. The mats keep building, in the dark, in the heat, on the plain that is not yet finished, and will not be for as long as there is a sun.
