# The Stack at Ouraveil

The mountain is not visible as a mountain anymore. What you see is a rising country of made things, and the rock survives only as an idea the structures keep faith with. The ridge runs up past five thousand meters, and every meter of it has been cut, poured, laid, roofed, terraced, or bridged, some of it so long ago that the walls have grown their own weather.

## Approach

From the lower cloud deck you come up by cable-tram, a long swinging climb through gray that suddenly thins to blue. Above the cloud the light is startling. The air is thin and clear, and the sun comes down hard on glazed roof tiles the color of green bottle glass, on whitewashed retaining walls, on sheets of hammered copper gone half to verdigris. Everything either shines or steams. It should be freezing at this height, and it isn't, because the mountain is heated. Waste heat from the foundries and the deep boilers is piped through the walls in clay and iron conduits, and the whole Stack breathes it out slowly. The stone under your hand is warm like the flank of a large animal.

## Terraces

The main form is the terrace: thousands of them, stepped up the slopes in ranks, each a shelf of mortared stone or old poured concrete, each backed by a wall two or three times a person's height. The walls are not bare. They are furred with ferns, creeping fig, moss thick as felt, and orchids rooted in the joints. Water seeps down through them constantly, so the stone is dark and glistening at the bottom of each wall and pale and dry at the top.

There is no natural soil up here. Every handful of earth was carried, composted, or manufactured. Sludge from the tanneries, spent malt, fish-tank silt, and ground slag go into the great mixing sheds at the mid-levels, and the resulting loam is hauled up in baskets and carts to fill the beds. The gardens on the terraces are deep and black and heavily worked. Bananas, tree ferns, tea, chayote vines, papaya, and bamboo grow in planters the size of ponds. Bamboo has been planted along the upper edges as living scaffolding, and in places its roots hold up walls that were once held up by iron. The two now depend on each other and nobody remembers which was first.

## Density

Nothing stands alone. Workshops lean against dwellings, dwellings against cisterns, cisterns against the flanks of chimneys. Stair-streets are cut through the buildings themselves, tunnels of stone and lattice with laundry, cages, and pipes overhead. Roofs are floors for other people. A market stall may sit on the roof of a bakery, which sits on the roof of a dye works, and the smell changes as you climb: bread, then indigo and lye, then wet ferns.

Bridges cross the gaps at every height, stone arches below and iron truss walkways above, with rope and plank ones strung between the tops of towers. On the bridges the gardens continue, with trellises, gourds hanging from the rails, and pigeon lofts bolted to the undersides.

## Industry

The tech is steam and water, with newer electric filaments here and there. High on the ridge, where the cloud sheds its water, are the catch-works: acres of fine-mesh screens on iron frames that comb the fog into gutters. The water falls down the mountain through a long vertical chain of mills, wheel below wheel, each using the water once and passing it on. Water runs through the Stack in a great organized fall, from cloud-catch to turbine to tannery to tank to terrace to drain, and it is used at least a dozen times before it leaves the mountain as a rough brown stream far below.

Steam engines in stone sheds thump behind grilles. Foundry chimneys smoke, but the smoke is thin and pale because the fuel is charcoal from the managed bamboo groves and dry biogas from the composting sheds. Rolling mills, spinning frames, glass kilns, and cable works all run through the day on the same shared pulse of heat and pressure.

## Sound

It is loud everywhere and never quiet. The layers of sound come from all directions and from different heights.

- **Low:** the pounding of drop hammers and the slow wheeze of the great bellows-vents on the ridge, which draw cloud air down through the works.
- **Middle:** water in gutters, iron wheels, belts slapping, gongs signaling shift changes, and voices calling across the gaps in several dialects.
- **High:** birdsong, thousands of swifts screaming around the towers, and the whine of wind through wire stays.

At dusk the tree frogs start up in the cisterns, and their chorus rises until it covers the industry, so that for an hour the mountain sounds like a swamp.

## Fauna

The abundance is startling. The Stack is a habitat, and the animals treat it as one. Swifts and swallows nest in the ventilation shafts by the tens of thousands. Green parrots strip the fruit trees. Bats live in the disused flues and pour out at sunset in long dark ribbons. Goats graze the highest terraces, and long-tailed macaques work the middle levels and are tolerated in exchange for their pest control. Carp and small eels live in the cistern ponds, and ducks patrol the drainage channels. In the wall-niches are hives of small stingless bees, tended by families who have kept the same hives for generations. Geckos cover every warm surface. At night, moths as big as hands gather around the lamps, and the bats come after them.

## Rules of the place

1. **The Cloud Line.** Every day, mid-morning, the cloud deck rises and swallows the lower third of the Stack, then sinks again by evening. The upper districts stay bright while the lower ones sit in warm drizzle. People plan their routes around it, and the catch-works are timed to it.
2. **Water is not owned, but its order is.** You may take water from the chain, but you must return it downstream in a usable state. Certain guilds are charged with checking this and carry brass tasting-cups on their belts.
3. **Nothing is discarded, only lowered.** Broken tools, scraps, ash, and husks all go downhill in a slow chain of reuse. The lowest districts are built almost entirely from the leftovers of the highest.
4. **The mountain is never finished.** A wall is always being repointed somewhere, a pipe replaced, a bridge rebuilt one plank at a time. The work is so slow and continuous that nobody sees the city change, and yet none of it is the same as it was a century ago.

## The unusual thing

The one strange feature is the Slow Vents. At several points along the ridge the old builders left great stone mouths, wide enough to drive a cart into, that exhale and inhale on a cycle of about eleven minutes. They are driven by air pressure in the mountain's internal chambers, shifting as the boilers cycle. Nobody built them to be uncanny, but the sound they make, a huge low breath, is felt more than heard. Children are told it is the mountain sleeping. The workers say only that it is the boilers, and they touch the wall as they say it.

## Evening

As the sun drops, the light goes amber across the wet tile and the whole Stack glows like something lit from inside. Lamps come on, first at the top, then down through the terraces, each district a shade warmer than the one above it. The steam over the works catches the last light and turns rose. The frogs begin. Below, the cloud deck rolls slowly back up over the lower terraces, and the Stack goes on thumping and breathing above it, warm and green and unfinished, under the first stars.
