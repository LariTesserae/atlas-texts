# The Slack Below

You arrive by a crack. Above it is a country of sun and wind, but that is a rumor here, and the crack is the last place it holds. Below the lip the floor of the world falls away into a cavity so large that its far wall is a matter of belief. It holds a sky of stone and a horizon of warm dark. The air is body-warm and dense, and it presses on the skin like a hand that does not intend to leave.

## The Ground

Everything is sharp. The cavity is young, a blister of gas that opened in cooling basalt and was then left alone. Nothing has softened it, because nothing here does the softening. There is no rain, no frost, no wind strong enough to carry grit. The floor is a field of black volcanic glass in plates, ridges and shards, each edge as crisp as the day it cooled. Walking is a slow negotiation. The glass is not fragile, but it is bladed. It rings faintly underfoot, a short dry *tik*, then silence again.

Where the glass ends, salt begins. Pale beds of it lie in the low places, crusted into terraces and ribbed flats. They are the residue of brines that seeped up once, long ago, and dried. They have not grown or shrunk since. Their edges are clean and their surfaces are unmarked, except where the animals have been.

The ceiling is the other landscape. Far overhead the roof of the world bristles with stalactites of glass, drawn out thin as hair while the rock was still soft. They hang in millions. Some are a hundred meters long and finer than a wire. When the air moves they lean together in slow, coordinated arcs and never touch.

## The Air and Its Weather

The cavity is large enough to have weather, but the weather is a single settled habit. Heat rises from the deep floor, where the rock is warmer, and drifts to the roof. There it cools slightly and sinks along the distant walls. The whole volume turns in one enormous, patient loop, a circulation that takes days to complete and has never varied. The wind at any place is always the same wind. It blows from the same quarter at the same strength. Someone who knows the loop can navigate by it with eyes shut.

Nothing condenses, because there is nothing to condense. The air carries a faint mineral haze of salt dust and glass powder, so fine it registers only as a dryness in the throat and a sweetish, metallic taste. Where the loop slows and the air pools, the haze settles into pale drifts. These drifts are the only soft things in the place.

## The Light

There is almost none. The dark is the natural condition, not an absence. But it is not complete. A few things glow, and they glow at the same steady brightness always:

- A cold green-white fur lies across the walls in patches. This is the felt, a mat of simple filamentous life that feeds on the mineral seep and the warm gas. It gives off a glimmer just past what the eye can be sure of. It is the nearest thing to vegetation the place has.
- Some of the animals carry lamps: dim amber spots along the flanks, or a single cold blue dot at the tip of a limb.
- Very rarely, on the far side of the cavity, a vein of something in the rock glows dull red, a leftover heat that has not faded in ten thousand years and will not.

You learn to see by these and by the dark's texture. Your eyes stop trying for detail and start reading masses.

## The Living Density

The place is crowded. Life is the most surprising fact of it, because nothing grows and everything eats. The whole ecology stands on the felt and the salt: chemical appetite, thin and endless, spread over an enormous surface.

The creatures live in layers.

**On the floor** are the grazers, low, wide, and flat like carapaced plates. They graze the felt from the glass with rasping mouthparts. They move in slow tides, hundreds together, shoulder to shoulder and shell against shell, so that a herd looks like a shifting pavement. Their shells are dark and faceted, lightly scored by the glass. They hold an inch of stillness between each other, a habit of near-touching.

**On the walls** cling the pickers, spidery and pale, many-jointed. They work the felt in the places the grazers cannot reach. They are slow and precise, and they leave neat combed trails through the glow.

**In the air** drift the lanterns: soft buoyant bodies, gas-filled and translucent, hanging in the rising warmth. They filter the haze through fringed openings and carry their amber lamps in loose drifting constellations. They are so numerous near the ceiling that the roof seems to have its own faint weather of sparks. They ride the great loop, up the middle and down the walls, and complete the circuit over and over, each one a slow orbit.

**Among the glass hairs** of the ceiling live the threaders, thin things that walk the stalactites like ropes. They hunt the lanterns. A strike is silent and takes a long time.

Everything eats something else, and all of it is patient. Nothing here is hurried, because there is no season to hurry against.

## The Makers

Scattered across the floor are small, deliberate things. They are not many, and most are far apart. The makers themselves are low, four-limbed, and blind, with heat-pits along their brows and large sensitive palms. They are the tool users of the Slack, and their tools are primitive: flakes of glass struck from cores, bound with fibers pulled from the felt, hafted onto sticks of fused salt. They make blades, scrapers, and hooks. They make little else.

They build in a single style: cairns and cells. A cairn is a stack of glass plates, balanced dry, each set in the same direction as the wind. A cell is a small ring of the same plates roofed with a slab, big enough for three or four bodies. Some cells are new. Some are older than anyone can say, though age is hard to read in a place that does not change. The glass shows no wear and the salt shows no stain. A cell abandoned a thousand years ago looks like one finished yesterday.

The makers mark territories with rows of tiny standing shards, glass needles pushed upright into the salt. Walking among them, you realize each row is a sentence, or a name, or a count. The rows lean very slightly in the direction of the wind, which is also the direction of the past.

## Sound

It is mostly quiet, but the quiet is textured:

- The *tik* of glass underfoot, or of a grazer's shell settling on a plate.
- A rare low tone from the high vents, air passing across a stone lip, sustained and unchanging, the note the cavity was built around.
- The faint chime of the ceiling hairs when the loop leans them together, a sound so high and thin it seems to come from inside the head.
- The makers' speech: soft clicks of tongue against palate, or two glass flakes knocked together, patterned and sparse. Conversation is like distant rain in a place that has never had rain.

## Rules of the Place

Nothing changes. This is the deepest law of the Slack. The warm air circulates and returns; the felt glows and does not fade; the herds graze and the felt regrows at exactly the pace it is eaten. The whole system is balanced to a point. It formed young and sharp, found its equilibrium, and has held there. A visitor can stay a week or a year and see the same circuit of the same lanterns, the same drift of the same herds. The cairns will not fall. The glass will not dull. Even death here is folded into the pattern, since bodies go to the felt and the felt goes to the grazers.

If you stand still for long enough in the dark, with the warm wind at your neck and the constellations of amber sparks turning far overhead, you may notice what the makers know. The place is not waiting for anything. It is complete, and you are the only thing in it that is new.
