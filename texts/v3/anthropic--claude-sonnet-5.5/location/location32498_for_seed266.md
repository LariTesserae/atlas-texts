# The Upper Kiln

Begin with the fact of height. The Kiln is a plateau lifted nearly eleven kilometers above the dead basin that surrounds it, and it is still not thin-aired. This world's atmosphere is heavy and slow, a dense sea of mostly nitrogen and carbon dioxide with a sour undertaste of sulfur. Even at this altitude the air presses on the skin like warm cloth. The temperature stays around that of a comfortable summer afternoon, day and night, because the air holds heat the way a stone wall does. You could stand on the rim and feel the whole continent's warmth rising past you.

The plateau is so large that it makes its own weather. From the eastern scarp to the western, it takes a good walker most of a season to cross. Over that distance the air develops habits. Dust-fronts form on the sunward side in the afternoons, thicken into amber walls a hundred kilometers long, and drift across the tableland for days. When one has passed, the sky holds a residue of pale brown light for a long while, so that everything looks like an old photograph of itself. Dry lightning walks inside the fronts, silent from far off and then, when close, a single cracking sound like a plank breaking in a distant room.

## The ground

The Kiln has been eroding for longer than anything on it has had a name. Nothing is sharp. The great ridges are rounded and fluted like the inside of a shell, and the flutes run in the direction of the prevailing wind, which has not changed in a million years. Between the ridges the plateau is cut into long avenues of stone pillars, some as thick as cathedrals, some as thin as a mast, all leaning very slightly downwind. These are called *the Combed Country*, because from above they resemble hair drawn by a wide hand.

The rock is layered in colors that only look bright when the light is low: rust, bone, a greenish grey like old bronze, a bruised violet in the deepest strata. Where the wind has been at it longest the surface is polished to a soft sheen, and the polish is warm to the touch. In the hollows lie drifts of fine red dust, and in the drifts are the ripple patterns of storms that ended before anyone's grandparents were born. The dust is the oldest thing that moves.

There is almost no water. What there is arrives as breath. On cold-ish nights, when the plateau loses some of its heat to the black above, a fog too thin to see gathers on the salt flats, and the salts drink it. By morning the flats are faintly damp and faintly bruised in color, and then the damp is gone again. These are the *dew-pans*, white basins ringed with crystalline lace. They are the closest thing the Kiln has to springs, and every living thing on the plateau knows where they are.

Nothing green grows here. The nearest thing is a black varnish that stains south-facing rock, a film one cell thick that lives on trace minerals and the small dew, and that the eye takes for a shadow until you touch it and feel a slight grain, like a cat's tongue. It is the base of everything.

## The animals

Life is abundant, and that is the first strangeness, because there is so little to eat. The animals have solved the problem by eating the world's stone.

The most common are the **grinders**, six-legged grazers the size of oxen, with broad, low bodies and a mouth like a rasp. They move in herds across the pillared avenues, scraping the varnish and the salt-lace from the rock with slow lateral sweeps of their jaws, and in doing so they file the ridges down a hair's breadth each year. They are a part of the erosion, and older than the wind's current direction, some say. Their dung is a hard, glittering pellet of ground silica and salts, and these pellets are gathered by everything else.

Above the herds, riding the rising columns of warm air, are the **sailwings**, wide, thin, membranous creatures with wingspans of several meters, patient and nearly silent. They glide in loose spirals over the dung fields and drop to pick pellets apart for the metal salts inside. When they die, they fall and dry and crumple into a paper-fine substance the color of tea, and the wind moves them out onto the flats, where they become part of the dust.

Between and beneath there are small things, in enormous numbers: beetle-like **tinkers** that click and rasp in the crevices, pale burrowers that live in the salt-crust, mites that crowd the varnish so densely that a handspan of rock may be alive with them. At dusk the flats are stippled with their tiny motion, and if you kneel and listen you can hear it, a faint dry ticking like rain that never lands.

Near the dew-pans the animals gather without fighting. This is one of the rules of the place: at the pans, at the hour the moisture comes, nothing hunts. Predators exist. There are long-jawed **wardens**, lean and grey, that stalk the herd margins. But at the pans they stand in the line with the rest and wait. Nobody knows who enforced this originally. It is simply how the animals behave, and when a young warden breaks it, the herd does not flee. The others simply turn and look, all together, and the young one stops.

## The works

The Kiln is not empty of makers. Scattered across the tableland, at intervals of many days' travel, are the towns, and between them the long infrastructure that ties them into one slow machine.

The towns are built into and around the pillars. A pillar is hollowed, floored, and stacked with rooms, a hundred meters of habitation inside a single leaning column, its windows small and deep against the sun. Around the base cluster the workshops, the kilns, the low sheds, and the wide, flat threshing-floors where the pellets are dried. From a distance a town is a grove of stone with smoke rising from the roots. There may be a dozen such pillars, or forty. Between them run lanes worn smooth, and along the lanes run narrow-gauge rails, and along the rails, slowly, run cars.

The industry here is patient. The engines do not burn much. They are **gradient engines**, great slow machines that run on the difference between hot and less-hot. On the sunward faces of the ridges stand fields of black plates, each the size of a barn door, and under them sealed loops of a pale, heavy working fluid (a mineral oil, or something like it, never water) that boils at a gentle heat. The vapor drives pistons the size of houses. It condenses in deep shaded wells sunk into the rock on the leeward side, and the liquid sinks and returns. The whole cycle takes about a minute per stroke. You can stand beside one of the beam engines and watch its arm rise like the slow breathing of a sleeper, hold, and fall. The sound is a low soft thud, felt in the sternum more than heard, and a long sigh of valves.

The engines turn drawing-mills that pull wire from the metal salts, presses that shape fired pellets into plates and beams, looms that weave glass fiber into cloth stiff as sailcloth. The factories are large but quiet. A workshop that would scream on another world here merely hums. The dense air swallows sound, and the low and constant noise of a settlement is like a held breath.

Cable-ways run between the pillars, and between the pillars and the distant ridges: thin wire lines carrying buckets of ore and finished goods on long slow catenaries, one bucket every few minutes, so that the sky over a town is scored with dotted lines. On the highest ridges, wind-vanes taller than any building turn very slowly, and each is fitted with a great iron bell that the wind rings once every few hours, one note, deep. The people say they set the bells to count the storms.

## The people, and the strangeness

The builders are not entirely what you expect. They are tall, stooped, long-armed folk with dark, close-fitting clothes of glass-cloth, and they move with the economy of those who live where there is nothing to waste. They speak little. But the strange thing is this: they live alongside the animals in a way that is hard to call either domestication or accident.

The grinders walk through the towns. They are not herded, and they are not fenced. Their paths run through the lanes, under the archways, across the threshing-floors, and the builders arrange their work around them, leaving gaps in walls, building stairs with a step the width of a grinder's stride. In return the grinders scrape the sooty film from the engines' plates, which would otherwise stop them, and lick the salt-scale from the pipes. No one commands this. If the grinders left, the engines would choke within a season, and everyone knows it, and so the doorways stay wide.

Even stranger: the sailwings nest in the engine-houses. The heat from the boilers rises through the vent-stacks, and the great thin birds circle above the town in a permanent slow wheel, dozens at a time, like the hand of a clock that has no face. When an engine stops, they know before the engineers do. They drop from the circle and settle on the roof, and their weight, and their silence, is the alarm.

The oldest strangeness is at the center of the Kiln, where no one lives. There stands a single vast disc of dark stone, flat, forty kilometers across, polished to the smoothness of a lens, and it is warm all night and all day, always the same warmth. It is not natural, and it is not a work of the current builders, and none of the towns claims it. The grinders will not cross it. The sailwings will not shadow it. But at the turning of the seasons, when the amber fronts thin and the light goes clear for a few days, every animal on the plateau, herds, fliers, burrowers, wardens, the tinkers in their millions, drifts toward its edge and stands there, facing in. The builders come too, and stand among them. Nobody speaks. The disc gives no sound. After some hours they all turn and go back to their work.

## How it feels to be there

Mostly quiet. That is the abiding quality. The thick air, the muffling dust, the slow engines: everything moves at the pace of a long breath. You hear your own footsteps, the tick of the mites, a distant bell. Sometimes the wind rises in the pillars and every column sings a different low note, a vast unhurried chord that lasts a minute and dies. Then the quiet returns and seems deeper for it.

Nothing changes quickly. The dust-fronts take days. The ridges lose a fingertip of stone in a lifetime. The engines rise and fall. The herds pass, and pass again. The Upper Kiln is a place that has settled into itself, warm and high and dry, with its animals and its machines and its patient people all leaning, like the pillars, in the same direction, the way the wind has always blown.
