# The Boiler Cleft

You come to it by squeezing sideways through a crack in a hillside of pale, freshly broken limestone. The edges are still sharp enough to snag a sleeve. The hill split within living memory, when a slab of the ridge slid a few meters and opened a slot. Nobody has walked the slot smooth. Behind the slot is a room.

It is roughly the size of a chapel, tall and narrow, its walls leaning toward each other overhead until they almost close on a seam of white sky. The floor is a jumble of fallen blocks still clean along their fractures, with no soil, no rounded corners, and no polish from water. The place is young and raw, and it is completely full.

## The Packing

Every surface is occupied. The rock walls are upholstered in bodies: pale, palm-sized moths folded wing over wing in overlapping shingles, so the wall looks feathered and shifts slowly, like a breathing thing, when the warm air moves. Beneath them are beetles with flat copper-colored backs, packed so tight they form a continuous scaled skin. Between the beetles, in every crack, are threadlike centipedes, small translucent frogs the color of wet paper, and snails whose shells have been rubbed to a dull shine by their neighbors. You do not walk here so much as choose which living surface to lean on. The frogs shift aside one at a time, with irritated small hops, to make room for a boot. The beetles do not shift. You learn to step on stone, where stone shows.

Nothing here is loose or wandering. The animals are wedged, layered, stacked in shelves. The room is a single crowd holding still.

## The Heat and the Wet

The air is warm, like a laundry room, and heavy with a flat mineral smell: hot iron, wet chalk, and something faintly sweet, like boiled sugar. The warmth comes from below. Between two of the largest fallen blocks is a vent no wider than a barrel, and from it seeps a slow exhale of steam. This is the cleft's one source of water. It is not a spring but a breath. The steam rises, meets the cooler upper walls, and condenses into a fog that gathers under the ceiling seam. The fog thickens, sags, and falls as fat, slow drops.

The room keeps its own weather on a rhythm of about an hour. First the vent breathes, and the lower room clouds. Then the fog climbs and beads on the ceiling. Then comes the drip-rain, a soft, sparse patter of warm drops that sets every moth-wing trembling. Then the air clears and the walls steam faintly as they dry, and the beetles crack their wing-cases a hair open to vent their own heat. Then the vent breathes again. Every animal in the room is timed to this. Before the fog rises, the frogs all turn to face the same direction. When the first drops fall, the whole feathered wall of moths lifts its edges at once, a slow ripple traveling upward, as if the room were drinking.

## The Engine

In the middle of the floor, wedged where the slide must have dropped it, is a pump-house, one of those small brick-and-iron sheds that mining companies once scattered across the hills. It came down with the slab, tilted, one wall stove in, its roof of corrugated sheet folded like a hat. Inside, still bolted to its cast bed, is a steam engine: a single vertical cylinder, a flywheel the height of a man, brass fittings gone the green of old pennies.

It is running.

The vent's steam has found its way into the old feed pipe, or the engine has been re-plumbed by the collapse so that the earth's exhale drives it. Whichever it is, the flywheel turns slowly and unevenly, pausing at the top of each stroke as if considering, then pushing on. It makes the only regular sound in the room: a soft, damp *tock… tock… tock,* muffled by the crowd of bodies pressed against every part of it. Its slide valves sigh. A leather belt, cracked and slick, still runs from the flywheel to a shaft that turns nothing, or perhaps something under the rubble.

The engine is warm, the warmest solid thing in the cleft, and it is the densest part of the crowd. The cylinder housing is armored in sleeping beetles. The flywheel's spokes are hung with moths that lift off as the wheel turns and resettle a spoke later, so a slow flutter of pale wings circles with each revolution.

## The Strangeness

The fauna have taken to the metal. The copper-backed beetles collect it: flakes of verdigris, brass filings, rivet heads, washers scavenged from the wreck. They carry these in their mandibles to particular places and press them into the mud-and-secretion cement that lines the cracks. Their nests are small mosaics of green and gold, some clearly patterned, in spirals, in rings, or in a repeated shape that looks almost like a gear tooth. The pattern is oldest near the engine and grows cruder and sparser toward the walls, as if the idea were still spreading.

The frogs, when the drip-rain falls, sit beneath the leaking joints of the steam pipe and let the condensate fall on them. They are not drinking. Their skins slowly take on a faint metallic bloom, a bronze dust that shows in the light, and the oldest frogs in the deepest crannies have gone entirely coppery and stiff, sitting so still you might take them for fittings. Some may be. Some of the "fittings" have blinked.

At the top of each engine stroke, in the half-second pause, every moth in the cleft goes rigid. The pause is the room's held breath. Then the piston pushes, the wheel turns, and the wings soften again. If the engine ever stalls for a whole minute, and it sometimes does when the vent skips a breath, the cleft undergoes something unsettling. The crowd begins to move. Beetles unlock from their positions and pour down the walls in slow, glittering streams toward the pump-house. The moths lift in a rising pale column and hang below the ceiling seam. The frogs begin a thin, high piping, the loudest sound the room ever makes. It stops the moment the wheel turns over again. Everything resettles into place, slightly rearranged, as if the room had been shuffled.

## Plants

Vegetation is sparse and clings to the drip-lines: pale ferns the length of a finger, growing in tiny rings where the ceiling rain lands, and a felt of grey-green lichen on the north-facing blocks. Under the ferns, snails graze. The plants are too few to feed the crowd. Something else does. The moths feed on the beads of condensation itself, which carries dissolved mineral and a thin sugar from the vent, and the beetles feed on the moths' droppings and on the film of bacteria that coats the warm pipes. Predators exist in the packing: pale spiders in tunnels of silk woven through the moth-shingle, and a single large centipede, banded and slow, that patrols the frog shelves and takes one, unhurried, every few hours. The crowd closes over the gap almost at once.

## Sound and Light

Light falls from the seam far above, a thin strip of ordinary daylight that slides across the far wall through the day and turns the fog to a soft grey-gold. It never reaches the floor directly. Below, everything is lit by that diffuse glow and by a deep red-orange gleam at the vent mouth where some mineral crust catches heat.

The place is mostly quiet. The engine's *tock*. The drip-rain's whisper. Ten thousand wings shifting, which sounds like someone turning pages in the next room. The hiss of steam. Now and then a single snail-shell clicks against another. When the frogs pipe, it is startling.

## Leaving

When you squeeze back out through the crack, you carry some of it with you: a faint copper dust on your sleeves, one moth on your collar that you must coax off, and the sudden thinness of the outdoors, the hillside's dry wind and open air with nobody pressed against you anywhere. Behind you, in the sealed warm dark, the flywheel goes on turning, the steam goes on rising, and the whole full room goes on keeping time.
