# The Chorded Plain

The land here is only a few thousand years old. It came up out of an old sea bed in a series of slow ruptures, and it still has the look of something just unwrapped. The basalt is black and unrounded, split into hexagonal columns whose tops make a floor like a cracked mosaic. The edges are sharp enough to cut a boot. Nothing has had time to soften them. The plain runs flat and low in every direction, barely above the tideline of a sea you can't see, and it is so open that you can watch weather walk toward you for a full day.

Most of it is empty. You can cross ten kilometers of bare columnar rock with nothing on it but a film of moisture and the occasional pale lichen coin. The emptiness is only the ground between things. Everything alive and everything built is packed into three kinds of places: the fissures, the wells, and the Instruments.

## The Fissures

Where the crust has split, the cracks run for kilometers, some as narrow as a hand and some wide enough to swallow a house. Warm damp air rises from them, and the vegetation inside is extravagant. Tree ferns grow in shafts of dark. Fleshy vines hang in curtains forty meters long. There are mosses like green velvet, and pale bell-shaped flowers that open and close in rhythm with the noise. From the rim, a fissure looks like a seam of green sewn into black cloth. Inside, it is thick, wet, and crowded. Every ledge holds something growing, and every growing thing holds something feeding.

The fauna are abundant, but they are all down there. Beetles the size of loaves crawl the vine-walls. Translucent gliders drift between the fern-crowns on membranes lit faintly blue from within. Fish-shaped things with legs live in the seep-pools. Flocks of small, fast, many-jointed birds pour along the cracks like smoke. On the surface between fissures, you see only the odd scavenger crossing the rock in a hurry, bent low, as if the open were a cold room.

## The Instruments

Every few kilometers stands a structure that the old word "tower" doesn't cover. They are grown as much as built, tall spirals of a matte gray-violet material that seems to be ceramic and isn't. Some are needle-thin and some are wide as cathedrals. Their walls are patterned with slow-moving lattices of fine pores that open and close like breathing. Nothing about them looks manufactured by hands. No door or window is visible, though there are hollows and ramps that suggest use. Whatever built them worked below the scale where the eye can follow, arranging matter grain by grain until it held a shape and a purpose.

Their purpose is sound.

The Instruments sing. It is not music exactly, though there are pitches in it. It is a colossal, layered, low-frequency roar, a pressure more than a noise, so loud that on the plain you feel it in your sternum and teeth before you hear it. Standing within a kilometer of one is like standing inside a struck bell. Beyond that distance it merges with its neighbors into a continuous shifting wall of sound. The surface never falls quiet. Even the wind, when it rises, is only a texture inside the din.

The sound is how the plain gets its water. The air is warm and carries a great deal of vapor, but the vapor will not fall by itself, and rain comes only in brief, generous events. The Instruments force it. Their tones drive the humid air into standing waves. Fog nucleates along the nodes of these waves in great slow sheets, and it condenses out on every surface and drips. Thick beads run down the walls of the towers into channels cut in the rock, and the channels lead into the wells: deep, round, clear-water basins ringed with fern and busy with life. Water here is a product of noise. If the Instruments ever stopped, the plain would dry within a season, and the fissure-forests with it.

## Dim Light

The sun is a pale disc behind a high permanent haze, and it gives the plain a light like the hour after sunset. Shadows are soft and directionless. The vapor the Instruments raise hangs in thin banners across the flats, and the day is a long gray-gold dusk. Living things have taken advantage of it. The fissures glow: fungal threads in green, the blue of the gliders, and the amber flash of beetles signaling with their wing-cases. At night the plain is black, and the seams glow like embers under ash.

## Life in the Noise

The fauna don't fight the sound. They are built around it. Many have no hearing organs at all, and instead sense the world through their feet and skin, reading the standing waves like a map. The gliders navigate the nodes of the sound field and ride the updrafts the Instruments produce. Some ferns grow in strict rows along the lines of greatest vibration, so a fissure floor can look planted. Predators hunt by the tiny changes an animal makes in the pattern of vibration around it, and prey have learned to move in the deepest part of the roar, where such disturbance is hardest to detect. Life here is a constant quiet negotiation inside overwhelming loudness.

## Motion and Weather

The plain is active in its own slow ways. Fog banks are born, drift, and fall as drizzle. Now and then the deep crust shifts and a column tilts or a new hairline crack opens, releasing a hot breath of steam and a fresh green wound in the black. Within a year a crack like that will have moss in it. Within ten it will have a colony.

Once in a while, for reasons no observer has worked out, the Instruments change key together. The roar climbs or drops through the whole plain, and the fog shears, and every creature in every fissure stops and turns toward the nearest tower. For a few minutes the whole landscape holds its breath in the middle of an unbearable noise. Then the new pitch settles, and the rain begins, heavy and warm and total, running off the black stone in silver sheets and pouring into the wells.
