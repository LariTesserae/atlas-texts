# The Sump at Harrow Weir

You come down into it by a cutting so old the rails have rusted to two orange stains in the clay. The basin is small, perhaps three hundred paces across, and shaped like a thumbprint pressed into low ground. Everything in it is wet. The air is a cold, fine suspension that never becomes rain and never lifts. It beads on your sleeves and slides in threads down the iron.

There is no green anywhere. The colors are rust, slate, bone, tea-brown, and a bruised violet where films lie on standing water. The light comes through a lid of fog, and by midday it is the gray of the inside of a mussel shell. Nothing casts a sharp shadow. Things simply darken where they lean together, and here everything leans together.

## The ground

The floor is bog iron: soft, orange, and layered like pastry, dug into and slumped back a thousand times. Where you press it, water wells up around your boot, clear at first and then clouding to the color of strong tea. Underneath, the crust thickens into a plated hardpan that rings faintly if you stamp on it. Old channels cross it in straight lines, cut by people and softened by centuries of slump into shallow, meandering gutters. Rain and seep have rounded every edge. The brick sluice-walls have gone to sugar at the corners. The cast-iron bollards look melted, as if they were candles.

Water stands in every low place, in black pools, in ribbed flats, in the tilted hollows of fallen roof-sheets. It sits a hand's depth below the surface almost everywhere, and the whole basin trembles slightly when something heavy walks.

## The works

Scattered through the basin, not arranged, are the remains of the pumping works. Nobody built a village here. They built one purpose, and the purpose left buildings behind it like shed skin.

The **engine house** stands at the center, roofless on one side, its arched windows furred with mineral. Inside, the beam engine is still there: a great iron rocker as long as a barge, pinned on a wall of black stone. The strange fact of the Sump is that the beam still moves. Nothing burns and nothing turns a crank. But the water under the basin rises and falls on a slow cycle, some hours long, and the old plunger-rods sit in their wells like the fingers of a sleeper. When the water heaves up, the rods lift. The beam tips with a long, low groan and hangs. Hours later, when the water sinks, it tips back. The engine is being run by the ground's own slow breathing. People who study the place argue over whether this is coincidence, geology, or a use the animals have found for it.

Around the engine house, in no order:

- Two **boiler shells** lie on their sides like buried whales, ribbed with rivets, their fireboxes full of still black water. Inside, they are the densest habitat in the basin.
- A **chimney stack**, cracked, leans a few degrees off true. A tide-line of pale crust circles it at head height, marking some flood no one remembers.
- **Pipes** run everywhere: overhead on trestles, half-sunk in mud, up through the floors of sheds. Each drips from its joints, and each drip has built its own stalactite of ochre, so that a long pipe hangs with a fringe of small orange teeth and there is a small cone below each one.
- A **gantry crane** stands with its hook lowered into a pool, its cable gone to a brown braid.
- A few **sheds** of corrugated iron, folded like paper, sag on their footings. Some have doors. All the doors are open, and it is dark inside.

## Life

There are no plants. What passes for growth is either mineral or animal, and at first glance you may not tell which.

The pipes and rails wear **hydroid gardens**: colonies of pale, branching polyps that look like ferns and are not. They feather in the seep, close when touched, and open again in a slow, spreading ripple. Under them are **mats** of rust-bacteria in layered skins, iridescent where they tear, that make the bog iron and are the base of everything else here. In the wettest seeps the mats stand up as slick, gelatinous pillars a knee high.

The animals are countless. Where you would expect leaves, there are **lampsnails**, thumbnail-sized, thick on every vertical face of the boiler shells. Each carries a dim green-gold glow in the flesh at the lip of its shell. They graze the mats, and in the late afternoon dark inside the boilers they light the walls like a low, shifting constellation, brightening when a shadow passes and dimming when it stays. There are **iron-eels**, the length of a forearm, slate-colored and blind, that thread through the flooded flues and pipes and can be seen surfacing in the pools with a small kiss of air. There are **mud-skippers** that are not fish, or not quite, that stand on jointed fins on the wet flats and turn together, all facing the same way, like a crowd hearing something. And there are the **weir-crabs**, broad and pale, with one claw grown to a plate. They wedge that claw into cracks and sit there for days, plugging leaks, as if they were the ones keeping the works in repair. Perhaps they are. The pipes in the basin leak in surprisingly few places.

Overhead, in the roof-trusses of the engine house, roost **grey bats**, a few hundred, perfectly still, coated with beads of condensation so that they look strung with glass.

Everything eats something else. The eels take the skippers, the crabs take the snails, the bats take the small flying things that rise from the pools at dusk in fragile columns. Bones and shells settle into the crust and are re-dissolved by iron. The basin digests its own dead and builds them into the floor.

## Sound

It is mostly quiet, but not silent. There is the drip, patient and irregular, from hundreds of orange teeth, so that after a while the ear stops hearing individual drops and starts hearing a soft grain. There is the crackle of snails grazing, like distant frying. When the beam rises, there is the groan of iron on stone, which carries all the way to the rim and startles the skippers into a brief stampede of slaps on wet mud. Then the quiet closes over it again, and you can hear the fog settling on your hood.

## The cycle

Every few hours the water in the basin heaves. You feel it first in your boots, a slight lift. Then the pools brim and the low channels fill and run backward. The beam tips. For a while the whole floor is a shallow sheet, and the skippers scatter to the sheds and the eels spill out across the flats, hunting in inches of water. Then it drains again, slowly, with a long sucking murmur through every pipe at once, and the works settle back into their fine dripping.

Once a day, near dusk, the fog thickens and the lampsnails all brighten together, as if in answer to something. Then they dim. Nothing follows. The basin waits, and everything in it waits with it, folded into orange and gray, layered and crowded and wet, keeping a machine going for no one.
