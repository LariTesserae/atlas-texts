# The Weir Quarter

Below the last of the gray light the seabed folds into a bowl, and the bowl is full of town.

It is small. A person swimming could cross it in a few hundred strokes, if anyone were there to swim. The basin has a clear edge, a rim of slumped black clay that has been rounded by centuries of slow water into something like the lip of a dish. Inside the rim, everything is packed close. Roofs touch roofs. Gantries lean into the shoulders of other gantries. Pipes as thick as cisterns run through the gaps between buildings and sometimes through the buildings themselves, so that a wall will have a great iron throat passing through it, sealed at the entry with a collar of pale crust.

The cold is total and stable, just above freezing. Nothing here has known warmth in a long time, and the metal has settled into that.

## Materials

Everything is coated. Over the years the water has laid down a skin of lime and iron on every surface, and the skin has become the true surface of the place. Brick corners are softened to the curve of a bar of soap. Rivets have become blisters. Stairs have lost their edges and become ramps. Walls are the color of old teeth, with rust-orange streaks where a seam weeps iron, and black manganese freckles where the water has stood still longest.

Under the crust, the buildings are recognizably industrial. There are sawtooth roofs of ribbed glass, now clouded to milk. There are brick stacks with iron bands. There are long sheds with loading doors the size of barn doors, and a foundry with its cupola tilted and a tide-line of green bronze around its base. Cranes stand at the quay-edges of streets that were never quays. The streets were laid out for carts and carry only sediment now, in soft drifts that shift and re-form.

Nothing is clean, and nothing is broken in a violent way. Erosion here is a finished process. Each object has reached the shape the water will let it keep, and it holds that shape.

## Dark

There is almost no light. The surface is too far above, and what falls from it is only a faint gray suggestion, more a change in the quality of the dark than a glow.

What light exists is local and small.

- Mats of bacteria on the older ironwork glow a dim green-blue, faint enough that you see them best by looking slightly to one side. They trace the outlines of pipes and railings in pale ghost-lines, so the town appears as a sketch of itself, drawn in cold phosphor on black.
- A few lamps still burn. They are sealed glass bulbs on brackets at street corners, lit by a slow galvanic current between two dissimilar metals in the mounting. They give a weak amber light, about a candle's worth, and they flicker on long irregular cycles. A lamp may be dark for an hour and then warm slowly up to a soft orange. There are perhaps thirty in the whole quarter, and at any moment about a third are lit, so the light in the streets wanders.
- Sometimes something moves through the water trailing a spark-line of luminous plankton, and for a moment a whole facade is drawn in fine pale threads and then forgotten.

## The tide-engine

The strangest fact of the Weir Quarter is that it works.

Twice a day, or close to it, the basin breathes. A long slow current pours over the rim, fills the bowl, pauses, and drains out through a cut in the eastern side that the town's builders, or the sea, made deep and straight. Every flue, sluice, and turbine hall in the quarter was built to catch that flow. Great vaned wheels sit in the throats of the main streets. Smaller ones sit in the pipes, and smaller ones still in the housings of governors and valves. When the current comes in, the town wakes.

It does not wake loudly. The wheels turn with a slowness that feels like the turning of a planet. Belts of chain and old cable, black with growth, draw taut and begin to travel. In the long sheds, lines of iron arms lift and lower over benches, performing operations on nothing, or on things no one now can identify. A press descends into a die and rises again. A rack of shuttles crosses and recrosses in a hall of frames. A stamping-mill in the north corner falls at intervals as regular as a heartbeat, and the fall does not make a sound so much as a pressure, a soft thud you feel against your sternum a half-second after you see the head come down.

When the current slackens, the machines slow and stop, mid-motion, and hang. Arms remain half-raised. Chains slacken in curves. For an hour or two the whole quarter is a still photograph. Then the drain begins, the flow reverses, and the reverse stroke starts. The machines were built to work both ways, and they do, running backward through their operations. Shuttles unweave. The press lifts before it falls. Whatever the town makes, it makes on the inflow and unmakes on the outflow, and no one has been here to see whether anything remains at the end.

## Sound

Sound is very low. Water carries it well, but there is little to carry. What there is:

- the pressure-thud of the stamping-mill,
- a fine ticking from the governors, like a room full of clocks heard through a wall,
- an occasional groan from a hull-plate flexing as the current rises,
- and, at the turn of the tide, a long low tone from one particular organ-pipe-like standpipe near the center, which the water sets humming for about a minute before it fades. It is the loudest sound in the quarter, and it is not loud.

Otherwise there is the near-silence of deep cold water, which is not empty but thick with the small noises of things settling.

## Snow that rises

Marine snow falls everywhere in the ocean, a slow rain of dead plankton and mineral flakes. In the Weir Quarter it falls too, but in the streets it does something else. The tide-driven flues draw water upward through the chimneys and stacks, so all through the town there are tall brick columns with pale flakes drifting up their throats in a slow ascending column, like ash in a chimney seen in reverse. Above the stacks the flakes disperse into the dark. In the streets, in the eddies, they turn and hang and sometimes spiral. So the snow is falling and rising at once, and it is easy to lose your sense of which way is up.

## Life

Vegetation is sparse and mostly not plant at all. There are pale, ribbon-like mats of filamentous bacteria draped from railings. There are clusters of tubeworms at the few places where warm brine seeps up through cracked cellars, red-tipped white stalks in tight bunches, swaying in unison. In one old courtyard, a meter-wide garden of sponge-like growths has colonized a fallen boiler, translucent gray, faintly rimmed with light.

Animals are rare, and the ones present are old, slow, and matched to the town.

- **Pipe-eels**, long and colorless, live inside the larger conduits. They move against the current and are almost never seen outside iron. Sometimes a length of a pipe will bulge for a moment as one passes.
- **Crust-grazers** are pale, flat, many-legged animals about the size of a dinner plate. They crawl over the buildings and scrape the lime from the ironwork. They are the town's only maintenance. Where they have grazed, the metal shows dark and bare, and where they haven't, it stays sealed in its shell. You can read the town's history in the patchwork of their appetites.
- **The Warden** is a single large animal, or possibly several. It is a slow gray thing, something like a ray but thicker, that glides along the main streets on the inflow and settles in the town square on the slack. Its back is encrusted with the same lime as the buildings, and when it rests, it is very hard to tell from a low roof. Nothing hunts it. Small fish gather in the shelter of its wings.
- **Blind cardinal-fish**, a handful, sleep in the mouth of a cold boiler and leave only when the current is strongest.

Predation exists but is quiet: an eel takes a fish in the dark, a grazer is lifted by something unseen from the edge of a wall. The town is not empty, only sparsely inhabited, and every inhabitant has settled into a niche the industry left behind.

## The rules of the place

A visitor learns a few things quickly.

1. **Everything follows the tide.** Movement, light, sound, and life all shift with the breath of the basin. In the slack water the town is nearly dead. On the strong flow it is almost crowded with motion.
2. **Things stay where they settle.** Loose objects drift into corners and are gradually cemented. A dropped tool is sealed in crust within a season and becomes part of the wall.
3. **The crust grows toward stillness.** Anywhere the water stops moving, the lime thickens. Anywhere the water runs strongly, the metal stays clean. So the busiest parts of the town look newest, and the quiet parts look oldest. The town is slowly closing itself off from the edges inward, and the working center shrinks by a few centimeters every year.
4. **No one is here.** But the lamps are lit, the machines run, and the grazers keep the flues clear. Something has been maintaining the arrangement, whether by design, habit, or the slow logic of a system that has learned to keep itself going.

## The center

At the middle of the quarter is a small square, closed in on four sides by tall brick buildings whose upper windows are dark and whose lower doors stand open. In the square is a single great wheel, lying flat, half sunk in silt, wide as a room. It turns very slowly with each tide, a quarter turn in, a quarter turn back, and never completes a rotation. Its rim carries a ring of bronze plaques, all worn smooth by the water, all crusted and unreadable.

The Warden rests beside it during slack water, with its wings spread over the silt and the small fish sheltering underneath. The nearest lamp brightens slowly to amber, and the snow rises through the chimneys above, and for a little while nothing moves at all.

Then the current turns. The wheel shifts, a chain tightens somewhere in the dark, and the whole town begins again.
