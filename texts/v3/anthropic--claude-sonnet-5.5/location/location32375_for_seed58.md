# The Sink at Vellum Row

The place is a collapse, and it is still collapsing. Some decades ago the ground gave way over a hot chamber, and what fell in left a pit about two hundred paces across and four storeys deep. The pit was not cleared. A town grew inside it, packed against the broken walls.

## The Rim and the Fall of Light

From above you see only a ragged oval of dark in the flat, cracked plain. The edge is sharp. The limestone has not had time to round off. It breaks in clean planes, in stepped slabs with pale, chalky faces, and in overhangs whose undersides still show the fresh yellow-white of newly exposed rock. Fine grit trickles down the faces at intervals. A hand-sized flake lets go every few minutes, and the town has learned its rhythm.

The light that comes down is thin and tea-colored. The sun arrives only through the opening, and most of the day it reaches only the upper walls. A slow bar of brightness crosses the town's roofs around midday and then withdraws. The rest of the time the town is lit by amber work-lamps in wire cages, and by the dull orange glow of the fissure at the bottom. Everything sits in a brownish half-dusk, and the air holds a haze that makes distant things soft.

## Heat

It is very hot. The heat comes from below, rising through the floor and the lower walls, where the rock is warm to the hand and in places too hot to lean on. The air near the bottom is thick and tastes of mineral and warm iron. Higher up, near the rim, it is merely oppressive. Metal is never quite cool. Rails, ladders, and handles all carry a faint fever, and the town is fitted with cloth-wrapped grips for this reason.

## The Town

The buildings are stacked, not spread. There is no room to spread. Structures climb the walls in terraces, bolted to the rock with thick iron pins, and they overlap so that one roof is another's floor. Foundations are often the wall itself. Doorways are cut straight into the stone and framed with riveted steel. Stairs zigzag up the faces and cross each other, with bridges between them, and bridges between the bridges. Walkways of perforated plate let the hot air rise through them, so the floors ring and glimmer with slots of orange from below.

The materials are few and repeated: the pale local stone; blackened steel; copper, gone green in some places and blue-black in others; and a heavy ceramic, fired locally, in glazed tiles the color of dried blood and mustard. Walls sweat. Lower on the walls, thin crusts of gypsum and salt have begun to form in white blooms, lacy and brittle, and the maintenance crews scrape them off on a schedule. The blooms return within weeks.

Streets in the usual sense exist only on the floor. There the lanes are narrow, roofed over with plate and pipe, and lined with open workshops, heat-exchange halls, and long sheds of condenser racks. Above, the town is vertical, and its real avenues are the stairways and cable-lifts. Small counterweighted cars run on cables from level to level with a dry, ratcheting sound.

## The Engines

The industry is thermal. Nothing burns here. The town has no smoke and no need for it. Its engines run on the difference between the heat of the floor and the cooler air near the rim. Great slow machines of pipe and piston stand at intervals along the floor and at several levels. They are fed by sealed working fluid, a heavy oil or a sort of alcohol, that boils in the hot rock and condenses in the cool. Their cylinders rock back and forth with the patience of something with no reason to hurry. Each one drives a flywheel, and the flywheels turn shafts, and the shafts run out along the walls in long, gently spinning lines under hoods of sheet metal, feeding belts and gears to the workshops.

What the town makes is unclear from a single glance. Some sheds turn out long bars of drawn copper. Others cast the ceramic tiles. A large hall on the west side draws fine wire, and its drums spin with a quiet hiss. Racks of glass vessels catch and condense water from the steam. The products go up the cable-lifts to the rim in baskets and vanish. No one is seen taking them.

## Water

Water is scarce, and it moves in a cycle that the town depends on.

Steam seeps from the fissure and from a dozen smaller vents. It rises, meets the cooler rock higher on the walls, and condenses. This is the town's only rain. It falls in brief, irregular showers from the ceiling of haze, warm as blood and tasting of chalk. The showers come and go over an hour or two, and then the air dries again, leaving the lower streets steaming. Between the showers, water gathers in the town's gutters and tiled channels, running in small brown streams to cisterns cut into the rock. Every surface is a collector: sloped roofs, drip-lips on the walls, hanging screens of mesh that shed droplets into troughs. The whole town is shaped to catch a breath of water.

In a few pockets there are standing pools, shallow, opaque, and warm, ringed with white and rust-colored mineral rims. They are the only still water, and they are fenced.

## Growth

Vegetation is sparse and odd. Nothing green grows in the open. What lives here lives in the wet places: thin, rust-orange and olive mats across the drip-lines on the walls; a pale, feathery fern that grows only in the mouths of vents, where the steam feeds it, and whose fronds curl up when the air dries; and low cushions of moss in the gutters, grey-green and crisp at the edges. In the largest cistern hall a single pale tree, or something like a tree, stands rooted in a crack, with thin leathery leaves and no flowers. The town's people tend it, if anyone does. It is kept alive with a trickle from a copper spout.

There are no animals at all. No insects, no birds, no rats in the walls. The silence of small life is part of the quiet here. Nothing scuttles or sings. The only living things are the mats, the ferns, and whatever grows in the pools.

## Sound

The town is mostly quiet, and its quiet is made of small sounds. Under everything is a low, steady thrum from the engines, felt in the feet before it is heard. Over it come the ticking of metal as it expands in the heat, the slow ring of a flywheel, and the ratchet of a cable-car. Drips fall from the walls into troughs in loose, uneven rhythms. Now and then comes a soft hiss where a vent gives out, and a sharper crack when a slab shifts in the rim, followed by a little rain of grit. When the showers come, the whole town breathes out with a long, wet whisper.

There are no voices in the lanes, at least not near. If people live here, they keep to their sheds and rooms. Occasionally a bell is struck once, low and dull, and nothing follows.

## Rules of the Place

The town keeps some habits, written in the way it is built and never spoken.

- **Nothing is left in the sun-bar.** When the light crosses the roofs at midday, the exposed metal becomes too hot to touch, and the crews cover their tools.
- **The lower walls are never leaned on.** The rock there is warm and slightly soft with heat, and some of it has begun to slump.
- **Every level has a second way out.** The walls are young and can drop a slab without warning, so every stair has a twin, and every bridge has a rope beside it.
- **The condensers are never allowed to dry.** If the racks stop, the town's water stops, and the engines stop with them.

## The Bottom

At the floor, where the fissure opens, the air is thick and dim red. The crack runs across the pit like a seam, a few paces wide and unlit by anything but its own glow. Steam pours from it in slow, curling sheets. Pipes descend into it, wrapped in asbestos-colored cloth, and are drawn up hot and dripping. Nothing stands within a few paces of the edge except the pipes. It is the heart of the place, and it makes almost no sound. It exhales and it glows.

The rim above is a small pale ellipse of sky. From the bottom, it looks very far away, and the town looks like a thing left to grow inside a wound, bright at the edges and warm at the center, still slowly and steadily working.
