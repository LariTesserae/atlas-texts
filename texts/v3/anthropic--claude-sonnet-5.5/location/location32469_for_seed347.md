# The Sink at Harrowmere

The ground here fell in, and not long ago. You can tell because the walls still have edges.

The plateau is limestone, and beneath it a river ate the rock out from below for ten thousand years. Then the roof let go. The basin is about six miles across and three hundred feet below the surrounding country. Its rim is a ragged cliff of pale gray stone, still sharp, still bearing the scars where slabs peeled away, and the talus at its foot is only lightly furred with moss. Some of the fallen blocks are the size of houses. They lie where they landed, tilted against each other, and everything alive in the basin has since organized itself around them.

From the rim it looks like a green lake, but it is a roof. The canopy of alder, willow, and bent birch stands so close that the ground is invisible, and a low white breath of fog hangs in the hollows between the crowns. Cold air pours down the cliffs at night and pools on the floor, so the basin is always a few degrees colder than the plateau above. Frost comes early and leaves late. Yet everything is lush, because water arrives from every direction and has nowhere to go.

## The Wet

The basin has no outlet except the sump: a dark, cold throat at the southern end where the old river still swallows what it is given. The sump is slow. The basin therefore holds water at every scale, from the seep to the shallow lake.

Down the cliffs come hundreds of threads and veils, thin as hair in dry weeks and ropelike after rain. The wall stone is always dark with wet. Ferns grow from the cracks in green fists, and where water drips steadily off an overhang, tufa is beginning to form, a pale sponge of lime that is building itself, one grain at a time, into shelves and curtains. These are the only smooth things in the cliff.

The floor is a checkerboard of pond and peat and island. Water stands in the footprints of large animals. Sedge hummocks stand knee-high, and between them the black water is clear as tea, an inch or an arm's length deep, so that walking anywhere means wading. Sphagnum forms cushions in rust and lime and wine red, and each cushion is saturated, so that stepping on one wrings out a cupful of cold water around your ankle.

The air is close to saturated too. Fog forms on the water at dusk, thickens through the night, and burns off in ragged pieces by mid-morning, rising in slow columns wherever a shaft of sun reaches the floor. Everything carries beads of condensation: spider silk, alder catkins, the fur of the animals, the underside of every leaf. When a breeze crosses the canopy, it rains for a moment under a clear sky.

## The City

Nothing here uses tools, but the basin is more built than most places that do.

The builders are beavers, a great many of them, and they have been at work since the first ponds formed on the new floor. The result is less a collection of dams than a single structure, a city that has grown by accretion across the whole low country. Dams stand in long curved terraces down every slope, each holding a pond that spills over into the next. The ponds are stair-stepped, mirror-still, and edged with peeled white stumps and drowned gray trunks. Where the dams meet, a shared ridge of mud and sticks becomes a causeway wide enough for a family of otters to travel along it side by side.

Between the ponds, canals run straight for hundreds of yards, dug to float timber. Their banks are worn smooth, slick and dark, and the floor of each canal shows the flattened line of a thousand tails. The canals branch and rejoin. Some are choked with duckweed. Others are kept swept clean as if by a municipal crew, because the animals working them are territorial about their lanes.

The lodges are the buildings. They rise from the ponds as domed mounds of stick and mud, six to twelve feet high, and they are packed so closely in the central lakes that there are places where you could not slide a canoe between them. Some are new: raw, yellow-barked, the sticks bright and the mud still gray. Others are old and have been built over, layer upon layer, so that their domes have taken on a grassy skin. Ferns and small birch grow on their roofs. Steam rises from the tops of the occupied ones on cold mornings, a thin pale plume where the breath of the sleeping family escapes through the ventilation gap.

In the deepest part of the basin, one lodge complex has become a kind of tell, an island of accumulated wood and silt sixty yards across, riddled with tunnels and chambers, its top heavy with grass and young spruce. Generations have built over the ruins of generations. The oldest layers are dark, dense peat threaded with roots. It is the closest thing here to a capital.

## The Other Residents

Each layer of the city has tenants, and every surface has been claimed.

**On the cliffs:** Swallows have built a second city in the overhangs, thousands of mud gourds glued in rows to the underside of the rock. Their mud comes from the beaver dams, so the two cities are literally made from one another's spoil. The nests hang in clusters, stacked wall to wall like honeycomb, and the birds pour out of them at dawn and return at dusk in shifting clouds.

**On the ground under the birch:** Wood ants have raised mounds of needles and twig fragments, some waist-high, in a sprawling supercolony linked by trails. Each trail is a worn brown groove through the moss a finger wide, and the ants run along it in a steady two-way stream. Every mound steams faintly in the cold, warmed by the labor inside.

**In the water:** Small trout and sticklebacks hold in the tea-colored channels. Frogs fill the shallows in the spring and sit tightly packed on the floating mats, their chins pulsing. Water beetles skitter across the surfaces. Dragonfly nymphs crawl the pond bottoms, and after a warm day the adults hatch by the hundreds and hang drying on the reeds.

**In the trees:** Herons nest in the drowned snags, ten or twenty to a tree, bulky stick platforms crowded against each other, white with droppings. Warblers and wrens flit through the alder thicket. Owls sleep in the hollows of old willows and are mobbed at dusk.

**In the reeds and along the canals:** Otters, mink, and muskrats. Elk come down from the rim in winter and stand knee-deep, steaming, in the marsh. Wolves follow them, then leave. A single lynx patrols the talus, where hares live in the gaps between the blocks.

Every square yard seems to hold somebody. A fallen log has a fungus shelf, a frog under the bark, a vole nest in the hollow, a wren at the end, and moss over all of it. The place is full to the brim, and the fullness is quiet.

## Sound

It is quieter than a place this crowded should be. The wet moss and peat swallow noise, and the fog dulls what is left. You hear the small sounds: a slow drip from a leaf, the tick of the ants' trails in dry needles, the low knock of a beaver's tail on one of the far ponds, a heron's croak as flat as a hinge. Water moves everywhere without splashing. The falls on the cliffs are thin and far away, so that you hear them as a faint hush, as if someone were always exhaling in the next room.

At dusk the swallows return in a rush of small wings, and the sound lasts a few minutes and then stops. Later, in the cold, frogs begin, but a dozen at a time, not a chorus.

## Processes

Nothing here is finished, and the basin changes on several clocks at once.

**Daily:** Fog builds in the evening, fills the bowl by midnight, and burns off by late morning. Frost rimes the sedge and alder before sunrise and melts in a wave that runs across the floor. The animals arrange their days around it: the beavers work in the dark, the swallows in the light, the herons at the edges of both.

**Seasonal:** Snowmelt from the plateau surges into the basin each spring. The ponds rise, the dams overtop, the canals run brown, and the beavers spend weeks patching. Some dams fail, and a whole terrace will drain in an afternoon, exposing black mud that fills with sedge seedlings within days. New ponds form elsewhere as new dams go up. The city's map is redrawn every year.

**Slow:** The rim is still collapsing. A few times a decade, a slab lets go with a sound like a distant door slamming, and a fresh pale scar opens on the cliff. Fallen blocks dam new pools. The sump slowly enlarges. Peat thickens by a hair's breadth per year.

**Predation:** The marsh is a hunting ground as much as a home. A heron stands motionless for an hour and then spears a frog. A mink slides down a canal bank and takes a muskrat kit. Wolves cut through the elk herd at the edge of the ice. The lynx eats a hare on top of a boulder and leaves the feet. All of it happens without much noise, and the place closes over it quickly.

## Light

The sky is a small bright lid, gray-white most days, sometimes cut with blue. Light reaches the floor diffused and soft, greenish under the alder and silver over the open water. It never gets harsh. Sunlight in a clear spell slides down one cliff in the morning, crosses the ponds, and climbs the opposite wall by afternoon, so the basin is always half in shadow. The tufa curtains glow when the sun catches them, wet and pale as candle wax.

Standing on the central lodge-island at midday, in the cold and the diffuse light, you see it whole: the terraced ponds fanning outward, the domes steaming, the white columns of the dead snags, the swallow-studded cliffs standing all around. The whole city breathes in the same slow rhythm. Everything in the sink is crowded together, cold, and wet, and all of it is living, because the ground fell in and the water and the animals came to fill it.
