# The Kettle

The mountain broke here recently enough that the break still looks raw. A caldera about three hundred paces across sits on a shoulder of young volcanic upland. Its walls are black basalt that has not yet learned to crumble. The edges are still sharp enough to open a palm, and the columns still stand in their cooling-cracks like the ribs of something not yet finished.

The basin is full. That is the first wrongness: the water stands higher than the rim.

From outside, on the dry scree, you see it as a lens, a dome of dark green glass a little taller than a house, resting on the crater without spilling. Its edge is a wall of moving water, slick and faintly steaming, held by nothing. Spray falls from it in sheets and hisses on the warm rock. Where the wall meets the ground there is no lip, no seam, only a line where the gravel is wet and then is not.

You can walk through. The wall takes you like a hand closing, and then you are inside, standing on the crater floor with the whole lake over you.

## The Sound

Nothing prepares you for the sound, and after a while it stops being sound.

It comes from everywhere at once. The vents on the floor scream in long tearing chords, and the gas pockets under the rock burst and re-form in a rolling percussion. The basalt columns along the wall are hollow in places and resonate like organ pipes, each holding a single vast note. Beneath all this is a low grinding pulse that is not any one source. It seems to be the lake's own heartbeat, or the mountain's, and it arrives in the sternum a fraction before it arrives in the ears.

It is deafening in the old sense. Thought has trouble finding purchase. You stop hearing it as noise and begin to feel it as weather, as pressure with a grain to it. The warm water carries it into your teeth and the backs of your eyes. Your own voice, if you scream, is a small dry thing that never leaves your mouth.

The sound acts on the world. Fine pale sand lies over the flat floor between the vents, and the vibration drives it into figures: concentric rings, star-lobed rosettes, long trembling lattices, ridges standing a hand high. They hold for as long as a note holds. When the vents change key they dissolve and reform in seconds, hundreds of small architectures collapsing and rising across the basin. Some hold longer, where two notes sit in a steady interval, and these look almost like foundations or the floorplans of rooms. They are the nearest thing to a built place here, and no one built them.

## The Light

Dim, and it comes from more than one direction. Some is daylight, filtered down through the dome as a bruised green-gold dusk, brightest on the upslope side and swimming with shadow-bands from the churning surface. Some is the vents themselves, which glow a dull orange at the throat and throw the columns into a red relief. Some, and this is part of the breakdown, comes from nowhere. Certain patches of water hold a pale even luminance with no source, as if a room's worth of daylight had been folded into them and forgotten.

Shadows do not agree about where the light is.

## The Water

It is warm as a bath and slightly sweet with dissolved minerals, and it is rarely still. Plumes of hot fluid rise from the floor as rippling columns, and where they meet the cooler layers they spread into broad, shivering ceilings. Cold currents fall from the walls in slow avalanches. The whole basin turns in slow, contradictory gyres, and the current will lift you, drop you, and set you down again a hundred paces from where you stood.

Breathing works, though nobody could say why. The water enters as something between fluid and thick warm air, and the chest accepts it and does not drown. The rule seems to be that you may breathe here so long as you do not think too hard about the mechanism. Those who stop and reason about it are said to cough up the lake.

## The Rules That Have Gone Loose

Direction is unreliable. Bubbles rise from the vents, but some rise sideways, and some fall in slow bright strings toward a floor that is at that moment also above them. In places the surface, the silver underside of the world, hangs not overhead but across the middle distance like a wall, and you can watch a school of ash-colored motes swim up it as if it were a floor.

Time frays near the loudest vents. A single bubble may be seen rising in three places at three different heights, all the same bubble, all unhurried. A sand-figure may collapse and, a breath later, collapse again, having re-formed exactly in between. A dropped stone falls and does not seem to arrive, and then is simply on the ground, half-buried, as though it had been there for years.

Sound sometimes takes on shape. In the thickest roar you may see the vent-notes as faint bands of pressure, like heat-shimmer drawn as ribs, and they can be followed to their source.

## Life

It is sparse. Rock is the main fact here, and what lives on it holds on hard.

Tufts of red-green filament grow on the warm ledges, lashing in the current like hair in a wind. In the quieter pockets stand thin pale reeds with beads of mineral along their stems, each bead a tiny resonating chamber, so the reeds hum faintly against the din like dry seedpods. Between the columns, mats of orange bacterial film glisten over the vent-lips in fans and folds.

Fauna is rare and slow. Translucent grazers the size of a thumb crawl over the mats, their organs showing like beads in glass. A long, eyeless, pale eel-thing, thin as a rope, drifts near the vents and feeds on the heat. Once in a long while, at the edge of sight, something very large moves through the far green, and you feel it as a change in the pressure before you see any shape. Nothing here hunts. There is nothing to hunt with.

## Marks

Scattered, faint, and of uncertain origin. A single cairn of seven flat stones stands on a ledge near the north wall, each stone furred with algae, so old it must have been there before the lake, or the lake has always been here and the cairn has always been under it. A curved low wall of fitted basalt runs for ten paces and stops. On a shelf above the vents lies a ring of pale stones with a hollow at its center, the size of a sleeping body, and the hollow is warm.

No one is here. But the sand-figures near the ring seem always to settle into the same pattern, and the pattern, if you look long enough, resembles a hand, spread wide, palm down, pressing on the floor to feel the mountain's pulse.

## Leaving

The wall on the way out is as sudden as it was coming in. You step through, the water lets go of you, and for a moment the dry air seems unbearably thin and silent. But the ringing follows you down the scree, and the rocks under your boots are trembling, very faintly, in the key of the lake.
