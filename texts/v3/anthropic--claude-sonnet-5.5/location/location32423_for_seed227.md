# The Throat Garden

You would not find it by looking. You would find it by feeling it in your chest first, a pressure through the soles of your boots, a slow tremor in the peat, while the country around you was still ordinary: wet sheep pasture, black-green hedgerows, a low sky the color of pewter. The land there lies barely above the level of the sea. It was scoured flat once and has since had time to forget how. Then the ground simply opens, a fold no wider than a farmyard, and the sound rises out of it like heat from a kiln.

## The Bowl

The hollow is a sink in old limestone, roughly circular, perhaps a hundred paces across and thirty deep. Its walls are not sheer. They have slumped over thousands of years into terraced ledges, each softened by a thick pelt of moss, so that the whole bowl looks upholstered. The stone underneath is pale grey where it shows, rounded and pitted, and fluted by centuries of water running down it in sheets too thin to see.

Lush is the right word for what grows there, though it is a cold lushness, slow and dense. Hart's-tongue fern crowds the ledges in glossy strap-leaved ranks. Moss lies over everything in cushions deep enough to lose a forearm in, and each moss is a different green: bottle, olive, a bruised near-black, and a yellow-green that seems lit from within, though nothing lights it. Liverworts spread like wet green lace over the rock. Filmy ferns, thin enough to be translucent, cling where the spray is heaviest. Hazel and yew lean out from the rim and knit their crowns together overhead, and on the floor is a soft duff of a thousand autumns of leaf.

Light barely gets in. Midday here is a deep green-blue dusk. On clear nights a coin of sky shows through the canopy, and moonlight comes down in pale pieces that lie on the moss and hardly move. Your eyes take a long time to open to it. When they do, the place resolves in shades of slate and pewter with no edges, only differences of dark.

## The Sound

At the bottom, against the north wall, a stream enters the world and leaves it.

It is not a large stream. It arrives from the pasture through a crooked cut in the rim and falls the last fifteen feet over a lip of stone into a basin. From the basin it disappears into a black slot in the rock floor, a swallow hole the width of a doorway, undercut and shaped like a lower jaw. You could stop the stream with your two arms. And yet the sound is the loudest thing that most people who come here will ever stand inside.

The reason is geometry. The falling water strikes the basin and the noise goes into the hole, and the hole is only the mouth of something enormous. Below is a cavern, or a chain of them, and the water drops again and again down there in the dark, and the whole system speaks up through the one narrow throat. The bowl above works like the bell of a horn. The sound does not come from a direction. It comes from everywhere at once. It is a wall of broken white roar with a deeper note beneath it, a sub-bass moan you take in through the ribs and the teeth. Loose water trembles in every hollow of every leaf. Ferns shiver. Fine spray hangs in the air, and the vibration keeps it hanging, so that the hollow always smells and tastes of wet stone and iron.

Speech is not possible. Shouting into someone's ear produces only a warmth on the skin. Thought itself is difficult at first, like trying to hold a conversation in the mind under a heavy weight. After an hour you stop fighting it. Somewhere in the second hour the roar goes strangely still, as constant sound does, and becomes a kind of enormous silence you are standing inside.

## The Breathing

Once you have been there long enough, you learn that the sound is not steady.

The cavern breathes. In the cold half of the year, cold air sinks into the swallow hole, drawn down by the warmer rock, and the roar is low and thick, the bass note full. In the warm half the flow reverses. Air rises from below and the throat sings higher, thinner, with a keening edge at the top, and a faint mineral-smelling wind lifts the fronds around the basin and lays them down again. Twice a year, in the weeks between, there is a slack: a strange pressure-less hush where the roar drops a little and the moss seems to hold still. Then it turns. The change takes days and is too gradual to catch. You only notice you have been listening to a different note.

The rest of the year moves as slowly. The moss on the north ledges advances a few thumbs' breadth in a lifetime. A yew on the rim has been leaning toward the hollow for six centuries and will fall in when it falls. The stream cuts its lip back a little each winter flood.

## What Was Made Here

People have been here, and they left little, and what they left is careful.

On the floor, on the one dry shelf of level ground on the south side, is a hearth ring of seven flat stones, the inside faces blackened. It has not been lit in a long time, and a low mat of moss has crept over the ash. Near it stands the ribs of a lean-to: yew poles lashed in a pitch with strips of bark, the thatch long gone to brown crumble. It could shelter two people lying close.

Up the eastern wall runs a stair of sorts. Steps have been pecked into the ledges with hammerstones, shallow and worn to dishes, each one holding a little water and a skin of algae. There is a handrail of sorts: a groove worn smooth in the rock at shoulder height, polished by palms. You can see where the rail pauses, where people rested before the final descent.

Along the wall above the hearth, in a sheltered overhang, are hands. Dozens of them, pressed in red ochre, fingers spread, the pigment gone dull brown and sunk into the limestone. Some have fingers missing or folded, arranged in patterns, and there are rows of short strokes, and one careful spiral. In a place where a voice is nothing, this is where people spoke. Anyone who looked at the wall could read the hands: come here, stay, leave, danger, the water is rising, I was here.

In a niche beside the hands lies a small awl of green-crusted copper, its bone handle rotted to a stain. Someone set it down carefully, point inward. Nobody has taken it.

A cairn of river cobbles stands on the rim, at the point where the roar first becomes audible as sound rather than pressure. The old custom, if it was a custom, was probably to stop there, to say what needed saying aloud, and then to go down and say nothing.

## What Lives Here

Very little, and none of it hurries.

A dipper nests in the wet rock beside the falls, a small round dark bird with a white bib. It walks into the basin and under the water and comes out again unbothered, bobbing on a stone. It has never heard a quiet day, and does not seem to need to. In the leaf litter on the floor lives a pale salamander, cold and slow, a few of them at most, that spend the winter in the crevices and creep out onto the moss on the wettest nights. Small moths cling to the undersides of the ferns, and their wings are made to stay still. Snails leave silver lines across the leaf mould. Once in a season a fox or a badger will come to the rim, smell the air that rises from the hollow, and go back the way it came.

There is a fine white fungus on some of the fallen yew, and a pale root system that seems to feed on the dead hazel. Otherwise the life is green and sessile, patient and packed together, and it fills the space between the stones.

## The Rules of the Place

Nothing here is supernatural. The place simply has a set of conditions that hold, and the older visitors seem to have understood them:

You come down slowly, because the steps are wet and you cannot hear a slip.
You do not come alone, because if you fall no one will hear you call, and the only safeguard is a companion who can see you.
You do not stay through the change of the breath, when the wind reverses, because the air at that time is thin and heavy with what rises from below, and people who slept in the lean-to then are said to have not woken.
You leave what you find.

At the deepest hour of a cold night the hollow is at its truest: moonlight in pieces on the moss, the stream white and faintly visible as it drops, breath smoking in the spray, and the sound so total that it seems less a sound than a condition of the world, like weather, like being underwater. You stand in it, small and warm, a single living thing among green shapes in the dark, and the ground hums against your feet, and the hands on the wall beside you go on saying what they always said.
