# The Sounding Ward

You arrive by a spiral stair cut into wet stone, and for four hundred steps the only information is heat and noise. The heat comes up the stairwell like breath from a sleeper. The noise is a chord so low you feel it in your molars before you hear it, with higher things moving on top of it.

Then the stair opens into the ceiling of the place, and you stand on a gantry looking down into a dark that has a floor.

## The Shape of It

The Ward is a single hollow in the earth, roughly the size of a hamlet with its fields: eight or nine hundred paces across, a vault overhead that you sense as a change in how sound returns. It is a small world, and almost none of it is occupied. Streets were laid out for ten thousand people, and there is a great deal of empty ground between the few things that stand. A block of tall, narrow houses rises on the left, and then nothing for two hundred paces except a plaza of black tile. Then a single lamp-post, then a bridge over a channel where no one has crossed for a long time.

The buildings are old in a way that has stopped being decay. Centuries of acid drip and warm condensation have taken the corners off everything. The stone and the grey-white ceramic of the walls have the softness of river cobbles. Stairs are dished in the middle. Window mouths have rounded into ovals. Door lintels sag in gentle, stable curves, like wax that ran once and settled. Nothing is ruined. It has reached equilibrium with the damp, and it will look like this for another thousand years.

Between the older masonry stand the other structures, which nobody in the Ward's history could have poured or carved. These are the **resonators**: tall, hollow, seamless columns of a dull pewter-colored material that feels faintly greasy and is always a few degrees warmer than the air. They are set in rings and lines throughout the city. Each one is a hundred feet high and thin as a birch. Each one sings.

## The Sound

The sound is the truth of the place, and everything else is arranged around it.

The resonators hold a standing wave, a layered, slowly shifting chord in which each column carries one voice. The deepest voices sit below hearing and press on the chest and the fluid of the inner ear. The middle voices drone like an organ pipe played by wind through a door. The highest are a bright, glassy shimmer that runs through the chord like light through water. Under and around these are the noises of the working city: the knock of pistons behind grilles, the long liquid gulp of pumps, the hiss of vented steam, the tick and shudder of valves far off in the dark opening on schedules nobody remembers setting.

It is loud enough that speech must be shouted at arm's length, and after an hour it stops feeling like noise and starts feeling like weather.

The Ward's makers built it loud on purpose. The vault above is not stone. It is a shell of fractured rock, saturated and pre-stressed, held up in part by the sound. The resonators' standing wave sets up a pressure field in the rock that keeps the tension distributed, like a bowed string keeps its shape. The engineering is well past anything a current physicist would sign off on. It treats vibration as a load-bearing material. If the chord falls silent, the ceiling will be a different shape within the hour.

The chord has faltered before. The oldest scars in the city are from the Quiet Days, when a ring of columns failed and a district a quarter-mile wide came down. It is now a rubble field, worn smooth, that the moss is only beginning to take. The remaining resonators have since retuned themselves around the gap. You can hear the adjustment: one voice in the chord holds a note slightly wrong on purpose, compensating for what was lost.

## Darkness

There is almost no light, and what exists is thin and low.

The resonators glow faintly at their bases, a dim amber like the last of a banked fire, that climbs a few feet up each column and fades. Along the old canals, strips of pale-blue luminous algae cling to the waterline and pulse slowly, in phase with the deepest note. In the walls of the tall houses, some windows hold a green, dying lamp-glow behind glass so clouded it looks like breath on a mirror. Those lamps have been burning without fuel since before the stone was worn.

Beyond these, the dark is complete. Your eyes learn to read shapes by where the glow is absent: a dark rectangle where a wall blocks the blue, a long dark bar where a bridge crosses a canal. Distance is very hard to judge. The empty plazas have no edges you can see, and you find them by the change in your footsteps' echo.

## Water and Weather

The heat is a steady, damp warmth, like the inside of a greenhouse in the night, and the air is always close to saturation. Warmth rises from deep vents and from the machinery, and it meets the cooler rock of the vault. It condenses there, and the vault sweats.

The result is the Ward's weather, which has a rhythm. Every few hours the ceiling rains. It starts with a single drip, then a hundred, then a soft fine downpour that lasts twenty minutes and falls in sheets you can hear approaching across the dark as a rising hiss. The resonators' chord bends slightly under the rain, the high voices dulling and the deep ones swelling. Then it thins, stops, and the drips continue for another hour, each one a small bright note in the roar.

The rain feeds the canals. These are narrow channels of dark, warm, slow-moving water, lined with old ceramic, running in loops the makers cut to carry water to the pumps. The pumps lift it to cisterns high in the tall houses, where it cools, drips down, and returns. The whole system is closed. The water has circulated for longer than any language in the city has been spoken. It tastes of iron and old coins, and it's safe to drink if you let it stand.

In a few places the water pools instead of flowing: shallow basins in the plazas, brimming after rain and shrinking through the following hours. In the damp dark they are the only places where the floor reflects the amber glow.

## What Grows

The plants are few and specialized. There are no gardens and no fields, only what can live on warmth, water, and a trace of light.

- **Vent-ferns** grow in colonies around the steam grilles at the feet of the resonators: pale, translucent, nearly white, with fronds as wide as a hand, slick with condensation. They shiver constantly in the vibration, so each colony looks lit from within by a small tremor.
- **Bell-moss** carpets the north faces of the older houses, where the drip is steady. It's deep green, almost black, and grows in little domes that ring faintly when the chord reaches certain pitches. Walk through a patch of it and it whispers around your ankles.
- **Ceramic lichen** is a crust of rust-orange and ash-grey that grows only on the pewter columns. It feeds on the heat and on something in the material itself. Where it spreads, the column's note deepens slightly. The resonators seem to tolerate it and may need it.
- In the rubble field of the Quiet Days, tall, leggy, pallid shoots have begun to push through: the first attempt at a pioneer forest, most of it dying, some of it holding.

Nothing flowers. Nothing needs to.

## What Lives Here

Life is rare, and most of it is small and quiet enough to be lost in the noise.

**Glass-newts** live in the canals: translucent salamanders as long as a forearm, with visible organs, that cling to the ceramic and feed on the algae. They can be seen only by their own faint blue reflection. In the roar they communicate by touch, pressing snouts together in slow rows along the waterline.

**Drip-spiders** hang from the vault on threads too fine to see and harvest the rain. On wet nights you can see the sparkle of their webs where droplets catch the amber glow, thousands of them, swaying far overhead like a second, sideways rain.

**The wardens**, if that's what they are, are the largest thing in the Ward and the least seen. They are pale, long-limbed, eyeless animals, perhaps ten feet from forefoot to tail, that move through the plazas at the pace of a slow walk and stop for long periods with their heads pressed against the resonators. They seem to be listening, or feeding on the vibration, or tuning. They are gentle when approached and simply turn away. There are perhaps a dozen in the whole city. When one dies, the others gather. Their bones, when you find them, have been cut and set into the columns' bases in careful patterns, as if the city and its keepers had long ago agreed to each other.

**Machines** also move here: low crawlers the size of dogs that patrol the pipes and canal edges, clicking as they go, maintaining valves and wiping lichen from vents. They are not friendly or hostile. They repair. Sometimes you see one halted mid-street, waiting for a signal that may not come again.

## Rules of the Place

- **The chord must not stop.** Everything native to the Ward understands this. Animals cluster near the columns and press against the humming metal. The crawlers prioritize tuning above every other repair. If you carry noise-canceling devices, or anything that interferes with the resonance, the machines will find you and quietly remove it.
- **Rain marks the hours.** With no sun to count by, the wardens, the crawlers and the moss all move on the rain's schedule, and you will find yourself doing the same.
- **The dark is not empty, but it is patient.** Emptiness is the Ward's dominant condition. Most of the city is quiet in every sense except the sound, which is total. You can walk an hour and meet nothing but the chord and the drip.
- **Nothing here is abandoned.** It is unoccupied, which is different. The tall houses are aired, dried, and repaired. The canals are cleared. The lamps are lit. Someone or something expects residents. It has expected them for a very long time, and in the meantime it takes care of the rooms.

## Standing in the Middle

At the center, in a plaza of black tile that has worn to a shallow bowl, stands a ring of the largest resonators, forty feet around, close enough that their voices blend into a single held tone. Stand inside and the sound stops being sound. It becomes pressure, warmth and a kind of attention. The rain, when it comes, falls around the ring but not inside it, deflected by the field into a curtain of silver threads in the amber glow. You can stand dry in the roar, in the dark, at the bottom of the world, in the middle of a city that keeps holding itself up by singing.

Somewhere out in the dark a warden turns its head toward the note, and the vault above you, a thousand feet of wet rock, holds.
