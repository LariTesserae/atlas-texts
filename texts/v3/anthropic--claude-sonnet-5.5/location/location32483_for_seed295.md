# The Roofed Valley

At about three thousand meters the road of rock ends, and what follows is a road of something else. A wide, shallow valley lies between two ridges, and every surface in it has been made by something small. The rock underneath is deep and old, and nobody sees it. The ground you walk on is packed carton and chewed earth, laid down in layers by termites over more centuries than anyone has counted. Where a stream cut through, the banks are worked into terraces and lips and small spillways, all of it mortared with saliva and dung.

## The approach

The ridges above the valley are open grassland and cold wind. Then the slope tips over and you drop into warm air. The valley holds its heat. It sits low between the ridges, sheltered from the wind, and it breathes out the fermenting warmth of a billion bodies and their buildings. The air thickens and smells of wet clay, ammonia, crushed leaves, and something sweet and rotten, like fruit left in a closed room.

The light goes out gradually. The valley has a ceiling.

## The roof

Over many generations, social spiders have strung the treetops together. Their sheeting is not a neat web. It is a gray-white canopy layered like felt, meters thick in places, sagging between the crowns of the great trees and held up by their limbs and by the weight of everything that has landed on it. Dead leaves, husks, and the wings of a hundred kinds of insect lie caught in it, and over the decades they have become a soil. Ferns and small orchids root in the silk. Mosses hang from it in long drips.

Light gets through only as a dim, greenish-brown glow, like light at the bottom of a pond. At midday you can see your hands and the shapes of large things. At night the valley is properly dark, and the dark is full of small sounds and a faint, cold glow from fungi on the walls.

Weaver birds have hung their nests from the roof's underside, thousands to a tree. The nests are long woven flasks, some fresh green, some brown and frayed, some so old they have gone black and sprouted their own gardens. They swing when anything moves. The birds fill the upper dark with a steady chattering, a dry, rattling, communal sound that never stops entirely.

## The towers

The floor of the valley is a city, though nothing in it planned to be one. Termite mounds rise from the ground in every direction, some knee-high, some ten meters tall. The tallest are buttressed and ribbed like cathedral spires, with vents and chimneys and fluted flanks that funnel the warm air upward through channels the size of a wrist. Their color is the color of the soil they were made from, red-orange in one district, gray-ochre in another, nearly black where the ground is richer. Rain has streaked them, and over centuries has rounded every edge. The old towers are smooth and softened, and their bases have sunk into the ground they grew from, with moss on the north sides and fungus gardens breathing at the vents.

Between the towers, the ground is threaded with covered galleries. These are tunnels of carton built above the surface, running from mound to mound like alleys roofed for privacy. Columns of ants and termites move through them in traffic so dense that the walls hum faintly if you put your palm against them.

Where the towers crowd close, the passages between them are barely shoulder-width and often dark. Their walls are damp and cool. Mud-dauber cells cling in rows along the upper edges, and lizards, geckos, and small pale frogs live in the seams. The passages twist, and after a hundred meters you have lost any sense of direction except up and down the valley's slope.

## The trees

The trees are lush and heavy. They are fig, cedar, tree fern, and a tall, rough-barked timber whose crowns form the roof's scaffolding. Every trunk has been colonized to the first branch. Ants have built carton galleries up the bark. Wasps have suspended gray paper globes from the limbs. Stingless bees have plastered resin funnels to the crotches, so that the trunks glisten amber and smell of pine and honey. Vines run between trunks in thick ropes, some as thick as a thigh, and animals use them as roads.

Roots do not go into soil the way they do elsewhere. They go into the mounds, into old nests, into the deep humus that has built up in the roof and rained down for centuries through the silk. Some roots grow upward, into the hanging nests and the tangled mats of debris, and seek out the water held there.

## Water

The valley is damp, not wet. Rain is occasional. When it comes, it arrives all at once, usually in the late afternoon, and the sound of it on the silk roof is like a great hand drumming on a tent. Most of it never reaches the ground directly. It is caught, held, and slowly let go. For hours afterward the whole valley drips, and every leaf-tip and nest-spout and vine-end lets fall a bead of water. The drips make a second layer of sound under the animal one.

Between rains, water lives in pockets. There are bromeliad-like rosettes in the tree forks, each holding a liter or more and each with its own colony of tadpoles, mosquito larvae, and beetles. There are hollows in the old carton, sealed and cool inside, where dew condenses overnight. There are small pools in the lowest places, dark and tea-colored, ringed with mud-caked hoofprints and tracks of small mammals. A single stream runs through the valley's center, narrow and slow. Its banks are worked so thoroughly by animals that its course has been changed several times, and old dry channels still show as pale curving lines under the moss.

Mist gathers in the early morning and hangs in the roof, so that the silk sags with water and drips on everything until the day's warmth lifts it.

## The animals

The fauna is not merely large in number. It is total. It is the medium the valley is made of.

At the base of it are the insects, in quantities that are difficult to hold in the mind. Termites and ants form living pavements. Beetles, cicadas, crickets, katydids, and moths fill every layer. At night the air is thick with wings.

Above the insects are the things that eat them. Frogs sit in rows on every ledge. Geckos hunt on the walls. Bats hang from the roof's underside in clusters as thick as fruit, and shrews, pangolins, and anteaters work the mounds. Small monkeys move through the vine-roads in family groups, fearless, picking the wasp paper and scraping the resin. Snakes lie coiled on the warm flanks of the towers. Owls and nightjars call from the dark tree-hollows.

Higher still are the birds: weavers, swifts that nest in the tower vents, small parrots that shriek through the roof in the evenings, and hornbills that seal themselves into the trunks. Larger predators exist, leopard-sized cats and long-limbed civets, but they live in the valley the way anyone lives in a crowded city, cautious and always brushing past other lives.

Nothing goes to waste. Every dead body is a feast within an hour. Every fallen fruit, every shed skin, every husk enters the cycle at once. The ground has no smell of decay for long, only a kind of continuous warm turnover.

## The sound

The sound is deafening, and it is not a noise but a substance. At dusk especially, when the frogs begin, the cicadas rise, the bats wake, and the weaver colonies settle for the night in a chorus of rattling, the valley becomes so loud that speech at arm's length is a shout. The sound has layers. The lowest is the deep, steady thrum of insects in the earth and walls, felt in the chest more than heard. Above that is the frog chorus, a wall of trills and clicks and bell-notes stacked in a hundred pitches. Above that are the cicadas, in shrill metallic waves that swell and fall as one mass. And on top are the birds and monkeys and bats, cutting across it all in bursts.

The roof traps it. Sound doesn't leave the valley; it circulates. Standing in a gap between towers, you can feel the noise pressing on your ears like water pressure.

There are brief silences. When a hawk crosses above the silk, or a leopard moves through, a hush travels outward in a ring, and for a few seconds only the drips can be heard. Then it closes again, and the noise resumes as if nothing had interrupted.

## Time

Change here is slow. The towers grow a few centimeters a year and collapse in their own time. The roof thickens by a hand's breadth each decade, and the trees beneath it lean and sometimes fail under the weight. When a tree falls, a hole opens in the silk and a shaft of real daylight comes down, and for a season the floor beneath it explodes with green. Then the spiders begin to close the gap, thread by thread, and within a few years the light is gone again.

The valley has settled into its own weathered form. Its shapes are old. Its balance has been tested by droughts and fires on the ridges and has held. Nothing is fixed, but everything is patient, and the changes that matter take longer than a human life to see.

## What it is like to be there

You move through it slowly, because you must. Your hands touch damp carton, wet moss, warm resin, the rough silk. Your ears ring with the roar. Things walk across your skin, and you stop noticing after the first hour. The air is warm as blood, and the dark presses close, only slightly lightened by a glow overhead that might be day.

You understand, after a while, that there is no ground here that wasn't made. Every step is on something that a creature built, and every creature that built it is still around you, working, in the dark, in enormous numbers, all of them talking at once.
