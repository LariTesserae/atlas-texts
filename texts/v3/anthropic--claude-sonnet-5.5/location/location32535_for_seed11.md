# The Loud Table

Start with the ground, because the ground is the one calm thing here.

You are at the top of a very old world. The plateau stands nearly five thousand meters above a sea no one on it has seen, and it has been worn down for so long that it has no ambition left. It is a wide, faintly tilted floor of dark quartzite and iron-stained sandstone, rubbed into low domes and shallow dishes. The horizon is a long, unbroken, slightly bowed line. Anything that could have been a peak was taken down to the roots before the first fern learned to unroll. What remains is bedrock that has finished changing. Its surfaces are smooth as knuckles, rust-black on top and the color of dried blood where they have split. Nothing here has sharp edges except the newest cracks, and "newest" means a few hundred thousand years old.

The rock is warm. It is dark and it drinks the sun, and in the hours after noon you can feel it through boot soles, a low, steady heat like an animal's flank. The air is thin but mild. The heat coming up from the stone and the cold coming down from the sky meet a body-height above the ground, and that seam is where the weather lives.

## The Air

Everything that moves here moves in the air.

The sky over the Table never holds one state for long. Cloud forms from nothing, a gray thumbprint that swells into a wall in the time it takes to eat. The wall rolls across the dishes of stone, drops a curtain of fine warm rain, gets torn open by a downdraft, and lets in a shaft of hard white light, and the whole thing may then invert. Wind arrives from one quarter, stops as if a door had shut, and comes back from the opposite one. Small whirlwinds of mist, some tall as houses, wander over the domes and lift pale dust and shed petals into slow spirals. Lightning is frequent but far apart. It does not strike so much as flicker inside the clouds, so the storms seem to be thinking.

None of this deposits or carves anything now. The rain is warm, fine and brief, and the rock has long since given up whatever it could give. So the change is enormous and leaves nothing behind. It is turbulence on top of permanence: a boiling sky above a floor that will be here unaltered in ten million years.

## The Sound

The sound is the first thing you meet and the last thing you get used to.

Across the plateau run the joints, the old fractures widened by ages of water into slots and shafts. Some are hair-thin and some are open canyons a few paces wide and a kilometer long. Many are roofed over by rock bridges, and many are pitted with vertical wells that drop into caverns. Over hundreds of millions of years the stone has been bored into a natural organ of unimaginable size: pipes, reeds, cavities, throats. When the wind crosses them, and the wind is always crossing something, they sound.

The result is not a howl. A howl is a single voice. This is a wall of overlapping tones: a bass so deep that it arrives as pressure in the sternum and teeth, a middle register of groaning and fluting like a thousand ships' horns tuned a little apart, and above them a shrill, glassy shimmer where the wind skims the narrowest cracks. The tones slide. When a wind-front sweeps the plateau the whole chord bends downward, as if the land were exhaling. When the wind stops, the caverns keep sounding for several seconds and then, one by one, drop out. On a rare still hour you can hear a single deep well finishing its note alone, as if it were unsure.

It is deafening in the plain sense. Speech at arm's length is lost. You learn to read faces and hands, and to feel rather than hear the approach of a big gust, as a swell of pitch under your feet.

The vegetation adds its share. The rosette-plants have leaves stiff as sheet tin, and in a gust they clash with a dry, ringing rattle. Seed-pods the size of gourds, hollow and thin-walled, hang in clusters and knock against each other like wooden bells. Where they are dense, the ground-level noise is a continuous crackling rain that has nothing to do with rain.

## The Green

The plateau is lush, and the reason is not soil. There is almost no soil. There is grit, decomposed quartz, and a black felt of accumulated plant matter that is everywhere and rarely deeper than a hand. The reason is air. The cloud is nearly permanent, and every leaf is a comb for it. Condensation drips constantly from the tips of everything. Water does not run across the rock so much as pass through the living things standing on it.

So the green is stacked and layered, and it clings.

- **Cushion-mat** covers the domes: a spongy moss-and-lichen carpet in mustard, olive, oxblood, and a startling electric teal. It is springy and soaked, and it squelches even on days that felt dry. Its surface is freckled with tiny red flowers.
- **Tree-ferns and stilt-palms** grow in the shelter of the shallow dishes, where the fractured rock lets roots down into pockets of damp. They rise five to eight meters, trunks matted with aerial roots and epiphytes, fronds thrashing constantly in the shifting wind.
- **Tank-plants**, huge bromeliad-like rosettes, are the reservoirs of the land. Each holds a few liters of clear cold water in its funnel of leaves, and each is a small world of its own, with larvae, scum-algae, and jewel-colored frogs the size of thumbnails. A traveler can live off them.
- **Tin-leaf giants** are the rosette-plants that clash in the wind: thick, waxy stems topped with silvery-gray leaf crowns, standing alone or in sparse groves across the flats. In cloud they look like rows of standing figures with their hands raised.
- **Drape-vines** hang from the rock bridges over the slots in long green ropes, and in the bigger blasts the wind lifts them straight out sideways so that an entire canyon seems to be leaning.

The vegetation has the look of something that has been at peace with the land for a very long time and at war with the sky for just as long. Everything is low, gripping, flexible, or armored. Nothing tall stands that has not been shaped by wind.

## The Water

There are no rivers on the Table, only films. After a shower, sheets of water a fingernail deep slide across the smooth black domes in braided threads, shine for a while, and drain into the joints, which swallow them. The slots and wells then carry the water down into the dark, where it feeds hidden lakes and springs far below, and eventually the falls that spill off the plateau's rim into the lowland forests. You can hear those falls from the edge, but the sound is smaller than the pipes and gets lost in them.

In the dishes, water gathers in pockets: black tarns the size of rooms, clear and cold, rimmed with cushion-moss and floored with pale sand. They are quiet, in the sense that wind ripples them but they make no sound of their own. Standing at one, you can feel the huge noise all around you as if from behind glass.

## The Dead Fog

The greatest strangeness is a matter of physics that feels like magic.

When the low cloud settles thickly on the plateau, when it is really down, the sound goes out of it. Inside the densest fog banks the deafening organ-roar drops away to almost nothing. It is not muffled so much as *taken*. Voices carry a few paces and then vanish. Your own footsteps are dull. The whole hemisphere of noise thins to a distant thrum, as if the world had pulled a blanket over its head. Inside those banks you can hear your own pulse, water dripping from leaves, and the small ticking of insects.

The people of the Table know this. To them the fog is the place of rest. The rest of the plateau is the place of work.

## The People, Few

Almost nobody lives here. A person can walk for days and see nothing of the human kind but marks. But the marks are there and they are recent, in the geological sense.

Small groups move across the Table in the fog seasons, hunting the few animals and gathering tank-water, seed-pods, and the pith of stilt-palms. They carry stone blades, bone needles, fiber cord, and a few beaten pieces of native copper hammered into hooks and awls. Their gear is simple, hand-made, and repaired constantly. They wear plugs of wax and plant-down in their ears, and their communication is a system of hand shapes, arm positions, and cloth signals, fluent and quick, that must have grown up specifically to defeat the noise. Their faces are very expressive. They rarely raise their voices, and when they sing, which they do in the fog, it is soft, close, and private.

Their built things are small and scattered:

- **Cairns and standing stones** mark routes between fog-refuges, each a stack of flat rocks capped with a hollow bone that whistles a particular note in wind. A traveler learns to navigate by ear, knowing that the bone with the high double note means *water two hundred paces east.*
- **Windbreak huts**: low, round, corbelled stone shelters with turf roofs, half-sunk into the cushion-moss, with entrances turned away from the prevailing gust. They are empty most of the year. Inside, a hearth-ring, a sleeping shelf, and often a single painted hand on the wall.
- **Old terraces** on the lee side of a few domes, faint now, where earlier people grew a tuber that no one grows anymore. The wild descendants still come up through the moss, small and bitter.
- **One or two tall stone piers**, older than the huts and made by other hands, whose purpose is lost. They stand in the open with hollow tops, and when a very particular wind comes they sound a note nothing else on the plateau matches, one that cuts through everything. The people do not build near them, and nobody says why.

## Animals

Fauna is rare and mostly small: frogs in the tanks, beetles in the moss, wren-like birds that flit low and never sing, or if they sing, do so in vain. Larger things exist but are seldom met. There is a deer-sized, thick-coated grazer that moves in silent herds through the fog, almost blind, navigating by touch through its whiskers. There is a slow, heavy, scaled thing that sleeps in the slots and is said to be deaf as stone. And at dusk, high above, long-winged birds ride the updrafts, holding station for hours without a wingbeat, watching the moving sky.

## The Rules, Such As They Are

1. The ground never changes. The air never stops.
2. The louder the day, the drier the air. Wet weather is quieter, and fog is nearly silent.
3. Do not go into a slot canyon in rising wind. The tones climb, then the pressure builds, then the wind squeezes through.
4. Always carry something that whistles, because in fog you will need to be found.
5. The old piers are not to be slept near.

## Toward the Rim

If you walk far enough in any direction, the flatness begins to tilt, the domes stretch out, and the green grows thinner. The noise thickens toward the edge, where the wind pours off the plateau's rim and the whole Table sings its lowest, longest note. Then the ground simply stops. Below you is cloud, and below the cloud, a forested world going down and down into warm haze, silent at this distance.

Behind you the whole roaring, rattling, fluting plateau goes on, ancient, level, and green, a great worn stone instrument left out in the weather, played by nobody and never quiet.
