# The Sump Flats

Start with the warmth. It comes up through the soles of your feet before anything else, a wet, body-temperature heat, as if the ground had recently been occupied. The air is the same temperature as your skin, so after a few minutes you lose the boundary of yourself. Humidity beads on every surface. It runs and gathers in the seams, then drips from anything that projects.

There is no light. This isn't dusk or a moonless night, where the eye keeps hoping. Nothing here has ever been lit. Eyes exist in this place, but they are vestigial or decorative, or they are used for something else. So the place has to be known by other means.

## Ground

The land is low, barely above whatever sea or aquifer it came from, and it is new. It was raised, or it bled up out of the crust, so recently that nothing has softened. Basalt shelves break in clean conchoidal edges. Mud terraces stand with their cracks still square, each polygon lifting at its rim like a dish, and the rims cut skin if you drag a hand along them. Nothing has had time to round. Ridges are knife-thin. Small ravines drop straight down a body's length and end in standing black water, warm as tea.

The water is everywhere and mostly shallow. It lies in sheets over the flats, ankle-deep and glass-still, and it seeps from the rock faces in slow, continuous weeping, so that every wall is a low-pressure rain running the wrong way. You hear it as a fine ticking, millions of drops leaving stone. Under the ticking there is a deeper sound, the water table shifting under the crust. In places the ground gives like a wet sponge and a pool wells up around your foot, then slowly, patiently drains away again.

The flats run out farther than anything can be measured. This is a region, not a room. It has valleys of ridges, then basins, then a long tilted plain, then more ridges. You know it is large because sounds take a long time to come back from it, and because when you walk for what feels like an hour the wind on your face doesn't change.

## What grows

Vegetation is sparse, and what exists is not green. Nothing here photosynthesizes. Thin black stalks rise from seeps and vents, some knee-high and some taller than a person, each as brittle as burnt reed. They feed on heat and on the mineral broth of the seeps. Where warm water bubbles up they cluster, and elsewhere there are none. Between the clusters lie mats of pale felt, fungal or bacterial, spread like wet cloth over the low rocks. They are slick and faintly warm, and they give off a smell of iron, yeast, and struck match.

Sometimes the stalks move when there is no wind. They lean toward your hand at a distance of a few inches, then away again, like something sensing heat and deciding against it.

## The made things

The place is scattered with industry, not densely, but enough that you keep finding it.

A pump house stands on a basalt shelf, a squat concrete box with a rusted intake pipe as thick as a tree trunk dropping into the water. A conveyor gantry crosses a ravine and ends in the air over a drop, its belts long rotted to strings that hang and drip. A boiler shed leans with its chimney cracked halfway up, and inside it a cold furnace door yawns, ringed with white mineral crust. A rail spur emerges from a mud bank, runs eighty meters over the flat, and goes back into the mud with nothing to show where the rest has gone. A generator hall the size of a cathedral holds a row of turbines under a ceiling you cannot hear the top of.

Everything is corroded to the same wet orange. Everything is coated in the pale felt. Everything smells of oil and rain.

The strange thing is that they are not entirely dead. The turbines turn very slowly, one revolution in perhaps a minute, and nothing drives them. Gauges have needles that tremble. A relay somewhere clicks once every few hours. A pipe warms under your palm, and you feel a pulse in it, a low regular knocking as if something were being pumped through it, though the pipe is open at both ends and empty. These machines do not run on any power you could name. They run on habit, or on the place remembering that it was an industry.

## The animals

The fauna is abundant, more so than you would think possible for a landscape with so little to eat. But the sump is thick with life. Every ledge, every wet crevice, every underside of stone is occupied. The density is nearly total. Bodies lie against bodies in layers, and you begin to understand that a rock face is not a rock face but a wall of animals so still and so numerous that you took them for stone.

Some categories:

**Slate-backed crawlers,** the size of a hand, live in mats on the mud terraces. They move in unison, and when one is disturbed the whole terrace rustles like a sigh going outward across a hundred meters.

**Long pale things** coil in the pump-house intake, thick as your forearm, blind, with heat-sensing pits along the jaw. They lift toward you as you pass, and you feel the faint pressure of their attention on your face.

**Clickers** hang in the gantry rafters in the thousands, small and furred, and speak to each other in ultrasonic ticks. You cannot hear it directly. It comes through your teeth as a faint prickling. They navigate by it, and it is why you sometimes feel a wall before you touch it.

**Wading birds** or something like birds, tall, thin-legged, wingless, stalk the shallows with delicate high steps. They stab at the water without a sound. They have no eyes at all, just smooth skin over the skull, and they turn their heads to follow you exactly.

**Swarms** hover in the dead air of the generator hall, motes in the millions, a shifting warm density you push through like a curtain. They settle on skin without biting, only tasting the salt.

There are predators, but hunting here is slow and patient. Most of the violence is a matter of one animal sitting on another for a very long time.

## Sound

It is mostly quiet. That's important. Beneath the dripping there is a great softness, broken now and then by a single event: a heron-thing's step, a stalk snapping, the far click of a relay. Sounds carry strangely. Because the damp air is dense, a whisper can arrive at a hundred meters as clearly as if it were said in your ear. At other times a shout dies in three steps, swallowed as if by wool.

## Where the rules fail

The weirdness runs deep, and it works by attrition. The place is fraying at its foundations.

Distances are not consistent. You can walk toward a pump house for an hour and not come closer, and then take three steps and be inside it. Some people say the land keeps a different measure for each traveler. Others say the flats are simply larger on the inside than the outside, which is not a metaphor here.

Direction is unreliable. The seeps run uphill for stretches, then remember themselves and run down. A pool will hold its surface tilted at an angle against gravity, level with nothing, for as long as you look at it and no longer.

Echoes come before their sounds. You step, and the echo of your step returns from the dark, and then a moment later you hear the step itself, as if the world were rehearsing you.

The dead machines run backward sometimes. A turbine slows, stops, and begins to turn the other way, and every pipe in the hall runs cold, and the water in the ravines runs back to the seeps that gave it up. It lasts about as long as one long breath and then resumes.

Objects are sometimes there and sometimes are not. A rail spur you walked yesterday ends in mud today. A boiler shed you passed twice is now a shelf of bare basalt. Yet when you put your hand on the shelf you feel rivets under the rock, and hear, faintly, a fire.

Counting fails. You cannot count the crawlers on a terrace twice and get the same number. You cannot count your own steps. Numbers here are slightly loose, like a fastener that keeps working itself free.

The animals seem to know all of this and take no notice. They have adapted to a world without stable geometry. They sense you by heat and by ticking, which are not distorted, and they go on eating and coiling and waiting.

## Time

Everything changes slowly. The water rises over days and falls over days, a tide with no moon. The mud terraces crack a little further each week. The felt spreads by inches over the iron. A stalk grows a hand's width in a season, and when one dies it drops its black length into the shallows and is eaten within hours by the crawlers, who tick softly over it. Nothing in the sump hurries, and if you stay long enough you stop hurrying too. Your pulse slows toward the knocking in the pipes. You begin to feel the ground's warmth as company, and the dark as a texture rather than an absence, something you are wrapped in, wet and close and tolerant.

Far out on the flats, a turbine turns once in a minute, and you cannot tell whether it is turning forward.
