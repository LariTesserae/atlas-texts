# The Held Breath

The mountain split open here not long ago, perhaps a few thousand years, which for a mountain is yesterday. The fissure is a mile long and a few hundred paces across at its widest, with walls of dark, glassy stone that still have their edges. Nothing has rounded them. Where slabs sheared away, the faces are flat and bright as broken slate, and the scree beneath is angular and unsettled, each piece still remembering the shape of the place it left. The whole cleft sits high, on a shoulder of the range where the air is thin and cool enough that breath shows in the morning.

It is a green place, which the rock does not seem to expect.

## Water

There is almost no rain. The water comes as fog. Warm air from the lowlands climbs the fissure at night and meets the cold walls, and the stone sweats. By dawn every ledge drips. Threads of condensation gather in the vertical seams, braid together, and run down into a chain of small black pools along the floor. None is bigger than a threshing floor. Between them the ground is damp rather than wet, a sponge of moss and root-mat that gives underfoot and releases a cold, mineral smell.

Nearly all the water in the cleft passes through a living body before it reaches the ground. Moss holds it, then fern, then the fat leaves of the shelf-plants. Then the animals drink from the leaves, and the soil takes what remains.

## Growth

The vegetation is lush in a vertical way. The floor is thick with tree ferns, their trunks furred in rust-colored fiber, and with heavy, dark-leaved shrubs that grow in slow, deliberate spirals. Moss climbs the walls in cushions as deep as a forearm, green so dark it is nearly blue. In the dimness it seems to hold its own faint light. Pale bracket growths step up the cliff like stairs. Vines drop hundreds of feet from the rim in single unbranched lines, each one tipped with a small closed flower that opens only when touched by fog.

Because the sky is a narrow strip far above, the light is dim and diffuse. It arrives green-gray, filtered through leaves, and lies in slow columns where the canopy opens. Some plants have given up on the sun. They glow instead, a low bruise-colored luminescence along the veins of their leaves, brightest just before dawn.

## The Quiet

This is the strangest fact of the place, and everything else bends around it: the cleft makes almost no sound.

The animals are innumerable, and they are silent. The fissure is a resonant chamber. A cry in one part of it returns from three walls at once, smeared and enlarged, and tells every predator in the mile exactly where the crier is. Over a long time the fauna lost their voices. There are no birdcalls, no insect drone, no frog chorus. What you hear is small: fog dripping from leaf to leaf, the soft rasp of a great many legs on moss, the pat of wings, and now and then the dry click of scree settling as the mountain continues to adjust. Your own footsteps sound like an accusation.

The animals talk in other ways. They use scent, flashes of light along the flanks, and pulses passed through the root-mat, which carries a feeling of stepping and settling to any creature with a sensitive foot. In the pale hour before sunrise the whole floor ripples with these signals. Rings of faint light spread from one animal to another and fade.

## Fauna

The fauna is overwhelming. Wherever you look there is something alive, and where you don't look there are more.

Moths the size of a spread hand hang on the cliff faces in overlapping sheets, wings patterned like lichen. When a shadow crosses them they lift all at once and fall again, and the movement passes along the wall like a shudder through hide. Beneath them, pale-shelled snails the size of loaves graze the moss in slow, massed fronts. Beetles glint like wet coal. Long, many-legged things, part millipede and part something older, wade through the leaf litter in processions. Flat lizards with translucent throats sit motionless on warm stones and pulse light at one another.

Larger animals move through the ferns without a sound. There are shaggy, low-slung grazers with padded feet and enormous eyes, herds of them thin and spread out among the trunks. Above them, tall, gray, long-necked birds nest in tiers on the ledges. Their nests are woven of fern fiber and hair, and the birds stand over them so still they look carved. Predators are here too. Pale snakes with heat-sensing pits in their faces, cats with fur that smears into the shadows, and a thing like a huge spider that hangs curtains of wet silk between the tree trunks. They are silent as well, so a kill is a brief disturbance in the leaves and then nothing.

The animals crowd every crevice, yet the space between them stays open. Grazers keep their distance in the ferns. Nests stand a wingspan apart. The thousands of creatures are spread like beads on a very wide net.

## The Settlement

People live here, in a town that is small but old, its layers stacked like sediment.

It has no center. It is a scatter of dwellings and workplaces strung along the floor of the cleft, each one at least a stone's throw from the next, with long green gaps between. The oldest buildings are the lowest: round huts of fitted scree, chinked with clay and roofed with fern trunks split lengthwise. On top of those, later builders added wooden storeys lashed with cord, then hide walls stretched on bone frames, then hanging platforms slung from the cliff by braided vine. In places the layers reach thirty feet from the ground, and the whole structure leans slightly with the shifting of the earth.

Their tools are simple. There are flaked glass-stone blades, bone needles, stone axes hafted with sinew, and a little soft copper beaten cold into bowls and pins. They make cord from fern fiber and rope from vine. The craftwork is elegant, and it favors things that do not clatter. Doors hang on leather hinges. Floors are layered with moss mats. Every tool has a padded rest, and every hammer is wrapped in hide.

The people have lost their voices to the place, or chosen to. Speech happens in low breath-whispers, and most talk is done with the hands, or with knotted cords worn at the wrist and read by touch. A message travels by a length of colored fiber passed from person to person, each adding a knot. Children learn quiet before they learn to walk. A cry is the rarest and most serious thing a person can do. It is kept for one purpose, to call the whole town to the rim when the mountain begins to shake.

## Motion

The place never stops moving, though it moves quietly. Each dusk the moth sheets lift off the walls and climb in a slow spiral into the strip of sky, and the predators follow them upward along the cliff. Each night the fog rises. Each morning the drip begins, and the pools fill, and the grazers come down to drink in single file. The spiders rebuild their nets. The snails advance a hand's width. The glowing plants dim as the sun reaches the floor.

And the rock is still changing. A ledge may give way, a slab slide, a new seam open and start to weep water, and by the next season moss has already found it. The townspeople repair, shift, and rebuild. The mountain is only beginning to be a place, and everything in the cleft, plant, animal and person, is learning to live inside its held breath, quietly, so as not to wake what is still settling.
