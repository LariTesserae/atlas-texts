# The Throat of Ossary Col

You cannot see it from anywhere. The ridge at this height is a blade of grey-violet rock, scoured smooth by ten thousand years of wind, with nothing on it but lichen-crust and the thin glitter of frost that never quite melts. Then a seam opens in the rock, a vertical slot barely a body wide. You turn sideways and press in. Behind the seam is a room the size of a cathedral nave, roofed by the sky.

It is a pocket, perhaps sixty paces long and twenty across, a fold in the mountain that the erosion finished and abandoned. The walls are fluted like the inside of a shell. They curve inward as they rise, so that the sky above is a long ragged lens of dim, bruise-coloured light, and the air is thin, cold, and sharp with a smell of hot pennies and pine tar.

## Everything is full

There is no bare ground. The floor, the walls, and the ledges are all occupied, layer over layer, with a density you feel in your chest. Plants grow on plants. Roots braid through the bodies of older roots. Fronds overlap like roof slates.

The plants are dark green shading to black-violet, with undersides the color of rust. The tall ones are **tuning-ferns**, rising to the height of a person, with stems stiff as wire and leaves cut into long comb-teeth of different lengths. No two teeth on a frond are the same, and no two fronds are cut differently from their neighbors. Each is a set of slots and tines that fits a note, so the whole thicket is a tuned instrument. Beneath them lie **plate-moss**, springy cushions the thickness of a mattress, and **wick-vine**, a wiry rope of a plant that climbs every surface and hangs in curtains from the overhangs. Its leaves are coin-sized and glossy, and each carries a bead of dark amber fluid on its tip.

That amber is the trick of the place. There is no water here and never has been. The air is desert-dry, the rock is dry, and the frost on the ridge is a thin oily rime that is not ice. These plants run on a different chemistry. The mountain seeps a heavy, slow hydrocarbon from cracks deep in the pocket's floor, a black tar that wells up cold and thick as honey. The roots drink it. Their sap is a pale oil, and their leaves sweat it out as beads that catch the light like tiny lamps. The vegetation grows lush and rank on what should poison it, and does so without any hurry.

## The sound

It is loud, and always the same.

The slot in the ridge is a throat, and the wind of the high col is forced through it. That wind is steady, a river of cold air that has blown from the same quarter at nearly the same speed since before the plants grew. It enters the pocket, and the curved walls turn it into a standing wave. The result is a deep chord: a low organ tone you feel in the sternum, a fifth above it, and over that a shimmering whistle from the tuning-ferns, whose teeth ring in sympathy. It is not painful, but you cannot speak over it. You learn to touch shoulders, gesture, and wait.

Nothing in the sound changes. It does not rise with gusts or fall in lulls. Some balance of pressure and temperature between the pocket and the open col holds the wind at one rate, and the chord has held for as long as anything has been here.

## The machines

Someone came here once, in an age of engines, and made use of this.

At the pocket's far end, half swallowed by moss, stands a **pump-house** of riveted iron plate, squat and square, with a corrugated roof gone the same rust-violet as the fern undersides. Inside is a single great flywheel the height of a doorway, still turning. The wind-driven turbine bolted to its outer wall is a drum of curved vanes, fed by a funnel of sheet metal aimed at the slot. It turns at exactly one speed. The flywheel hums against the chord, and the two have long since settled into a slow beat, a pulse about once every eleven seconds, felt more than heard.

Pipes leave the pump-house, heavy cast sections flanged and bolted, and run along the floor in a crooked line. Moss has grown around them and over them, and the wick-vines use them as trellises. They lead to a **tar-well**: a stone-lipped shaft in the floor where the seep collects, its surface skinned over with a dull sheen. The pump draws from it in long slow strokes. Where the oil goes, nobody knows. The pipe climbs the far wall through a bored hole in the rock and vanishes, presumably to a cistern or a refinery on the other side of the ridge, long since gone or perhaps still working.

There are other traces, scattered and faint:

- A **ladder** of forged rungs set into the wall, leading up to a ledge and a small iron door sealed with a wheel-lock. The paint has flaked to bare brown metal.
- A **brass gauge** on a post, its needle resting at a value it has held for longer than anyone has kept records. Wick-vine has grown through the glass.
- A **stack of ingots or casks**, so overgrown they read as a hummock of moss with sharp corners.
- A **hand-lettered plate** riveted beside the pump-house door. The letters are worn to ghosts, and the moss has been trimmed back from it, the only such trimming in the pocket. The plants have left it clear on their own, or something did.

Nothing here has been repaired, and nothing has failed. The dry cold preserves iron better than any workshop could. Rust has formed a stable violet skin and gone no further. The machine has not worn out because the load never changes, the speed never changes, and the temperature never swings.

## Light

The sky above is a narrow band, and the col sits high enough that the daylight coming through is thin and blue-white, with the sun rarely in the slot. Below, the light is dim and dusky. The amber droplets on the wick-vine take what comes and hold it, so the walls glow faintly in rows of tiny warm points, like a city seen from a great height. The iron and the ferns are dark shapes against this, and everything has the color of old lamplit rooms.

## The rules of the place

Things here are done by equilibrium.

- **Nothing moves except the wind-driven parts.** The fern-teeth tremble, the flywheel turns, the wick-vine's droplets sway a hair's breadth. Otherwise the pocket is still, and no season disturbs it.
- **There are no animals.** No insects hum in the moss, no small hungry things in the tar. The ferns reproduce by loosing spores into the standing wave. The sound gathers the spores into ridges of dust along the nodes of the chord, where they settle and sprout, which is why the thickets stand in bands, spaced evenly along the pocket, each a half-wavelength from the last.
- **The plants are shaped by the note.** Cut a frond and count the teeth, and you can measure the chord. The growth has been tuning itself to the place for so long that the pocket and the plants are one instrument, and the machine turns inside it.
- **It has been like this before.** The mountain's erosion is finished here. The flutes of the walls are smooth, their edges rounded, with no fresh breaks. Whatever was going to fall has fallen, and what remains has been standing a long time.

If you stay, the chord fills your skull, your breath slows to the eleven-second pulse without your meaning it to, and the amber points on the walls seem to be keeping time. It occurs to you that you have not seen anything change since you came in, and that this may be the point of the place: someone found the one room on the mountain where nothing ever has to end, and built a machine to sit inside it and turn.
