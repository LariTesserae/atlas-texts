# The Chime Basin

At a little under five thousand meters, the mountain has a bowl in its top. It is about two kilometers across, ringed by worn gray shoulders of rock that look like the knuckles of a fist resting on the sky. The rim is old. Every edge has been rounded and every scree slope has settled to its resting angle, and nothing up there is in any hurry to fall. The inside of the bowl is another matter.

## The Noise

Sound is the first fact and remains the last. It doesn't come from a place. It stands in the basin like a fluid, a roar with a grain to it, and beneath the roar are thousands of distinct tones: bell-strikes, sheet-metal shudders, a descending whistle that never lands, and a bass pulse felt in the sternum and the fillings of the teeth. Visitors stop trying to speak. Sentences are exchanged by touch and by the writing of shapes in the dust.

The sound is not chaotic. It is *structured*, and the ground has learned it. Fine glassy grit, a pale gray-violet sand of broken quartz and something older, covers the floor between the plants. The standing waves push it into ridges, rings, and lattices, so the whole basin floor is a slowly rewriting diagram. Where the pressure nodes fall, the grit gathers into knife-edged walls a hand high. Where the antinodes fall, it is scoured down to bare bedrock, dark and polished as a skillet. Every few minutes a new pulse crosses the basin and the pattern *breaks*. Walls slump, rings collapse inward with a hiss lost in the din, and a different geometry crystallizes from the wreckage. The floor is never the same twice, yet its repertoire is limited. The same seven or eight figures return in changing order, like the settled habits of a very old storm.

## The Plants

There is no water here. Air at this height is bone dry and cold enough to make the nostrils ache, and breath does not fog. But the basin is lush, deeply and improbably so, with a life that has found another way to drink.

The plants are called **reeds** by the few who work here, though they are not reeds. They rise from the grit at wide intervals, sometimes thirty meters apart, so that the basin is mostly air. Each stands alone: a trunk of fused, ribbed filaments as thick as a cistern, rising six or eight meters, then unfurling into a crown of enormous fronds. The fronds are lacquered green-black on top and copper-bronze beneath. They are thin as foil and stiff as slate, and they *ring*. Each one is shaped to a pitch. When the roaring pressure passes, whole crowns shiver into sympathetic tone, and the plant converts vibration to sugar-analogue in a chemistry based on a viscous, colorless, non-aqueous solvent that circulates in its trunks. In cutaways it looks like slow silver oil. It never freezes at these temperatures and it never boils away.

Between the great crowns, life is denser but lower: mats of wiry moss the color of verdigris, flowering cushions whose blooms are hard as porcelain and open and shut in rhythm with the bass pulse, and threadlike vines that lie across the grit in perfect spirals, avoiding the nodal walls. Everything here grows in the shapes the sound allows. The emptiness is deliberate: each plant claims a great hollow of air around itself, a territory measured in wavelengths.

## The Animals

Fauna is abundant, though it takes some time to see it, because most of it is airborne and most of it is the same color as the sound.

**Tuners** are the commonest: flocks of thousands, each bird-sized animal a hollow lattice of bone with a translucent, glassine wing. They ride the pressure fronts like petrels riding swells, and the flocks flex and bloom in great slow arcs through the empty volume of the basin. Their bodies are resonators; each contributes one thin high note to the whole. When a flock passes overhead the pitch of the roar bends slightly around it.

**Grit-walkers** are low, many-legged grazers the size of dogs, armored in overlapping plates. They move along the nodal walls, where the sand is quiet, and browse the moss. They are the only creatures that move in straight lines. When a pattern collapses they freeze, all at once, as if the sound had switched them off, and then walk to the new quiet.

**The Long Ones** are rarer. They are ribbon-bodied things, ten meters or more, that lie coiled around the trunks of the great reeds and appear to do nothing. But the plants they coil around are the healthiest in the basin. It's thought they tune them, tightening and loosening in slow spasms, though no one has proven it.

Nothing here is silent. Even the predators, sleek dark things that hunt the Tuners from the crags, announce themselves with a deep two-note call. It hardly matters, since prey cannot hear it over the din. They hunt by watching the flock's shape change.

## The Made Things

The basin is a small place, but it was not left alone. Scattered across it, far apart, are the structures of whoever made the noise.

At the rim stand nine **spires**: slender, fluted, the color of dull pewter, each taller than the tallest reed. They are not worn like the rocks. Their surfaces have the look of something that repairs itself, and the grit that blows against them does not scratch them, only slides off in a fine bright stream. They hum at frequencies that beat against one another, and it's from these beats that the basin's slow patterns arise. Some are cracked and some lean. One has fallen and lies across the scree, and its interior is not hollow, not solid, but a dense, fine-grained lattice like packed frozen smoke. Its ends are warm to the touch.

Halfway down the northern slope there is a **shelter**, a low, round-shouldered structure of the same gray material, sunk half into the slope. Its door is an open oval, and inside it is quiet, truly quiet, and the change is so abrupt it feels like being struck. Whatever the engineering is, it does not simply block sound. It seems to *fold* it, to hand it around the room like a held breath. Inside, the dust on the floor lies perfectly flat. Things left there by travelers are always found again: a cup, a coil of cord, a wind-up light. Nothing has ever been taken. Nothing has ever moved.

And at the basin's exact center, on a slight rise where the grit will not settle, stands a single **stem**. It is neither plant nor spire: a thin, pale column of the pewter material, six meters tall, ending in a small dark opening like a mouth. The sound of the basin is loudest here, and the source is not obvious. Some say the stem is the source; others say it is the drain, where the sound is taken in and returned to the spires. Standing beside it, the roar becomes almost visible: the air shimmers, the grit dances in a tight ring, and a light hair-lifting static charge climbs the skin.

## Weather and Light

The light is ordinary, a hard clear mountain daylight in a sky of deep blue. Shadows are crisp. Its ordinariness is the strangest thing about the place, because everything else is so tumultuous. The sun crosses the rim, the plants catch it and throw bronze light onto the sand, and the flocks flash like scattered coins.

There is no rain, and no snow. The weather is the sound: fronts of pressure roll from the spires through the center and back, and the quality of the day depends on their rhythm. In a "long" hour the patterns hold and the animals graze in calm. In a "hard" hour, which comes without warning, the whole basin shudders, walls of grit rise and fall, the flocks scatter in shrieking spirals, and the reeds thrash their foil crowns until they seem to be applauding a storm.

Then it settles, and the basin resumes its long and deafening argument with itself. It has been having that argument for longer than the rim has been rounded, and it seems in no danger of losing.
