# The Warm Ledger

Start with the ground, because the ground is still being written.

It is basalt, and it is young. Across the lowland the rock still shows how it was poured. Ropes of pahoehoe lie coiled in the direction the lava slid. Toes of stone have frozen mid-advance. Blisters the size of cottages have collapsed into shallow bowls with glassy floors. Where the flows are only decades old, the surface is black with a blue-violet sheen and cracks underfoot like a dropped plate. Where they are a few centuries old, they have gone the brown of strong tea, softened by a skin of lichen, then moss, then a thin dark soil that is mostly the dead bodies of things that lived here first. There are no river valleys. Nothing has had time to cut one. The land is a vast, faintly tilted apron of rock, a few hundred feet above a sea you cannot see, and it runs in every direction farther than any animal in it will ever walk.

The rock is warm. Not hot, except near the fissures, but warm like a flank. Put a palm on a shaded stone at midnight and it gives back the day and something else beneath the day. Below, the magma is close and restless. The crust is thin and full of seams, and along the seams the world breathes out.

## Where the water comes from

There are almost no streams and no lakes, and rain is rare and brief. What water there is arrives as breath. Steam rises from thousands of vents, some a single thread from a crack in the rock, some broad shimmering sheets over a mile of fractured ground. It cools as it climbs and hangs in low, slow banks of mist that drift downslope in the mornings and pool in the hollows. Everything alive here has arranged itself around that mist.

The plants drink fog. Tree ferns with trunks as thick as barrels hold their crowns in wide, cupped rosettes, and every frond is grooved so that droplets run to the center and down into a reservoir where the stalks join. Those reservoirs are small worlds: tadpole-like larvae, pale beetles, tiny leeches, fungi. Shrubs carry leaves furred with hooked hairs that comb the air. Vines hang in long curtains of wet-beaded cord. Moss lies thick as a mattress on every north-facing surface, and it drips all day, one sound at a time, into the leaf litter below.

The vegetation is lush and it is layered. Tree ferns and stubby, thick-barked cycads make the canopy, and beneath them are broad-leaved understory plants in every green from lime to near-black, epiphytes clotted in the forks of branches, and a floor of liverwort and clubmoss so dense that fallen fruit sinks into it and vanishes. In the sunnier gaps, where the flow is still too young for trees, there are mats of succulent mat-plants with pink-tipped leaves, and grasses that root in nothing but cracks.

Nothing here is strange. It is a hot, damp, crowded, green place where the wetness happens to come from beneath and above rather than from the sides.

## The cities

Between the trees, and often in them, are the settlements. No one made them with hands or tools. They are the work of insects, birds, and small burrowing mammals, and they are enormous.

The largest are the **spire-cities** of the termite-like builders, which the rest of the fauna treats as geography. A single colony's mound rises twenty, thirty feet from the black rock, a fluted tower of chewed wood, clay, and saliva, buttressed at the base and pierced with vents. The mounds aren't isolated. They stand in ranks and clusters, sometimes a hundred to the square mile, connected by covered roads, sunken lanes an inch deep and a foot wide, roofed over with pressed earth, which run for miles between one mound and the next. Along the roads the traffic never stops: foragers hauling leaf pieces bigger than their bodies, soldiers spaced at intervals with their heads facing outward, couriers moving faster than the rest in the center of the lane.

The mounds are warm inside, fed by heat from the ground. The builders sited them over the seams deliberately, or as deliberately as anything without a plan can. In the deep chambers, fungus gardens grow in combed, spongy tiers, and the smell of them, damp and sweetish, leaks from the vents and drifts along the ground in the mornings.

The trees have cities as well. In the crowns of the great cycads, **weaver colonies** have knitted communal nests out of frond fiber, each a thatched dome the size of a small room, hung with hundreds of entrance holes like the windows of an apartment block. A single nest may hold three or four hundred birds, small olive and yellow things, and the nests overlap and join until whole ridgelines of forest wear a continuous roof. Beneath a nest, the ground is white with droppings and seed husks, and grows the brightest, most aggressive green in the forest.

In the gaps where the soil is thin, the ground is **warrened**. Small furred burrowers, something like a cross between a vole and a hyrax, have tunneled the basalt's soft ash layers into a honeycomb, and their towns show on the surface as low domes of packed earth, each with dozens of entrances worn smooth and dark by traffic. They graze the grass to a lawn around their doors. Above them, on posts of dead wood, their sentries sit upright and still.

None of this space is empty. That is the defining fact of the place. Wherever you look, something has already claimed the spot. The gap between mounds is a road or a hunting ground. The bark of a tree is a wall for spider-cities, whole sheets of silk pulled taut across trunks and filled with thousands of tiny weavers sharing a web the size of a barn door. The underside of a fern frond is a nursery. The hollow of a stump is a household. A traveler has to pick a path through the settlements, across bridges of roots, along the roads, around the guarded doors.

## The animals

The fauna is abundant and, at the small end, overwhelming. Beetles walk in the litter in numbers that turn the ground into a slow current. Ants run lines up every trunk. Moths as big as hands sleep on the undersides of leaves by day, gray and lichen-patterned, and lift off at dusk. There are tree frogs that never need standing water, because they breed in the fern reservoirs and cling to the wet fronds, translucent green with gold eyes. There are lizards, quick and heat-loving, that bask on the warm basalt in rows, tilting their throats to the sun.

The larger animals live off the small ones and off each other. Long-legged birds stalk the mound roads, snapping up the couriers. A low-slung, armored digger, something like an armadillo with a longer tongue, ranges from mound to mound, breaking into the outer walls, eating for a few minutes, and moving on before the soldiers mass. Pale snakes hunt the warrens. Hawks and owls cut across the gaps between the trees. Small carnivores, mongoose-sized and banded, work the ground in family groups, and the burrowers' sentries whistle when they come.

The world is a food web at full occupancy. Every niche is filled and defended. Predation is constant, but rarely dramatic. It is mostly quiet, efficient, and unremarked.

## The day

Mornings begin in mist. Steam and fog lie down over the ground until you can see perhaps forty feet, and the whole forest is beaded and dripping. The light is soft and pearl-colored. Sound carries poorly. The first birds are a thin, tentative sequence of notes, and then the weaver colonies start up a low, continuous chatter like far-off rain on leaves.

By mid-morning the sun burns through. Steam still rises from the vents but in distinct columns now, leaning downwind, each one lit gold at its edges. The air is warm and thick, the kind of warmth that sits on the skin without pressing. Lizards come out. The mound roads fill.

The middle of the day is the quietest. The heat is highest, the light is flat and clear, and most of the animals withdraw into shade and stone. What remains is the low sound of the forest itself: the tick of cooling and warming rock, the small settling of leaves, the drip from moss, the far, dry rasp of one insect somewhere in the canopy. The silence isn't empty. It is the sound of a very large number of things, all busy, all making very little noise.

In the afternoon the steam gathers. Columns merge and spread into a flat gray ceiling perhaps a thousand feet up, and beneath it the air darkens and feels heavier. Sometimes rain comes, a short, warm downpour that lasts twenty minutes and hammers the fern crowns and fills every reservoir to overflowing. Then it stops. The ceiling thins and tears. The whole forest steams again, and the light, coming in low through the wet air, turns amber.

Evening is the busiest hour. Moths lift. Bats, or things like bats, pour out of the spire-cities' upper vents in long, curving streams. The burrowers come up and graze in the last light. Frogs begin, not loudly, a scatter of soft clicks and low bubbling notes in the fern crowns. The rock gives back its heat in slow waves you can feel against your shins.

At night the forest is dark and warm and never fully still. Glowing fungus outlines a few of the mound roads in pale green, and here and there the vents show a dull orange at their throats where the rock beneath is close to molten.

## What is still being made

The geology hasn't finished. Every few years a fissure opens somewhere and a new flow runs out, slow as cooling honey, a wall of orange-black rubble advancing at a walking pace. It buries roads and mounds and warrens and the roots of trees, and the forest waits at its edges. Within days the first colonists arrive, spores and windborne mat-plant seed, and small pale insects that live on nothing but the dead. Within a decade there is moss. Within a lifetime there is soil, then ferns, then the first builders, sending a road out across the cooled black rock to test whether it will hold.

The spire-cities on the edge of a fresh flow are sometimes doomed, and sometimes the flow parts around them and leaves them standing on an island of clay. The termite-builders seem to treat both outcomes the same way. They begin again, always, in the nearest warm place, and the road advances.

There is no erosion to speak of yet. No canyon, no scree, no delta. The land has not been worn into any shape by time. It has only been laid down, layer on layer, and lived on, layer on layer, by things that build on top of one another. What you see, standing anywhere in it, is a surface: black rock, green growth, brown towers, white mist, and life on every inch, all of it warm, all of it quiet, all of it very slightly moving.
