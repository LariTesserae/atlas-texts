# The Cistern Works

Under the seabed, in a void the size of a cathedral nave laid on its side, there is a town. It sits inside a bubble of old volcanic rock that never collapsed and never drained. The sea found its way in through fissures long ago and stayed. The water is warm as a bath, faintly sweet with dissolved minerals, and it is never still.

## The Chamber

The ceiling is low for its width, a broad dome of dark basalt sagging in places into stalactites of mineral crust. These are not limestone but iron, copper, and sulfur, banded rust-orange, verdigris, and yolk-yellow. Water seeps along the roof and leaves these deposits as slow, dripping curtains. The floor is a shallow bowl of silt and slag, and the town fills the bowl.

You cannot see across it. The dim light gives out at about forty paces. Everything beyond is a brown-green haze lit from within by scattered lamps and the red breath of the vents.

## The Town

It is a settlement of maybe two hundred structures, packed close and stacked tall to save the precious floor. The buildings are riveted iron and cast brass, plated over with centuries of accretion. Barnacle-less, weedless, they wear instead a smooth, lumpy pelt of mineral scale, so that a pump house and a natural boulder differ only by their right angles. Staircases climb the outsides of tanks. Catwalks of grated iron cross overhead on tall trestles, and the water above them is dense with drifting particulate, so that walking them feels like walking through slow snow moving sideways.

Pipes are the streets. The largest are wide enough to crawl through, and they run everywhere: along the floor, up the walls, threaded through the buildings and out again. They carry superheated vent-water, cooled brine, and pressurized oil. Their joints are wrapped in wound rope that has petrified into something like stone. Some pipes are hot enough to shimmer, and the water around them rises in bending threads of visible distortion.

At the center stands the Engine Hall. It is not a building so much as a great compound machine, a cluster of pistons the width of barrels, flywheels half sunk into pits, and governors with brass balls spinning at the ends of iron arms. It is driven by the vent beneath it, a black-and-copper chimney that pulses. Every eleven minutes, more or less, the vent surges. The whole town feels it first in the floor, then in the water, then in the teeth.

## The Sound

The sound has no source, because it has no direction. Water carries it faster than air and without loss, so every hammer stroke, every valve slam, every cough of steam-in-brine arrives everywhere at once. It is not heard so much as inhabited. It comes through the chest and the jaw and the bones of the inner ear. Beneath the hammering is a deep continuous roar, the vent and the great flywheels, a tone so low it is closer to pressure than pitch. On top of it are shrieks of cavitation, where bubbles collapse against the propellers of the pumps with sharp, glassy cracks like ice breaking underfoot. Somewhere there are bells, big cast ones, ringing without ringers, driven by the surge, and their tones smear into the roar and reappear a moment later, changed.

Nothing here can be spoken. What talk there is happens by touch and tapping. Rails, pipes, and handholds are worn bright at the places where hands and knuckles have rested and rapped a code against the metal for generations. Vibration travels along a rail better than through open water. To tell someone something, you find a pipe, put your palm on it, and knock.

## The Turbulence

The town is never quiet, and it is never the same twice. The surge every eleven minutes throws sediment up in blooming clouds, and for a minute the lamps become smudges. Valves fail and open. Jets of hot water punch sideways from cracked seams, and the town's few workers learn to read the water by its color and rhythm the way a sailor reads a sky. Slag-avalanches slide from the bowl's edge. Whole flights of stairs are sometimes torn loose and drift, turning, until they snag on a trestle.

Because of this, the town is rebuilt constantly. New plates are bolted over old. Patches accumulate on patches. Nothing here is ever finished, only held.

## Light

Light is dim and comes from three sources. Ranks of oil-and-glass lamps, sealed in brass cages, burn a low amber at the corners and stair landings. The vents give a red glow, dull as a banked forge, that flares on each surge and paints the ceiling briefly the color of the inside of a mouth. And on the older walls there are bacterial films that glow a thin, cold blue-green, so faintly they can only be seen after standing still a long minute. The films are the nearest thing here to a garden. There are also pale mats of something between fungus and slime in the cooler corners, grey-white and slowly rippling, that the workers scrape off pipes and never quite eliminate.

## Life

Very little of it. The workers are few, perhaps a couple of dozen, in heavy suits of canvas and brass, with round glass faceplates fogged at the edges. They move slowly between stations, checking gauges, turning wheels, and leaning on rails to knock messages. Many stations are unattended, and the machines run on regardless, governed by their own spinning balls.

The fauna is rarer still. There are small white shrimp that cluster around the warm pipes, translucent as fingernails. There are eyeless fish, long and thin, that drift like scraps of ribbon and flinch, all together, at each surge. And sometimes, low in the silt, a large pale crab the size of a table moves between foundations, feeding on whatever settles. The workers leave it alone. They say it was here first.

## Unusual Things

The bells never ring the same twice, yet the workers can tell, by the tone and interval of a given bell, what is failing and where. Some claim the bells are not only ringing from the surge, that something in the geometry of the chamber makes the town sing and that the machines were only built to be tuned to it. Nobody has ever proven this. But when the vent surges, and every bell speaks at once, and the roar swells and the floor lifts, the sound for a moment resolves into something that is almost a chord, and every worker in the town, wherever they are, stops for a breath to feel it.

Then the surge passes, the hammering resumes, and the town goes on holding itself together, warm, dim, and loud, beneath the ocean floor.
