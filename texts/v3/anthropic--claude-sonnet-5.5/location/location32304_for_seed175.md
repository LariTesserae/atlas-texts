# The Sweating Basin

The basin sits on the shoulder of a high plateau, a bowl of green about four hundred paces across. Its rim is low and rounded, worn down over so many ages that there is no clear line where the slope ends and the bowl begins. You climb through thinning cloud forest and the ground levels, softens, and lets you in.

It is warm here, warmer than the altitude should allow, the way a hand-warmed stone is warm. The air is thick and faintly mineral. Light arrives whole and unhurried, filtered through a high, thin veil of mist that never quite becomes cloud, and it lays no hard shadows. Everything looks slightly upholstered in it.

## The Ground

The floor is a broad staircase of pale, cream-gray stone laid down by the water itself, terrace after terrace, each with a lip a few hand-widths high. Behind every lip is a shallow pool, and the pools are stacked down the basin like the steps of a very old, very patient stair. The lips are rounded and scalloped and finished as smooth as soap. Nothing about them is sharp. Erosion here finished its argument long ago and has settled into maintenance, so the stone is no longer being carved but only gently kept.

The pools are warm as a bath is warm. The water is clear, faintly green over the stone, and tastes of iron and something like cut grass. Where the terraces are dry, a foot of dark, springy soil has built up over the stone, and it gives underfoot with a slow wet sigh. The water table is so near the surface that any footprint fills. You leave a line of small bright ponds behind you, and by the time you turn to look, the nearest ones have already begun to close.

## The Weeping Stones

Scattered through the basin, far apart, are the stones. There are perhaps thirty, each the size of a seated person or a little larger, standing alone with wide green space between them. They are dark, close-grained, and finely pitted, and they are the only dark things in the place. They look as though they were set down, though nothing suggests anyone set them.

They weep. Water beads from their pores, gathers, and runs down their flanks in threads, so that each stone wears a glistening skin. Where the threads reach the base, they have cut small radiating channels in the soil, and each stone stands at the center of its own tiny delta of moss-lined runnels. If you put your palm to one, it is warmer than the air and very slightly, steadily *pulsing*, or you think it is, and you take your hand away and put it back and cannot decide.

The strangeness is in the timing. The stones do not weep evenly. Over the long day, a slow wave of wetness passes through the basin from stone to stone. One darkens and streams while its neighbors stand nearly dry, and hours later it is drying while another, some sixty paces off, begins to bead. If you sit long enough at a point where you can see many of them, the pattern is undeniable. It travels in a slow, wandering loop through the whole assembly, taking about a day and a half to return to where it started. The stones are passing something among themselves, or the basin is breathing through them one at a time.

## The Green

The vegetation is lush without being crowded, because the plants keep their distance from each other in the same way the stones do.

**Tree ferns** are the tallest presences, thick-trunked and shaggy, their crowns a dozen feet up and spread like held umbrellas. Each stands in its own clearing. Their trunks are wrapped in a felt of moss and filmy fern, and at the top the new fronds uncurl so slowly that the coil seems fixed. Come back after several days and it has opened by a hand's width.

**Old gnarled trees**, low and wide, with leathery leaves like pale green coins, grow at the terrace lips where the water spills. Their roots grip the travertine and have grown into it, so that bark and stone flow into each other and you cannot say where one stops. Their branches are hung with epiphytes: orchids without color, only cream and green, and bromeliad-like rosettes that hold small brimming cups of rainwater. The cups never overflow and never empty.

**The carpet** between all this is a low, continuous, springy mat of moss, liverwort, and tiny succulent-leaved plants, in every green from yellow-lime to near black. It is soft enough to sink into to the ankle. In it, at intervals, grow *lantern-heads*, a strange plant. Each is a single stalk as tall as a knee, topped with a translucent, water-filled globe of tissue about the size of a fist. Light passes through the globe and pools underneath as a small bright coin on the moss. As the day moves, the coins slide across the ground in slow arcs. There are hundreds of them, sparse and unhurried, and each casts its circle of concentrated, warm, gold-green light. The moss directly beneath is paler and thinner than the rest, as though bleached by long attention.

There is no fruit-eater in the basin and no pollinator, no wing or claw of any kind. The plants seem to have worked out something else. Spores and seed-fluff hang in the still air in slow drifts, and the air, being so still, carries them only a few paces. This is part of why the plants stand so far apart. Each one has, over ages, come to be exactly as far from the next as its seed can fall.

## The Quiet

The sound is very little. What there is comes in small, separate pieces, each distinct in the stillness:

- the tick and trickle of the weeping stones
- the soft, continuous lipping of water over the terrace edges, a sound like a large animal drinking, though there is no animal
- now and then a single fat drop from a fern frond into a pool, and the ring it makes, which seems to last too long
- a very low, almost inaudible hum that you feel in the teeth more than hear, and that rises a little when a stone begins to weep nearby

There is no wind and no birdsong. Voices, if you use them, sound flat and swallowed by the moss.

## The Trace of Hands

At one point on the western side, where a terrace pool is deeper and rimmed with darker stone, there is evidence that people have been here, though long ago and briefly. It is not much. Set into the lip of the pool are several stones worn into shallow bowls, grinding hollows, smooth and glassy from use, now brimming with clear water and floating with pale moss. Beside them, half swallowed by the root of one of the gnarled trees, lies a small, leaf-shaped blade of flaked stone, and threaded through a crack in the same root is a length of twisted fiber cord, black with age and stiff as wire. The tree has grown around both. It holds them the way a hand holds something it has decided to keep.

Nothing else was left. There are no walls, no hearth, no path. Whoever came here took what they needed, or gave something, and went back up and out over the rim. The basin absorbed the small disturbance the way it absorbs a footprint.

## What the Place Is Doing

Over long spans the basin changes, but at the rate of sleep. The pools rise a little through the wet months and sink through the dry, and the terraces creep almost imperceptibly downslope as the travertine adds a fraction of a hair's breadth to each lip every year. The tree ferns unfurl and drop their crowns and unfurl again. The circling wave of wetness goes on and on through the thirty stones, never faster, never stopping.

If you stay long enough, an odd feeling settles that the place is not empty so much as *occupied*, though by nothing you can name. The stones weep in turn, the lantern-heads track their small suns, and the water keeps rising through the rock and running down. The whole basin is in slow, warm, quiet motion, like something asleep and dreaming of rain.
