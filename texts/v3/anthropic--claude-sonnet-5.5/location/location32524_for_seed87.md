# The Seep Warren

Nothing here can be seen from outside. On the open seabed there is only a low shelf of gray mud, a few hand-widths of pale crust, and a seam where the crust has cracked and been sealed again. Beneath it is a room of water about the length of a rowboat and no taller than a kneeling person. The room is full of a city.

## The Room

The chamber is a blister in young rock. The stone is still deciding what it is. The ceiling is a slab of fresh basalt glass, black and unweathered, with edges so sharp they look freshly broken. The floor is a soft breccia of shards that have not yet been ground into sediment. Nothing has had time to round anything. Along the north wall a crack is widening by a hair's breadth each season, and you can see the bright unstained rock inside it, the color of a cut potato.

The water is very cold, just above freezing, and heavy with dissolved minerals. It enters through the floor in slow, shimmering threads, warmer and saltier than the water above, and it bends the light as it rises. Where the seep water meets the cold, it precipitates. Every surface is slowly being upholstered in white stone.

## The Architecture

The city is grown, not built, though the difference is thin here. Its foundation is the chimneys: hundreds of narrow spires of carbonate, some as thin as a finger, some as thick as a thigh, leaning and branching from the floor toward the ceiling. In places they have reached the ceiling and fused into colonnades. The spires are hollow, and the hollows are streets.

Over the chimneys, the residents have added their own work:

- **Tube-wrights**, pale worms as long as a forearm, secrete flexible sheaths of chitin and mineral. The sheaths bundle into thick, ribbed towers that sway when the seep pulses. Some are ancient and blackened at the base, with newer white tubes budded on top like storeys.
- **Silk-terracers**, small translucent crustaceans, spin mucus into sheets and stretch them between spires. They harden into membranes the color of dried honey. Their floors and awnings hold pockets of stiller water where eggs are kept and where the water is a degree or two warmer.
- **Shell-masons**, snail-like grazers, plaster the gaps with a paste of ground shell and mud. They work in slow lines, and their seams look like stitching.

Nothing was planned, yet everything has a street's logic. Avenues run along the seep threads because that is where the food is. Dead ends are nurseries. The densest quarter is around the main upwelling in the center of the floor, where towers press so close that their sheaths touch and the animals pass through one another's walls by tiny sanctioned apertures. Nothing is ever fully separate from anything else.

## Light

There is no sun and no lamp. The light is the animals' own, a dim milky blue-green that comes and goes in slow waves. Tube-wrights glow at their tips when disturbed. The silk-terracers carry a faint gold along their gills. A curtain of luminous filaments hangs from the ceiling, and when a shockwave of movement passes through it, whole districts brighten and dim in sequence, like a slow thought crossing a face.

Between the glows the shadows are deep, and the city is legible mostly as silhouette: the leaning spires, the draped membranes, the cloudy rise of the warm threads.

## Vegetation, Such As It Is

There is almost nothing green. In place of plants there are mats: felted, cream-colored films of chemosynthetic bacteria that coat the lower walls in the seep zone. They feed on what rises from the rock, and everything else feeds on them. A few ribbons of kelp have drifted in through the crack and lodged, brown and slowly disintegrating. To the residents they are luxuries, and each one is picked over by a crowd of grazers until only a lace remains. In one corner a single stubborn tuft of red algae grows in the slant of light from a fissure, and the whole city seems to know about it.

## Fauna

The density is the most striking thing. Every cubic hand of water holds something.

Amphipods drift in clouds, dozens abreast, moving like a single exhalation. Ribbon-worms coil through the tube-towers, patrolling. Tiny pale fish, blind and nearly clear, hover in the shelter of the ceiling colonnades, and you can count their hearts. Sea-spiders walk the spires on legs like bent wire. At the base of the tallest tower, a slow, thick-bodied animal, half slug and half starfish, sits in a hollow it has occupied for decades and lets the city's waste rain onto it.

Predation is constant but quiet. A ribbon-worm strikes a silk-terracer through the wall of its own awning. A blind fish ghosts up on a cloud of amphipods and takes three in one gulp, and the cloud shivers and closes over the gap. Eggs are stolen, towers are raided, and every raid leaves a scar that the shell-masons come to mend.

## Silence

The room is almost silent, in a way that feels like a rule and not an absence. Sound barely carries in the mineral-thick water, and few of the animals make any. What you would hear, if you had ears there, is tiny: the tick of a mason's radula on stone, a faint crackle as carbonate grows, and a dry, patient hiss where the seep threads pass through the narrowest tubes. Communication is by touch, pressure, and chemistry. A tower rocks, and the message passes from wall to wall. A cloud of scent goes up from a wounded animal, and a whole street empties.

## The Pulse

Every few hours the seep surges. Pressure from below builds, the threads thicken and brighten, and the entire city leans. Tube-wrights extend to full length, silk awnings belly upward, and the amphipod clouds rise and pour toward the ceiling in a great slow cascade. For a few minutes everything feeds. Then the pulse ebbs, and the towers retract and settle like a held breath let go.

Over years, the pulses build the city. Each one lays down a fine skin of mineral, and each skin buries a little more of the past. The oldest quarters are sealed under white stone, with the shapes of former residents still visible inside it like insects in amber. The city stands on its own ancestors.

## What Is Coming

The north crack is widening. Some season soon it will open enough to let in a real current, cold and fast, and the warm seep-water will be swept out. The chimneys will stop growing. Or the crack may close, and the room will seal, and the city will fill with its own chemistry until it slowly poisons itself. The residents cannot know this. The tube-wrights sway in the pulse, the terracers spin their gold sheets, and a blind fish hangs in a doorway and waits for the next cloud of prey.

The whole world is contained in a room no one has ever entered, under a crust no one has ever cracked. It is crowded, cold, and luminous, and it is building itself out of stone one layer at a time.
