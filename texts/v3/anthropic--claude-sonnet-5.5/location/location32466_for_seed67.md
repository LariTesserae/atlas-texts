# The Tally Flats

The land is a staircase so shallow you'd never call it one. Each terrace drops the height of a hand and runs level for a kilometer or three, then spills over a lip into the next. The terraces are shallow basins of pale cast stone, a fine-grained ceramic-concrete the color of unbaked bread. Warm water lies across them ankle-deep, perfectly still until the lip, where it goes over in a sheet as smooth as a pane of glass. The staircase runs down toward a horizon that never resolves into anything but more terraces, more sheen, more haze.

Nothing here is ground. Dig through the basin floor, if you could, and you would find pour after pour of the same stone, then armature, then older channels, then a floor of poured foundation that no one has seen the bottom of. The whole lowland was made, and it was made recently. Every edge is crisp. The lips of the weirs are sharp enough to read your thumbprint. Nothing has weathered, and nothing will, because the animals prevent it.

## The animals

They are everywhere, and they are the reason the edges stay sharp.

The commonest are the **combs**: pale, fan-shelled filter feeders that stand upright in the shallows in ranks, forty or fifty to a square meter. They lean slightly downstream like a field of wheat in a steady wind. Each is the length of a forearm, ribbed, translucent at the margins, and faintly warm to the touch. They comb the water for dissolved minerals and lay it down again at their bases as fresh, glassy cement. Where a basin floor is scuffed or a lip chipped, the combs crowd toward the wound and heal it in a season. They are the mason, the plaster, and the paint. A comb-bed looks like a tiled floor until you notice the tiles are breathing.

Between the combs move the **tenders**, low, many-legged grazers the size of loaves. They scrape the slick films of microbial mat that are the only living surface not an animal: rust-orange, green-black, or violet, laid over the stone in thin skins that smell of warm pennies. There are no plants, no root, no leaf, nothing that reaches up. The mats are as high as the vegetation gets. Tenders drag them off the walls in long ribbons, eat them, and leave the stone bare and bright behind them.

Above the waterline, the vertical faces of the sluices are furred with **beads**, sessile things like barnacles crowded so close they touch, a quilted gray skin that goes on for kilometers. When a wall is really dense with them, the surface has a soft, slightly greasy give, like the skin of a mushroom. Eel-long **threaders** pour through the culverts and pipe mouths in braids, hundreds abreast, unhurried, feeding on what the tenders miss. And on the gantries and the crane arms sit the **roosts**: long-necked, wet-feathered creatures that stand shoulder to shoulder along every rail like a second row of rivets. They hold still for hours, and they make no sound.

The density is hard to take in. Everywhere you look, some surface is alive, and the surface beneath that, and the one beneath that. Step into a channel and the water parts around a hundred bodies at once. Nothing is afraid of you. You are simply one more presence in a space that is entirely occupied.

## The machinery

The machines are old in design and young in surface: iron-gray and brass-colored, oil-black at the joints. Great **sluice-wheels** stand at the lip of every fourth terrace, turning at a constant slow rate driven by the falling water. They lift a portion back up through pipes as thick as houses to the head of the staircase. The water circulates forever, a closed loop. Overhead, rail-mounted **gantries** span the terraces on legs like bridge piers, carrying hoists, spray-arms, and sorting-combs. They travel their rails on a schedule so regular that they seem to be standing still. Conveyor belts, ribbed rubber gone soft with condensation, run from the sluice-houses to the edges of the terraces and stop against blank walls, and their loads are carried away by tenders and threaders long before they arrive.

Nothing is produced that anyone can point to. Or rather, everything is: the animals are the product, and the animals are the workers, and the machines exist to keep the water moving so that the animals may keep feeding, and so on around. The old logic is that a factory need not have an outside. The Tally Flats do not ship, and they do not consume. They keep.

## Weather and light

It is warm, the warmth of a held breath: 30 degrees or so, damp, without wind. Steam lifts from the warmer basins all day and gathers in a permanent, slow-turning canopy of cloud, ragged at the base and flat-topped, at perhaps five hundred meters. The sun comes through it as a bright white disk without heat, and the light is ordinary daylight, soft, shadowless enough that the gantries cast only faint gray outlines. Rain falls, but always at the same places, on the same terraces, at the same intervals, in fine warm curtains that drift a few hundred meters and then stop. The sky has a circulation of its own and it never varies. A visitor learns the pattern in a day: here it rains, there it does not, and it has done so since the last pour.

## Sound

The place is mostly quiet, and the quiet is textured. Water sheets over the weir-lips with a sound like tearing silk. Somewhere far down the staircase a sluice-wheel turns, and you feel it in your soles rather than hear it, a pulse a little slower than a resting heart. The combs make a faint, dry ticking as they close and open their fans. It sounds like rain on paper, and it comes from everywhere at once. The roosts say nothing. The tenders make a light scritching. The machines are almost soundless, since their moving parts are submerged or packed in grease-thick housings. Sometimes a single gantry hoist ticks over a joint, and the sound carries a full kilometer over the water before it dies.

## The rules of the place

Nothing changes, and the reason isn't stillness, because everything is moving. It is balance. The combs lay stone exactly as fast as the water dissolves it. The tenders eat the mat exactly as fast as it grows. The threaders, the beads, and the roosts keep their populations by the same arithmetic, and the wheels lift back precisely what the lips let fall. Every gradient has been used up. If you stood on one terrace for a year you would see nothing different, and if you stood there for a thousand you would see the same, except that you would have become part of the accounting, a body the tenders would learn to graze around.

Somewhere at the top of the staircase, they say, is the first terrace, and the first sluice-wheel, and whatever poured the first stone. The distance is enormous. The water reaches you already warm, already worked, having passed through every animal between there and here.
