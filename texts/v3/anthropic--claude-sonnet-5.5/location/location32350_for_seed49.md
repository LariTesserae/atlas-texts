# The Sump of Ossary Reach

From the air, if anything up there could hold still long enough to look, the land is a single brown-green-silver shine, flat to every horizon. It is the color of wet slate with light on it. No hill rises more than a few hundred meters, and most of the relief is built. The ground is a delta that stopped being a delta ten thousand years ago and became a city, and the city has since been drowned, silted, rebuilt, and drowned again so many times that no one can say where the land ends and the construction begins.

## The Flats

The outer margin is warm mud, the temperature of blood, laced with channels that braid and unbraid with the tide. Nothing green grows here. The only living surface is mat: layered films of slime, purple and rust and a bruised blue-black, stretched over the silt in sheets the size of counties. The mats hum faintly when the sun hits them. Under a hand, a mat is warm, faintly elastic, and slightly greasy, like a bathed animal's skin. Where the mats thicken they raise domes and ridges, and where the domes split, fungal cauliflowers push out, pale as bone and wet as tongues. They grow, spore, and slump in the space of a day.

The air is thick enough to drink. Fog forms on the flats every dawn and does not burn off so much as get pulled upward into the city, which breathes it in.

## The Wall of Structure

The city begins as a shoreline of towers, but the towers are not separate. They are ribs of one continuous mass, a stacked, cross-braced, terraced thing rising a few hundred meters and running left and right until it curves out of sight. The lowest courses are the oldest: dark, rounded, deeply weathered, their edges softened by ten thousand years of weeping water into forms like river cobbles the size of cathedrals. Higher up, the material changes. Gray-white ceramic gives way to something glassier and more translucent, and above that to newer, brighter, still-growing lattices that shimmer faintly, because they are not finished and may never be.

Everything weeps. Every wall carries a skin of moisture, and every overhang drips in long threaded strands that are gathered, channeled, and fed back into the system. The water is not incidental. The builders, who are long gone or perhaps have become the city, made a masonry that condenses water out of the air on purpose, a hydrophilic engineered stone that sweats and drains, sweats and drains. The result is a thousand-meter cliff of constant, delicate rain, falling from nowhere in particular.

## The Roar

The sound comes first, before sight, and it is the defining fact of the place. It is not noise in the ordinary sense. It is a mass, a pressure, a physical weather. Beneath it are the pumps, huge slow pistons of the drainage arteries, thudding at frequencies felt in the sternum. Above them is the rain on ten million resonant plates, a continuous hiss that rises and falls like surf. Over that come the animals: shrieks, clacks, whoops, and the long ratcheting calls of the tube-nesters. Above all of it, in a register that seems to come from the sky itself, is a tone.

The tone is the city's structural note. The upper levels do not stand by strength alone. They are held in shape by standing acoustic fields, coherent vibrations pumped through the lattice by machines that no one now fully understands, and that physics as taught can only partly describe. The sound does not merely accompany the building. It is the building's posture. When the tone wavers, the far upper terraces sag and flow like warm wax. The old word for that is *slump-fall*. The residents fear silence the way people elsewhere fear fire. A quiet block is a dying block.

So everyone contributes. Children beat resonant tubes at fixed hours. Traders sound their stalls with chimes tuned to the local lattice. The animals, unknowingly, have been bred or drawn to sing.

## Packed

There is no empty space. The word "open" has no useful referent. Every stair is a market. Every ledge holds a stall, a nest, a shrine, a still, a pump, or all of them stacked in strata. Bridges crowd the gaps between towers so thickly that the ground below reads as a dark ceiling of cables, awnings, and hanging cages. Corridors branch and merge and double back, and each surface is occupied: mollusk-farms along the drip lines, glow-culture racks, cloth and hide and sheet-glass strung across every span.

The air is a collage of smells: hot iron, wet ceramic, frying, fermented brine, animal musk, ozone from the discharge-vanes, and under it all the warm, sweet mud smell of the flats. The heat is steady and damp, about like the inside of a mouth. Everyone sweats and no one minds.

## Fauna

There are no plants, but the city is a zoo of the sessile and the scuttling. The food chain begins in the mats and the fungal fields and the algal slime that coats the drainage walls, and it runs upward through grazers, filterers, and predators of every size. Common things:

- **Tube-nesters**: gull-sized, hairless, pinkish gliders that cluster in the honeycombed vents and call in coordinated bursts. They are the primary voice of the upper city.
- **Mat-grazers**: slow, domed, many-legged things the size of dinner tables, who move across the lower walls in herds, leaving clean stripes.
- **Sump eels**: thick as a thigh, black, living in the flooded galleries and rising through the grates at dusk in glittering ropes.
- **Rag-flocks**: thousands of tiny wing-membraned insectivores that swirl above the markets in coordinated clouds, so dense they cast moving shadows.
- **Glass crabs**: translucent, hand-sized, clinging in millions to every drip surface, their shells filled with faint lights that flicker in response to sound.

The people, wherever they are from, live among these creatures as one lives among weather. Some are kept, some eaten, some ignored, and some are treated as part of the machinery. The glass crabs, in particular, are believed to carry information along the lattice, and their flicker-patterns are read like tea leaves.

## Turbulence

Nothing here holds still. The city produces its own weather from its heat, its condensers, its billions of animal breaths, and its rooftop vents. Storm cells form above the towers and then are eaten by the intake shafts, or break loose and travel over the flats in slow, purple-bellied columns. Rain here may fall sideways, or upward through the condensers, or stop mid-air in a shimmering curtain when the tone strikes it wrong. Lightning is common and is harvested.

Beyond weather, the structure itself churns. Wings of the lattice are grown, dissolved, and regrown by autonomous maintenance swarms, a fine gray dust that eats old stone and lays down new. A district may be a canyon on Monday and a gallery by Friday. Stairs migrate. Doors open where walls were. Residents mark their homes with sound-signatures, not addresses, because addresses do not last.

## What It Feels Like

To stand on a mid-level terrace at noon is to be inside a great chewing, humming, dripping animal. Light is ordinary, a flat gold pushed through mist. Underfoot the floor trembles. The air tastes of metal and mud. On every side surfaces shine with water, and creatures move over them in currents, and above, in the endless stacked distance, the towers hold their shape only because the whole roaring place agrees, moment by moment, to keep singing.
