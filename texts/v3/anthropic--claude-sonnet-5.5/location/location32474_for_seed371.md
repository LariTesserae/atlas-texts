# The Foundry Cleft

The mountain is at 5,000 meters. The cleft is a slot in its north shoulder about four hundred paces long and eighty wide, opened by a fissure eruption within living memory of the oldest moss. Its walls are still sharp. Scoria edges cut like broken crockery, columnar basalt stands with its corners intact, and no talus has yet softened the base of any cliff. The rock is young, black, and rust-red, and it hasn't had time to become anything else.

It is always night here, or something that behaves like it. A ceiling of cloud sits on the ridge and never lifts. Under it the only light is what the ground makes: a seam of dull orange along the cleft floor where a fissure still breathes heat, and the sullen cherry-red of a few vents that brighten and dim with a slow pulse like a bellows. Everything else is shape, wet black gloss, and the suggestion of green. Your eyes give up on color and start reading texture instead.

## The Engine

The place works like a plant, in the industrial sense. It has pressure, throughput, and shifts. Meltwater and cloud-drip percolate down through the shattered rock, meet the hot root of the fissure, and come back as steam. The steam has to go somewhere, and where it goes is the whole character of the cleft.

Jets scream from cracks in the floor, white columns that lean with the wind and vanish into the cloud roof. Fumaroles hammer in a low register, a thudding like a slow press stamping. Farther up, a geyser the size of a chimney stack fires every eleven or twelve minutes. First comes a gulping, then a shuddering in the rock underfoot, then a detonation of boiling water and gravel that rains back down as hot grit. Between eruptions the water hammers in hidden conduits, clank and shudder and long metallic groan, as if huge valves were opening and closing in the walls. The noise is continuous and overlapping, a roar with a ribbed texture. You shout to be heard by someone at arm's length. Under all of it, the ground trembles constantly, a fine hum you feel in your teeth.

There is one made thing here, and the mountain is slowly swallowing it: a length of iron wellhead pipe, left by some earlier attempt to tap the heat. It is flanged, bolted, and rusted the color of the scoria. It leans out of a mound of moss, and a thin steam still leaks from a seam in it with a flute-like whistle. Nobody has come back for it. Ferns grow from its flange bolts. It is the only straight line in the cleft.

## Change

Nothing stays put. The cleft is rearranged weekly, daily, sometimes between one breath and the next. A mudpot swells, skins over, and bursts, throwing gray clay onto the ferns. A vent that roared yesterday is silent today and a new one has opened three meters over, ringed with fresh yellow sulfur crystals like sugar. Tremors drop slabs from the walls with a crack and a long clatter. The floor is a shifting crust over hot voids, and the ground sometimes sags under a step, releasing a warm breath that smells of struck matches and wet stone. Streams change course as new cracks open. Where a channel was, there is now a steaming dry gully, and the water has gone to feed something further along.

The fissure's heat drifts across the cleft, so the microclimate moves. A patch that is bitterly cold and rimed with frost in the morning may be a warm fog bank by afternoon.

## What Grows

The air at this elevation ought to be barren and thin, but the cleft is a heat trap under a cloud lid, and it is thick with growth. It is dense and layered, plants crowding every ledge and crevice.

At the warm floor grow tree ferns with trunks thick as thighs and black with old fiber. Their crowns are so tightly overlapped that the space beneath is a dripping, lightless undercroft. Their fronds shiver constantly in the vibration and the steam-driven wind.

Among them are giant rosette plants, waist-high in column form, each crowned with a tuft of silver-felted leaves. The felt beads with condensation and sheds it in a steady drip. Their dead leaves hang in brown skirts down the stems, and rain never stops falling from them.

Woody shrubs with peeling red bark and small leathery leaves fill the middle layer, twisted into knots by wind and heat. Their bark curls off in paper sheets and collects in the mud.

Over everything lies moss. It forms deep sponge cushions on every horizontal surface, hangs in long wet beards from every branch, and climbs the basalt columns in bands. Where fumarole steam touches it, it is an intense, almost violent green, visible even in the dark as a paler shade against the black rock. Lichen in rust orange and mustard crusts the sulfur-splashed stones. Liverworts coat the seeps in a slick green film.

The vegetation is loud in its own way: the pattering of drips on leaves, the creak of stems, the rasp of dry frond against dry frond in the warm gusts.

## Water

Water is scarce in bulk but present everywhere in fragments. There is no lake and there are no real rivers. There are seeps, drips, thin steaming rills, condensation running off every leaf, and small hot pools rimmed with mineral crust in white, ochre, and rust. In the pools the water is opaque and pale, and it boils gently at the edges. Runoff from the geyser forms short-lived streams that vanish into the porous scoria within a few dozen meters. The air itself is wet, and the mist deposits itself on your sleeves, your hair, and the lenses of anything you carry.

## Rules

- Nothing in the cleft is alive except the plants. There are no birds, no insects, no small scurrying things. The altitude, the heat swings, and the sulfur keep them out, and the plants are pollinated by wind and spread by spores and gravity. The noise is all geological, mechanical, and vegetal, and there is no call or cry anywhere in it.
- The heat is the economy. Wherever it goes, the green follows within a season, and wherever it withdraws, the growth browns and rots into black peat, which then feeds the next flush elsewhere.
- Nothing lasts more than a few years in its current form. The cleft is a single event still in progress, and one day the fissure will seal, the noise will stop, and a cold quiet forest will stand in the dark on the ridge.

For now it hammers, hisses, and steams, and the ferns shake in their own wet weather, three thousand meters above where forests are supposed to end.
