# The Warm Throat

Above the last snowfield, at an altitude where the sky is nearly black at noon and breath is a chore, a slab of grey limestone the size of a barn leans against the mountain. Beneath it is a mouth. Stone that cold should not breathe warm, but this mouth does. The mountain is volcanic underneath, a long-dormant heat that has never quite finished dying. Air rises from the deep and leaves the cave at about the temperature of a held hand. Frost stops a body-length outside the entrance, and the line where it stops is as sharp as a drawn edge.

Inside is one chamber, perhaps sixty paces across and as tall as a cathedral nave. It has no wings or side passages, just a single vaulted room whose walls have been rounded by water over more time than anyone has counted. The limestone is scalloped like the inside of a shell. Each scallop is a palm's width, all facing the same direction, and together they record the direction of an ancient current that stopped flowing long ago. The stone is worn to a settled smoothness. No edges are left to lose, and nothing here is being carved anymore, only slowly furred, stained, and layered.

## The dark

The light is almost gone. A crack high in the vault, far above reach, lets in a thin grey blade at midday, and it slides across the wall so slowly that you notice it only by looking away and back. The rest is darkness of the thick, warm kind. Your eyes invent shapes in it. Then the shapes turn out to be real, because the darkness is inhabited and moves.

Some of the dimness is a faint glow. A pale fungus spreads in coin-sized patches on the damper walls, giving off a green so weak it seems to be the memory of a color. It is the only vegetation of any consequence. Near the entrance, where the grey blade of light falls, grow a few sad tufts of fern, some cushions of moss, and orange lichen crusted on the lip of the stone. Farther in, the plants give up. Life there runs on what falls from above.

## The ceiling

The ceiling is the main fact of the place. It is furred with bats, layer upon layer of small brown bodies, so dense that they hang against each other like wet felt. Where the stone should be visible there is only a slow rippling texture, a pelt that breathes. They are high-altitude bats, thick-coated and blunt-faced, with wings shorter than their cousins' from the lowlands. There may be a million of them. They shift, mutter, and groom. When the warm air pulses, the whole mass shivers in a wave that travels from one end of the vault to the other.

Between the bats, in the crevices, are swiftlets. They are dark, quick birds that nest on the ledges in gluey cups of their own spit. They clatter all day and half the night, each one navigating by clicks of its own. The cave never falls silent, only changes register.

## The sound

The sound is the second great fact. It is loud in the way surf is loud, a continuous pressure more than a series of noises. The bats give a high papery chittering that runs beneath hearing and crawls up the spine. The swiftlets add a dry rattle like a stick dragged along a fence. Under both is the drip, since this is a damp place. Water condenses on the cold upper rock and gathers into beads, which fall into the guano below. Each drop lands with a small soft *tock*, and there are thousands per minute. Inside the chamber it is always faintly raining, though no rain has fallen from the sky here for weeks. Water is a matter of moisture and patience. It never arrives as a flood. Some hollows in the floor hold shallow pools the color of tea, and these are rimmed in orange flowstone that grows a hair's width in a lifetime.

The cave has a breath, and this is the mildly strange thing about it. Every day at dawn, as the mountain warms and the pressure shifts, air pours out of the mouth in a long exhalation. The entrance sings then, a low tone somewhere near the note of a bowed cello. All the animals seem to know the moment before it happens. The chittering rises to a peak, then drops, and the colony goes quiet for a few heartbeats as if listening. At dusk the air reverses and the cave inhales, drawing in the cold smell of snow. Some of the bats use that draft to leave. They pour out into the black sky in a spiral column that unwinds for an hour, and their wingbeats, together, make a sound like a very large cloth being shaken.

## The floor

The floor is a slope of guano, heaped like a dune against the back wall and deeper than a person is tall. It is dark and springy, warm from its own slow decay, and it stinks of ammonia and sweet rot. It is also the busiest ground in the region. Beetles by the hundreds of thousands work through it. Pale cave crickets with legs longer than their bodies fling themselves away from any footstep. Mites, springtails, and pale centipedes make a moving skin over everything. Blind, translucent salamanders, the top predator of the floor, wait in the seeps at the edges and eat whatever the swarm leaves.

Everything touches everything. There is no empty patch of stone, no bare floor, no clean water. Even the walls are alive with a moving crust of insects, and every crack holds an eye or an antenna. Overwhelming is the only word for it, but the crowd is orderly. Each creature has its layer: bats and birds on the ceiling, crickets on the walls, beetles in the heap, salamanders in the wet. Nothing escapes the pattern for long.

## Old visitors

The cave is not entirely untouched, though almost. Someone has been coming here for a very long time, likely people from the lowland valleys, who climb for the guano and for the swiftlet nests. Their marks are small. Along one wall is a rope of braided grass, blackened and stiff, tied around a knob of rock and trailing up to a ledge. Below it a patch of stone has been rubbed shiny by hands. There is a smear of soot from resin torches, and a scatter of small bone hooks and a flaked stone scraper half swallowed by the guano. Nothing has been built. Whatever the visitors made, they carried away or let the cave take. Even the rope is being slowly digested by mold. Compared with the age of the walls, their comings and goings are one breath.

## What it is

The Warm Throat is a place where the rules are simple and slow. Heat rises from below, water condenses from above, and life falls between them. The colony grows a little each year and the guano deepens by a finger's width. The flowstone thickens, the stone smooths, and the mountain breathes out at dawn and in at dusk. Outside, the ice and wind scour bare rock under a black sky. Inside, in the dark and the noise, a million small bodies hang together, sleeping and muttering, warm in the cold.
