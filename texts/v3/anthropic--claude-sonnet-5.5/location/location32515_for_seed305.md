# The Bellows Plateau

Start with the altitude. The land has been shoved up into a table the size of a small continent, and the air on it is thin, cold, and so clear that the sun seems to press on the skin. The edges of the plateau are young and unrounded: fault scarps of black basalt and rust-red gabbro, ridgelines like broken plate, screes that still shift when nothing touches them. Nothing here has had time to soften. Water has cut only a few gorges, and those are narrow, vertical, and dripping.

The temperature hovers just above freezing at midday and drops below it each night. Everything is damp anyway. The plateau makes its own cloud. Warm exhalations rise from the ground, meet the cold air, and hang as a low shining fog that never quite becomes rain. It condenses on every surface as beads and then as rime. The light passes through this fog, scatters, and comes down from all directions at once. There are almost no shadows. The whole place glares softly, like the inside of a lit pearl.

## The green

It is lush in the way of cold places, dense and low and layered. Moss lies in cushions a meter deep, red, lime, and bruise-purple, and the cushions are so tightly packed that they hold a footprint for a year. Above them grow tussock-grasses with blades like green wire, tree-ferns that never pass four meters, and a waxy, mirror-leafed shrub whose leaves are angled to bounce sunlight down onto its own lower branches. Lichen crusts the bare rock in colors too saturated to look real: sulfur, cobalt, a metallic copper-green.

Nothing is spaced out. Moss grows on the ferns, on the shrubs, on the backs of animals that stand still long enough. Vines of a stiff, hairlike plant bind the tussocks into mats that a person could walk across without touching soil. Every gap has an occupant. Where a rock is bare, it is bare because something scrapes it clean every day.

## The engines

The strangeness is that the industry here is alive, and nobody built it.

The plateau's meltwater runs red with dissolved iron, and the fauna have made a trade of it. The largest animals are the **bellows-herds**. Each is a slab-sided, barrel-lunged grazer the size of a house, with a hide of overlapping mineral plates. They stand in ranks across the basins, tens of thousands of them, and breathe in slow, enormous cycles. A ventral pouch filters iron from the water they drink. A hot internal chamber, fed by fermenting moss, smelts it. The animals exhale steam and a fine metallic dust, and at intervals they shed slag in clean glassy pats. Those pats are the nearest thing to construction on the plateau, and the smaller animals build with them.

Around the herds work the **riveters**, dog-sized, many-legged things that gather the cooled iron and hammer it into the plates of the herd-beasts' hides, patching, thickening, and repairing. A herd in full repair sounds like a shipyard. Other creatures run rails: **draggers** haul heavy loads of ore in long lines along grooves they have worn into the moss. The grooves are polished by generations of use, so the plateau is scored with shining parallel tracks that run to the horizon and converge at smelting sinks, where hot springs meet iron seams and the water boils red.

There are pump-animals with hollow proboscises fixed to seeps and vents, throbbing at a fixed rate. There are spinners in the trees that draw iron-laced silk into cables, and flocks of thumb-sized flying things that act as couriers, carrying grains of flux from one colony to another in tight, humming clouds. Nothing is idle. Wherever you look, something is producing something at scale.

This counts as tech only if you squint. There are no factories, no walls, no roofs. The slag-glass mounds, the rail-grooves, and a few standing cairns of stacked ingots are the whole architecture. The animals do everything with bodies and inherited routine, and the whole apparatus runs like a machine that no one designed.

## The sound

It is loud everywhere, all the time. The bass is the bellows-herds: a breathing roar, a long drawn *hhhhhaaa* as tens of thousands inhale, then a shuddering release like a freight train of steam. Over it comes the ringing of the riveters, thousands of small hammers with slightly different pitches, so the air has a bright metallic shimmer of tone. The pump-animals thud. The draggers' plates clank in sync down the rail lines. Around dawn the whole plateau shifts into a resonant hum as the herds' breathing falls into phase, and the ground buzzes underfoot. You can feel it in your teeth. Then it drifts out of phase and dissolves into ordinary din.

Silence exists only inside the moss, if you bury your head in it. Even there you hear the ground ticking as iron cools.

## Rules of the world

- **The great phase.** The herds' breathing has a long rhythm, and on the scale of a decade the entire plateau slowly shifts from a "hot" state, with more smelting, more steam, thicker fog, and warmer nights, to a "cold" state, with clear air, hard frost, and quieter work. Nothing about it is sudden. It is a tide across years, and the moss fields change color with it.
- **No waste.** Slag becomes nest material, dust becomes soil, and dead herd-plates are pulled apart by riveters and recast. Corpses vanish in days. The plateau consumes itself and rebuilds.
- **The rust line.** Below a certain altitude the iron-eaters cannot live, because the air is too thick and warm and the metal oxidizes before it can be worked. The plateau ends there in a wall of orange mist and dead red slopes. Above it, the cold keeps the iron pure.
- **The bright hour.** Once a day, near noon, the fog thins and the whole land throws back the sun: wet moss, polished rails, plated backs, mirror-leaves. For an hour you cannot look across it without squinting. Every animal turns to face the light, and the sound drops one register, as though the plateau were taking a breath.

## Moving through it

You would walk on the rail-grooves, because everything else is too dense. The moss gives underfoot like a mattress. Clouds of couriers part around you. Riveters skitter across your boots and take a flake of your buckle. A bellows-beast the size of a barn shifts its weight, and its exhalation rolls over you, warm as a kitchen and smelling of hot pennies and boiled grass. Beyond it are ten thousand more, their plated backs steaming under the blank white sky, the light coming from everywhere, the noise a single thick wall of it, and the land itself, young and sharp-edged and green, going on and on toward the far black teeth of the ranges.

The mountains are still rising. Each year the scarps get a little taller, and the fauna keep building their slow machinery out from the middle, as if they were trying to get there first.
