# The Stair of Growing Stone

You come up onto it from the north, through dwarf rhododendron and knee-deep moss, and hear it long before you see it. A mile out it is a low, continuous roar, like a crowd breathing. Closer, the roar separates into hundreds of small voices: drips, trickles, slaps, and the hollow gulp of water dropping into stone cups.

The place sits at the shoulder of a high pass, cool and bright. The sky is a clean pale blue with high thin cloud, and the light is ordinary daylight, unfiltered. The ground under it is very new.

## The ground

The mountain here is still being made. A fault has opened along the pass, and warm mineral water rises through it. The water is only lukewarm, but it is loaded with lime. Where it breaks the surface it lays down stone, a finger's width in a year, sometimes a hand's width in a wet one. The result is a broad stair of terraces descending the south slope, each a rimmed basin holding milky blue-green water. The rims are scalloped and sharp-edged, and the stone is the color of bone and old cream, stained rust at the seams and green where algae has taken hold.

Nothing here has been softened. Ridges are crisp and edges still have their casting-marks. Where a spring has shifted course, the abandoned terrace stands dry and pale, like a shell, with a fine lace of crystal in its pools. Rain has barely begun to work on any of it. The mountain is embryonic, and everything angular and glistening is a kind of first draft.

The rock also weeps. Every overhang carries a beard of beaded moisture, and every wall holds a sheen. Water stands a hand below the surface almost anywhere you dig. The moss is so saturated it rings faintly if you press it, a small wet chirp.

## The green

The vegetation is dense where the water has not yet buried it in stone. Moss covers the drier terraces in cushions the height of a sleeping dog, in yellow-green, rust, and a deep blue-black. Tree ferns grow in the hollows, and gnarled dwarf trees lean across the rims with their roots wrapped in lime crust. Their lower trunks are slowly being turned into stone, and some are entirely stone, a ghost tree standing in a pool with every leaf-scar cast in white.

Sedges and pale flag-irises fringe the pools. Cushion plants grow in the warm seepage, and in early summer a small orange orchid blooms on the newest terraces, apparently nowhere else. The people say it feeds on the stone as it forms.

## The town

The town is called the Stair, or in the older speech something closer to "the place that is climbing." About two hundred houses stand here, and perhaps sixty people live in them.

The town is built in layers because the ground rises. Every generation finds its floors sinking, as the terraces grow up around the foundations and seal them. The oldest houses are entombed to their eaves in white stone. Their upper windows now open at ground level, and their roofs have become paths. The people cut doorways into these old roofs and go down into them to store grain, keep cold things, and bury their dead in the dry chambers. Below those lie older streets that no one has seen in a long time.

New houses are built on top of the old ones, in the same footprint, with the same walls. This is the custom and almost the only law. A house is a continuation of the house below, and its hearth sits in the same place. So the town is a tall, quiet heap of rooms with big gaps between the inhabited ones. A visitor walks long distances across pale roofs and lime-crusted plazas without meeting anyone. There are wide empty terraces where houses once stood, and terraces where nothing has yet been built.

The building is primitive but careful. Walls are of cut tufa, the light porous stone that forms here, sawn with toothed copper blades and greased with tallow. Roofs are turf on cedar-like poles, with lime plaster spread over them to seal the seams. Water is led everywhere in split-log channels and hollow reeds. Hand tools are of copper, bone, and flint. There is a modest bronze, used almost entirely for bells.

## The noise

The Stair is loud in a way that shapes everything else.

Water falls from terrace to terrace, and each fall has its own pitch depending on the height and the hollowness of the basin below. People have known this for generations and tuned the town to it. They cut hollows and set channels so that certain falls ring like drums, others like flutes, others with a long low gurgle. Conversation across the town is nearly impossible in daytime, so they talk in signal: knocks on hollow stones, plucks on tensioned reeds strung along the channels, and the bronze bells. Every household has a bell-name, a rhythm of three or four strokes. A message can cross the town in the time it takes a person to draw a breath.

At dawn and dusk the bells all sound together in a rolling, overlapping peal that has no melody. People call it "the counting." Each person listens for their own household's rhythm to know that they have been counted, and that no one has fallen into a pool overnight.

The sound is not oppressive. It has a rhythm, and visitors say it takes three days for the silence inside their own heads to become unbearable. Farther up the pass, where the roar dies away, the quiet feels wrong to the town's people. They call it "the dry ear."

## Creatures

Fauna is scarce. A small brown wren-like bird nests in the moss walls and sings from the rims, its song lost in the water-noise, so that you only see its throat vibrating. There are tiny pale amphibians in the warm pools, translucent, with pink gills. The people do not eat them and are careful not to step on them. A few goats are kept in the drier terraces, and their bells are the only bells not made of bronze, but of hollow tufa.

At night, in the deepest of the warm basins, a faint blue-green glow appears in the water. The people say it is the stone thinking. Whatever it is, it is not fire, and no one has ever caught it in a cup.

## Rules and habits

- **Nothing is removed from the new stone.** You may break it for building only from terraces that have already gone dry. The active ones are called "the growing" and are treated like a living thing that can be insulted.
- **Feet are washed at every threshold.** Lime scale is caustic when it dries on skin, and the pools are cool enough to soothe it.
- **A child's first tooth is set into a wet rim** and left to be swallowed by the stone. The family visits it yearly, and it slowly vanishes under a bead of white.
- **The dead are laid in the buried houses** and not sealed. The dry chambers are kept open, and in time the seep closes them by itself. No one hurries this.

## Time

Change here is slow but real. A terrace may take decades to cover a doorway. A spring may migrate half a street in a lifetime. The town moves with it. Nobody plans to abandon the Stair, but people accept that it is a slow flow, and that some year the water will choose another slope, and the white stair will go dry and begin to crack, and the lichen will have its turn.

Until then the water keeps falling and the stone keeps rising. Late in the afternoon the slanting light turns the terraces to pale gold and the wet moss to a fierce green, and the whole hillside rings softly, in every voice at once, with the sound of a place still being born.
