# The Sump of Ahl

You descend for most of a day, and the descent is the first thing to understand. The rim is a fresh fault scarp, a cliff of raw basalt and pale tuff only a few generations old, still angular, its edges unsoftened. Nothing has had time to round it. Below, the land has dropped away in long steps, each step a slab that slid down when the crust sagged. The lowest step lies ninety meters under the level of the distant sea, and the sea knows it. It comes in through the ground.

## The floor

The basin is thirty kilometers across, and from the last terrace it looks like a pan of dull pewter lit from underneath. The light comes down through a permanent lid of vapor: warm, grey-gold, and low, like the hour after sunset that never quite finishes. Shadows are soft and have no direction. Things at a distance dissolve into the haze in pale layers.

Underfoot the ground is a wet crust over wetter ground. Grey-brown mud is skinned with salt, and the skin breaks like crème brûlée. Beneath it is warm water, thigh-deep in places and only ankle-deep in others, and it is never still. Seeps rise everywhere, small bubbling boils no wider than a hand, each ringed with rust-orange and mustard bacterial film. The whole floor is a slow spring. Water arrives from the sea through fractured rock, is heated by the young magma below, and stands in the basin as shallow braided channels and lagoons that steam gently. The air is 30 degrees and close as a breath. Every surface beads. Rocks weep in long dark streaks. Cold metal drips.

The geology is still being made. Fault blocks are still tilting. Fresh cracks, some a hand-width and some a body-width, run across the mud in straight lines, their walls sharp and layered like cut cake in ash, silt, and salt. After a tremor, which comes about once a week as a low shudder with no drama in it, new water wells up along the crack, and within a day it is ringed with orange. Nothing here has yet been eroded into a valley. Everything is still an edge.

## Life

Vegetation is scant: mats of green-black algae on the warm shallows, stands of stunted salt-reed in the seep margins, and a few hundred wind-bent tamarisk near the northern terraces. Almost nothing grows tall. The green is thin, and the color belongs to the animals.

The animals are everywhere. This is the difficulty of the place. On the tuff banks and the low mud islands, in the shallow lagoons and on the crust between them, birds nest shoulder to shoulder: flamingos in the pink-grey ranks of the eastern pans, gulls and terns on the dry ridges, cormorants stacked on every projecting rock, stilts and avocets working the seep-margins in shuffling lines. There is no ground between nests. Each bird has exactly the reach of its own neck. The brine shrimp that feed them are so dense in the lagoons that the water turns rust-red and thick as soup. Flies rise off the mud in low, drifting clouds, and the birds walk through them with their mouths open. Amphibians live here too. A salt-tolerant toad breeds in the freshest seeps, and in the warm months the shallows are one long trembling skin of eggs and bodies.

Beneath the birds the mud is packed with worms, snails, and crustaceans, thousands to the handful. Dig anywhere and the hole fills at once with water and small pulsing animals. A predator here does not hunt. It simply stands in the food.

## The sound

You hear it long before you understand it, and then you cannot hear anything else. The basin is a bowl, and the bowl holds sound the way it holds heat. Hundreds of thousands of birds call at once, an unbroken roar that is not one noise but a lattice of them: the goose-honk of flamingos, the rasp of gulls, the toads' low ratcheting chorus, the wet clatter of bills in mud. Over this, the geysers thump and hiss. Vents scream steadily at frequencies you feel in your teeth. Under it, the ground itself hums, a low tremor of boiling water that runs through your boots.

You can shout into someone's ear and not be heard. People here talk with their hands and with lamp signals, and they wear wax or foam in their ears from the age of six. Newcomers find the noise physical, a pressure against the chest like standing near a waterfall. After a while it becomes a kind of silence, a solid wall you lean on. When the birds all rise at once, which happens a few times a day when some shadow crosses them, the sound doubles, then drops to a shocking hollow quiet. Then the wings come down, and the roar resumes.

## The machinery

Human traces are scattered, small, and industrial. The first people came for salt and sulfur. Later, engineers found the steam. Now, across the basin, at intervals of several kilometers, stand the old works.

There is a geothermal plant on a fault block to the west. It is a squat concrete shed with three rust-streaked pipes, each as thick as a tree, running out across the mud on trestles. It still turns, slowly, and its turbines add their whine to the din. Its walls are white with mineral crust. Cormorants roost on the pipes. In the middle of the basin, on a causeway of packed slag, sits a brine-pumping station with a single steel chimney, half of its riveted plates furred with orange growth. A rail line, its sleepers sinking and its rails gone brown, leaves the causeway and ends in the water where the ground fell out from under it. A crane leans out of a lagoon at forty degrees, hung with nests.

Beyond these are smaller marks: a salt-boiler's hut of corrugated iron, the ribs of a conveyor gantry, a row of iron drums, a generator in a shed, still kept running by a man and his daughter who walk out along a plank road every morning. Everything metallic is bloomed with rust and salt. Everything is warm to the touch. The buildings are not ruins. They are being slowly absorbed, coated with mud swallow-nests, algal green, and guano. The birds treat them as cliffs.

## The rules of the place

Change is slow, and it is patient. The floor sinks a finger's width each year, and the lagoons creep outward, drowning the oldest causeways. Every decade or so a new fissure opens across the works and someone has to move a pipe. The colonies shift with the water, walking their nests across the mud in a migration measured in seasons. The only clock is the tide of brine: twice a year the seeps run harder, the pans fill and merge into one shallow sheet, and the whole basin becomes a mirror of the low bright sky, dotted with pink and white bodies to the horizon.

At the end of the day, if you can call it that, the vapor thickens and the light goes a warmer, darker gold. The roar does not diminish. The engines keep turning. In the gathering steam the works look like ships anchored in a grey sea, and the birds, packed to every edge, settle and shift together like a single vast, restless, breathing thing, on ground that is still deciding what shape it will become.
