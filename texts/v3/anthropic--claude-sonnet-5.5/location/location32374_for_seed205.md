# The Drumlands of Ossary Height

Most of the world's ground is rock, or soil, or sand. Here the ground is plant. Over thousands of years the growth has thickened into a continuous living floor, in places four hundred meters deep. The floor is made of interlocked root-rope, dead leaf-felt, stem-lattice, and the slow brown ferment of its own lower layers. The rock is somewhere underneath. Nothing living on the surface has seen it.

## The Surface

The plateau lies about three kilometers above sea level and runs on for weeks of walking in every direction. It is hot the way a closed greenhouse is hot, though the air is thin and bright. Nothing here is bare. Moss-cushions the size of houses are dressed in smaller cushions. Where the sun is strongest, the low **plate-ferns** lie in overlapping shingles of dull blue-green, each frond as wide as a door, tilted like a field of tilted mirrors. Between them are seams where the plate-ferns give way to **rope-grass**, a springy tan turf that gives underfoot and sighs when stepped on. It sighs because the mat is hollow at its upper levels. A footstep pushes air through galleries of dead stem, and the whole ground exhales in small breaths around you.

Density is low. Across the tableland the big presences are far apart: a **spire-tree** every few kilometers, a **bell-thicket** on a rise, a slow-moving **grazing mound** of something. The plant is total, but its tall forms are rare. Most of the vegetation is a single low, continuous, patterned thing, and the tall things stand out of it like buildings in a flat city. A traveler can stand in one place and see three spire-trees on the horizon, each with weather of its own around it.

## The Spire-Trees

A spire-tree is a single organism, older than any language spoken beneath it. Its trunk is a fused bundle of hundreds of thinner trunks, twisting slowly upward like wrung cloth, rising perhaps two hundred meters before it flares into a flat, broad crown. The bark is pale gray and finely ridged, worn smooth at the base by ten thousand years of animals rubbing against it. The crown collects fog in the morning, and the fog runs down grooves in the trunk and drips into a moat of moss at the base. The spire-trees are the wells of the country.

Because the plateau is damp rather than wet, water is the thing that organizes everything. There is no river on the surface. Moisture arrives as dawn condensation on every leaf, and as the **noon build**. By late morning the transpiring mat has pushed so much vapor into the air that towers of cloud stand up over the plateau, flat-bottomed and bruise-colored. Around the fourth or fifth hour after noon the rain comes down for twenty minutes, hard, warm, loud. Then it stops, and the steam goes up. The plateau makes its own weather and has for so long that the weather has become part of its anatomy. Beneath the mat the water runs in channels, long slow dark rivers moving through root-tunnels and eventually finding the old canyons at the edges, where they fall as threads of white over cliffs no one has measured.

## The Edges

Erosion has had a very long time here. The tableland's margins are old, settled, and rounded. Cliff faces that once were sharp have been swallowed by hanging curtains of vine and the drooping fingers of **skirt-moss**. Ancient slumps have made terraces, and each terrace has grown its own forest. The canyons are deep and green-black, their walls carpeted, and the sound of falling water inside them carries through the whole body. At the very rim you can lie on your stomach and look down eight hundred meters of slope that is entirely leaf.

## Fauna

The fauna is overwhelming. It is not in big herds that you can see from far away. It is in the layer of the world you have to lean close to notice, and then you cannot stop noticing. Every square meter of the mat is a city. **Stilt-beetles** the size of thumbs walk on hair-thin legs across the plate-fern tops. **Lacework spiders** hang webs like ballroom chandeliers between the moss cushions. There are gliding frogs, flat as leaves, that live and breed in the water cups of a plant called **cistern-lily**. There are ground birds like bundles of lit coals, red and yellow, that run in flocks of thousands through the rope-grass and vanish into it. There are pale, blind, long-bodied things that live in the stem galleries and can be heard passing under your feet like water in pipes.

The larger animals are few and far between, but each one is a landscape feature. The **grazing mounds** are broad, slow, armored creatures, three or four stories tall, that walk across the plateau in long circuits, eating the plate-fern in a swath. Behind them the fern regrows in a pale new green, so their paths can be read from a distance as lighter ribbons across the darker country. They carry whole ecosystems on their backs, gardens of moss, small trees, nesting colonies of birds. When two mounds meet, the sound is like a slow avalanche.

Above everything, the **kite-swarms** move: thousands of translucent, membrane-winged animals turning in unison, catching the light like a flung handful of glass. They feed on the insects the mounds stir up. From below, in sunlight, they turn the sky itself into something alive.

## Sound

The sound is deafening. It is not one noise but a pressure. Insects drone in bands, each at its own pitch, so that walking across the plateau you pass from a whining high register into a throbbing low one as if traveling through a chord. Frogs in the cistern-lilies ring like struck pipes. Birds call in relay. The plants themselves take part. The **drum-reed** grows in stands, hollow-stemmed, each stem tuned by its length. When the wind moves, or when animals brush them, whole stands sound with a low bowed hum, a bass note felt in the sternum. After the noon rain, when the water hits the taut leaf-drums of the plate-fern, the roar rises to something almost solid. Then afterward, for an hour, everything is dripping, ticking, popping, and calling in an enormous relieved chorus.

In the deep of night the sound changes but never stops. The high voices drop out and the low ones remain, the frogs and the drum-reeds and something enormous and unidentified that booms once every few minutes from very far away.

## People

Few people live here, and they live lightly. They are small groups, walkers, who move between the spire-trees in seasonal circuits. Their tools are simple: stone blades, bone needles, fire-hardened digging sticks, fiber cord twisted from rope-grass, baskets so tightly woven they hold water. Where they stay they build **hanging huts**, tented from woven leaf-plate and lashed high into the lower branches of a spire-tree, above the flood of insects. The huts are the only buildings for a hundred kilometers. Around a few of the older trees are worn stairways cut into the bark, each step smoothed into a dish by generations of feet, and small stacks of stones balanced on knots of root, markers whose meaning is held only by those who pass them.

They speak, when they speak, in a language with many hand signs, because the sound makes speech difficult at any distance. They also tap: a stick on a hollow stem sends a coded rhythm through the mat itself, and answering taps come back from far away, through the floor of the world.

## The Rules of the Place

- Nothing lies bare for long. A cleared spot is a green scar within a season, and a dead animal is a garden within weeks.
- The ground is alive and breathes. It is never quite still, and walkers learn to feel its slow pulse of intake and release.
- Water is carried, not found. Travelers plan their days by the position of the next spire-tree and the timing of the noon rain.
- There is no silence. The rare few who have found a place where the sound briefly stops, a dead pocket in the mat, say it feels as though the world has stopped listening.

The heat pulses, the cloud towers rise, and the mounds move across the green. The mat is still growing, a little, under everything.
