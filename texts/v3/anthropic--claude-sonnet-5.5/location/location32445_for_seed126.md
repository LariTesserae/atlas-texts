# The Long Anneal

The plain has no natural ground. If a natural ground exists, it lies so far below that no one has reached it. What you walk on is floor: pale, hard, faintly warm, tiled in hexagons the size of a hand at your feet and the size of a town at the horizon. The tiles are the same material at every scale, and that is the first strange thing. Look closely at one and you see it is built of smaller tiles, and those of smaller ones, down to a grain your eye can't resolve. The surface is a fractal of intention.

It is also very old. The floor was laid so long ago that it has weathered the way rock weathers. The hexagons have rounded shoulders. Their seams have silted with a fine grey grit that is the floor's own powder. Great swells and shallow basins ripple across the plain at wavelengths of many kilometers, the slumped remains of towers, ducts, and ribbed vaults that once stood here. Now they are low, soft-edged hills whose flanks still show the ghost of structure: a run of conduits like fossilized veins, a stair that descends into a slope and stops, a colonnade worn to knuckles. Nothing here is natural, yet everything has been eroded into a shape that looks like nature. The world has been aging into a landscape for longer than it was ever a machine.

## Light

The light is the second thing you notice, because there is so much of it. The sky is a white-gold haze, and above the haze hangs the sun, but the sun is not the only source. Across the plain stand the **heliostats**, ranks of tilted mirrors on stalks, thin as reeds and thousands of meters tall, each one turning very slowly to follow a sun that moved on long ago. Most are cracked, clouded, or fused in place. The ones that still work throw beams of concentrated brightness across the flats, and these beams cross and recross in the dusty air like the strings of an enormous loom.

Shadows are rare and sharp-edged and shift while you watch. Standing in one is like standing in a pool of cool dark liquid. Ten paces out of it the brightness returns as a pressure on the skin, and the floor gives back a glare from below, so the light comes from everywhere and the world has no underside.

The heat is warm rather than punishing, an even, well-fed warmth like stone in late afternoon. It rises from the floor as much as it falls from the sky. The plain is a heat engine, and it breathes.

## Weather

At this scale the plain makes its own weather.

The floor's slow metabolism warms the air unevenly, and over the darker basins, where the old grit has gathered and absorbed the heat, **thermal columns** stand up like translucent trunks. You can see them by the wobble they put into the far horizon. They rise for kilometers and lean together at altitude, and where they meet, thin ribbed clouds form. Those clouds are not water. They are condensed vapor of some pale metallic compound, and they hang in the haze like sheets of brushed silver. They rarely rain. When they do, what falls is a fine glittering mist that beads on the tiles, beads and then *wanders*, running uphill along invisible gradients until it vanishes into the seams. The floor drinks it. The floor is thirsty for something that isn't water.

Real water is nearly absent. There is dew, an hour before the brightest part of the day, and in a few basins the dew is harvested by **fog-combs**, fine filaments strung between leaning posts that shiver very slightly as they work. Follow the trickle they collect and you might reach a cistern: a cool, square-mouthed well with a stair cut into its side, holding a dark thumb's depth of the only true water for a hundred kilometers. Nothing drinks from it that you can see. It is kept anyway, the way a lamp is kept lit in an empty house.

Some mornings a **grit-tide** rolls out from the high ridges, a low wall of suspended powder that carries a faint charge. It crackles when it passes, and blue fire runs along the edges of the tiles. The dust is the floor's own dust, and it is not entirely inert. It settles into the seams and tightens them. By afternoon the tide has been reabsorbed, and the surface it crossed is a shade smoother.

## The Rules of the Ground

The plain **repairs itself**, slowly and without hurry, and this governs everything on it.

Leave a mark on the floor, a scratch or a footprint or a pile of stones, and it will be gone within days. The tiles soften, flow, and reset. You can watch a scar close over an hour like the slow lip of a healing cut. Stand still long enough and the floor begins to assess you: a faint tingling under the soles, a warmth that gathers, a scatter of tiny bright motes drifting up from the seams to hover at ankle height and read you. Then they lose interest and drift away.

There are no roads because the plain doesn't permit them. There are only **grain-lines**, faint directional textures in the tiling, and if you follow them the ground stays firm and cool. If you walk against them the surface goes slick and faintly warm, as though the floor were mildly, wordlessly discouraging you.

## Life

Vegetation is sparse and clings to the seams. In the joints between the largest hexagons grows a **thread-moss**, dark green shading to bronze, that lives on the trickle of condensate and on the spilled light from the mirrors. It is engineered, or was; it may have gone wild after so long. Its leaves are thinner than paper and tilt to follow the beams as the mirrors do, so whole hillsides of it seem to breathe in unison. Here and there stands a **wire-thorn**, a lone black tree of twisted carbon filament with sparse leaves like foil. It grows where the grain-lines knot. Its roots go down into the floor, and the floor lets them, and no one knows who is feeding whom.

Fauna is rare. The most common creature is the **sweeper**, a long-legged grazer the height of a person's waist, jointed like a heron, with a low head fitted with soft brushes. It walks the seams in slow lines, combing the grit out of the joints and eating it, and it leaves a trail of clean, bright tile behind it. Whether it is an animal or a maintenance device that reproduces, it has no answer for you. It ignores you. Sometimes a sweeper stops, folds its legs, and sits motionless for a whole day in the full light, and a thread of moss begins to climb it.

Above the columns turn **kites**, pale membranous things that ride the thermals for weeks without landing, rarely visible except as a flicker where they cross a mirror's beam. On the rare occasions one descends, it settles on a wire-thorn and unfolds like a page.

## Sound

The plain is quiet. The sound it has is thin and constant: the tick of tiles expanding in the heat, the hiss of grit shifting in the seams, the faint chime of the fog-combs when the air moves, and under everything a hum so low you feel it in the bones of your feet rather than hear it. It is the sound of the whole floor working, patient, at a pitch too deep to be a note. Footsteps make almost no noise, because the tiles give slightly and take the sound into themselves.

## What Emerges

Walk far enough, a week or a month, and the swells begin to look less like ruins and more like the *intended* shape. The erosion did not destroy the design. It finished it. The soft hills, the slumped towers, the wandering mist and the combing sweepers, all of it has settled into a final form, a deeply aged equilibrium in which the builders' plan and a million ages of drift are no longer separable. The world was made to last, and it has lasted so long that it has become natural, and it continues, warm and bright and nearly silent, to repair itself for no one, under the light of ten thousand tilted mirrors.
