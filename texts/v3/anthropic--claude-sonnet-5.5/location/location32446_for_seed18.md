# The Flywheel Terraces

## Approach

The mountain has no summit anymore. Somewhere above five thousand eight hundred meters, the peak was cut down and hollowed, and what stands there now is a bowl of riveted plate and poured stone, about three hundred meters across and less than two hundred deep. The whole rim is a cast-iron parapet, thick as a road and the color of dried liver. It has weathered for so long that lichen has written itself across it in slow orange script. Reaching it means climbing a stair of iron grating that hugs the outer wall for four hundred meters. The steps ring, but you can't hear them. You feel the ringing in your shins.

The sound reaches you before you see anything. Below the rim it is a pressure, a hand flat against the chest. Once you look over the parapet, it becomes the whole world.

## The Bowl

The interior is mostly air.

That is what you notice first, once your eyes adjust: how empty it is. The terraces step down in wide concentric rings of iron, brick, and slag concrete, each ring a few meters tall and twenty deep, and every one is green. But between the terraces and across the center there is only a great hollow of warm, moving, faintly misted space. Nothing crosses it except birds and, once every few minutes, a drifting curtain of steam. From the rim, the bowl looks like a cup with a single green skin and nothing in it.

The green is dense. Tree ferns grow in the old soot-soil that has collected in the terrace beds, their trunks furred with epiphytes. Moss lies over the plates in layers as thick as carpet, in at least nine shades, from a yellow-green that looks lit from within to a black-green like wet slate. Orchids hang from rivet lines. Vines with leaves the size of doors descend from the upper terraces in long slow ropes and stop a few meters short of the floor, as if they had been told how far to go. Nothing has been planted. Everything has come in by wind, bird, and patience over what must be centuries.

The metal beneath is old and settled. The plates have bowed and softened at their edges. Rust has run down them in long vertical streaks of orange and violet. Every seam and bolt-head has rounded, and every stair tread has been worn into a shallow dish. Nothing is broken. The place has weathered into a stable form, the way a river stone does, and it looks as if it has been in its present state longer than anyone has kept records.

## The Wheel

At the center, in a round pit, turns the flywheel.

It is forty meters across, a rim of black iron as broad as a lane, and the spokes are girders. It turns once every fifty seconds, so slowly that you can watch a single bolt travel down and around, and so heavily that the terrace stones vibrate in sympathy. Moss grows on its upper rim, where the rim is above the pit, and is carried down into the dark, and comes back up still green. Something in the pit keeps it damp. Small ferns ride the spokes and survive the passage every fifty seconds. Some have been riding so long that the spokes have grown a sort of green fur.

No one runs it. No fuel line reaches it and no chimney feeds it. The engine that drives the wheel is a vertical beam engine half buried in the pit's far wall, and its great walking beam rises and falls with a stroke that takes eleven seconds. Its only source of power seems to be the mountain itself: warmth from below, and the wind, which enters through a ring of tall valve-stacks around the rim and leaves as steam through others. Standing above the pit, you can feel heat rising from it, moist and mineral. That heat is why the bowl is warm on a mountaintop, and why a cloud forest lives above the snow line.

## The Sound

The sound is the character of the place, and it is not a single sound.

There is the engine's stroke, a low boom on an eleven-second cycle, felt in the sternum. There is the wheel's groan, a long bowed-metal note that slides slowly up as each spoke passes some resonant point and then falls. There are the valve-stacks, dozens of them, each tuned by chance to a different pitch, so that when the wind rises the whole rim becomes a colossal organ played by weather, chords swelling and collapsing over the boom. And beneath all of it, a wide roar of steam, like a waterfall heard from inside the water.

It is deafening in the strict sense. Without ear protection, a visitor would be stunned in minutes. But the sound is also so constant, and changes so slowly, that the mind treats it as a kind of substance, like air. After an hour it begins to feel like silence with a great deal in it.

## The Silences

The bowl's geometry does something odd with all this noise. The sound waves from the engine, the wheel, and the stacks meet and cancel across the terraces in fixed places. These are standing-wave nodes, small quiet zones, circular or lens-shaped, a few meters across. In them the noise drops away almost completely. You step into one and the roar falls off as if a door had closed. You hear your own breath, and a drip somewhere, and the ticking of cooling iron.

There are perhaps forty such places in the bowl, and they do not move. Two or three shift by a meter or so when the wind changes direction. Almost everything alive in the bowl lives in or beside them.

## Fauna

The abundance of animal life is the second great surprise, after the emptiness of the space.

**Frogs.** Small, dark-backed frogs with orange-ringed eyes cover the terraces near the silences. They have lost their calls. Their tympanic membranes have shrunk to pinpricks, and instead they signal with their throat sacs, which flash pale blue and are pumped in rhythm across whole terraces. From a distance, a hillside of frogs looks like a shifting constellation, and courtship is conducted in patterns of light. They sit motionless in pools that collect in the plate seams, where condensation drips all day.

**Swifts.** Thousands nest in the pipes and in the gaps behind the plates, and they are the bowl's only real traffic. They wheel through the central void in long spirals, and they are the only animals that seem to enjoy the noise. Their flight is timed, obscurely, to the engine's eleven-second stroke. On the boom, a whole spiral of them tightens and drops together, and it looks like a rehearsed maneuver. No one has ever established whether this is coincidence or something the swifts have learned.

**Beetles and their kin.** Iron-blue beetles graze the moss. Long-bodied centipedes hunt among the rust. Pale spiders spin webs across the valve mouths, and the webs vibrate visibly with the wind, so that each seems to hum.

**Bats.** They emerge at dusk from the dark pit walls. Their calls are lost completely in the din, and they navigate by some other sense, perhaps the nodes themselves: they fly from quiet zone to quiet zone, like people crossing a river on stepping stones.

**Goats.** A few small dark goats have found their way down from the outer slopes, or perhaps have always lived here, and they browse the upper terraces. They ignore the sound entirely. They look up when a person comes, but not at a sound. They look at the shadow.

## Water

Water is present but intermittent, and always moving. Steam rises from the engine, cools against the upper bowl, and falls as a fine, warm rain across the terraces, usually in the late afternoon. Rain also comes from real clouds, more rarely: dense gray masses that pour over the rim and fill the bowl for an hour. Then both mix, and the whole place drips, and every plate shines.

Water runs through iron gutters cast into the terraces, along channels wide enough for a person to walk in, down to a sump in the pit, where the wheel churns it into brown foam. The rust-stained water is not safe to drink. The frogs live in it happily.

## Light

The light is plain. At this altitude the sun is hard and white, but the bowl's walls shade half of it, and the steam diffuses the rest into a soft silver. The greens are muted by mist and lit from within by sun. In the mornings, the eastern terraces glow orange with rust. In the evenings the pit throws up a dull red light from its furnace-heat, and the swifts turn copper-colored as they pass through it.

## Rules of the Place

- **Nothing here is fast.** The wheel turns slowly, plants advance by millimeters, and rust moves down the plates over decades. The change you can see is the change of the eleven-second stroke, and that is the only quick thing.
- **The quiet zones are the only places where people can speak.** Any conversation must happen in a node. Visitors learn to walk between them, stop, talk in normal voices, and then step out and fall silent again. There is a kind of etiquette: you do not linger in someone else's quiet.
- **The plants are slowly taking the place apart.** Roots work into the seams. Tree ferns split brickwork. In a thousand years the bowl may be a green crater with a flywheel in it. But the engine is also slowly changing the bowl, heating it, feeding it moisture. The two processes are in a kind of balance, and it has held for as long as anyone can tell.
- **No one is in charge.** Nothing indicates an operator. No stairs lead to a control room. The valves, if they are valves, open and shut by their own weight, tuned by long-forgotten calibration. The place runs itself, as a garden does, or a river.

## At the Center

If you walk down through the terraces, past the hanging vines, past the frog constellations, past the nesting swifts, to the lip of the wheel-pit, you find the last strange thing. Around the pit's rim, set in the iron in a ring, are hundreds of small brass plates, each the size of a hand. They are worn nearly smooth. Only a few can still be read, and those show a single repeated mark: a small spiral, cut by hand, no two alike.

Each spiral has moss growing in its groove. The moss is thickest in the oldest ones. And on the newest, cut so recently that the brass still shines, someone has left a single fresh fern frond, laid across it, still green, still wet.
