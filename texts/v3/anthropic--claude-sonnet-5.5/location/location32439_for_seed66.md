# The Shelf of Ossel

The plateau is so wide that it makes its own weather. From the eastern rim, where the road climbs out of the lowland haze, you can see a permanent cloud-bank standing over the interior like a second, softer escarpment. It is not going anywhere. It has been there longer than the oldest surviving records. Air pushed up the slope condenses against the cold of the altitude, and the water falls back on the Shelf as slow, warm, fine rain. It comes most afternoons and lasts until the light turns amber.

Nothing green grows here. The height is not the reason, since the air is thick and mild, and the water is nearly everywhere. Something older than the city removed the plants, or they never arrived. The ground is a mineral skin instead: travertine, iron crust, and pale sandstone worn round as loaves. Over all of it lies a film of living color, orange where iron-eating microbes work the seeps, slate-blue where the sulfur-lovers are, and a felty rust-brown on the sunward walls. To a visitor the whole place looks like an enormous wet stone that has been slowly breathed on for a very long time.

## The stone that weeps

Water sits just under the surface. Press a boot into the low ground between terraces and the crust gives, then wells up cold and clear into the footprint. Every cliff face, retaining wall, and foundation course seeps. The seeps leave long pale beards of calcium deposit, which harden into curtains and flowstone ribs. Rock here does not sit dry. It is always darkened and beaded, and it makes a faint continuous ticking as droplets fall from a million small lips onto ledges and tin and leaves of slate.

The travertine is also how the Shelf keeps its history. Whatever is left standing long enough gets slowly wrapped. Abandoned pump-houses have softened into white hummocks with a chimney or a wheel-spoke showing through. A fallen crane arm is now a fluted ridge you could mistake for a natural rib of the hill. The city grows partly by accretion, and part of its labor is chipping itself free. Crews with pneumatic hammers work down the streets in slow rotation, opening doors and clearing gutters, and by the time they finish a district the first street has started to close again.

## The city

It is one city, or one continuous urban condition. Streets, stairways, aqueducts, and factory ranges run for days in every direction, thinning to farmland-that-is-not-farmland only near the rim. The buildings are stone, cast iron, and riveted plate, five to nine stories, stepped down the Shelf's shallow undulations so that from any rise you look across a slope of roofs like the scales of a great flat fish. Because nothing can be built out of timber, everything is masonry and metal, and every building is thick-walled and rounded at the corners by centuries of wet.

The industry runs on water and on heat. The rock beneath the Shelf is warm, and the deep wells give off steam. Great beam engines and later turbine halls stand over the wells, wheezing pressure into mains that run through the city like veins. Mills, foundries, glassworks, and fertilizer plants sit shoulder to shoulder with tenements and markets. There are no clean edges between working and living. A stairwell will smell of hot oil and wet lime and, faintly, of the animals.

Everything has been weathered into settledness. Iron rails are dished and polished by traffic. Stone steps have hollows worn deep enough to hold a hand of water. Signs are legible only because they have been repainted in the same places for generations, so the letters stand out in thick, crusted relief. Nothing looks new. Even the newest engine is installed into a bay that has held engines for eight hundred years, and it sits there like a swallow in an old nest.

## The animals

This is what the visitor does not expect, and it is the thing that makes the place. The fauna is overwhelming. With no plants to feed on, life on the Shelf grazes the mineral film and eats its own kind, and the film is bright, dense, and endless. The animals have multiplied to fill every surface the city offers.

Mat-snails the size of loaves move across the walls in slow herds, rasping the biofilm and leaving clean silver trails. Below them, in the gutters and cisterns, live the gulpers, pale flat-headed salamanders that fill the drains from March to November and sing in the rain. Their chorus is a low, wet, rhythmic clucking, thousands deep, that blends with the pumps until you cannot tell which is which. Over the roofs swirl swifts and bats and a third kind of flier that is neither, a leathery, hand-sized glider that nests in the cracks of cornices and rides updrafts off the factory stacks in dense, roaring clouds at dusk. Crabs work the flowstone stairs. Long eels live in the aqueducts and are fished for food. There are herd-animals too, low, broad, hairless grazers kept in the terraced pens and fed on cultured fungus, whose bawling carries across whole districts.

The city was built around them as much as for them. Walls have drain-slots for the snails and nesting-niches for the fliers, cut by masons who knew that without the animals grazing, the crust would smother the stone in a season. The people do not think of the fauna as separate from the city. They are maintenance. They are also food, and fertilizer, and weather-signs: when the gulpers go silent it is about to stop raining, and when the fliers come down early there is a heavy night coming.

## Sound

The Shelf is loud, and the loudness is layered. Under everything is the low pulse of the beam engines, one beat every few seconds, felt in the chest before it is heard. Over that lies the hiss of steam mains and the constant tick and drip of the seeping stone. Then the animal voices: the gulper choruses, the cries of the fliers, the bawl of the pens, the scrabble of a thousand shells on slate. Then the human noise on top of it, hammers, whistles, cart wheels, the cry of the water-sellers, who still sell water to a city standing on it, because the good water is the cold water from the deep seeps and people like to know where theirs comes from. At night the noise does not stop. It drops a register and slows.

## Time

Nothing here happens fast. The cloud-bank shifts a few miles over a season. The travertine creeps over an unattended doorway in a decade. A district may be repaired, buried, and dug out again over a lifetime, and no one who lives there has seen it change. The rain comes at the same slow hour. The herds of snails cross the same wall each week and arrive a little farther along. People measure their years by the wall-cleanings, by the passage of the gulpers into the drains and out again, and by the pale line on the stone that rises and falls with the wet.

The light is ordinary and clear, filtered gold through rain-haze in the afternoons and hard white on the pale stone by noon. It falls on a city that is at once a machine, a habitat, and a slowly growing piece of the rock it stands on. It could be a thousand years old or ten thousand. The stone is warm underfoot and always a little damp, and something small is moving on it wherever you look.
