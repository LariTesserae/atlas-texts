# The Sounding Uplands

You arrive by hearing.

Before anything touches you, the place has already told you its size. Your first footfall comes back from a hundred directions at different delays: a slap off wet stone near you, a softer answer from the leaf-walls, then a long, tapering shimmer from somewhere so far off it might be another weather system. There is no light and there never has been. The plateau sits at high altitude under a lid of cloud so thick and old that the sun is a rumor. The dark is not an absence here. It is a medium, warm and close, that everything has learned to move through.

## The Ground

The land is a raised shield of ancient basalt and sandstone, tilted and folded, cut by drainage into a maze of ridges, sinks, and terraces that runs on for weeks of walking in every direction. Everything is old. Cliffs have rounded into shoulders. Boulders have sunk to their waists in moss and been sculpted by centuries of drip into fluted, spoon-shaped hollows. The rock weeps. Every overhang has a bead of water hanging at its lip, and each bead falls at its own interval, so that beneath a ledge you hear a slow, unsynchronized rhythm, a thousand small clocks.

Water is always near the surface. Step off a path and the ground takes your foot to the ankle with a warm sucking sound. The soil is black, fibrous, and smells of iron, rot, and something sweet like split fruit. In the low basins, marsh spreads in braided channels, each a hand's depth of tea-colored water over deeper mud. The water is body-warm, heated from below.

That is the first secret of the land. Under the plateau, the earth is hot. Seams of thermal water climb through faults and fill the caves and root-mats with steam. This is why anything lives up here without sun.

## The Growth

It is lush past reason. The plants don't need light, or need so little that they have found other tables to sit at. Most feed on the warm mineral seep: sulfur, iron, and dissolved heat drawn up through their roots. Others feed on each other.

The dominant form is the **flute-tree**, a pale, hollow-stemmed giant, some as thick as houses, rising in dense stands with their crowns lost in the cloud. Their trunks are pierced with natural apertures, and wind moving through them makes long, reedy, sliding notes. Whole forests sound like a cathedral tuning up. Beneath them hang curtains of rope-vine, wet and cold-skinned, and beneath those, shelf-fungi the size of tables, pulsing gently as they exhale spores that smell of warm bread.

Nothing is green. In the dark, pigment is a waste, so the plants are the colors of things that grow in cellars and caves: bone, cream, gray-violet, the translucent pink of a fingertip held to a fire. If a light were carried through, it would find a world of ghosts. But no light is carried. The plants have no need of being seen.

Density is the rule. Ferns lace through vines, vines through branches, branches through the long dripping beards of moss. Moving through it is a slow negotiation. You push a curtain aside and it is already warm from something's breath.

## The Animals

The fauna is abundant and, being blind or nearly so, loud in a way that only makes sense once you understand it. Sound is sight here. Everything that lives on the plateau either emits, listens, or senses heat and electricity, and most do all three.

**Clickers** swarm the canopy, small furred gliders that fill the air with fast ticking calls and read the echoes to fly through the lattice at speed. When a hundred do this at once, the forest crackles like a fire.

**Bell-frogs** occupy the marsh, each one tuned to a single pitch that it holds for hours. The marsh at night, which is to say always, is a chord. Predators know it. When a note drops out of the chord, something has eaten the frog, and the hunters turn toward the silence.

The largest animals are the **drifters**, enormous soft-bodied grazers that wade the marshes in herds, the size of buildings, browsing the fungal shelves with slow, wet, tearing sounds. They communicate in infrasound. You feel it in the sternum rather than hear it, a pressure that rolls across the basin and makes the water tremble in rings. When a herd moves, the ground itself carries their conversation for miles.

Hunting them are **lattice-spiders**, sensing prey through vibration in silk strung between flute-trees over hundreds of feet. And there are things stranger than these: pale, eel-like creatures that swim through the *air* in the warmest fog banks, propelled by rippling fins, sensing the electric halo of every living body. They are harmless mostly. They hang in clusters around any warm thing, including you, and follow.

## The Weather

The plateau makes its own weather, as all very large, very wet, very warm places do. Hot vapor rises from the geothermal seams and climbs into the cool ceiling of cloud, where it condenses and falls again. It rains constantly somewhere. The rain is warm and heavy, and it arrives as a roar approaching across the canopy for a full minute before the first drops reach you. Then it is everywhere, drumming on a million leaves, and every animal raises its voice to be heard over it. This is the loudest hour, a full-throated riot of clicks, bells, hoots, and infrasound under the thundering water.

Then it passes, and the drip begins, and the land steams. Fog fills the low places to the height of a tall man. Storms of a different kind build on the highest ridges, where electrical charge gathers in the cloud and lightning walks the heights in white, silent, invisible-to-nobody-because-nobody-sees-it strokes. You feel it only as a flat crack and the smell of ozone drifting down, and you might catch a fleeting afterimage on your eyelids, the only light most creatures here will ever meet.

## The Machines

The industrial age came here, and left, and did not entirely leave.

Scattered through the uplands, at irregular distances, stand the **stations**: squat, riveted, iron-and-brick buildings, the size of chapels or barns, built over the hottest vents to harvest steam. Many are ruined, roofless, wrapped in vine, their boilers furred with pale fungus. But some still run. No one tends them. Their turbines turn on the pressure of the earth, and they hum, clank, and hiss in the dark, driving nothing to no one. Belts still spin. Valves still burp steam on their old schedules. A pump somewhere keeps moving water uphill through a rusted pipeline, so that wherever the pipe crosses the marsh you hear its slow, iron heartbeat: *thud... thud... thud.*

The animals have adopted them. Clickers roost in the rafters, warmed by the boiler-skin. Bell-frogs have tuned themselves to the pitch of certain engines. There is a station in the eastern basin whose whine sits at a steady, piercing note, and for miles around, the frog chorus is built upon it as though it were a tuning fork. Take the station away and the whole ecosystem might lose its key.

Between the stations run old cable-ways and narrow tracks, mostly swallowed. Occasionally a line of rusted rail emerges from the moss, follows a ridge for a hundred yards, and vanishes again. Occasionally there is a gear the size of a wagon wheel sunk in a stream, worn smooth, colonized by snails. The makers' marks are faint. Nobody knows where they went.

## The Rules

- **Nothing is silent.** A silent place is a place where something is hunting.
- **Warmth is the map.** Follow the heat and you find water, food, and machines. Leave it and you find cold, thin moss and empty rock.
- **The ground is alive with pressure.** Drifters, steam, and the plateau's slow breathing all speak through it. A traveler learns to stand still and read it through the soles of the feet.
- **What glows is dead.** In the rare places where rot produces a faint phosphorescence, the creatures avoid it, because light means decay, and decay means the thing that came to eat it.

## Standing in It

Stop at the edge of a marsh terrace, hand on a warm iron rail that some vanished engineer bolted to the rock. Below you, the bell-frogs hold their chord. To the left, a station groans and exhales steam. Far off, the drifters send a long, low sentence through the basin that you feel in your ribs. Overhead, clickers pour through the flute-tree crowns in a crackling stream, and the whole forest, tuned and hollow, breathes a slow wind-song through ten thousand pale throats. Warm rain begins, a distant roar building toward you.

You cannot see any of this. You do not need to. The place is fully, overwhelmingly there, an old wet continent in the dark, singing to itself.
