# The Pour Country

At this height the sky is a thin, hard blue, and the sun has nothing to argue with. Everything here is lit twice: once from above, and again from below by the glazed and burnished surfaces that cover the land like a second weather.

There is no ground in the old sense. Where you would expect soil or scree there is **slab**, a pale grey-green cast mineral, poured in courses and still faintly warm in the deep seams. Someone made the mountains here, and the mountains are not finished.

## The shape of it

The region is a high basin about a day's walk across, ringed by ridgelines so sharp they look unweathered because they are. Their edges still carry the fine parallel grain of the formwork that held them. You can put a fingernail into the seam-lines and feel the ghost of the plank. Nothing has had time to round them. Frost has only begun: small bright flakes at the arêtes, a few fans of grit at the foot of each wall, clean and angular, the first crumbs of the first erosion.

Inside the ring, the basin is packed. Terraces stack on terraces. Retaining walls lean on other retaining walls. Foundry sheds sit shoulder to shoulder with condensing houses, and both are threaded by pipe-runs as thick as trees, lagged in white cloth and stitched with copper bands. Gantries cross at three or four heights, so that a walker on the middle level sees the undersides of one road and the tops of another. Stairs branch off stairs. Wherever a gap opens between structures, something narrower has been built into it: a ladder-way, a duct, a shelf for lamps. Nowhere is empty, but nowhere is crowded either, because every part has been fitted to its neighbors with the tolerance of a good drawer.

The scale still opens outward. From the high catwalks you can see the whole basin: a mile of roofs like fish scales, ranks of slow smokestacks, and beyond them the cast peaks, which are stepped and faceted and bright as salt.

## The engines

Industry is everywhere and it is almost silent. The engines are enormous, and their cycles are so slow that they have fallen below sound. A flywheel the size of a cistern turns once in several minutes. A beam-piston in the central works rises for the length of a long breath, pauses, and descends. You feel these engines in your sternum and the soles of your feet, a pressure that arrives and recedes, and you hear almost nothing of them but the small consequences: a tick of expanding steel, the sigh of a valve, a chain settling on its sprocket.

The dominant sounds are therefore small ones. Water dripping into cast basins, each drip with its own pitch, so that a wall of condensers plays a slow, shifting scale. Wind moving through louvers. Wings. The dry clicking of insects in the pipe-lagging. A person speaking at normal volume seems loud, and people mostly don't.

## Water

Water is scarce and everything is arranged around it. The air is cool and holds mist. It never quite rains, and then it does: a brief fine fall, once every few days, out of a cloud that seems to have wandered in by mistake. The whole basin is built to receive it. Roof-planes tilt toward gutters, gutters toward channels, channels toward cisterns, and the cisterns are glazed white so that a filling one flashes.

Between rains, condensation does the work. Mesh screens stand on every ridge, and cold plates hang in shaded courtyards beaded with moisture. The drip from them is collected, filtered through beds of pumice, and sent down through a hundred small locks and weirs. In a few places the water is allowed to remain uncollected. Small pools sit in cast basins where the workers have decided not to drain them, black and perfectly still, each one reflecting a fragment of the sky at a slightly wrong angle.

## Green things

Vegetation is sparse and cared for. Cushion-lichen, orange and pale green, spreads across the north faces of slabs in slow rosettes, and the workers know individual colonies and note their growth in chalk on the wall beside them. Tough tufts of grass grow from expansion joints. In sheltered courtyards a few dwarf trees, gnarled and wind-bent, grow from iron tubs, and their leaves are small and waxy, adapted to a place that has just recently become habitable. Moss stitches the seam beneath every drip. The green is precious because there is so little of it, and it looks unreal against the pale mineral.

## Animals

The fauna is abundant and lives in the structure of the place as though it had grown there.

- **Slab-goats**, small, compact, rust and cream, move along catwalks and up the stepped terraces, grazing lichen. They have learned the gratings and the stair-risers; their hooves make a precise, dry patter that carries far in the quiet.
- **Flues**, small grey birds, nest in the warm housings of the great engines and sleep in the exhaust plumes. They fly in tight, whispering flocks that pour through the pipe-gaps like smoke.
- **Condensation beetles**, blue-black, stand head-down on the cold plates at dawn and drink the drops that gather on their backs.
- **Lamp moths** cluster on the bright walls in thousands, and at midday the white glaze crawls with slow pale wings.
- Small foxlike things live in the drains and are rarely seen, though their tracks are printed in the dust on every stair.

The animals are unafraid. They treat the industry as terrain, and the workers as weather.

## The breakdown

Here the country stops behaving.

The slab is still curing, and where it has not fully set, the rules are unfinished. The workers call these places *soft*. They are not marked, but everyone can feel them, in the way you feel a step that isn't there. In a soft place:

- **Distance is uncertain.** A gantry that looks fifty paces long takes two hundred to cross, or eleven. Counting steps doesn't help; the count comes out different each time and is never wrong.
- **Stairs fail to agree with themselves.** A flight descends to a landing that turns out to be the underside of the landing above. People walk through it upside down without noticing until they look back.
- **Light comes from the wrong place.** Shadows lean toward the sun. A wall will be lit on both faces and dark in the middle, and if you put your hand in that dark it is cold as a well.
- **Water misbehaves.** In one gully the rain falls upward, beading on the underside of a bridge. In another the cistern is full whenever you look at it and empty whenever you walk to it.
- **Copies appear.** The animals suffer most. Flocks of flues include birds that are identical in every mark and feather. A slab-goat may have a second shadow, or a third eye that does not blink. The workers do not chase these away. Some think the country is still deciding what a goat is.

Softness is not chaos. It advances and recedes on a cycle of seasons. A soft stair may harden in a year, and a stable terrace may turn soft after a long dry spell. When a soft place sets, it sets exactly as it was in its last moment, with its errors made permanent. The upside-down landing becomes simply the way that landing is, and children raised beside it walk it without a glance. This is how the Pour Country grows: new ground, cast slightly wrong, becomes correct by staying.

## Work and habit

The people, and there are many, though you will notice them last, live in the gaps. They wear pale wool and reflective visors against the glare. They tend the engines by touch and by counting, listening for the change in a pressure they can't hear. Their days are spent adjusting valves, chalking lichen, scraping mineral scale from condensers, and walking the boundaries of the soft places with long rods, tapping.

Each evening the sun drops behind the eastern ring and the whole basin holds its light a while longer, as the burnished terraces give back the last of the sky. Then the lamps come on, thousands, each in its own niche, each reflected in a hundred wet surfaces. The engines go on turning at their impossible pace. The cold deepens. The drips continue. Somewhere in the dark, the slab is setting, slowly, into whatever it has decided to be.
