# The Shelf of Unfinished Things

You are two hundred meters down and the light is still good. It comes through the water the color of thin milk-glass, green at the edges and blue-white overhead. When you look up you see the underside of the sea's surface, a hammered silver ceiling that never stops moving. A storm is always working up there. Its waves arrive below as slow pressure, a breathing in the body more than anything visible. Sometimes a squall's footprint passes over, a dark bruise of scattered light a few kilometers wide, and moves on.

This is a plateau, and it is high. The old basins that hold the rest of this world's water lie four or five kilometers below its edges, and the shelf stands above them like a table in a flooded hall. The water on top of it is cool, about the temperature of a spring that never sees the sun. It is clear enough that you can see a long way, and there is almost nothing in the way of seeing. That emptiness is the first thing you notice. Sight runs out to the limit of the blue with nothing in it, no shoals, no drifting bodies, no shadow of anything alive. Nothing here has ever been alive in the sense of having a mouth.

## The Floor

The floor is not finished. Rock is being made here, not worn away. In most places there is a pale gray-white crust with the texture of wet plaster, and in the low places a dark, glassy basalt, freshly chilled and unweathered, with edges sharp enough to cut. Nothing has yet had the time to round them. Erosion is only a rumor on this shelf. It is starting here and there, as a slight furring on the oldest ridges.

The crust grows. If you hold still near a seam, you can watch the water cloud with a fine white precipitate, like milk poured into tea. It settles as a slow snow of carbonate and silica. It lands in a skin, and the skin thickens into layers, and the layers curl into terraces with rims like the edges of rice paddies, each holding a shallow pool of denser, colder, more mineral water. Some terraces are the size of dinner plates. Others are the size of cities. Their rims are ruler-straight for a while, then bend abruptly where the current has argued with them.

Vents open in the plateau's fractures. Some are warm and cloudy, and some pour out a clear, heavy fluid that runs downhill along the bed like smoke, a river inside the sea. These brine rivers cut real channels, with banks and meanders and small waterfalls where the dense fluid spills over a step. They shine and shimmer where they meet the ordinary water, like heat over a road. The channels are the only rivers this place has, and they flow in the wrong medium.

## The Sound

The place is loud, and you feel it before you understand it.

Water carries sound four times faster than air, without the softening that air gives, so everything arrives in the chest and the teeth. There is a constant low roar from the surface storm, a smeared pressure at the bottom of hearing. Over it come the cracks. When a terrace rim gets too heavy with new stone, it lets go with a report like a rifle shot, and the sound rolls across the plateau, comes back off the far scarps, and comes back again. Those cracks are the shelf's clock. Every few seconds one goes off somewhere in the dark, and some are close enough to make you flinch.

Under the cracks is the singing.

Out in the open water, strung between points you cannot see, hang the filaments. They are hair-thin lines, perhaps a finger's width of braided something, taut across kilometers. Each is anchored at one end to the floor and at the other to a pale buoy that drifts at depth like a lantern with nothing lit inside it. The current plucks them, and they sound. Because each one is tensioned differently, the plateau has a chord that changes with the flow. It is huge, mournful, and slightly out of tune, and it moves between major and minor as the currents shift. Where two filaments run near each other, their notes beat against each other in slow throbs, like a pulse.

Nobody strung them for music. That is not their function. But the water has made them a choir all the same.

## What Grows

The vegetation is sparse. It is one kind of plant, if it is a plant. It is a sheet-frond, a translucent ribbon of a dull olive-gold that grows straight up from cracks in the rock. Each ribbon is a meter wide and up to thirty meters tall, and it flutters and snaps in the current. They stand in loose, distant groves, ten or twenty together, then nothing for a kilometer, then another grove. They photosynthesize in this good light and give off streams of tiny bubbles that rise in silver threads, so each grove looks as if it were pouring upward, quietly, while the water around it roars.

Their roots eat the raw rock, and this is the closest the shelf comes to soil. Where a grove has stood for a while, the stone under it has softened to a gray paste, and the first true sediment has begun to gather. That patch is the oldest place on the plateau, and it is perhaps a few centuries old.

Between the groves is a thin film of green-black mat on the terraces that lie in the light. It looks like nothing at first, and then you notice how it is slick on your palm, and how it leaves a stain that lasts.

## The Machinery Nobody Sees

The tech level here is beyond anything a modern reader would recognize, and none of it looks like technology. There are almost no structures. There are the filaments and their buoys, and a few pale, wide, patient disks on the floor, smooth as river stones and as big as houses, set in a ring around each major vent. They hum below the level of the singing. They are the only obviously made things on the plateau. Their surfaces are warm, and a fine dust of something lifts from their edges and drifts up into the current.

The dust is the point. The whole shelf is being seeded. The clouds of white precipitate do not fall wherever chemistry happens to drop them. They fall where the dust has been, and the dust has been laid in patterns. If you follow one of the long cloudy plumes for an hour you will see it fold into a terrace that turns out to be a curve of enormous, precise radius. Zoom out, and if you could rise a few kilometers, you would see that the terraces make a pattern, a slow arithmetic of arcs and spirals across a hundred kilometers, growing in one place, stalled in another.

Someone or something is building a continent from the bottom of the sea, and it is doing so by persuading the water. It cannot be called finished, and it cannot be called abandoned. Sometimes a whole quarter of the plateau goes quiet for weeks. The disks dim, the singing thins, the precipitation stops. Then something wakes, a wave of seeding rolls across, and the floor starts growing again.

Some engineering here breaks the physics you know. Certain brine channels run uphill for short stretches, and cold pools stay separate from warm water with no membrane between them. There are places where the sound just stops, sharp as a wall, and the silence behind it is total. Divers say those silences are the most frightening thing here, and they cannot explain why. They feel like a held breath.

## Turbulence

Nothing here stays still. The plateau is a weather system of its own, because it is large enough to have one. Cold water drains off its edges in enormous slow waterfalls that plunge into the basins below. The plateau's surface layer, warmed by the sun and stirred by the storm, rides in at the top to replace what drained. Those are the great currents, and they run for days at a stretch in one direction and then, with no warning, reverse.

When they reverse, the whole plateau shudders. Turbulence fills the water with silt and shredded frond and scraps of white crust. The filaments howl. Visibility drops from a kilometer to arm's length. Then sometimes, in the middle of it, a mineral storm arrives. Precipitate comes down as a blizzard and the floor vanishes under a foot of new stone within an hour. Objects set down during the blizzard, a tool or a glove, are entombed and become part of the geology of the place. There is no stopping this. A month later there is a fresh terrace where you left something, and it looks a thousand years old.

Sometimes, at the edge of the plateau, you can stand at the lip and watch the shelf pour itself into the abyss. Dense water spills over the rim in a slow, seething, silver curtain, and beneath it the dark goes down for miles.

## The Rule of It

Everything here is decided by a simple law: the water is always slightly too full. It carries more than it wants to carry, and it wants to set something down. Everything on the shelf is the water's attempt to set something down. That is where the rock comes from, and the terraces, the seeding, and the choir. It is also where the shelf's stillness ends. Nothing rests until it has been laid down, and nothing is laid down without something else being torn up to make room.

If you stay long enough, the noise stops being noise. It becomes a kind of weather in your body. You learn the cracks, the chord, and the dim hum of the disks. And you learn the empty water, the great cool clarity with nothing in it, as the most complete thing here. It is a vast unpeopled blue with a whole continent being made in it, slowly and loudly, for no one.
