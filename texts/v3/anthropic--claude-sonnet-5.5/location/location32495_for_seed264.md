# The Governor Flats

You hear it long before the ground gives you anything to look at. The sound is a low, even roar, like a great mill running in the next valley. It doesn't rise or fall, and it doesn't stop. Under it lie other sounds: a piston-like *chuff* every few seconds, a high whistling that never changes pitch, and a knocking in the ground like a loose bearing in a slow wheel. After an hour you stop hearing it as noise. It becomes the silence of the place, and when you speak, your voice seems to come from somewhere outside it.

## The ground

The flats lie low, barely above the level where the land would turn to marsh, and they spread in every direction until warm haze takes them. The surface is a pale, hard sinter, the mineral crust left by ten thousand thousand evaporations. It is cream at the center, then ochre and rust-orange toward the margins, then a dull iron-grey where the old water has gone. The crust is not smooth. Time has worked it into low terraces with rounded lips, each a hand's width high, stepping down in broad contours like a topographic map drawn in stone. Their edges are worn as soft as soap. Nothing sharp survives here. This landscape finished being carved so long ago that its shapes have stopped remembering the carving.

Between the terraces sit shallow basins of standing water, warm as a bath and clear enough that you see the bottom as a mosaic of white and green-gold grit. Some are round and some are long, with the shape of a bootprint or a leaf. Their rims are beaded with tiny knobs of mineral like cauliflower. The water doesn't ripple, or rather it ripples in a fixed pattern that never moves.

The air is damp, with the warmth of a laundry. Condensation beads on every cool surface: the undersides of overhanging sinter lips, the stones, your sleeves. A soft rain falls now and then, but it is not weather. It is the steam column's own exhaust, cooled at height and drifting back as a fine warm drizzle, always on the same downwind arc. Walk out of the arc and you are dry. Walk into it and the mist beads on your skin, smelling of struck flint and boiled eggs.

## The engine

The whole basin is a machine, though nobody built it.

Beneath the sinter, two aquifers lie one above the other: a hot, gas-rich one deep down, and a cool one nearer the surface. Between them, over some unimaginable span, the rock has grown a set of mineral valves. Vents open in a ring around the center and seal themselves with silica, and the pressure behind them builds until they open again. There are perhaps forty of these, in three sizes.

- **The Stacks** are cone-mouthed chimneys of sinter, shoulder-high to house-high, each with a throat worn glassy smooth. Each breathes out a white plume in a fixed rhythm, and the plumes stand in ranks like the exhausts of a factory that lost its walls. They fire in sequence, from the nearest to the farthest and then back, and a slow wave of *chuff-chuff-chuff* rolls around the ring and returns to its start.
- **The Whistlers** are narrow fissures in the crust, no wider than a finger, which force out steam at such speed that they scream a single clean note. There are seven, tuned by their slot widths. Together they hold a chord, a slightly sour one, that sounds like a distant train horn held forever.
- **The Drum** is a single vast blind cavity under the middle of the flats. Every eleven seconds, gas collects in it and bursts through a flap of sinter with a thud you feel in your sternum. The knocking sound is this. Its rhythm is the pulse of the whole system.

The Drum's flap is the governor. When pressure runs too high, the flap opens sooner and vents the excess. When pressure runs low, the mineral in the seam precipitates and stiffens it, so it holds longer. The system regulates itself the way a steam engine does with its spinning flyballs. It has been doing so for so long that no variation is left. Nothing has changed in the basin for as long as the terraces have had their present shape. The eruptions never grow, the pools never rise, and the wave around the ring never skips a beat. The place isn't dead. It is in perfect balance.

## The thing at the edge of the machine

A few relics lie among the terraces, and they are the only made things here. Long ago, and it might have been any age, someone drilled here. On the northern margin an iron casing pipe rises from the sinter at a slant, the width of a barrel. It is so thickly mineral-coated that it looks like a natural spire of orange stone with a rust-red hole in it. Beside it lies the drum of an old boiler, half swallowed in crust, with a row of rivet-heads still showing beneath the mineral like knuckles under skin. The land has been digesting it for centuries. Perhaps one day it will be entirely stone. Those who cannot forget the world before the basin sometimes come here and put an ear against the boiler. It rings faintly, in sympathy with the Drum.

## Life

Nothing green grows here. The water is too hot, too mineral, too full of sulfur. But the pool margins are streaked with mats of microbial colour, orange, lime, brick red, laid in bands by temperature like the stripes of a flag. They are the only vegetation, and they have no stems or leaves, only a slick, gelatinous skin. On the cooler outer terraces they thicken into pale ropes that hang from the lips like candle wax.

Fauna is rare. In the middle-warm pools lives a small, translucent, many-legged creature, hardly bigger than a grain of rice, which grazes the mats in slow circles. Through the day, gulls or something like them, grey and thin-winged, come in from the far coast. They stand at the cool edges, and their calls, which would be loud anywhere else, are lost entirely under the roar. You see their beaks open and hear nothing. Once in a long while, a larger animal, some pale, long-legged wader, will pick its way across the terraces from one pool to another. It doesn't flinch when a Stack fires beside it. It has never known a moment of quiet.

## Light

The sky is a plain, bright white-blue, and the light is normal and even. The steam plumes catch it and scatter it so that the whole basin glows softly, without hard shadows. Around midday the column tops flare gold. Shadows do lengthen as the sun crosses, but the plumes shift them in a rhythm so regular that you soon feel the light is also part of the machine.

## The rule of the place

Everything in the Flats repeats. The plume wave circuits the ring. The Drum strikes every eleven seconds. The Whistlers hold their chord. The mists fall on the same arc of ground. If you stay long enough, and few do, you find that your own breath and heartbeat start to fall in with the Drum, and you walk in its time. Then the roar falls away completely, and what remains is something like being inside an enormous, patient, unsleeping clock, with all its parts turning exactly as they always have and always will.
