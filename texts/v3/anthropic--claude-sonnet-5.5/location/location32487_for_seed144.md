# The Sweating Plateau

The land is five thousand meters up and still hot. This is the first thing the body has to reconcile. The air is thin enough to make you count your breaths, and the sun is hard and white, but the heat doesn't come only from above. It rises through your boot soles. Somewhere under the tableland, not very deep, an old magma body sits close to the surface and warms the whole plateau from below like a hand pressed under a table. Snow-line air lies over a floor as warm as a bread oven's shoulder.

## The ground

The plateau runs for many days' walking in every direction, a wide, faintly tilted upland of pale volcanic tuff, ash-flow sheets, and cracked mineral crust. Long ago it was cut into low mesas, shallow canyons, and rounded, patient hills. The erosion here is old and settled. The gullies no longer deepen, the mesa edges have slumped into stable aprons of rubble, and everything has reached an angle it can hold. Nothing is raw. The rock has the look of something that stopped arguing with the weather a very long time ago.

The surface is packed with small features. Every few paces there is a fumarole cone the height of a knee or a house, a sinter terrace stepping down in rims of cream and rust, a mud pot ticking to itself, a crust of yellow sulfur crystals furred like frost, or a pool the size of a tabletop or a lake, held in a bowl of its own deposits. Nothing here is spacious. The eye moves from one presence to the next with hardly any blank ground between. Even the "empty" tuff is scored with polygonal cracks, dotted with vent-holes, stained in bands of ochre, green-gray, and oxblood.

## Water

Water is the rare thing, and it arrives in strange ways. Rain is occasional and mostly theatrical. In the afternoon, towers of cloud build over the rim ranges, drag gray curtains beneath them, and the rain evaporates before it touches the ground. These are the virga, and they hang like unraveled wool. Sometimes the rain does land, in a brief hard burst that steams off the hot rock and fills the air with the smell of struck flint.

The dependable water comes from below and from the air's leftovers. Steam rises from the vents all night and condenses on anything cooler than itself. At dusk, when the surface temperature drops, the porous tuff begins to sweat. Its shaded faces darken and bead, and thin sheets of water run down them into catch-hollows that the animals know by heart. These are the *weeping walls*, and they are the reason anything lives on the plateau in numbers. By full dark the walls are wet from top to bottom and glitter under the stars. By mid-morning they are dry and chalky again.

The pools are hot, warm, or merely tepid, and the color tells you which. Near-boiling water is clear and blue-black. Cooler water is edged with mats of orange, green, and brick-red microbes laid down in layers like felted cloth. In the coolest shallows, brine shrimp and tiny red mites swarm so thickly that the water looks stirred with paint.

## Life

Vegetation is sparse, and it is scattered in tight clusters where the water is. There are cushion plants like green stones, so dense you could stand on them, with tiny leaves that hold dew. There are wiry tussock grasses in the seeps, and low resinous shrubs with gray felted leaves that grow only on the warm side of the vent-hills. Between the clusters there is lichen in black, orange, and chalk-white, and nothing else for long distances.

The fauna is out of proportion to the plants, which is the plateau's first oddity. It is abundant, and it is packed around the water like a crowd around a fire. Herds of slender, long-necked grazers, thick-woolled and pale, move between the weeping walls in the evening, and lick the stone until their tongues are gray. Flocks of pink-legged wading birds fill the warm shallows shoulder to shoulder, sifting shrimp with sideways sweeps of their bills. Lizards flatten themselves on the vent-lips and shuffle around the rim as the sun climbs, to keep their bellies at the right temperature. Beetles the size of thumbnails plow trails through the ash. Small burrowing rodents live in the warm slopes and use the ground heat as their winter. Foxlike hunters patrol the seeps at dusk and work the edges of the herds. Above them all, broad-winged scavengers ride the thermals that rise off the vents, tilting on air that smells of sulfur.

The predator-prey drama plays out around the water in a tight, constant, low-key way. Every seep is a negotiation: the grazers come in shifts, the birds scatter and resettle, a fox lies in the shadow of a sinter rim for an hour without moving, and a lizard is gone in one snap. It is busy without being loud.

## The made things

People, or the industry of people, are scattered thinly across all this. A geothermal station stands on one of the larger vent fields. It is a riveted, sun-bleached complex of steel pipe, insulated manifolds, and a squat turbine hall, with a single tall stack that emits a soft white plume. It still runs. A few engineers or none tend it, and the turbine gives off a low steady hum that is the plateau's only mechanical sound, felt in the sternum before it is heard.

From the station, pipelines run out across the tuff on stilts, wrapped in silver foil and rust, to wellheads a kilometer or two away. Some of the pipes have been colonized. Mineral crust has grown over their joints in cream-colored scabs, lizards sun on the warm insulation, and small birds nest in the gaps under the supports. Farther out is an abandoned sulfur works, a scatter of iron ore-carts on a narrow-gauge track, a winch house with its cable snapped, and a line of rusted buckets that once rode a tramway down the mesa wall. The sulfur has crept over them all in yellow blooms. The animals use these ruins like any other landform.

## Sound and weather

The plateau is mostly quiet. The thin air carries little, and wind is a dry, sighing presence rather than a roar. What you hear is small: the tick and gurgle of mud pots, a hiss from a hidden vent, the trickle of the weeping walls at dusk, the scrape of hooves on sinter, and now and then a shrill bird-call that seems to cross the entire upland. Under everything runs the turbine's hum.

The day goes in a cycle. Dawn is steam and pink light, with fog lifting off every pool. The morning grows harder and whiter. At noon the animals go still in whatever shade they can find. Clouds gather by afternoon, and the virga stroke down and dissolve. At dusk the walls begin to sweat, and the whole plateau comes alive, moving and drinking. At night the stars are enormous and crowded, the ground stays warm underfoot, and the vents breathe pale plumes up into a sky that has no other clouds.
