# The Sweating Cordillera

The mountains here are only a few million years old, and they have not finished arriving. Ridges stand at forty degrees with edges you could cut a hand on: black basalt and green-veined andesite, unrounded, unsoftened, still shedding fresh scree that has not yet learned to lie flat. The range runs on past every horizon. Its plateau lies three thousand meters up, and it is wide enough that it makes its own climate. Storms form over it, orbit it, and die in it without ever seeing the sea.

It is warm at that height, which is wrong, and nobody who stands there stops noticing. The warmth comes up from below. The crust is thin and the ground has a pulse, a slow heat that rises through moss and root mat until the air under the canopy is close and body-temperature, like breathing in a mouth.

## Light

There is very little sun. A permanent cloud deck sits a few hundred meters above the plateau and glows the dull pewter of a lamp behind paper. Under it the forest is brown-green dusk at noon. The trees hold what light there is: bracket fungi glow the color of weak tea, and the undersides of leaves give off a faint phosphor-blue. Every trunk wears a collar of pale luminous lichen at the height where the mist thickens. You can walk all day by the light of the plants and never once cast a sharp shadow.

## Water

There are almost no rivers, and rain is rare and brief, arriving as a single heavy, warm, metallic-tasting curtain that lasts four minutes and is gone. The water here is mostly fog. It condenses on everything, all day, in every crevice, from a cloud that never fully lifts. Leaves drip, moss holds it like a sponge, and every surface wears a skin of moisture. The ground is damp but not wet. It is the wetness of a cellar or a held breath.

Where the water gathers, it gathers in pockets: tarns in the craters of dead vents, black and perfectly round, and cisterns of rainwater in the cups of giant bromeliads, each a small ecosystem that has been fermenting for centuries. Between the pockets the rock is dry and brittle under its film of mist, the moisture skating on top without ever soaking in. The whole range is a dry thing wearing a wet coat.

## Vegetation

The growth is dense to the point of contact. It has no room, so it has layers. Tree ferns as tall as houses hold canopies of thumb-thick fronds. Beneath them are strangler-vines, then epiphyte gardens hung in the vines, and beneath those, mats of moss so thick that a fallen trunk is only a rumor in the shape of a ridge. Nothing has bare bark. Every surface is someone else's ground. When you push through, plants brush against you from all sides at once, damp and warm and faintly oily, and there is no way to walk that is not also a way of being touched.

The colors are wrong for a jungle. The leaves run to oxblood, verdigris, and the blue-black of iron slag, because the plants here draw metal from the young rock. The soil is raw, mineral, and unweathered, still full of everything that hasn't yet been leached out. Flowers open in rust orange and copper green. The bark of one common tree flakes off in plates that ring faintly when they fall.

## The Works

Here is what the place does that nothing has ever asked it to.

There are no buildings, no roads, and no made thing anywhere. But there is industry, and it is everywhere, and it belongs to no one. The vents vent in rhythm. Steam rises through channels in the rock, and where the channels narrow, the steam whistles or pushes. Over a million years, slow deposits have shaped those channels into chambers with valves of mineral flowstone, and those valves open and close under pressure like the stroke of a piston. Whole hillsides breathe in twelve-second cycles. Nothing designed this. It simply *works*, and the working has no purpose the mountain could name.

Living things have found the work and joined it. Mineral-eating bacteria cover the vent-fed slopes in orange and black mats and run continuous reduction reactions: iron oxide in, pure metallic threads out, which the mats extrude as bright hair-fine wires that curl in the mist like fiddleheads. Beetles the size of fists harvest the wires and pack them into their nests in tight coils. Silverfish-like things weave the coils into sheets. The sheets are carried by larger animals, who don't know why, to the rims of certain craters, where they are left in stacks. Then the steam comes and presses the stacks flat. Something later eats the pressed plates. Something else excretes them as pellets. The pellets fall on the bacterial mats and become the feed for the next round.

That is a production line, and it runs continuously across a hundred and forty kilometers of plateau with no factory, no owner, and no beginning. Each stage belongs to a different species, and none of them knows the others exist. Sometimes a stage fails and the whole slope stalls, silent and still, for days. Then some unrelated accident restarts it. The mountain treats the loop as a habit, not a purpose.

## Fauna

The animals are numerous and mechanical in their motions. There are ranks of long-legged herbivores that move in strict two-beat gaits, and they are not quite deer. There are pressure-chambered frogs whose throats fill with steam from the vents and discharge in one clear pop. There are heavy, armored, slow, patient things like walking boilers, with plates that vent heat at regular intervals, leaving small clouds of steam trailing behind them in the undergrowth. Birds are abundant but nearly silent. They glide between the trunks on wide flat wings, and the only sound is the faint tick of their feathers against one another, like a clock heard through a wall.

They are everywhere, in every layer: in the litter, in the bark, on the underside of every leaf. The air is full of small wings. A patch of forest a body wide holds more species than some continents.

## The Quiet

The plateau is mostly quiet. The moss and the mist eat sound. Footsteps make no echo. The steam-vents' rhythmic pulse is felt in the feet before it is heard. Even the storms are muffled: lightning is a flash in the cloud deck and the thunder arrives so softened that it feels like a pressure on the eardrums, a change of altitude with no altitude.

When sounds do come, they come cleanly. A single ring of a falling bark plate. The pop of a frog. The long low exhale of a valve-hill letting go. Each is very distinct, because there is so little to compete with it.

## The Breakdown

This is a place where the rules are not fully in force. Reality here is loose at the seams, and the looseness comes and goes.

The first thing you may notice is repetition. On one slope every fern frond is identical, down to the tiny chip in the third leaflet from the tip, stamped ten thousand times as if from a die. A day later the die has changed, and the chip is gone or moved. The flaw itself seems to be what is being manufactured.

Then you notice that the sound of something is arriving before the thing itself. You hear the pop of a frog and then, a few seconds later, the frog swells its throat and makes the pop. Sound leads cause. Elsewhere the reverse happens, and things happen in silence and the noise comes hours after, from somewhere else, from nothing.

Distances are unreliable. A ridge that you climb toward recedes; a ridge you turn away from is suddenly at your shoulder. The horizon has faint seams, thin lines where two textures of land meet that don't belong together. The ground on one side of a seam is old and mossy, and on the other it is raw and glassy, still hot, still cooling, as though the range were being assembled from parts of different ages and hadn't yet decided which to keep.

Shadows sometimes fall toward the light. Mist sometimes falls upward. Metal threads growing from the mats will occasionally grow *back into* the rock, as if the mountain were absorbing its own product. Once in a while a beetle carries a coil to a crater rim and finds a stack already waiting there that it has never made, and puts its own coil down beside it, and neither is more real than the other.

## Turbulence

Nothing is stable. Ridges rise while you watch. A slope that was forest at dawn is a slump of steaming mud by evening, and the plants on it are already reseeding, greening in a matter of days. New vents open, old ones choke. The mist changes direction without warning, and weather cells the size of countries wander over the plateau, spawning and dissolving. The production loops shift their routes constantly, so that a trail of beetles and pressed plates may vanish and appear again forty kilometers to the west.

The whole plateau seethes, though quietly, with a warm damp pressure that never quite releases. It is a landscape in the middle of making itself, under a dim lid of cloud, with all its machinery running and nobody at the controls. It keeps producing something, and nothing living or unliving there could tell you what the product is, or who it is for, or whether it is already all around you, condensing on your skin and dripping from your fingers into the moss.
