# The Settled Basin

Everything here has finished falling. The hills that once ringed this place were carried out grain by grain over a span no one counted, and what remains is a shallow dish about a kilometer across, so level that a dropped marble would hesitate. The rim is a suggestion, a slow lift of the ground to the height of a man's shoulder, furred with fern. Beyond it the land is just as flat and just as green. The basin is only where the flatness holds its water.

Water is never more than a hand's depth below your foot, and usually it is above the ground: a bright skin a finger deep over pale clay, moving so slowly that the only proof is a drifting petal. The clay is the color of the inside of a shell. It is the last residue of mountains, sorted so many times that it has no grit left and feels like cool flour under the water. In a few places the old bedrock shows through as low, rounded backs of black stone, polished by so many ages of sheet-flow that they look like the shoulders of sleeping animals. Water beads on them and weeps from their seams all day. Nothing cuts here anymore. The streams have no incision in them, only a slight preference.

## Light

The sun is high, warm, and unhindered, and the water throws it back so that the underside of every leaf is lit gold-green. Shadows are thin and pooled tightly under things. The air is thick and heated, like the breath of a greenhouse, and it condenses on anything cooler than skin. Mornings come up out of the ground as a low white steam that burns off by mid-morning and leaves the whole basin ringing with brightness.

## Plants

The vegetation is lush but arranged with wide gaps, like a garden that has been curated for a very long time by something patient. Tree ferns stand alone at long intervals, each with a skirt of dead fronds and a crown that tilts a few degrees toward the center. Between them are rafts of floating mat-plants, round and dense, each an island of small white flowers that open only when a shadow crosses them. Reed-stands grow in perfect ellipses, always aligned in the same direction, as if combed. Under the surface, long ribbon-grasses stream in the current and hold fine silver bubbles along their edges.

The moss on the black stones is the deepest green you will ever see, almost violet in shade. It is warm to the touch. Something in it metabolizes hard enough to heat itself.

## The Animals

The fauna is overwhelming, and almost none of it makes a sound.

You notice it slowly. First the water seems to have a grain, then you see that the grain is alive: clouds of clear shrimp-like things the size of rice, so dense in the shallows that the water turns to a moving lens. Above the surface hang drifting veils of winged insects, each flickering a pale pulse of light, thousands in sync, so the air seems to breathe in slow waves of brightness that pass through the haze. Frogs the size of thumbnails cover the reeds like beadwork, and when disturbed they pour off in a sheet without a splash. Long translucent eels slide through the clay and leave trails of tiny phosphor. Birds are there, big-eyed, heron-tall, standing motionless in the shallows at intervals so regular they seem placed, and their throats flutter with calls pitched too high or too low to hear. You feel them mostly as a pressure behind the teeth.

Everything speaks in light, scent, and ground-vibration. Put a palm flat on a black stone and you feel the whole ecosystem murmuring through it, a fine continuous tremor like a purring cat the size of a hill. It is the most crowded place you have ever been, and the quietest.

Predation happens constantly and without drama. A heron's neck unfolds and a shoal of silver simply becomes less. A carnivorous mat-plant closes, and a small green flash goes dark inside it. The dead sink into the clay and the clay takes them back within hours. No bones survive anywhere.

## The Built Things

Scattered across the basin, very far apart, are the works.

They are pale, seamless, and matte, and they do not weather. The nearest to the rim is a slender spire about four times a person's height, leaning at a small angle, its surface faintly pearled. It is not stone and not ceramic. Dragonflies avoid it, but the frogs sleep pressed against its warm side. If you stand close you hear the only mechanical sound in the basin: a low, slow, three-note hum, like a distant cello held for a very long time.

Farther in there is a ring of eleven low arches standing in the water, each just wide enough to step through. The water inside the ring is perfectly still while all the water outside creeps along. Light falls into it wrongly. Objects seen through the arches appear a half-second ahead of where they are, so a heron seems to strike before it moves. Nothing lives inside the ring, but nothing dies there either. A leaf placed on that water stays green for days.

Near the center is what might once have been a building, or a single enormous instrument. It is a shallow white dome, half sunk, its curve broken by a wide round mouth into which water slowly runs and does not fill. The lip is lined with fine, ordered growth, coral-like, that the animals have clearly tended or been bred into. Tiny creatures move along it in files, cleaning, carrying, replacing. Whatever the dome does, it has been doing it since before the hills were gone, and the ecology of the basin is arranged around it the way a village might crowd around a well.

## Rhythm

At a fixed hour of the afternoon, a single cloud forms directly over the dome, dense and low, no larger than a field. It builds in minutes, gathering the day's steam into itself with a soft pulling sensation in the ears. Then it rains, hard and warm, on exactly the basin and nowhere beyond. The surface film thickens, the mats lift, and for an hour the water runs slightly toward the center instead of away. Every animal stops. The insect-light goes out in a single ripple. In that hour, and only then, the basin makes real noise: a broad hush of falling water, and beneath it the spire's hum climbing one full step.

Then the rain lifts, the light comes back in a hot rush, and the pulses start again, first one, then a thousand, until the air is breathing gold once more.

## What It Feels Like

You feel small, but not threatened. The place has no interest in you and no ill will. It is old beyond any drama, worn down to the last stable arrangement of water, green, and quiet machinery, and it goes on being alive at enormous density in the smallest possible space. You could walk across it in twenty minutes. You could stand in it for a year and still find a new thing turning in the shallows.
