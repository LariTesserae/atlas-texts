# The Drum Beneath the Snowfield

From outside there is nothing to see. A high shoulder of a young mountain, five thousand meters up, is covered in old blue-grey ice and wind-scoured snow. Under one unremarkable acre of it, sealed in the rock, is a room full of water about four hundred meters across. It is roaring.

## The Ceiling

The roof is glacier ice, thinned by heat from below to a lens the color of a gas flame's base. Daylight comes through it as a hard, clean, almost ordinary white light, dimmed a little and split into shifting bars. The bars swing as the surface above changes with cloud and sun. They cross the chamber like the arms of a slow clock. In their beams the water is clear enough to show every grain of the floor sixty meters down.

The chamber is not calm. Where the light goes, bubbles go too. Gas seeps up from the floor in ropes and sheets and hangs against the ice in silver blisters that merge, break loose, and fall upward in slow avalanches. Each bubble catches the light as it rises. The whole upper volume glitters and shudders like a struck sheet of foil.

## The Sound

The noise is the first fact of the place and the last. It is not a single noise. The chamber is a closed vessel of water and rock, and every disturbance in it has nowhere to go. It rings.

- **The vents** on the floor blast hot mineral water in columns, with a sound like a furnace being torn open.
- **The ice** groans, pops, and shrieks as the lens flexes. Cracks run across it with a bright whip-crack that arrives from every direction at once.
- **The rock walls** tick and grind. The mountain is still rising, and the pocket is being squeezed and stretched. Every few minutes a slab lets go somewhere with a concussion you feel in your teeth and sternum before you hear it.
- **The bubbles** make a fine, continuous hiss, like sand poured on a drumhead.

Under all of it is a deep pulse, a single throb about once every eleven seconds, from the whole chamber acting as one resonator. Nothing that lives here can stop hearing it. Nothing that lives here is quiet.

## The Floor, Under Construction

The rock has barely finished being made. Its erosion is only beginning. Edges are still knife-sharp, and there are no soils, no rounded cobbles, no valleys. The floor is a raw terrace of fresh stone and precipitated mineral, still being deposited from the vent water.

The strange part is how it grows. The mineral does not settle at random. It falls out of solution along the **nodal lines of the chamber's sound**, the places where standing waves cancel and the water is most still. Over decades the floor has been drawn into pattern: concentric ridges of white travertine, ruled radial fins, and fields of evenly spaced stone pillars, each as tall as a person and thin as a wrist. The pillars grow in the dead zones between the loudest pressure peaks. They are hollow at the tip, and some of them hum.

When a rockfall or vent change alters the sound, the pattern begins to move. Old ridges stop growing and start to dissolve. New ones nucleate elsewhere. The floor is a score being written and erased by the noise it responds to. If you could see the chamber in time-lapse, you would watch the sound draw its own architecture in stone.

## Life, Sparse

Vegetation is scarce and mostly a matter of vents. Where warm water seeps, there are tufts of pale green filament, stiff and hair-fine. They sway in the current in synchronized ripples, all bending together at the same instant with each deep pulse. A few rooted, reedlike stalks stand near the warmest fissures, ribbed and translucent, tipped with small dull-gold seed cases that rattle.

Fauna is rare and wary. Three kinds have been noticed:

- **Ghost-eels**, colorless, hand-length, with no obvious eyes. They lie in the dead zones between pillars and thread through the stone flutes as if listening for something. They vanish when the pulse is strongest.
- **Lantern shrimp** in slow clouds that flare when a nearby crack goes off. This may be a startle response, or it may be how they talk.
- **The Sitter**, seen perhaps twice a decade: a single pale, heavy-bodied thing the size of a large dog, with a wide flat head, that rests on the terraces with its face against the stone. It stays motionless for days. It seems to feel the growth of the pillars. Sometimes it moves a stone by a hand's width, and then leaves.

## The Hand-Made

There are marks of people, though nobody lives here.

**Tethers** hang from a bore hole drilled up through the ice. It was made long ago with fire, antler picks, and patience. The lines are braided hide and sinew, weighted with drilled stones. Some are still taut. Others drift loose, frayed at the ends, and swing with the current.

On a broad ledge sixty meters down, held in place by lashed stone weights, is a **diving bell**: a dome of stitched, tallowed hide over a frame of bent wood, with a trapped pocket of air inside. The air has gone stale and cold. Inside are a bone flute, a coil of cord, a scraper of chipped obsidian, and a flat stone incised with tally marks, thousands of them, grouped in elevens.

Scattered along the terraces are **cairns**: small stacks of flat stone, each three or four pieces high and each placed at a node where the sound is quiet. Whoever built them understood that the quiet places could be found. Some are freshly stacked, though no human has come down in living memory. The Sitter, or something else, is maintaining them or making its own.

## Rules of the Place

1. **Everything here is shaped by sound.** Sediment, growth, stone, and probably behavior follow the standing waves. To move through the chamber is to move through a hidden map of loud and still.
2. **The quiet places are real, small, and precious.** A person who finds a node feels the noise fall away to a distant thrum. The tally-keeper must have lived in them.
3. **The chamber changes without warning.** A slab falls and the map is rewritten. The floor you crossed yesterday is no longer where it was.
4. **Nothing is finished.** The rock is still forming. The mountain is still climbing. The room will eventually crack open and drain, or freeze, or fill with stone. Until then the drum keeps beating under the snowfield, and no one on the surface hears it.

Once, a cold wind crossed the ice above and a thin shaft of light dropped straight down through the lens onto a single pillar. The pillar hummed. For a moment, every ghost-eel in the chamber turned toward it.
