# The Sump Terraces of Ahn-Vessel

## Approach

You arrive by the ridge road at four thousand meters, and the first thing that reaches you is sound, not sight. There is no sun to see by. The cloud deck sits a few hundred meters overhead and hasn't lifted in living memory. Under it, the air is close to freezing, not quite cold enough to make ice, and so saturated that your breath doesn't show. It just joins everything else. Your sleeves darken. Your eyelashes bead.

The sound comes up from the basin ahead: a broad, layered roar, part surf and part cicada-choir, with a slow throbbing beneath it like a pump the size of a cathedral. From the ridge you feel it in your sternum before you can name it. Then you see the basin, and it is lit in dim points and threads, as if someone had spilled a sky into a bowl.

## The Ground

The mountains here are young. The range is still rising, a few millimeters a year, and the rock has not yet learned to be weathered. Fault scarps stand raw and pale where whole faces slipped within the last few centuries. The stone is dark basalt and a green-gray glassy rhyolite, shattered into clean-edged blocks that still cut a palm. Scree slopes are made of fragments no rain has had time to round. Cold water seeps from every seam. Rock faces weep in sheets, and the wet gives the stone a lacquered, newly poured look.

Nothing green grows anywhere. The mineral surface isn't sterile, though. It is filmed with slick mats of chemosynthetic microbes, pink-gray and rust-orange and a deep bruised violet, feeding on freshly exposed iron and sulfur. Where seeps cross the mats, they leave braided colored streaks like spilled dye. The mats smell of struck flint and wet pennies. Everything alive here eats stone, or eats something that eats stone.

Water sits just under the surface everywhere. The basin floor is a lattice of black pools, sheet-flow, and saturated gravel that squelches and gives underfoot. Streams run only a hand deep, and their sound is lost in the greater noise.

## The City

Ahn-Vessel fills the basin in terraces, and it wasn't built so much as coaxed. The structures come from a technology that no longer has a clear name. The oldest builders left no diagrams, only the buildings, and the buildings keep working.

Look closely at a wall and you see it is not stone, not metal, not ceramic. It is a dark, faintly iridescent lattice with a grain like wet slate, and it is always slightly damp because it is drinking. Every surface condenses fog and channels it inward through capillary systems too fine to see. The walls are cold, humming very faintly, and if you press an ear to one you hear the water moving inside like a slow argument.

The towers lean. They lean deliberately, in long tilted ranks, because the ground beneath them is rising unevenly and the lattice adjusts, creeping a fraction of a degree each year to keep the whole city load-balanced against the mountain's slow growth. Old tension-lines, thick as tree trunks and humming a low note, span the streets from tower to tower. They are tuned. Walk beneath them and you pass through a chord.

Streets are narrow, stepped, and dense. Buildings press shoulder to shoulder and share walls. Bridges and galleries stack four and five levels deep, so that standing at any corner you look up through a cross-section of stairwells, gutters, cable-runs, and lanterns. Nothing is empty. Every ledge is occupied by something, whether machine or animal or both.

## The Dark and the Lights

There is no sun, no lamp-grid, and no broad illumination. The city is dark by design, or by long habit. What light exists is small and biological or nearly so: green-blue glow from cultured mats along the gutters, amber pinpricks in the lattice where some old process still sparks, and the pale drifting lanterns of animals.

Human eyes would fail here, but the city is built for touch and hearing. Handrails carry raised patterns, cool ridges for a left-hand turn and warm ones for a right, since the lattice can hold a temperature gradient on command. Junctions are marked by pitch: each has a tuned resonant stone that sings when struck, and travelers carry small hammers of bone or glass to ping their way through. In the dark you hear the city's whole geography as a slow, layered chime.

## The Fauna

The animals are overwhelming, and this is the truest thing about the place. Nothing here is quiet, and no surface goes unused.

The walls are alive with **lantern-mites**, pale crustacean things the size of a thumbnail that graze the microbial films and glow softly when they feed. They cover the lower terraces in shifting carpets, so that whole streets appear to breathe with green light. They pour off ledges in slow cascades. They get in your collar and your boots and are considered good luck.

Above them, in the tension-lines and archways, hang the **choristers**: soft-bodied, long-limbed, amphibian-like animals the color of wet clay, gripping the cables in clusters of hundreds. They are the source of the great roar. Each species has a pitch, and together they produce the city's broad, shifting harmony. At the slow turn of the cycle, when the fog thickens, they all sing at once and the air itself pulses. Conversation stops. People hum along or press their palms to the walls.

In the deep drains live **sump-eels**, blind, slow, and long as a person, that feed on the runoff of everything above. Mist-bats, or something like them, thread the upper galleries by echolocation, adding a high dry ticking that runs like static over the choral bass. Armored beetles the size of loaves plod along the gutters, hauling loose grit to the lattice, which in some way feeds on it. Everywhere there are things that click, drip, scrape, rasp, gurgle, and trill.

The city and its animals are one system. The lattice feeds the mites through its seep-channels. The mites clean the walls of mineral scale. The choristers' singing sets up standing waves in the tension-lines, and those waves drive the pumps that move water up the terraces. Without the animals the city would clog and drown. Without the city, they would starve. Nobody knows which came first.

## The People

There are people, though they are fewer than the animals by a factor no one has bothered to count. They are compact, wrapped in oiled, water-shedding layers, and they move constantly, because standing still in the cold and wet is dangerous. They speak little. Ordinary speech is lost in the roar, so they use touch-signs, tapped rhythms on the shoulder, and hummed pitches that cut through the choir. A greeting is a low two-note phrase. A warning is a rising slide.

They keep the city the way one keeps a very old, very large, very slow animal. They clear blockages from the capillaries, retune the cables, and coax new lattice growth along the terrace edges, where the material extrudes in dark, glistening ribs that take years to harden. Every generation adds a few streets, and the process is so slow that a child born in one lane might never see it finished.

## Rules of the World

- **Nothing is dry.** Anything brought in dry will be wet within an hour. Wet is not a problem to solve but the medium of life.
- **The fog cycle governs everything.** Over roughly thirty of the local days, the cloud deck thickens and thins. In the thick phase the choir swells to a roar, the pumps run hard, and the lanterns dim. In the thin phase the animals go almost silent, and you can hear the individual drip of every seep in the basin.
- **The mountain has the last word.** The range rises, the city bends, and now and then a fault gives a shudder and a whole terrace drops or lifts a hand's breadth. When it does, the choristers fall silent all at once. For a single breath the roar stops and the city listens. Then it resumes.
- **Never light a fire.** Flame is nearly impossible in the wet, and where it works, it frightens the mites into stampedes.

## From the Highest Terrace

Stand at the top, where the last leaning towers give way to raw scarp, and look back down. The basin is a bowl of dark, faintly glowing in green and amber, veined with slow pulses of light where lantern-mites migrate along the streets. The sound rises in waves. Fog condenses on your face and runs down your neck. Behind you the mountain is still weeping, still rising, its broken edges bright with new mineral color, unworn.

The city is not finished. It has never been finished. It is one very slow sentence, still being spoken by things that could not say it alone.
