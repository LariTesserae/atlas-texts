# The Casting Terraces of Upper Wenlo

The place sits at about three and a half thousand meters, in a shallow bowl on the shoulder of a range. It is barely a quarter-mile across. You can walk its rim in the time it takes tea to cool. Everything you stand on was poured.

## The ground

There is no native rock in view. The bowl was scraped to bedrock generations ago and then rebuilt in lifts, each about the height of a forearm, cast from a pale grey-green aggregate that was mixed on site from crushed volcanic tuff, fibrous binder, and something organic that gives it a faint warmth under the hand. The lifts stack into terraces, and the terraces step down toward a central sump in tight, uneven curves, like a contour map that someone stretched into three dimensions.

Because the material is so young, nothing has softened. Every edge is still crisp. You can read the grain of the plank forms pressed into the vertical faces, and the seams where one pour met the last are ridged like healed scars. Bolt-holes stay open where the shuttering was pulled. In a few places the surface is still dark and glossy with curing, and if you press a palm to it you feel it giving off heat. The first rills are just starting: thin brown threads where water has begun to carve the tops of the lifts, each no deeper than a fingernail. The place is at the very beginning of being weathered, and the beginning is visible.

## Water

It is always nearly raining without ever quite raining. The air at this height is warm, held so by geothermal steam lines that run under the terraces in ceramic conduits. Cloud drifts up the valley every afternoon, meets the warm surfaces, and condenses. The whole structure sweats.

Water beads on the vertical faces and runs down in braided threads. It collects in cast gutters, and the gutters feed cisterns, and the cisterns feed the sump, where a slow brown-green pond stands with its surface a hand's breadth below the lowest terrace. Water sits under everything here. Press a heel into the lower lifts and a dark ring seeps up around it. The pipes weep at every joint, and each weeping joint has grown its own small garden.

## The plants

The vegetation is wildly, almost greedily lush, and every bit of it grew from the built surface. Nothing is rooted in earth. The tuff-binder mix holds water like a sponge, and moss took to it within one season of the first pour. Now the terraces wear mats of it, thick as rugs, in a dozen greens from acid yellow to near black. Above the moss come filmy ferns, then tree ferns rooted in cast planter-pockets that are part of the terrace geometry, then epiphytic orchids and bromeliads hung from the conduit lines. Small trees with peeling red bark lean out from the upper terraces, their roots pouring down the faces in ropes and gripping the seams.

The plants are not decoration. They are part of the process. The moss binds the surface against the first erosion. The roots knit the lifts together. The transpiration cools the steam-warmed stone. The engineers who designed the place call this the *skin*, and they schedule it: certain terraces are seeded with certain species in a certain order, so the structure can be handed over to the plants as it matures. Where the skin has not yet been laid, the terrace is bare and pale and looks almost embarrassed.

## The machines

Industrial here does not mean smoke and noise. It means a great deal of patient machinery, mostly hidden or half-overgrown. A line of squat steam-turbine housings runs along the eastern rim, their brass fittings gone green. Belt-driven mixers stand in open sheds. A gantry on rails travels the whole rim of the bowl on an iron track, carrying a pour-hopper that swings out over the terraces and lets down a slow gray tongue of fresh material. The gantry moves at the speed of a walking person. It stops, pours, waits, and moves on. Vines have already found its lower struts, and someone prunes them back on a schedule you can almost see written in the way they're cut.

Copper and iron pipes wrap every corner. The steam conduits are lagged in woven fiber that has become a nursery for lichens. Valves the size of dinner plates sit at junctions, turned by hand-wheels worn bright. Overhead runs a lattice of insulated cables, and on humid afternoons blue-white beads of corona light crawl along the wires at dusk.

## The strangeness

The pours are not random. The terraces are casting a *specific shape.* The lower lifts, seen from above, trace the contour of a mountain: a real one, or once real, with a saddle, a hanging valley, and a pair of unequal summits. The work is a scale-reproduction, at about one part in four hundred, of a peak that is not visible from here and that none of the workers will name. Some of them say it was lost. Some say it is being *prepared* for. When the model is finished, they say, the water in the sump will be drained and poured into a channel that runs straight through the rock, toward somewhere else.

There is another oddity. The cast surface is slightly warm, and it is very slightly, unmistakably *slow*. If you stand still on a fresh terrace long enough, you can feel it settle. The stone breathes out a little, a fine grain of movement, as if it hadn't yet decided to be solid. Workers step lightly on the newest pours and speak of them in the way one speaks about sleeping children.

## Fauna

Animals are rare. There are tree frogs the size of a thumbnail, translucent green, living in the bromeliad cups and calling in ones and twos at dusk. A single species of swift nests in the turbine housings. Dragonflies patrol the sump. Once in a while a spectacled bear comes over the rim at night, drinks, and goes away again, and everyone pretends not to have noticed. Otherwise the place belongs to snails, springtails, and the pale, tireless millipedes that browse the moss.

## Sound

It's mostly quiet. What you hear is dripping: thousands of separate drips in a dozen pitches, from the eaves of terraces, from the conduits, from leaf-tips, into cisterns and pools and onto the broad leaves below. Under that runs a low turbine hum you feel in your sternum rather than hear. Now and then a valve exhales a long hiss of steam. The gantry ticks along its track. The frogs start at dusk, and then the drips seem to organize themselves around them.

## Motion and time

Everything is moving at a slow, deliberate pace. The gantry crawls. The cloud rises through the afternoon and dissolves by evening. Steam bleeds off the terraces at dawn in long white banners. Moss thickens by the day. The rills deepen a hair with each rain. Every morning a crew opens the sluices and lets the night's condensate run down through the whole structure, and for an hour the terraces flow with thin, bright sheets of water, and the entire made mountain shines.

The light is plain and clear, the honest white light of high altitude, filtered gently green by the canopy. In it, the place looks less like a factory than like something being *grown*: a hill under construction, or a hill that is slowly, patiently learning how to be one.
