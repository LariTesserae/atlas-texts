# The Ringing Vug

You come down through a shaft cut square, its walls still bearing the parallel scars of a drill that no one remembers running. The air thickens as you descend. By the bottom it is body-warm and tastes of pennies and struck matches. The shaft opens through the roof of a chamber, and the sound reaches you before the sight does.

## The Room

The chamber is about the size of a cathedral nave, though it has no nave's intention. It is a blister in the rock, roughly egg-shaped and two hundred paces long, with a ceiling that rises and falls in shelves. The stone is young, freshly broken, and has never been rounded off by anything. The walls are basalt the color of wet slate, fractured into planes and blades, and every edge is still an edge. Crusts of yellow sulfur and clear, tabular gypsum-like crystals stand out from the fractures at angles that look drafted. Some of the crystal blades are as long as a forearm and as thin as a knife, and they are dense, growing shoulder to shoulder across whole walls like the pages of a burned book.

Nothing in the room is wet. Stone, crystal, dust, and the powdery sulfur underfoot are all dry. The only liquid is the tar.

## The Tar

Black seeps well up through cracks in the floor, slow and glossy, warm as a held hand. They gather in shallow pans and creep toward low points, where they thicken into ropes and terraces. Their surfaces are marked with fine concentric ripples that never quite settle. The whole room lives off this chemistry: heavy hydrocarbons, sulfur compounds, and a warm dry breath of gas leaking from below. The fluids here are oils, solvents, and melts. No one who lives in the vug has a word for water, and no one has needed one.

## The Machines

They are scattered, with long stretches of bare rock between them, and they are not abandoned. Each is a squat industrial thing of riveted iron and blackened brass, a pump-house or a compressor shed or a crude still. There are perhaps eleven in all, each sitting over a seep like a tick. Their flywheels turn slowly. Their pistons knock. Belts of some cured, ropy hide run up through holes in the roof, carrying power from somewhere above or below. Nobody in the vug has seen a hand on them.

Each machine draws tar, cracks it, and vents the lighter fractions through long copper stacks that bend along the ceiling and end in fluted bells. The gas escapes through the bells with a throaty, reedy note. Eleven machines make eleven notes, and they are tuned. You can hear it: a chord, huge and stable, with a beating undertone from two pipes that are very slightly apart in pitch. That beat comes once every four seconds and has never been any other interval.

Other traces are fainter. There is a ladder that ends in mid-air. A scatter of stamped tin tags is half-buried in sulfur dust. A workbench has been grown into by a slab of gypsum, which has taken its vise and one glove. A single brick chimney rises from the floor with no building around it and breathes warm air. These are the only signs of anyone who came before, and they are all industrial: tools, not dwellings.

## The Chorus

The fauna is where the density lives. Every crack, ledge, and crystal blade holds something. The main animals are tar-beetles, dull-shelled and palm-sized, their carapaces slick with a varnish of the oil they feed on. They cluster in drifts on the warm floors and walk in slow columns to the seeps and back. Their legs are combed to skim tar from the surface without sticking. Above them, in the crystal thickets, live thin, pale, many-jointed things like mantises made of glass. They hunt the beetles by tapping on the blades and listening to how the vibrations return. Below the floor, in the fractures, are worm-shapes that you only ever hear.

All of them make sound. The beetles rasp their wing-cases in a continuous dry hiss. The crystal-mantises tick and chime against the blades. Something in the ceiling makes long, sliding, falling whistles. Cover your ears and it is still there, in your ribs.

The strange fact, which anyone who spends an hour there hears, is that it is all in time. The rasping rises and falls with the four-second beat of the pipes, and the ticks land on the pistons' downstrokes. The beetles do not follow the machines. The machines do not follow the beetles. The whole room breathes on one clock, and the clock is nobody's.

## The Sparse Green

Vegetation exists but does not dominate. A few kinds of lithophyte grow where the warm gas vents. They are pale, matte, leafless crusts and cushions, the color of old bone, dotted with dark spore-cups. They feed on the sulfur breath, not on light, and they grow in tight rosettes on the vent-lips, each rosette fringed with a lace of hair-thin filaments that vibrate in the sound. In two places, on a ledge near the roof, there are small stands of something taller, a brittle, jointed, reed-like growth with translucent pipes for stems. The wind of the vents plays them. They are the only part of the vug that seems to have been designed to be listened to.

## The Light

The light is dim and low, from an ember-orange glow that lives in the deepest seeps. A cold phosphorescence flickers along the beetles' varnish when they move quickly, and there is a faint gleam from the crystal edges, which catch and multiply what little there is. Shadows are soft and everywhere. Your eyes take a long time to give you the room, and it comes in pieces: an edge, a chimney, a drift of beetles like a moving rug, a stack, a bell.

## The Rules

The vug seems to hold to a few principles, in the way a room holds a shape.

**Nothing here changes.** The chord has not moved. The crystals are sharp and fresh, and they have been sharp and fresh for as long as anyone can say. The tar rises and drains at the same rate. The beetle columns take the same paths. A blade that you break does not regrow, but the room hums in a way that makes you suspect it would if it could.

**The sound holds the room.** The crystals ring at the chord's frequencies. Where a blade does not ring, it is missing, and where a blade rings too hard, it shivers off and falls. The stable shape of the vug is a standing-wave pattern, and the stone itself, over the long time here, has been sculpted and kept sculpted by it, a chamber tuned like an instrument. Break the chord, by silencing a machine or staying quiet too long, and the beetles hush, the mantises freeze, and the crystals begin, faintly, to grind.

**No one is in charge.** The machines are not tended and the animals are not herded. The feedback between them, tar to pump, gas to bell, bell to beetle, beetle to seep, is closed and complete. It has settled into equilibrium, a frozen and roaring one, and it is not so much alive as it is finished.

## Leaving

When you climb the shaft again, the sound narrows behind you to a single hum, then to a pressure, then to nothing. The warmth stays on your skin a long time. Above, the air is thin and quiet, and you have the strong impression that below you, with no one listening, the chord is still sounding, exactly as it was and exactly as it will be.
