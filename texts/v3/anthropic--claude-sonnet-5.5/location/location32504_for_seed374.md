# The Basin of Standing Noise

You hear it before you see it, and for the first hour you do not hear it as sound. It presses on the chest and the teeth. Then the ear finds layers in it. The lowest is a continuous roar from the ground, steam forced through rock. Above it is a mid-register clatter of a million small bodies, and above that a shrill grinding of insects. Under all three, slow and regular, are drums.

The road down into the Basin is a ramp of cooled black glass, still sharp-edged, unrounded by any weather. The Basin is low, barely above the level of the sea that lies somewhere behind the steam. It is wide enough that walking across takes most of a day, and you cannot see the far side.

## The ground

The land here is new, or behaves as if it were. Nothing has had time to soften. The plain is a mosaic of raw mineral, and every surface keeps the crispness of a thing recently made:

- **Sinter terraces**, white and cream, stepped in shallow rimmed pools like a thousand cupped hands. The rims are knife-thin and brittle underfoot. Water sheets over them in a film so thin it only darkens the stone.
- **Basalt flats**, black and vesicular, pocked like bread, with edges that will open a palm.
- **Ochre and rust crusts** around the vents, where iron and sulfur have dyed the rock in bands of orange, mustard, and a bruised green-black.
- **Mud pots** that heave and pop in slow grey blisters.

The water is everywhere and always near. It seeps from the terrace rims, weeps down the black walls in beaded lines, and stands in every hollow, so that the ground shines even in the dimness. Dig a hand's depth anywhere and the hole fills with warm water that smells faintly of eggs and iron. The air is warm and thick, like the breath of something asleep. Steam rises from a hundred places and merges into a low ceiling that turns the sun into a pale coin, a smear of brightness with no shadow-casting edge. The light is dim, pearl-colored, and it comes from everywhere at once.

Nothing grows that you would call a plant. But the water is alive with color: green mats, orange ropes, and violet slicks of microbial growth that ring each pool in concentric stripes according to its temperature. These are the only paint the land has. They are slick and faintly sweet-smelling, and they are the base of everything else.

## The animals

The fauna is the source of most of the noise, and it is abundant past reckoning.

**The chorus-frogs** live in the cooler outer pools, gold-throated and no bigger than a thumb, in numbers that make the shallows look like they are boiling. Their calling is a wall of pulsing sound, each pool with its own pitch, so that walking between pools is like walking between struck bells.

**The rookery-gulls** are pale, heavy birds that nest on every horizontal surface the warm ground offers. They do not migrate. The ground is warm enough to hatch eggs without brooding, and they lay in the sinter pits in ranks so dense that you cannot put down a foot. Their cries are hoarse and constant, and no single voice is ever distinguishable.

**The steam-eels** and **mat-grazers**, small armored things like flat-headed shrimp, crowd the warm channels and keep the microbial mats cropped to a fine velvet.

**Clouds of biting midges** hang over the standing water in columns, humming at a pitch that seems to live inside the skull.

And there are the **wallowers**, broad, low, hairless animals the size of calves, which lie in the warm mud in herds. They grunt and belch in an unbroken bass. The people of the Basin keep them, or are kept by them. The distinction is not clear.

## The city

The settlement is called simply the Press, because of how it is packed. It covers the central terraces and runs outward for miles, and it is dense in the way an anthill is dense, with no open ground, no plaza, and only the thinnest lanes between walls.

The people build with what the ground gives them. They cut sinter into blocks with hafted obsidian and chert, and the stone is soft and easily worked when wet and hardens as it dries. They stack the blocks into thick, rounded houses with roofs of hide, reed-less thatch of dried mat-felt, and slabs of black glass. The walls are chalk-white and always beaded with condensation. Houses lean against one another, share walls, and climb the terrace steps in tiers, so that from a distance the whole city looks like another terrace formation, one that happens to have doors.

Their tools are the tools of the hand: obsidian blades, bone awls, stone mauls, fire-hardened digging sticks, and a little beaten native copper from a seam near the north vents, used for ornament more than work. They have no wheel because they have no need of one. Everything moves on backs, on sledges pulled over the wet sinter, or on the flat rafts that ply the warm canals.

Water is managed rather than fetched. Channels cut in the stone carry hot and cool streams through the city and mix them at stone sluices, so every household has warm water in a choice of temperatures. The people bathe often, and communal soaking pools occupy every third courtyard. The city's ordinary sound is therefore also the sound of splashing, talk, and laughter echoing off wet stone.

## The sound, and how the people live in it

Deafening is the Basin's normal state, and the people have built a life inside it.

They speak with their hands and faces at close range, a fast, expressive gesture-language, and with whistles and struck-stone signals across distance. Their houses have thick walls and low doorways, and inside there is a relative hush, a thing the people prize and call *the held breath*. Sleep is done in the innermost chambers, packed in felt.

Their drums are not music alone. Great skin drums stand on the terrace crests and are struck in fixed patterns that carry through the roar as a felt pulse in the ground, since ears cannot pick them out. The patterns mean the hour, the mixing of the water, the changes of work. Children learn them before they learn to speak.

## The rules of the place

Nothing here changes, and this is the Basin's deepest law. The geysers erupt on schedules that have not shifted in living memory or in the memory of the drums. The great vent at the city's heart throws its column to the same height every seventh drum-cycle, and the people time their lives to it without needing to look. The pools hold their temperatures, the mat-stripes keep their bands, the gull-ranks keep their places, and the terraces, though raw and sharp, do not grow or crumble, because the water deposits exactly as much stone as the steam and wear remove. The land is forever new and never advances. It has reached a balance at the moment of its making and stays there.

The people take this as a gift and as the nature of things. They have no word for *season*. They count days by the vents. They have no story of a time before the Basin or a time after it. When strangers come, as they rarely do, and speak of rivers that change their courses or forests that turn with the year, the people listen politely, the way one listens to someone describing a dream.

At the edge of the city, where the steam thins, the ground rises slightly toward a rim of dark cliff, and there the noise falls off, hour by hour, into a strange silence. The people say it is not a good place to stay. There the air is cooler and clear, the light is sharper, and things seem to be waiting to happen. They go there to mourn, and come back quickly to the warm, wet, roaring dark.
