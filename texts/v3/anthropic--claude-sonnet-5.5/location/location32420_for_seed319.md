# The Plateau of Throats

The land is high, but it doesn't feel high until you notice how far the clouds have come down to meet it. The plateau lies at the altitude where warm wet air from the lowlands rises and stalls. Its surface is a broad, tilted table of grey-ochre limestone and older, darker stone beneath, worn so long and so evenly that nothing on it is sharp. Ridges have become long shoulders. Boulders have become loaves. Every edge has been rounded by water, and the water is still at it, patiently, in warm air that smells of wet iron and crushed stone.

The surface is mostly moor and pan. Shallow basins hold standing water the color of weak tea, ringed with rust-red algae mats and stands of pale reed. Between them the ground is a sponge of cushion plants, liverwort, and grey-green moss, with dwarf willows bent all one way. Trees are rare, and each has a name and a keeper. The vegetation is sparse but it never looks dead. It looks thrifty, as if the plateau has calculated how little it needs. Rock faces weep continuously, with dark stripes of wet stone and thin veils of water sliding down them, so the whole country glistens without a drop falling from the sky.

## The Throats

The plateau is drilled through. Across some forty miles there are perhaps two hundred shafts, called the Throats, ranging from a well's width to a quarter-mile across. They are the ends of an immense cave system beneath. The moor's streams run to their rims and go over, and from below the warm cave air comes up in long, slow exhalations that condense into standing columns of white cloud. From a distance the plateau looks staked with pillars of steam, each leaning in the wind at its own angle.

The Throats are why everything here is loud. Water falling into the deep makes a sound that no one who lives here can describe as a sound. It's a pressure, a weather of the chest. Near a large Throat, the roar is a solid thing you lean into. Farther out it thins to a fabric of overlapping tones, and each Throat has its own pitch, set by the width of the shaft and the depth of the cavity below. Between the great ones the sound never falls below the level of a crowded machine hall. Silence is not found on the plateau. What can be found are places where the noise is merely enormous rather than total.

The pitch drifts. In the wet season, when the streams swell, the lowest Throats drop a tone over some weeks. In the dry months they climb again. The old city keeps a record of this, an unbroken column of tuning numbers in a hall nobody visits.

## The City in Pieces

The city is not one place. It is a scatter of districts, each built on a rise or a rim, with wide empty moor between them. Viaducts of dark brick and riveted iron cross the pans on tall, narrow piers, carrying rails, pipes, and footpaths. From any high point you can see five or six districts at once, each a dense knot of roofs and stacks separated from the next by miles of reed and standing water. Density is low overall, but where the city exists it is thick, stacked, and layered. New brick sits on old brick sits on older cut stone, and the oldest courses are worn to soft curves by centuries of mist.

The industry lives off the Throats. Iron and brass turbine housings, big as cottages, are bolted into the shafts' upper walls. Falling water and rising air spin them, and the power is carried by cable and by shafts of rotating rod to mills, foundries, and pump houses. There are ore-crushers, wire-drawing sheds, and long glass-roofed halls where looms run in ranks. Smoke is minimal because power is mostly water and air, and the sky above the districts stays clean apart from the steam. The machinery adds its own din to the roar, but no one can hear it as separate. It is all one substance, the plateau's voice.

The architecture is shaped by this. Buildings turn blank stone shoulders toward the nearest Throat and put their doors and few windows on the far side. Walls are thick, sometimes double, with sand or wet clay packed between. Streets bend in long, deliberate curves to break the line of sound. Gutters are cut with ribbed channels that hum faintly when the rain runs through them. Some old stairways are carved so that a foot on the step produces a sympathetic tone. Nobody can hear this, but people feel it in the soles of their feet and know which stair they are on in the dark.

## Living in the Roar

People here speak with their hands, in a complex and quick sign language that has hardened over generations into regional dialects. They also use touch: two fingers on a wrist, a palm flat on a shoulder. Signal flags and paddles hang from every corner. Lamps flash coded messages between districts across the moor. When a child is born deaf, no one thinks of it as a hardship. They simply have an easier time.

Everyone else wears plugs of beeswax and cork, or fitted caps of felt and leather. Even so, the sound reaches the body: a hum in the teeth, a slight blur at the edge of vision, a quiver in water held in a cup. Visitors get headaches for a week, then a strange calm. Locals say the noise rinses out thought. There are workers who have never heard their own voice in decades and don't miss it.

There are, however, Still Rooms. These are chambers deep inside thick masonry, lined with felt, cork, wool, and hanging sand-filled baffles, where the roar drops to something bearable. Entry is rationed. People go there to be married, to sign contracts, to mourn, to be told hard news. A Still Room is not silent, but it is quiet enough that someone can whisper and be understood. Coming out of one feels like surfacing from water.

## The Weirdness

The plateau has some strange habits.

At certain Throats, moisture condenses on the underside of overhanging ledges and falls not as rain but as a fine, slow drip that seems to fall upward when the updraft is strong. Droplets hang, tremble, then climb a hand's span before reversing. The locals call it the Turning Rain and build small shelters in which to sit and watch.

The oldest structures on the moor, low round towers of a stone that isn't native, are hollow and have no doors. Each one resonates at exactly the pitch of the nearest Throat, and in the wet season, when the pitch falls, the towers seem to lag a moment behind, as though they remember. Engineers have measured this and don't like the measurements. The towers are cared for, whitewashed each year, and never opened.

Fog gathers in the pans at dawn and does not disperse with the sun but slowly lifts, in a single sheet, until it joins the cloud ceiling. Then the cloud ceiling comes down again at dusk. This daily exchange is so regular that the districts set their shift bells by it, though the bells can't be heard. They are read by sight, by the fall of a weighted vane.

## Small Life

Fauna is rare. There are swifts that nest in the walls of the Throats, flying in the columns of steam, apparently indifferent to the sound. There are pale, translucent salamanders in the pans, and they seem to prefer the vibration. A few long-legged wading birds stand in the reeds at dusk, motionless for hours. Cats live in the districts, deaf and self-assured. Stray dogs do not last. Beyond these, the plateau is empty of animals, and the emptiness is part of the character of the place. It feels less abandoned than reserved for machines and water.

## Time

Change is slow. The Throats widen by a finger's breadth each year as the water eats their rims. Every few generations a district must be abandoned and its stones carried, brick by brick, to a new rise farther from the crumbling edge. The abandoned sites go quiet in the way a bell goes quiet, slowly, with moss softening the streets and reeds moving in. Over centuries the whole city has walked across the plateau this way, leaving a trail of ghost districts, each rounded and green, each still faintly humming in the wet season.

The plateau doesn't hurry. It has been roaring since before the first brick, and by all appearances it will roar after the last. The people who live in it have made their peace with that, by making the noise a home.
