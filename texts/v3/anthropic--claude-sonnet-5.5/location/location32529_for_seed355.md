# The Sinter City of Low Water

It begins as a smell: hot stone, sulfur, and under them something sweet and mineral, like the breath of a cellar that has been wet for a hundred thousand years. Then the dark resolves into a plain so flat that the horizon is only the place where the warm mist thickens into black.

The air is body-hot and completely saturated. Nothing dries here. Sweat, cloth, and stone all stay at the same damp temperature, so the boundary between skin and atmosphere blurs. The sky is low cloud with no stars, lit only from below.

## The Ground

The land is a delta with no river. Broad shallow sheets of water, ankle to thigh deep, lie over pavements of pale mineral. The water is the temperature of a bath and slightly viscous. It carries a faint glow, the dull orange of an ember seen through a closed eyelid, coming from vents and seams in the bed. Where the flats are shallowest the light pools in long, wavering veins. Where they are deep it disappears, and the water is a black lid you can only judge by the slow drag against your legs.

The vegetation is sparse. There are stands of black-stemmed reeds, brittle and hollow, each standing a few paces from the next as though the plain enforced a courtesy. There are mats of dark green-purple slime along the warm channels, and pale grey lichens on the tops of walls where condensation drips steadily. Nothing flowers. Nothing rustles. Leaves are rare, and the plants that exist mostly look like stems asking a question.

There is no animal life. No insects hang over the water and nothing moves beneath it. The absence has a texture. You keep expecting a ripple to be a fish and it never is. The ripples come from the water itself, from the slow convection of hot springs and from drops falling from great heights.

## The City

The city is not built. Or it is built the way a coral reef is built, or the way a cave grows a chandelier.

Hot mineral springs have been depositing travertine and sinter here for longer than the concept of *long* holds up. The water, following the logic it follows everywhere, fell, pooled, overflowed, hardened, and overflowed again. Somewhere in that immense patience it learned the shapes of streets. Terraced basins step down in rounded stairways. Overflow lips curve into arches. Columns of banded stone rise from the flats, thickened at the base and fluted by centuries of downward runoff. Walls curve inward to make rooms, and rooms open onto plazas that are really shallow, perfectly level pools ringed by rims as smooth as hand-rubbed plaster.

It reads as a city, a dense, layered, organized one, with districts, avenues, lanes, cisterns, stairs, and towers. Nothing was ever chiseled. There are no tools, no marks of tools, no hinges or handles. The doorways are cracks that widened into arches. The window-like openings are bubbles that burst in the stone while it was young.

The erosion is established and calm. Every edge has been softened by long weeping. The walls sweat constantly, and the sweat runs down in fine lines that have carved a filigree of grooves. The towers lean a little, in the way old things settle into an agreement with gravity. There is no rubble, because nothing is left to fall. The city is at rest with its own decay, like a riverbed.

It is not crowded. The city covers a region wide enough that walking it takes a day, but the structures stand far apart, clusters of towers separated by long stretches of open flooded pavement. Between the districts there are miles of dark water and a few reeds, then another terraced hill of stone streets rising from the flat, its edges beaded with glow.

## Light

There is very little. The vents in the water give a low ember-orange. The walls, where the hot water runs over them, hold a faint phosphorescence that fades as the stone cools, so that the newest terraces are the brightest. The older quarters, dry on their upper levels, are only felt: cool-looking curves at the edge of vision, a paler dark against the dark.

Shadows behave wrongly here. The light comes from below and from the sides, yet the shadows do not always fall away from it. Sometimes a wall's shadow lies toward its own glowing seam, a dark wedge pointing into the source. You learn to stop trusting shadows as directions.

## What Goes Wrong

Reality here is not stable, and the instability feels less like magic than like an accounting error the world has stopped correcting.

**The streets do not add up.** Walk a lane between two terraces and count your steps. Walk back and the count is different, sometimes by a few, sometimes by dozens. Intersections appear that were not there. A plaza you crossed reappears ahead, with the water at a different depth and the same reed standing in it at the same angle. Maps, if anyone drew them, would fail. The city has more streets than its ground can hold, and it sorts out the surplus by folding them behind one another.

**Interiors are larger than exteriors.** Step through a crack-arch into what looks like a chamber the size of a cistern, and you may find a hall whose far wall is lost in the dark, floored with warm shallow water, with columns going away in ranks. Some chambers contain their own weather: a fine warm rain falling from a ceiling that isn't there, a bank of mist rolling from one wall to the other and back.

**Rain has no consistent direction.** In some districts it falls upward from the flats and beads on the underside of the low cloud, which drips slowly back down as ordinary rain into a different district. You can stand in an upward shower and feel the drops climb your legs. Neither direction is louder or more natural than the other.

**Water is unreliable about level.** The flats rise and fall in a slow rhythm, a tide with no moon and no sea, taking about as long as a deep breath scaled up to a day. On the rise the streets fill to the second terrace; on the fall they drain, and the stone stands slick and steaming. But the rise happens in patches. One quarter can be high tide while its neighbor, a hundred paces away, is at dead low. Standing on the border you can see a knee-deep flood on one side and dry, glistening pavement on the other, with a clean line between them and no wall.

**Reflections lag.** The still pools show the arches above them, but a heartbeat late, or early. If you wait, you may see the reflection settle into a different arch than the one overhead: a taller tower, a break in the wall that isn't there. They never show you.

## Sound

Mostly quiet. What noise exists is water: the slow tick of drips from the sweating walls, each with its own pitch depending on the basin below; a deep, distant hum where hot water runs through the buried channels of the stone; the occasional soft collapse of a bubble in a warm pool.

Sometimes a sound arrives before its cause. A drop plinks in a basin, and a moment later you see the ripple begin, and later still the drop itself falls from the ceiling into the widening rings. It is always very quiet, and it always happens in the same order, running backward.

## Rules of the World

- Nothing living moves fast. Even the water hesitates.
- The city does not repeat exactly, but it rhymes. Every district has a plaza, a stair, a leaning tower, a basin with a single reed, and the rhyme grows stranger the farther you go.
- The heat is constant. It does not spike or ease. The whole place is one held breath at body temperature.
- The wet is the maker. Where the springs cool and die, the walls dry, crack, and go silent. Those are the oldest quarters, and the folded streets there are worst.
- There is no one here, and the city seems arranged as though there had been. Benches of stone that fit no body. Wide steps meant for slow processions. Small niches at eye level, each holding a shallow bowl of water that is always full.

You walk on through warm black water toward the next glow, and the mist closes behind you without a sound.
