# The Hollow Town of Marrow-Under-Leaf

## Approach

The ground here is only a little above the level of the water table, and you feel it in your boots before you see anything. The soil gives, then holds. It is black and fibrous and made almost entirely of last century's leaves. It is warm, like a hand that has been resting on something.

The place is small, perhaps a mile across, and you could walk around it in an hour if the walking behaved. It sits in a shallow basin where an old river once wandered and then left. The river left behind its habits. Water still arrives here by condensation, by seep, and by a brief rain that falls most afternoons. It never pools for long. It is taken up at once, by everything.

Before you see the town, you hear it.

## The Sound

The sound is the first fact of the place. It is not noise laid over a landscape. It is a load-bearing element.

The town is a resonator. Ten thousand kinds of frog, cicada, and beetle, and things that are none of these, sing inside a structure whose every hollow trunk and cupped leaf has been tuned by centuries of growth. The result is a pressure. Standing at the edge, you feel it in your sternum and your back teeth. Your eyes water. Leaves at the perimeter shiver in visible standing waves, and the puddles in the path form concentric rings that never disperse.

The loudest hours are dawn and dusk, when the sound climbs through registers until it stops being heard and starts being *worn*. At midday it drops to something merely enormous. There is no silence. Once, the locals say, a few seconds of quiet fell and every animal in the basin froze in fear.

Deep in the town the sound develops rules of its own, which I'll come to.

## The Substrate

There is almost no ground in the town, and almost no stone. There is plant.

The town is grown, not built. Its bones are strangler figs of a kind that doesn't exist elsewhere. They started as seeds in the crowns of earlier trees, sent roots down, and fused. The host trees rotted out long ago, leaving hollow lattices of root: columns as wide as houses, ribbed and buttressed, hung with curtains of finer roots that have thickened into walls. Between these, sheets of broad leaf and woven vine form floors, ceilings, and awnings. The result is *urban*. It has streets, quarters, arcades, bridges, cisterns, and stairs, all of it in the organized, layered manner of a place that has been added to for a very long time.

No hand built it. The streets are the gaps between the great root-trunks, and they run in a rough radial pattern because the original fig sent its first roots out that way. Later growth filled in the ring roads. Bridges are vines that found each other and thickened. The stairs are buttress roots, and their treads are worn into shallow dishes that hold rainwater. The walking surfaces are polished by the passage of animals to a dull green-brown gloss, like old leather.

Everything is a plant or the product of one. The mortar is a resin that hardened amber-gold in the joints. The windows are gaps where the leaf-wall thinned. The "glass" is a translucent membrane of stretched bark that glows green when the sun is behind it. Even the hard things, the lintels and the door-frames, are plant: heartwood gone dense as iron, or silica-rich cane packed in bundles.

## Emptiness

Here the parameters pull in different directions, and the place resolves them by being hollow.

The mass of the town is huge, but almost all of it is shell. The rooms are vast. Under the canopy there are halls a hundred feet high and a hundred feet across, where the only contents are green air, hanging roots, and a few fallen leaves. You may walk for a quarter of an hour through a great arcade and see nothing but the arcade. The plants are the walls and the ceiling, and the volume between them is nearly bare.

All the life is packed into the walls. Every root-column is a city of its own, every leaf-sheet a pasture. But in the open spaces, the streets and plazas and vaults, there is very little. A dropped leaf spirals for a long time. A single heron-like bird, pale and tall, stands in a plaza the size of a cathedral nave and does not move. The abundance of the place is in its edges. The center of every room is empty, as though the town had grown carefully around the idea of a room and left it unfilled.

## Fauna

Life is abundant, but it keeps to the substrate. Almost nothing crosses open space unless it has to.

- **Wall-frogs**, thumb-sized and translucent, live in the pleats of the leaf-sheets. There are millions. Their calls set the base pitch of the town: a rolling, wet, repeated *tock* that is the floor under everything else.
- **Bell-cicadas** cling in rows to the inner surface of hollow trunks and sing into the hollows, so each trunk is a horn. When the sun strikes a certain column, its cicadas all sound at once, and the note goes through the town like a struck gong.
- **Ladder-ants** build their columns up the aerial roots in unbroken lines, thousands wide, carrying leaf-fragments that catch the light. From a distance the roots seem to be slowly pouring upward.
- **Slow-cats**, long and low and green-furred with algae, drape across the broad branches and move once in a morning. They hunt the wall-frogs by patience.
- **Ribbon-birds** fly the long vaults in tight flocks, and their trailing tail-feathers make the only visible sign of air currents.
- **Water-carriers**, small marsupial creatures with cupped paws, run the cisterns and drip-channels. They shift water from one place to another for reasons of their own and are never seen to drink.

Everything eats something else, and the eating is visible: a slow-cat's tongue, a frog's throat pulsing, a sudden flurry of ants around a fallen fruit. The predator-prey rhythm is the town's clockwork, and the noise is partly its accounting.

## Water

The climate is damp rather than wet. Water comes in small, frequent, well-managed amounts.

- **Condensation.** The warm air meets cooler shadow inside the great hollows, and the inner walls sweat. Droplets run down the leaves in ordered rivulets to the drip-channels, and every surface is glazed with them.
- **The afternoon rain.** It falls for about twenty minutes, warm and heavy, and the sound of it on the leaf-roofs is the one thing that briefly rivals the animals. The town drinks it. Within an hour the streets are dry again.
- **The cisterns.** Great cupped leaves and hollow root-chambers hold standing water, dark and tea-colored, with a scum of duckweed. These are the closest thing to civic centers. Every animal in the district passes through them.
- **The Old Channel.** A buried thread of the vanished river still runs under the town. In some places it surfaces as a slow black stream between root-banks, and the water there is unnaturally cold.

## Weathering

The town is old and settled. Nothing about it is fresh. Erosion here is not the wearing-away of stone but the long negotiation between growth and decay.

The columns are scarred and furrowed. The steps are dished. Old walls have slumped and been re-grown over with newer walls, so that every surface is layered like bark, and you can read the town's history in the cross-sections where roots have split. Some quarters are dead and hollow, with gray, silvered wood and a fine dust of rot, and these are always being colonized again from the edges. Fallen trunks become soil, soil becomes seedbeds, seedbeds become the next generation of walls.

The town is in balance. It loses as much as it gains. It has been the same size for a very long time, and it has been continually replacing itself the whole while.

## Light

The light is normal, even ordinary, and that is odd given everything else. The sun comes through the leaf-roofs in green-gold shafts, and at midday the great halls are full of a clear, soft, underwater brightness. It is easy to see by. Shadows are sharp at the edges and deep green inside. Membranes glow when the sun is behind them. The quality of the light is calm, and against the sound it reads as strangely reassuring.

## Where Reality Fails

The rules of the place hold only at its edges. Toward the center, where the sound is densest, they begin to give.

**Sound has weight.** Near the middle of the town, the noise thickens until it acts on matter. Loose leaves stand in the air, held at fixed heights by the standing waves. Dropped water forms shivering spheres that hang for seconds and then burst into spray. If you clap your hands, you can see the ring of the clap moving outward through the mist as a visible shell, and it takes far longer than it should to reach the wall.

**Distance is unreliable.** A hall that took a minute to cross on the way in takes ten on the way out, or a few seconds. There is no consistent rule. The locals say the town measures you by how loudly you are heard, and adjusts. Speak quietly and the streets are short. Shout and they stretch.

**Doors repeat.** Certain archways, in certain quarters, lead back to themselves. You pass through the arch and find yourself on the same side, facing the same arch, with your footprints already in the wet floor ahead of you, and they are a little fresher than they should be.

**The rain falls up in one street.** On the Street of Cups, at the hour of the afternoon rain, the drops leave the cisterns and rise, slowly and in perfect lines, into the canopy, and are gone. The street stays dry, and the rest of the town gets a little wetter. Nobody has found where the water goes.

**The animals go silent in the wrong place.** In the heart of the town there is a plaza where, inside a circle about thirty paces across, the sound stops completely. You can watch a hundred wall-frogs at the edge of the circle, throats pulsing, and hear nothing from them. Inside the circle, the quiet is total and terrible, like a held breath. Anything that steps in is still there when it steps out, but changed in some way it cannot report.

**The town is one thing.** Follow enough roots to their sources and they all lead to the same plant. The thousands of columns, the ten thousand arcades, are the limbs of one organism, and the sound is its voice. The roaring is not a chorus of separate animals. The animals are its cells, and it speaks through them. In the silent plaza is the mouth, or the ear, and it is listening for something that has not yet answered.

## Dynamics

Everything is moving, all the time, though almost none of it fast.

- The ladder-ants pour upward. The slow-cats shift.
- The wall-frogs sing in waves that travel around the town in a slow circuit, so the loudest point drifts like a lighthouse beam.
- The cicada gongs cycle with the sun's angle.
- The roots creep, an inch a week, and gates that were open in spring are sealed by autumn.
- Vines cross new gaps, and the town's streets are revised year by year.
- The afternoon rain arrives, the water-carriers run, the cisterns fill and drain, and the whole thing breathes with a pulse you can feel in the floor.

## Departure

When you leave, the sound recedes behind you in stages. First the pressure lifts from your chest. Then the standing leaves fall. Then the frogs become individual voices. Eventually the roar shrinks to a hum at the edge of hearing, and then to a faint rhythm you might mistake for your own pulse. Your ears ring for hours.

Above the basin, when you look back, the canopy is a low green dome under normal sunlight, and a few pale birds circle over it. It looks like nothing, a patch of forest a mile across. Only the very faint tremor in the leaves at the top tells you that underneath, in the hollow rooms, something huge is still speaking to itself.
