# The Unfinished Shoulder

The mountain is still being made, and everyone here can hear it.

Start with the rock. It is fresh, black and slate-blue, and in places a bruised green like the inside of old bottle glass. Nothing has had time to soften it. Fracture faces are still bright enough to throw back a lamp. Ridges come to edges you could shave with, and cliffs step down in clean planes, as if the range had been cut out of the sky a few centuries ago and lifted into place. There is almost no scree. Wind and ice have barely begun their work, so the slopes hold no long aprons of rubble, only the occasional first spill of flakes at a cliff foot, each fragment still sharp. Where the uplift is fastest, the ground ticks. Put a palm to a boulder and you feel it creep, a tremor slower than a pulse.

The air is thin and cold, but never bitterly so. Cloud lives here. The plateau sits so high that the sun is a pale coin in a sky the color of dark plums, even at midday, and the light that reaches the ground is dim, sourceless, and silver-grey. Everything is lit as if by a lamp behind frosted glass. Shadows are soft and doubled at the edges.

## Water without rivers

There are almost no rivers. Water arrives as cloud. It condenses on every surface and drips, beads, and runs in threads too small to name. Mist rolls up from the lowlands each afternoon and sits in the folds of the ridges until the cold night pushes it back down. The rock sweats. Every overhang keeps a curtain of drops falling in slow, regular time, and the pools beneath them are perfectly round and perfectly clear, each floored with pale gravel that seems too orderly to be natural. Locals say these are the mountain's "first pools," and that they are older than the rock around them. This is one of the small errors of the place. Nobody argues.

## The green

Given how young and high the stone is, the vegetation should not be here, and it is everywhere. Moss lies over the ledges in cushions a body deep, in greens that run from lime to almost black. Tree ferns crowd the sheltered hollows with fronds the size of doors, beaded with droplets. Twisted, dwarfed conifers grip the cliffs, hung with grey-green beard lichen that sways in slow air like underwater weed. Between them are meadows of cold-loving flowers: blue gentian, pale bell-orchids, and a low plant with leaves like folded copper that rings faintly when the wind combs it.

The roots go down into cracks that were opened only recently. Life is moving into the new ground faster than the ground can be broken down into soil, so much of it grows on itself, moss on moss on fern-rot, deep mats of living and dead green that squelch underfoot and hide the fissures beneath. Hikers who leave the tracks go through to the thigh. Below, the raw rock is often warm.

## The animals

Fauna here is abundant, almost crowded. The green is full of it. Herds of small, dense, shaggy grazers move along the terraces in lines, shoulder-high to a person, with long lower lips and horns that curl inward. Great clouds of pale moths rise from the meadows at dusk. Thin-legged birds pick through the moss mats, and flocks of something like swifts pour along the cliff faces in loud dark rivers. In the wet places live pink-bellied salamanders, and in the sky, when the cloud breaks, hang slow scavenging birds with wingspans wider than the city's tram cars.

The animals are unsettled in the way everything is. In any herd, one or two individuals are slightly wrong: a grazer with too many joints in its leg, a bird whose beak has two shapes depending on the angle you look from. Local naturalists keep long ledgers of "drift." They hold that the species haven't finished deciding what they are, and they say it the way a clerk says a shipment is delayed.

## The city

Scattered across this uplift is a city, though it's not built like one you'd recognize. It is a constellation of districts, each perched on a separate shoulder, spur, or saddle of the range, with kilometers of emptiness and moss and cloud between them. There is no center. Wards are joined by cable-cars on iron hawsers, by narrow-gauge steam railways running along ledges cut into the fresh rock, and by pipelines that cross the gaps on lattice trestles. The pipes carry warm water, steam, and something the workers call *fix*, a pale oil that is pumped everywhere and matters very much.

Each ward is dense on its own small footing. Tall brick works with soot-dark chimneys crowd against cliff faces. Stacked tenements cling to terraces on steel brackets. Foundries and rolling mills glow orange in their sheds, the only warm light for miles. Gas lamps and a few electrics burn in strings along the streets. In the dim, each ward looks like a lit reliquary fixed to a dark mountain. Roofs are slate and copper, greened by the constant damp, and moss grows thick in the gutters. Ferns push out of walls. The whole built world is being slowly, gently, taken back, and the residents keep it at bay with brooms and scrapers and long-handled hooks.

Why is the city here? For the rock. The new stone comes up rich in ores, and the seams are so raw that they can be mined almost from the surface: copper in green veins, iron in grey slabs, and a translucent blue mineral, *lantern-stone*, that glows faintly when struck and that the engines and the *fix* are somehow made from. The mines are open cuts and terraced galleries stepping up the mountain, and the mountain keeps making more.

## The noise

It's loud, more than you'd think for a land so sparse. The wards hammer and roar. Steam whistles mark the shifts, and their notes carry for kilometers across the cloud gaps, bouncing off the cliffs so a single blast comes back three or four times. Rolling mills thunder. Chain hoists clatter, cable-cars groan on their sheaves, trains blow long ragged breaths along their ledges. Underneath all of it, and never quite drowned by it, the mountain itself booms: a low dull report as some deep plane of stone shifts, sometimes a long cracking rumble like ice on a lake, sometimes a single pure ringing note when a fresh face lets go and slides a hand's width against its neighbor.

Even in the quiet spans between wards you are never in silence. Water ticks off every ledge. The copper-leafed plants chime. Birds scream across the cliffs. And the wind, caught among the new-cut edges, whistles in chords the old miners call the *choir*.

## Reality breakdown

Here is where the place stops behaving.

The uplift is not just rock rising. It's the world's rules rising with it, and they are not yet fully set. In many places, the mountain is finished enough to trust. In others it isn't, and these zones, called *loose ground* or *soft country*, spread through the range like damp spots in plaster. They are the source of most of the stories.

In loose ground:

- **Sounds arrive before their causes.** You hear the shift-whistle a full minute before the steam rises. You hear a rockfall, brace, and then, a little later, the rocks fall. Miners have learned to treat the echo as a forecast and step back.
- **Distances vary.** The path from one ward to the next may take an hour on Tuesday and forty minutes on Wednesday. The surveyors re-run the measures each season and their books never agree. The maps are pencil-drawn and sold with an eraser.
- **Things repeat.** A street corner may appear twice, a hundred meters apart. A pool may exist on both sides of a ridge, with the same reflection. Some stretches of the railway pass the same signal-box more than once, though always with a different lamp burning in it.
- **Objects hesitate.** A dropped tool falls, but slowly, as though the floor were deciding where to be. A cup of water on a table settles level not all at once but in stages. Iron rusts in patches, then un-rusts.
- **The light misbehaves.** The dim silver sometimes stops at an invisible edge, and on the far side the air is lit by no sun at all, a flat green-white glow with no shadows.
- **The animals go through it.** Herds walk into soft country in single file and come out the other side in a different order, or in a different number, or not at all. The scavenger birds circle over particular empty places for days, as if looking down at something no one else can see.

The city's answer is engineering. That's what the *fix* is for. It is pumped through pipes to the wards, poured into the foundations, and painted along the seams of loose ground in long grey-white lines. It is said to hold. Wards on well-fixed ground keep their streets and their arithmetic. Every foundry maintains a *plumb-warden*, whose job is to walk the floors with a weighted line and a bag of iron filings and confirm, three times a shift, that the world is where it was. When the plumb hangs crooked, they call for more fix, and the whistles change their pattern, and the shift files out.

The wards nearest soft country are the cheapest to live in. The people there are the most cheerful and the most careful, and every door has two locks, because sometimes there are two doors.

## Time

The changes are slow. Nobody sees the mountain rise, though the mine-masters record the height of their portals and re-cut the entrances every few years. Nobody sees the loose ground spread, but old people can say which street used to be certain and which one is a "maybe" now. The moss grows a finger's width a season. The cloud comes and goes on a daily tide. The great cycles are measured in decades, and the city has the settled patience of a place that knows it's being slowly outpaced, or slowly completed, and can't be sure which.

At dusk the whistles all go up together across the gaps, ward answering ward, fifty echoes falling over one another across miles of moss and cloud. The furnaces glow like embers set into the black stone. The moths rise. Somewhere out on the dark plateau a piece of the sky-colored rock lets go with one clear ringing note, and a herd of grazers lifts its heads, all at once, and then, after a long pause, again.
