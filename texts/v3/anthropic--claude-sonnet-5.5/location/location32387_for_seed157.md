# The Full Hollow

It sits in a fold of high mountain, in a bowl of old stone about a hundred paces across. Mist enters the bowl and never leaves. The rim is a gray lip of rock, rounded like the shoulder of something sleeping. Inside, the ground drops in terraces, each a few body-lengths below the last, and every terrace is occupied.

## The Dark

Light arrives here only as a rumor. Above the bowl, the crowns of tree ferns and twisted, moss-heavy conifers have knit into a ceiling so thick that midday is a dim pewter, and the hollow's floor lives in something nearer to deep dusk. Eyes take a long time to open to it. When they do, the dark is not empty. Pale things hold the little light there is: the undersides of fungus shelves, the throats of white orchids, the wet backs of slugs. Here and there a cushion of moss gives off a faint green-blue glow, cold and steady, like breath on glass. It never brightens or dims.

## The Water

Everything is wet, and wet in several ways. The air is fog thick enough to bead on eyelashes. The rock weeps, with slow sheets of water down every vertical face, each face wearing a skin of liverwort and filmy fern. Underfoot, the ground is a sponge of peat, root, and rotted wood, and where it is pressed, water rises around the edges of the print and fills it. The lowest terrace is a shallow black pool, ankle-deep, with no visible inlet or outlet. It stays at exactly the same level. Water enters through the walls and leaves through the floor, and the two rates have long since agreed.

Drops fall from the canopy in a scatter that never thins or thickens. There is no rain here, only the ceaseless condensing of the fog on leaf-tips, which let go their beads one at a time. The result is a pattering so even that after a while the mind stops hearing it and hears the quiet behind it.

## The Stone

The bowl is old. The rock has been worked by water for so long that nothing sharp remains. Every edge is a curve, every ledge a lipped basin holding its own small pond. Grooves run down the walls in parallel, as smooth and regular as the folds of a drawn curtain. The terraces are steps the water cut and then stopped cutting, once the moss grew thick enough to protect what remained. This is erosion finished, a landform that has arrived at its shape and has no more to lose. The boulders sit in beds of their own rubble, long since ground to a fine gray sand that the roots have claimed.

## The Growth

The vegetation is lush to the point of having no gaps. Tree ferns stand in ranks, their trunks furred with epiphytes, and on those epiphytes grow other epiphytes, and on those, tiny ferns no bigger than a fingernail. Roots hang in curtains and braid down to the water. The conifers are dwarfed by altitude but ancient, their limbs bearded with pale lichen that trails a meter long and sways only when something living brushes it. Ground-level plants fill every space between: mosses in a dozen textures, creeping ferns, kidney-shaped leaves as wide as plates, and small bell-flowers the color of bruises.

Nothing is bare. A fallen log becomes a garden within its own length. Where a trunk has rotted, a second generation of seedlings stands in the hollow of it, and moss has begun to cover them in turn.

## The Animals

The fauna is overwhelming. It is not loud, which makes it stranger. It is simply everywhere, in a density that eventually stops feeling like nature and starts feeling like a texture of the place.

Salamanders line the wet rock, dozens to the arm's length, black-backed and orange-bellied, each holding still in its own film of water. Snails with translucent shells graze the fern fronds in slow constellations. Pale frogs the size of thumbnails sit on every leaf, eyes catching the moss-glow like dropped sequins. Millipedes as long as a forearm move through the litter in glossy, silent processions. Moths cling to the trunks, wings folded, so many that the bark seems to be made of them. Spiders hang webs in every gap, and the webs, heavy with fog, sag into hammocks of silver beads. Small pale fish, eyeless or nearly so, hang in the black pool in a suspended cloud.

The animals are mostly quiet. Their quiet is a habit of the place, not a wariness. Nothing here hunts by sound and nothing needs to call. Predation happens, since it must, but it is slow and mostly ambush: a tongue, a jaw, a web. The eaten are replaced at the same rate they are taken. Look long enough and you notice that the count seems fixed. Wherever a frog sits, another frog sits an arm's length off. If one leaves, the gap it made is filled by evening, though there is no evening.

## The Sound

Mostly there is quiet. Underneath it lies the drip, and beneath the drip an occasional small sound: the wet click of a snail's foot lifting from a leaf, a frog's throat pulsing, a soft, wet, rubbery *tk* from somewhere in the ferns. Now and then, from the deepest part of the bowl, a single low note rises, like a finger drawn once around the rim of a glass, and fades. No one has seen what makes it. It comes at no interval you can count.

## The Old Marks

There is very little here that was made. Wedged into the fork of a tree fern, gone soft with moss, is a hand-axe of dark stone, its haft a lashed length of wood so rotted it has become part of the tree. Further on, a cord of twisted bark fiber, thick with lichen, is strung between two trunks for no purpose that can now be guessed. Both are so overgrown they read as accidents of growth. Whoever left them came, stayed a short while, and did not stay. They did not change the hollow, and the hollow has not changed them except to swallow them.

## The Rule of the Place

The hollow does not change. There are no seasons in it, no day and night, no wind. The fog is always the same thickness, the drips fall at the same pace, and the glow of the moss holds one steady green. It has been balanced for so long that nothing in it is becoming anything else. The salamander on the rock is the descendant of ten thousand identical salamanders. The tree fern is a replacement for a tree fern that was a replacement for another.

To stand in it is to feel time not passing. You are cool and damp and closely watched by a thousand small eyes, none of them alarmed. If you stay, the moss will begin to take the edges of your boots. It is not hostile. The hollow is full, and it has simply found a place for you.
