# The Throat of the Cistern

At four thousand meters, on a ridge of scoured grey rock, there is a hatch no larger than a bread oven. It is flush with the slope and rimmed in green-black corrosion. Behind it is a crawlway, and behind that is the only warm place for a day's walk in any direction.

You come to it first by sound. Standing outside in the thin wind, you feel the rock hum in your soles. Put your ear to the hatch and the hum becomes a pressure. Open it and the noise arrives all at once, a wall of it. It is not chaotic. It is layered and rhythmic, a thousand voices sharing one enormous breath.

---

## The Chamber

The room is perhaps twenty paces long and shaped like the inside of a lung. Ribbed vaults curve overhead, close enough that a tall person would stoop beneath the lowest. The ribs are a ceramic-metal composite the color of old teeth, glazed once and now worn matte. Millennia of moisture have laid down mineral crusts over them: pale scallops of calcite, rust-orange tongues, and bruise-green copper bloom in the joints. Where the builders' surface remains, it shows a fine hexagonal texture, each cell smaller than a fingernail and each faintly different in pitch when tapped. Nothing here is natural rock. But it has been wet and warm for so long that it has grown its own geology, and you cannot tell where the making ends and the weathering begins.

Water is everywhere at a low, steady pace. The ceiling sweats. Beads gather at the tip of every rib, swell, and fall in long, unhurried strings into channels cut in the floor. The channels are worn into smooth spoon-shaped grooves, and they braid together and drop into a central basin, black and shallow, where the surface constantly shivers with rings from the drips. The air is thick as a held breath, warm as a mouth, and smells of wet clay, hot copper, and something sweetly fungal.

Light is scarce. Along the ribs run threads of a cold, steady, bluish-white glow, very old and very dim. They do not flicker or fade, and no one knows what feeds them. Between them, pale organisms add their own green luminescence, so the chamber is lit like the bottom of a shallow sea. Faces are readable only up close. Shapes at the far end are suggestions.

## The Choir

The fauna are why the place exists, or at least why it still works.

The most numerous are the **bellfrogs**, thumb-sized amphibians with translucent bellies and vocal sacs like tiny brass domes. They cling in thousands to the honeycomb walls, each in its own hexagonal cell, so the surfaces look like a living mosaic that breathes in and out. Each cell is tuned. A frog in a given cell can sing only the note its cell resonates with, so the whole wall is one instrument, and the frogs are its reeds.

Among them clicks and whirs a second population: **shrill beetles** with drum-tight wing cases, bristling in the dark seams, sawing out a brittle high shimmer above the frogs' throb. There are also **lace-winged moths** that feed on the drip-crust and ride the air columns in slow spirals, and long, pale, eyeless **pipe-eels** that lie coiled in the wet channels and vibrate their entire bodies against the stone, contributing a sub-bass so low that you feel it in your teeth and the water in your eyes.

Above it all, in the highest vaults, roost small grey birds with hooked bills and wide, sensitive throats. They are the only creatures that seem to hear the whole. When the chorus swells and thins, they shift and settle in sequence, as if being conducted.

The total volume is beyond hearing. It is a physical medium, like standing under a waterfall. Speech is impossible. People who have gone in and stayed learned to communicate by touch and by tapping on the ribs, whose hexagons carry taps to precise locations elsewhere in the room.

## What the Sound Does

This is the room's real purpose, and the reason it is still wet.

The frog-wall's resonance is not decorative. It is a machine of a kind no one alive can explain. The chorus, coupled through the tuned cells, drives some lattice in the walls that pulls water vapor out of the thin mountain air. It does this against every gradient, at an altitude where the air holds almost nothing. Somewhere far above, outside on the ridge, hidden intakes drink the cloud and the frost and send it down here to be pulled into drops by the song. The room sings, and the singing makes water. The water feeds the frogs, the beetles, the eels, the moths, and the birds. The animals sing.

It is a closed circuit of appetite and vibration, and it has run for longer than anyone has records of.

The oddities are small but consistent. The drops on the ribs fall in time with the dominant chord, so the whole ceiling seems to dance. In three places on the floor, marked by rings of blue-white glaze, the sound cancels. Standing in one of these circles you are in a silence so total it feels like pressure, with the roar visibly all around you and the drips falling soundlessly. The animals avoid these spots. Nothing sings there and nothing grows there. They are perhaps where the builders stood to listen.

## Growth and Decay

Vegetation is nearly absent. In a few wet niches a slow, dark moss carpets the calcite, and one gnarled tree-fern, no taller than a child, grows from a cracked basin near the far wall. Its fronds are pale and sparse, and its roots have wormed into the glaze and split it. Frogs perch on it in numbers. It seems to be the only thing in the chamber that no one designed.

The floor is a palimpsest. Bones and shed wing cases have drifted into the channels and been cemented by mineral crust into a pale conglomerate. Generations of dead creatures are part of the floor now. Some ribs have cracked and been patched by the animals themselves, with mud, chitin, and dried secretions, in a crude, sturdy masonry set right against the perfect hexagons.

## The Daily Rhythm

Once each day, briefly, the ridge above catches the first sun. Warmth travels down through the intakes as a slow pulse, and the room responds. The lamps brighten a shade. The drips quicken. The frogs' sacs swell in a wave from the entrance end to the far wall, the pitch climbs, and for a few minutes the sound intensifies beyond what seemed possible. Water pours in threads down every rib, the basin heaves, and the birds lift off the vaults in a spiral and circle once before settling.

Then it eases. By midday the chorus is a steady wash, and by night, when the mountain freezes and the intakes ice over, it thins to a few hundred voices, a dense and murmuring dark. Even then it never stops.

## The Feel of It

If you crawl in and sit against the warm wall with the drips falling on your hair, you will be shaken by the noise until it stops feeling like noise. It becomes texture and weather, something you are inside. Your heartbeat starts to fall into step with the bass of the pipe-eels. Your breath slows. The green light moves across your hands.

It is a very small place, a lung the size of a chapel in a mountain the size of a nation, and it is full to the point of touching: bodies against bodies, voices against voices, water against stone. Everything in it is made, and everything in it has gone half wild. It has been singing to itself for so long that it has forgotten it was ever built for anyone else.
