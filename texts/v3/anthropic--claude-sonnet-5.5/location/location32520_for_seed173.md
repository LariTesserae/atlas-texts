# The Sluice Hall at Harrow Deep

The hall lies eleven fathoms down in a basin of old seabed. Everything in it was made by hands, and the hands are long gone. The sea took the place so long ago that it no longer counts as a ruin. It is closer to a reef that happens to be shaped like a building.

## Shape of the place

The hall is a rectangle about a hundred and twenty paces long and forty wide, set into a hollow in the silt. The walls are thick, poured concrete gone the color of a dead tooth. Centuries of tide have rounded every edge. Corners that were once sharp are now the curve of a river cobble. The roof has fallen in along its middle in a long, ragged trough, so the interior is open to the water above. The two side aisles still hold their vaulted ceilings, low and dark, like the undersides of bridges.

Nothing stands upright for long here. The tall things have all leaned and settled. Gantry rails hang in slack arcs. A traveling crane lies on its side across the nave, its hook buried in sediment, its girders corroded to rust lace that crumbles into orange smoke if a fish brushes it. Iron has become a soft material here. Concrete has become a hard one. Their old relationship has inverted.

The floor is silt over flagstones, gray-brown and fine, ridged into small ripples by the tide. Where the ripples run out they meet the low humps of buried machinery: boilers, flywheels, the drums of pumps. From a few feet away they look like a field of barrows, each hump furred with pale growth.

## Water and light

The water is cool and slightly green, and it is never still. Twice a day the tide pushes through the hall from the seaward end, and twice a day it draws back. Each turn brings a slackening in which fine silt rises and hangs in the light like dust in a church, then a slow lean of everything as the current reverses.

The light is dim and comes from above. Through the fallen roof a broad, grayish column descends, brightest at the center of the nave and fading to dusk at the aisles. On clear days it wavers, netted with moving caustics. On overcast days it is flat pewter. At the far end, in the old control gallery, a few lamps still burn: caged amber bulbs on a cable that runs down the wall. They are fed by a small turbine that the tide keeps spinning, and they pulse faintly with the current's strength. When the tide slackens they dim to orange threads. When it runs hard they flare and buzz.

## Sound

Nothing about the hall is quiet. Sound carries far and fast in water, and the hall is a resonant box.

The main voice is the turbine. It sits in a flooded penstock at the seaward end, where the tide drives it, and it emits a deep, wavering drone that you feel in your sternum before you hear it. Its pitch rises and falls with the tide. Under the drone there is a slow *tonk… tonk…* from a loose sluice gate swinging on its last hinge, striking its frame. Every few minutes a chain sways against a steel drum and rings a long, sour note that the vaults hold for seconds.

Over these come the sounds of life. Fish grunt and drum. Cod and haddock make knocking noises with their swim bladders, a rolling wooden thudding at dusk. Crabs click. Mussel beds crackle faintly, like fire far off. Vents in the old pipework leak gas in strings of bubbles that rattle upward, and each pipe has its own note, so that some sound like whistles and some like a kettle nearly at the boil. From far above, faintly, comes the beat of a ship's propeller crossing the surface, a slow thrum that passes over the hall like weather.

## Ecology

Vegetation is sparse. Light is scarce and there is little to root in. Pale sea lettuce grows in green rags on the sunlit stretch of the nave. Tufts of sugar kelp cling to the broken roof edge, holdfasts gripping concrete, fronds streaming and whipping with the tide. Red turf algae film the walls in a thin maroon nap. Otherwise the plant life is a felt of brown diatoms over every surface.

Animals are abundant, and they are the actual inhabitants of the place.

- **Mussels** carpet the fallen crane and the tops of the buried boilers, blue-black and packed so densely that the iron beneath has become a footing for the shells.
- **Anemones**, white and orange, stand in colonies along the aisle walls, tentacles combing the current.
- **Cod and pollack** drift in loose schools along the light column, holding position against the tide. Larger cod lie in the shadow of the flywheels.
- **Conger eels** live in the pipe mouths, thick as a man's thigh, heads emerging like gray stumps. At night they hunt the nave floor, flowing along the silt.
- **Lobsters and edible crabs** cluster in the gaps of the collapsed masonry, antennae out of holes like bundles of wire.
- **Starfish** cross the floor in slow ranks toward the mussel beds.
- **Small fish**, gobies and blennies, live in the empty rivet holes of the boilers. Each hole has its tenant, with a face at the opening.
- **Seals** occasionally come down through the roof breach, pass through the light in a long, easy loop, seize a cod, and go up again. When they do, the whole hall's fish go still for a full minute.

## Rules of the world

The tide governs everything. It moves the turbine, lights the lamps, feeds the mussels, and decides which animals hunt and which hide. When it runs inward, the hall fills with plankton and the anemones flower open. When it runs outward, the silt streams and the eels come out. At slack water, life holds its breath. The lamps dim, the drone falls to a murmur, and the fish turn to face nowhere in particular.

Corrosion is the other law. Everything iron is slowly turning into something the sea can use. In a few more centuries the girders will be gone, and only the concrete will remain, rounded and colonized, a long low reef with a hole in its roof.

## What it feels like

You would hover in the column of gray light, your ears full of the turbine's slow hum, watching cod hang in the current above the ribs of a dead crane. Beyond them the aisles fall away into brown dark, ringing with knocks and clicks and the sour chime of the chain. A lamp at the far end would brighten and dim, brighten and dim, with the pulse of the sea.

This was once a place for moving water to do work, and it still is. It has simply changed who the work is for.
