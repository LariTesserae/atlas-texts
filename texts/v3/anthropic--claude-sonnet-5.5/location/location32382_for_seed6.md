# The Brine Gardens of Ostrel Step

At three thousand meters, a cup has been poured into the shoulder of a mountain. It is about four hundred paces across and shaped like the inside of a bell. Its rim is a sharp lip of cast basalt-colored concrete, still crisp enough to cut a glove. The forms that shaped it left their seams in raised lines, and the wood grain of the vanished planks is printed in the stone. Nothing has had time to soften any of it. The whole basin is full to the brim with warm, clear, faintly green water, and everything that happens here happens under it.

## The Water

The water is heated from below by a tapped vent, held at the temperature of a bath left an hour. It is clear enough that from the rim you can see nearly to the bottom, thirty meters down. Sunlight goes in at full strength and comes out as a moving lattice of gold and jade, thrown across surfaces that have never known shade for long.

The water has a slow, planned motion. Big low pumps at the bottom push it in one wide rotation, a single turn about every day. Loose fibers, pale seed-dust, and threads of rising gas all lean gently in the same direction, so the whole garden seems to be listening to something on one side. The current is too weak to push a person off their feet, but it is never absent.

## Everything Is Made

There is no natural ground here. The floor is poured terrazzo, ground smooth, with chips of green glass and white quartz that catch the light. Over it, the garden stands in ranks: trays, troughs, and terraces of cast ceramic, each filled with a dark, fine, manufactured substrate that was mixed, sieved, and dosed rather than dug. Even the roots grow in formulas. Numbers are stamped into the trough walls, batch codes and dates, in a numbering system whose earliest entries are only a few years old.

Between the troughs run the works: a dense understory of riveted pipes, brass-green valves as big as barrels, and turbine housings, each about the size of a cottage, turning at the pace of a sleeper's breath. Cable trunks bundled in glass-fiber sleeves cross overhead in shallow arches. Catwalks of perforated steel hang between them, and the plants have taken these too. The place is packed. You would struggle to put a hand down anywhere that is not touching a leaf, a rail, a pipe, or a strand of something in between.

## The Plants

The vegetation is dense and lush, and it is not decoration. It is the labor.

The dominant plant is a broad-bladed, ribbon-leaved thing, thick as a wrist at the base, ten meters tall, grown in ranks up lattices of steel wire. The blades are deep bottle-green above and pale silver below, and they flex in the current with a synchronized, lazy grace. This is the *mill-weed*. Along the edges of each blade it deposits a fine white mineral film, a kind of living ceramic. Pumps drawing across the blade edges gather this film into gauzy ribbons, which are drawn into the machinery at the base of each rank, pressed, and spun into thread.

Under the mill-weed grow low, cushiony mats of a rust-colored plant that is nothing but flat plates stacked like coins. These take up the heavy metals leaked by the older pipes and lock them into their tissues. When they blacken, the mats are cut out whole and replaced. Along the pipes hang vines with translucent, water-filled leaves. Here and there, bulbous, dome-headed growths sit on the valve housings, opening and closing their fringes over intake grates, filtering what would otherwise clog the works.

There is no clear line between the crop and the machine. Roots are wired to electrodes. Tendrils have been trained through cast-iron eyelets until they have grown around them, and the iron now is part of the stem. In one bay, a pipe that ends in nothing has sprouted a full crown of leaves at its open mouth, and the leaves are healthy.

## Light

The roof is glass, far above, a ceiling of wide lens-panes set in a steel frame at the level of the rim, so that from beneath you look up through the water surface and then through the panes to an ordinary blue sky. Daylight is normal light, bright and clean, with no eerie tint except the green of the water. It falls in wavering bands through the mill-weed, the shadows of the blades crossing the terrazzo like slow fingers. At noon, the floor looks lit from inside. At dusk, small lamps come on along the catwalks, tidy sodium-yellow beads, and the water becomes a dark greenhouse ceiling.

## Sound

It is very quiet. The sounds that exist are small and sit inside the quiet like stones in a glove:

- The turbines' low hum, felt in the sternum more than heard.
- The tick of a valve, and a beat later, its answering tick in some other part of the garden.
- Strings of bubbles from the substrate trays, rising with faint pops.
- The soft creak of the steel frame expanding as the sun warms it.
- The mill-weed itself, when the current bends it against the wires: a dry, papery whisper, like a great many pages turned very slowly.

No bird crosses the roof. No fish, insect, or snail is here, and nothing skitters or drifts on its own will. The water has been kept sterile of animals by a fine screen at every inlet, and the plants have never been called on to share it. The absence has a texture. The garden is doing everything, and nothing in it is alive in the way that looks back.

## Rules of This Place

**The Rotation.** Everything, including the plants, arranges itself along the daily turn of the water. Leaves lean into it. Ribbons stream in it. The oldest catwalk railings have been worn into faint asymmetric grooves by hands that always steadied themselves the same way.

**The Ribbon Rule.** What the garden makes, it makes without a customer. The white ribbons, once pressed and spun, are wound onto great spools in a bay at the center of the basin. When a spool is full, a low mechanism lifts it up a cylindrical shaft through the roof, and it goes out into the sky, on a cable that vanishes into the cloud. Nobody in the garden has ever seen where it goes. New spools are always ready before the last one is out of sight.

**The Growing Order.** There are no gardeners now, or none you would see. The trays are replanted by machines that seem to know the schedule. Each bay has a small brass plate stating the crop's designation and a single sentence, in a language that was made up recently and has already begun to fossilize: *Grow, and be drawn out.*

## What Is Strange

The strangest thing is the arrangement. Seen from the rim at midday, the whole works falls into a pattern: the main pipes, the catwalks, the terraces, and the ranks of mill-weed all follow the branching of a leaf's veins, from a central midrib down to finer and finer laterals. Nobody drew it that way on any plan. It seems the plants were allowed to lay out the factory, and the engineers followed the plants, and now the plants follow the machines that follow them. Each has taught the other its shape.

Sometimes, in the long, warm afternoons, when the current slackens for a few minutes at the top of its turn, every leaf in the garden straightens at once and stands upright. The light goes flat and even. The hum drops by a note. For that short time the garden seems to be waiting, in the calm of a held breath, for something to be said. Then the pumps catch, the blades lean, and the long slow rotation begins again.
