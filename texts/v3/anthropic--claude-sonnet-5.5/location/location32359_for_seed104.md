# The Held Sea of Ammerwane

Nobody climbed to this ocean. The land rose slowly under it, over a span so long that the water never had time to leave, and it now sits four thousand meters up, warm as a sleeper's breath, spread across a continent's worth of worn-down mountains.

## The Floor

The mountains that once stood here have been ground nearly to nothing. What remains is a rolling upland of rounded granite whalebacks, each a few hundred meters long and smoothed like river stones. Between them run valleys so shallow they read as a suggestion of valleys. Everything sharp has been taken. There are no ridgelines and no cliffs, only long convex shoulders of pale stone furred in green, gray, and the brown of old tea.

The stone is deeply rotted. Where it shows through the silt it crumbles into coarse quartz sand, still holding the ghost-shapes of its original crystals. Your hand goes into it like a hand into cold porridge. Below the sand lies saprolite, stone that has turned to clay without ever moving, and below that more, for tens of meters. The land is very close to its final form. Nothing here can get much lower or much flatter. It is settled, and it is tired in the way a finished thing is tired.

## The Water

The water is warm, close to blood temperature in the shallows and a little cooler in the long troughs. It is astonishingly clear and also full of light-eating life, so visibility swings from forty meters to arm's length within a few strokes. It tastes faintly of iron and green leaves.

It is not a still sea. Slow, wide currents move through it in layers, one drifting east at the height of your knees while another goes west above your head. Where they slide past each other the water shimmers like heat over asphalt. Swimmers learn to read these shears. Fall into the wrong one and you are carried for hours, gently and irresistibly, across a country of drowned forest.

The surface is somewhere overhead, a dim silver ceiling most places are too deep to reach. At the shallow margins it comes within a few meters of the tallest trees, and the tops of those trees break it and hold up small floating gardens of their own.

## The Forest

Everything is lush and crowded. Three layers of vegetation overlap, and the layers overlap each other.

**The ground-mat.** Across the silt runs a dense carpet of creeping green-black filament, thick as felt, studded with cup-shaped flowers that open and close on a slow pulse, roughly once a minute. When a current passes they all close together, and a shadow-wave of shutting petals runs across the valley floor ahead of it. You can see weather coming a full minute before you feel it.

**The trunk-woods.** Rising from the mat are trees that have no business being underwater: thick, pale, straight-boled things with bark like wet paper, their trunks swollen with buoyant gas chambers. They lean. Whole stands lean the same way at the same angle, bowed by centuries of a current that has since shifted, so the forest looks like a crowd caught mid-bow. Their leaves are long translucent ribbons, green shading to violet at the tips, forty meters long, and they stream in the currents in braided sheets. Walking under them is like walking beneath a slow aurora.

**The canopy-veil.** Above the trunk-woods hangs a suspended layer of drifting things: ball-mosses, tangled lattices of thread-weed, floating seed-cities the size of houses that hold their own tiny ecologies. Light comes through all of this in dim, tinted, shifting shafts. The whole landscape lives in a permanent green-amber dusk, brighter at noon, but never bright.

Vegetation and stone are nearly indistinguishable in places. Lichen-like crusts sheathe the granite in layers as thick as a hand, and the crusts have grown their own smaller crusts. Nothing here is bare. Anything that stays still for a season is colonized.

## The Slow Activity

Though the place is ancient and worn, it does not feel dead. Its processes are many and overlapping, all at the pace of living things:

- The flower-mats close in waves.
- Seed-cities detach from their parent trees with a soft crackle and rise, trailing threads, toward the light.
- Silt drifts down in a constant fine snow that the currents keep lifting again, so the valley floors are in a permanent slow blizzard of settling and rising.
- Trunks sway together in a rhythm that takes about nine minutes to repeat.
- Gas-bladders on the trees swell through the day and vent at dusk, sending strings of silver bubbles upward in columns. Whole hillsides seem to breathe out at once.

The one violent event is the **turnover**, which comes every few weeks. A cold layer slips in from somewhere deep and displaces the warm. The water clouds, the ribbon-leaves clench, and the entire forest goes briefly rigid. Then it relaxes, and everything smells of stone.

## The Fauna

Animals are rare, and this is felt as an absence with a shape. The forest is built for more life than it holds.

You may see a **long-walker**: a pale, many-jointed thing the height of a person that moves along the trunks in slow, deliberate arcs, feeding on the bark-paper. It has no face, only a ring of fine sensory hairs. It does not flee. It simply continues.

There are **lantern-shoals**, small, slow, dull-red fish that hold formation in loose spheres and drift through the trunk-woods like embers. They make no light of their own. They reflect what little there is.

And there is something large, glimpsed only as a shadow passing above the canopy-veil, unhurried and enormous. No one has seen it whole. It moves in one direction. When it passes, every flower-mat below it closes at once, and for an hour afterward the water is very quiet.

## The Built

The marks of makers are few and far apart, and none can be dated.

Across the uplands stand **stone cairns**: stacks of rounded cobbles, each perfectly balanced, each grown over with the same crust as the granite. They might be the work of hands or of some erosional accident too neat to trust. They mark nothing. They line up, if you climb high enough to see it, along a curve that bends gently around the bowl of the continent.

In one valley there is a **stair**: nine wide steps cut into a whaleback and leading up into open water. Erosion has softened its edges into loaves. It takes you up, and at the top there is nothing.

In another there is a **doorframe** of dark stone, standing alone in the silt, lintel and jambs and nothing else. The water on either side of it is not quite the same water. Pass through and the temperature changes, and the light, and the taste. You come out somewhere a little different from where you would have been had you walked around it.

And there is one **house**, low and round-shouldered, its roof grown into the hill. Its rooms are dry. The air inside is old and still and smells of rain that fell long ago. No door holds the water out. The water simply does not go in, and nothing explains why.

## The Breakdown

The world here has stopped fully agreeing with itself. Its rules have worn thin, like everything else, and they fail in patches.

**Seams.** In certain places the water thins into air without any boundary between them. You step from swimming into breathing, take three, five, or ten breaths of warm wet air, and the medium thickens back to water around you. There is no surface, only a change of mind on the part of the world. Locals, if there were any, would say the sea forgets itself. Experienced travelers learn where the seams are and use them, resting in the breathable pockets between long swims. But the pockets move, and they sometimes last a single held breath.

**Rain from below.** In some valleys, droplets fall upward through the water. Rain rises from the silt, gathers into slow shivering sheets on the underside of the surface, and there falls back down as something like light.

**Bent direction.** In the deepest folds, "down" is negotiable. Silt settles sideways. Bubbles drift toward the walls. A dropped stone will fall for a while, then slow, then hang, and then rise a little as if reconsidering. Trees in these places grow in whichever direction they were pointing when the rule broke, so the forest becomes a tangle of trunks at impossible angles, all of them healthy.

**Time seepage.** In the older valleys, the current carries something other than water. A flower opens and closes faster than it should. A drifting seed-city passes twice in the same minute, or not at all. A long-walker's track appears on the silt before the animal has arrived to make it. Some travelers report meeting themselves at a distance, swimming in the opposite direction, unhurried, and not looking up.

**The horizon.** The continent is vast enough to have its own weather, and its horizon is wrong. At the far edge of visibility the seafloor curves upward and the surface curves down, and they close over the distance like a hand slowly making a fist. You are swimming inside a lens. Nothing ever gets closer to the place where they meet.

## Sound

It is quiet, but not silent. Beneath the quiet is a pressure-hum, felt more than heard. You hear the tick and pop of seed pods opening, the papery hiss of ribbon-leaves shearing against each other, and the bubble-columns rising with a faint sound like a distant crowd applauding. Now and then a low tone rolls through the whole water, from no direction, lasting perhaps ten seconds. It sounds like a vast door being closed somewhere much deeper than the floor.

In the seams, sound changes. Voices carry too far and arrive before they are spoken.

## A Note on Being There

To be here is to feel very old company. The upland has been worn to its last shape, warm and green and lit dimly by a sun it can barely see, and it has kept going. It has grown its forests and its slow tides and its stairs to nowhere. Somewhere in that long patience, the rules that hold a world together began to loosen, and nothing has come along to tighten them again.

You drift. The flowers close ahead of you, one by one, in a long wave, and you are carried on above them toward a horizon that never comes.
