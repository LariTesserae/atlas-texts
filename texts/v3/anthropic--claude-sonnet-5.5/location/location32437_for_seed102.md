# The Chord Vug

You enter by a crack you have to turn sideways to pass, and for a body-length the rock is ordinary: cold, dry limestone, smelling of dust. Then the crack opens and the sound hits you before the sight does.

It is a hum, but a hum the way a bell is a tap. It comes up through your soles and jaw and the roots of your teeth, a chord of at least five notes held so steadily that after a hundred breaths you stop hearing changes and start hearing the place's *shape*. It never rises or falls. It has been exactly this loud for as long as there has been a room to be loud in.

## The Room

The cavity is a blister in the rock, perhaps forty paces long and twenty wide, with a ceiling you could touch in the low places and never reach in the high. It lies deep enough that the air has been warmed by the earth to the heat of a held hand. The air is thick and faintly sweet, like crushed stems and hot resin. It tastes of nothing you'd call water. Your lips dry and your tongue goes stiff, and yet everything here is green.

The walls are young in the way of a thing broken open yesterday and never touched since. Crystal plates of pale amber and smoke-grey stand out in blades and stepped ledges, edges still knife-clean. Nothing has rounded them. No drip has ever smoothed them, because nothing drips. They formed all at once, in one long pressured event, and then the world around them agreed to stop. The floor is a crust of that same crystal, broken into plates that tilt against each other like ice floes, and between the plates grows the garden.

## The Garden

The plants have no water to drink, so they drink the walls. From seams in the rock a slow, warm, dark syrup weeps, a resin-like exudate the color of strong tea, thick as honey in cold, thinner here. It never pools and never runs. It creeps, and the plants have arranged themselves to catch it.

They grow in **bands**. This is the first strange thing you notice, and the last you fully understand. The vegetation is thick, in tiers of leaf and frond and hanging cord, but it stands in parallel curved belts with bare crystal floor between them. Each belt is a hand's-breadth to two paces wide, and the belts are spaced at regular intervals across the room. Where a belt runs, the growth is lush enough that you push through it with an arm across your face. Where the floor is bare, it is bare as a swept table.

If you stand in a bare stretch and hold a leaf to your ear, or just stand still with your hand out, you feel the difference. The bare stripes are where the sound is *strongest*: the air shudders, your sleeve flutters, fine grit dances on the crystal in tiny standing patterns. In the green bands the air is nearly still, the sound loud but soft-edged, like being inside the bell rather than on its lip. The plants grow where the vibration is gentlest. Everything alive in the room has sorted itself along the wave.

The main growths:

- **Gong-fern.** Broad, leathery, rust-and-olive fronds, each curled at the tip into a tight hollow spoon. When the room's chord passes through them they ring faintly in sympathy, and there are so many that the ferns add a shimmer to the hum's upper edge. Tap one and it answers on the wrong note for a moment, then slides back to the chord.
- **Lantern-moss.** A thick pelt on the lower walls and in the shaded troughs, giving off a low amber glow, dim as coals under ash. It is the room's only light. It pulses with the hum, but so slightly and so steadily that the pulse reads as texture, not flicker. Everything is seen by this warm, sourceless dusk. Shadows are soft and doubled, and colors are mostly browns, golds, and a deep bottle green.
- **Cord-trees.** Not trees, but rope-thick vines that drop from ceiling-cracks and never touch the floor, ending in fat, blunt tassels of seed that hang in the air at exactly the height of a person's chest. They sway in no breeze. They vibrate. Their tips blur.
- **Sponge-cap.** Pale, pitted, knee-high growths that stand out in the bare stripes, the only living things brave enough to. They are full of small holes and they *sing*: they are the loudest single voices in the chord, a reedy, buzzing tone like a struck glass rim. Sparse, one every ten paces, each a pillar of sound.

## The Animals

The fauna is abundant in number but not in variety, and every species has a job in the chord.

**Tick-crickets** are the small ones, the size of a thumbnail, pale-shelled, thousands to a square pace of frond. They sing in unison, all on one pitch, so precisely matched that their choir reads as one instrument. Silence them, and you hear the room change: the whole chord thins, wobbles, and a low grinding creeps into the rock. They know this. If one falls silent, its neighbors press against it until it resumes.

**Drone-beetles** are the size of a fist, black and bronze, slow, and they crawl the stripe-edges in a single long procession, wings half-open and buzzing at a low note that forms the chord's floor. They are the room's basses. Their tracks around the bands have worn a faint path in the crystal, the only worn thing in the room.

**Long-lizards** are the largest creatures, knee-high and arm-long, slate-scaled with throats like inflated bladders. They lie in the bare stripes, where the vibration is fiercest, and hold a single note each, so deep it is more felt than heard, and they do it for days, breathing in gulps that never interrupt the sound. They eat the syrup and the beetles that come too close, slowly, without moving from their stripe. Their throats glow faintly amber when they sing.

None of them are afraid of you. They are busy. The room has a task and they are all at it.

## The Rule

Here is what the place means, if it means anything: **the room is in tune, and to be in tune is to exist.**

The chord is not made by the creatures alone. The crystal walls, cut in their sharp young facets, are a resonator, the whole vug a shaped hollow that rings when struck. But the ringing would die in a year without the singers to feed it. And the singers would have no place to stand without the resonance to sort the stripes and bands. Plants stand where the wave rests. Animals sit where their notes are needed. The syrup weeps at the rate the vibration allows, because the vibration loosens it from the rock at just that pace. Nothing here is growing, dying, ripening, or rotting on any schedule. A leaf that browns is replaced by a leaf the exact size and shade of the one that fell. The number of crickets does not change. The glow does not change. The room has been at its balance since the moment its walls stopped cracking, and there is no drift, no season, no dawn.

It is stable the way a plucked string is stable: not still, but repeating one motion so perfectly that it reads as stillness.

## The Marks of Hands

You are not the first. In the room there are traces of people, few and small and very old.

At the third bare stripe from the entrance stands a **hut**, a low dome of woven cord-tree fiber over a frame of long-lizard ribs, big enough for two people to lie curled in. It is dark with age and slick with syrup-varnish, and it stands precisely in a green band, never a bare one. Inside are a pallet of dried fern and nothing else.

Farther in, a **row of stones** set upright in the crystal floor, each the size of a loaf, each wedged into a socket someone chipped out with patience. They stand at the nodes, in the exact centers of the green bands. Whoever set them could feel where the sound rested and placed them there like markers on a ruler. Some lean; none have fallen.

Near the far wall, half-buried in lantern-moss, are **hand-tools**: a chipped flint scraper, a bone awl, a mallet of crystal lashed to a rib-handle with dried cord. The mallet's head is scarred on one face from tapping the wall. There are tap-marks in the crystal there, a neat line of them, each the same depth, as if someone once sat and tried to *add a note* to the chord, or take one away.

The room did not change. Whoever it was gave up, or stayed, or became part of the hum. There are no bones, though the long-lizards' bellies are very full.

## Leaving

At the far end the ceiling drops until you must crawl, and the crawl narrows to a seam too thin for anything but the chord itself, which slips through the rock in both directions and is still audible, faintly, a hundred paces on, through the stone. Follow it and you find no other room. The vug is the only one. It sings into the rock and the rock carries it away, and nothing above knows there is a garden of bells and bright amber moss, warm as a breath, dry as paper, and perfectly, endlessly in tune.

When you finally squeeze back through the crack into cold silence, your ears will ring for hours with the last note, and for a while everything you look at will seem slightly flat.
