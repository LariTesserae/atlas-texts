# The Undersong Fen

Nothing here has ever been lit from above. Whatever sun this world has is sealed behind a ceiling of leaf and vapor so deep that the idea of a sky has never occurred to anything living here. The dark is total except in a few small places. Eyes would do you no good, and you would learn the country through your skin.

It is warm, like breath, like the inside of a mouth. The air is thick enough to feel as a faint resistance against your face and hands. It smells of wet iron, cut stems, and something sweetly scorched, like a kettle left on a flame.

## The ground that isn't ground

The land is young. It broke open recently by the standards of rock, and the fractures still have edges. Basalt ribs and glassy fins stand up in long parallel ranges, hundreds of kilometers of them, running roughly north to south along the seams where the crust split and healed. Their crests are sharp enough to cut. Only their crests, though. Everything below the crests is covered.

The covering is the whole substance of the place. The plants have not grown on the land so much as taken over its job. Roots, root-mats, and the felted remains of older roots lie in a layer so deep and so continuous that you can walk for days without touching stone or soil. You walk on a springy, sodden fiber the color of dark tea, and it gives under each step with a soft wet sound, then slowly returns. In places you can push your arm into it to the shoulder and feel the warm water moving through it, slow as a tide in a sponge.

Water is never far. It sits just under the surface everywhere, in the fiber, in the hollows, and in the trunks, which are full of it. Pools open between the ridges, black and flat and perfectly still. They are not deep lakes but wide sheets of shallow water lying on the mat, roofed with floating leaves as big as doors. Where a ridge of basalt rises out of the mat, its face weeps. Thin films of water slide down the glassy rock and are taken up at the bottom by pale root-hairs that hang in curtains, waiting.

## The forest as an engine

The plants have no light to live on, so they live on the country itself.

The young crust leaks. Heat and dissolved metals seep upward through the fractures, along with sulfur and reduced gases, and a steady electrical potential builds between the deep rock and the wet air. The vegetation has organized itself into a machine for harvesting that difference, and it has done so on an industrial scale. Nothing was built, and there is not one made object anywhere. But the architecture of the growth is mechanical in every way that counts.

**The taproot columns.** Where the fractures run hottest, roots have fused into columns thicker than a house. They descend into the seams and draw up hot mineral water. In the columns' cores the water flows through channels lined with dark metallic crusts. Lay your palm on a column and you feel a tremor, deep and even, and a faint tingling as though the wood were mildly charged. It is.

**The trunk-mains.** Above the columns rise trunks, though the word is a poor fit. Some are fluted like pipes, some ribbed like bellows. They are hollow through much of their length and divided by valve-like membranes that open and close. They are pressure vessels. Sap heated below is driven upward in pulses, and the membranes clap shut behind each pulse with a deep, wet *tock*. Each trunk beats on its own slow interval, one stroke every few hours. There are billions of trunks, all out of step, so the sum of all these beats is a constant. The pressure in the forest neither rises nor falls. It holds like the pressure in a well-run boiler house, with no operator anywhere.

**The vane-canopy.** Far above, the trunks spread into layered sheets of leaf that are not for catching light. They are enormous radiators and condensers, black and ridged, hanging in tiers. They shed the forest's heat into the upper air and pull moisture from it. The canopy is where the country makes its own weather.

## Weather of its own

The forest is so vast that its climate is entirely internal. Warm humid air rises off the mat, meets the cool undersides of the vane-canopy, and condenses. Drops gather and fall. It is always raining somewhere, and in any given place it has been raining in exactly the same way for as long as the place has existed. Some districts have a fine steady drizzle that has never stopped. Others have a heavy rain that falls on a schedule set by the pulse of the nearest trunks, and every drop of it is taken up by the mat and pumped back to the canopy. The water cycle is closed and finished, a wheel that turned once into its final shape and has since only held that shape.

There are winds, but they are slow, broad, and reliable. They drift from the cool ridge-crests toward the hot fracture-lowlands and back again in long convection loops. Standing still, you would feel them as a slight, unvarying pressure on one cheek.

## The few lights

Almost nothing glows, but a little does. Where the potential between rock and air grows too steep, the trunks discharge. A faint blue-white glimmer, cold and dim, crawls across the bark of a column for a few moments, then goes out. These discharges are the only light in the country. They are rare, and each is so faint that it shows only the nearest few meters, briefly: the wet gleam of a leaf, the grain of the fiber, the pale threads of hanging roots. Then the dark closes over it again. The discharges are part of the equilibrium too. Their number in any hour is the same as in any other, and if you could see the whole continent from above you would see a scatter of pale sparks that never grows denser or sparser.

## Sound

It is nearly silent. What you hear, once your ears adjust, is soft and low:

- the slow *tock* of a distant trunk valve, felt in the chest more than heard
- the tick and patter of condensation falling on leaf tiers far above
- the drip and trickle of weeping rock
- under everything, a hum at the edge of hearing, the sum of all the pulsing trunks, a single note that has held for as long as the land has existed

The hum is the undersong. It never changes pitch or volume, and after a few hours you stop being able to hear it, except when you think of it.

## Density and life

There are no animals. Nothing crawls, flies, grazes, or hunts. The plant life alone is so packed that the space has no gaps. Vines lace between trunks, epiphytes crowd every branch, and mosses coat every vane. The layers stack so close that in places the passage between them is only a crawl-space of warm wet air between one green ceiling and the next. Under the black pools the roots lie so thick they form a second mat below the first.

The plants do not compete for light, which none of them has. They compete only for position along the flows of heat, water, and current. That competition finished long ago. Each species holds a place that suits it, each place is occupied, and the roles are fixed like the parts of a tuned instrument.

## What stays

Nothing here is waiting for something to happen. There are no seasons and no tides. The rock is young, but it broke once and has been quiet since. The fresh edges of the ridges will stay sharp, because the water that would round them is all being drunk. The forest is the same today as it will be in a thousand years, and it has no need of a witness. It hums, drips, and pulses in the warm dark, as vast as a sea and as settled as a held breath, running to no purpose that anyone made, since no one is here to have made it.
