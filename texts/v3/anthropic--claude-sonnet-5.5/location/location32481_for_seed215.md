# The Shelf of Second Morning

## The ground

The land is new, and it shows. The plateau sits about four thousand meters up and runs so far that the curve of the world flattens the horizon into a pale seam. It is still being pushed upward. Nothing here has had time to become soft. Fault scarps stand as clean as cut bread, a few meters to a few hundred, running dead straight across the tableland and stepping it down in long ledges. Their faces are fresh: grey-green and rust-banded rock with the hammer marks of fracture still on it, and no rounded shoulders. Scree lies where it fell, in angular heaps, sharp enough to cut a boot. The rivers haven't found their courses yet. They wander in braids across gravel flats, change their minds every season, and drop into canyons that are only knee-deep or waist-deep, raw slots with vertical walls and no valley to speak of.

Water is everywhere near the surface. The plateau is a shallow bowl of meltwater, condensation, and rain with nowhere established to go. In the hollows between scarps there are sedge-bogs, quaking mats of brown-green that shudder underfoot, and tarns so still and dark they look like holes cut in the sky. On the upthrust faces the rock weeps. Thin films of water slide down the scarps from seams and joints, and in the cold they freeze into slow glassy curtains that thaw at midday and refreeze by dusk. Each face has its own faint, repeating trickle, so in the stillness the whole escarpment seems to be breathing through its pores.

## The air and its weather

The shelf is large enough to make its own days. Mornings begin in a low white fog that fills every hollow and leaves the scarp tops standing out like islands. By mid-morning the sun, thin and clear at this altitude, has lifted the fog into cumulus, and by noon towers are building over the warmest gravel flats, each one dragging a grey skirt of virga beneath it. The afternoons bring sleet and sudden hard rain, then a bright clearing. The evening light is long and level and turns every wet surface to hammered brass. The cold is steady, a few degrees above freezing at midday and well below it at night. Rime grows on everything after dark, and by full sun it drips away.

The wind is the one constant voice, a low even hush with no whistling in it, because there are few tall things to catch it. That is most of what you hear. Under it are water sounds: seep, drip, the tick of ice releasing, the slow gulp of a bog settling. The shelf is mostly quiet, and the quiet feels large.

## Vegetation

The plants are low and grow slowly. Cushion plants form tight green domes the size of a hat or a table, some of them centuries old, their surfaces so dense you could stand on them. Sedge and cotton-grass fill the wet ground in pale tussocks. Lichen paints the rock in orange, black, and a chalky green, and on the newest scarps there is none at all, only bare stone. Dwarf willows creep along the ground a hand's height high and turn a dull gold in the short autumn. There are no trees. The plants gather in the wet places and thin out on the dry ridges, and the land reads as a pattern of green threads laid across grey.

## The fauna

Life here is abundant even though the plants are sparse, because the plateau is a crossroads and a summer larder. Great herds of a shaggy, long-legged grazer move across the flats in slow rivers of animals, thousands strong, their breath steaming in the morning. They eat the sedge and the cushion-tops and drink from the braided channels, and they know the ground. Before a tremor they bunch and turn, sometimes minutes ahead of any instrument. Behind them come the followers: pale wolf-like coursers that hunt in loose, patient packs, and a heavy, low-slung scavenger that worries the stragglers. Overhead the sky is full. Geese and cranes move through in skeins during the migrations, and a broad-winged raptor rides the thermals off the sun-warmed scarps. Small burrowing rodents honeycomb the drier gravel, and their alarm whistles are among the sharpest sounds on the shelf. The bogs hum with insects at midday, and the tarns are full of pale, slow fish that shouldn't be able to live so high and do.

The animals are also the shelf's clocks. Its people watch the herds turn and know that something is coming.

## The settlements

Cities are set widely apart across the plateau, one every few days' travel, each visible from far off as a low pale shape on the scarp-edge, and each nothing like what you might expect. They are not walled. They are grown along the faults, terraced into the scarp faces and following the steps of the land. Their material is a matte, faintly warm, ceramic-like stuff that neither frosts nor stains, and rain beads on it and runs off in clean sheets. The buildings are stacked in layers, older courses below and newer above, so a city looks like the scarp it was built into, extended by other hands. Streets run along the terraces and connect by long ramps and slow spiral stairs. There are courtyards, domes, and glasshouses of a green glass that holds warmth well past dusk. In the evening a few windows glow amber, and people cross the terraces wrapped in dark, thick cloth.

What the cities do is the part that outruns present physics. Every face of every building sweats. The walls draw water out of the fog and cold air, condense it, and channel it into cisterns, and no pump can be seen doing it. The people don't explain how, any more than you'd explain your own skin. Along the fault lines, in lines that run out into the empty land for kilometers, stand slender pale pylons a few meters high, spaced evenly and humming faintly when you put a hand on them. They are the strain-keepers. They read the slow movement of the crust and, in ways the local engineers describe only as "easing," bleed off the stress in small, frequent, harmless slips instead of large, terrible ones. Without them the plateau would be shaking apart. With them the ground moves constantly and very gently, like a sleeper turning over. You feel it as a faint shiver in your teeth, or as a ripple across a still tarn.

Between the cities are the roads. Some are graded gravel, worn by feet and hoof, and others are lines of the same pale material laid straight across the bog and gravel and lifted a little above it. A few carry slow, silent vehicles, long and low, like beetles or seed-pods, that glide a hand above the surface and give off no sound at all. The people who ride them look tired and unhurried. The bridges are the most impressive thing: some cross gorges or lake-arms in single spans of many kilometers, so thin that at a distance they look drawn in pencil on the air, with no visible piers, cables, or stays. They carry loads, and they are steady in wind. No one stops to look at them.

## The rules of the shelf

The ground is not finished, and everyone who lives here accepts that. Places move. A spring that ran last year may be dry this year, and a new one may open in a field. The cities keep ledgers of the land and update them every season. Travelers carry them, and the herders read the land's small signs, such as a changed bird call, a sudden clear pool, or a fresh crack, the way other people read the news.

Water is shared and never owned. A cistern in any city can be drawn by any traveler, and a frozen-over tarn is checked before crossing, by custom, with three taps of a staff.

The cold sets the pace of everything. People walk slowly and speak softly, and work happens in the bright hours between fog and sleet.

## Evening

As the last tower-cloud dissolves, the light comes in flat and amber across the whole plateau, and every weeping cliff catches fire in thin gold threads. The herds settle in the lee of a scarp, breathing in clouds. A pale city on a distant terrace lights its first windows, and its walls begin, unhurried, to darken with the night's first condensation. The pylons hum faintly along their line across the empty gravel. A tarn near the road shivers once, gently, as the ground turns over in its sleep, and a flock of cranes lifts off its surface and flies south across the darkening flat.
