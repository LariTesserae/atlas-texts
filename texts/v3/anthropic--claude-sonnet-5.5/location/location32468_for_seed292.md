# The Tilting Sea of Aru Hollow

Nothing on land is higher than this water. The sea sits on the roof of the world, five thousand meters up, on a plateau so old that the mountains that once rose from it have been ground down to the shape of sleeping animals. The stone finished changing a very long time ago. The water has not finished at all.

## The Surface

From above, the sea is a hammered pewter sheet under a thin, hard blue sky. The air is cold and thin, and the sun is fierce for how cool it is, so your face burns while your hands ache. Wind comes off the ice fields to the west and never really stops. It builds short, steep, close-set waves that slap against each other and throw spray sideways. The surface is never calm. Even on the best days it is corrugated and restless.

Old summits break the surface as low, round islands, each a bald dome of pale granite streaked with rust-orange lichen. They are the last stubs of a range that stood higher than the present sky, and they are worn smooth as river cobbles. On their leeward sides, cushion plants, dwarf willow, and wet green moss gather in the cracks. Gulls and grey-backed divers crowd every ledge and scream continuously.

## Beneath

Go under and the noise changes rather than stops. The water is cold, about seven degrees, and so clear that light goes down fifty meters in green-gold shafts that swing and flicker with the waves. The drowned landscape is visible in every direction: an undulating upland of rounded hills, each a few hundred meters across, separated by broad, shallow valleys. It looks like the swell of a frozen sea, and in a sense it is.

The stone is deeply weathered. Great rounded corestones, boulders the size of houses, sit half-buried in thick pale gravel that was once rotted granite. Nothing is sharp. Every edge was taken off ages ago, and what remains is a landscape of shoulders, knuckles, and shallow bowls. There are no cliffs, and no new cracks. The stone is in its final form.

Over it lies the green.

## The Weed Forests

The vegetation is dense, lush, and vertical. **Ribbonwood** is a pondweed grown to tree scale, with stems as thick as a wrist rising eight or ten meters from anchoring mats. Its leaves are long, olive, and translucent, and they stream in the current like hair. Between the stands grow carpets of stonewort, bright as new grass, and beds of pale feathery **lace-fern** that bows flat whenever a surge passes and springs upright after.

The forests are packed tight. In the thick stands you swim through corridors of moving green, with leaves brushing your arms and light coming down in broken coins. Everything sways in unison, a slow collective lean toward the east and then a lean back. Fine silt hangs in the water and catches the light. Bubbles of oxygen cling to every blade and rise in threads, so the whole forest seems to be quietly fizzing.

## The Rocking

The central rule of this place is the **seiche**. The sea is a long, shallow basin, and the wind pushes water toward its eastern end, where it piles up. When the wind drops or shifts, the water runs back, overshoots, and sloshes again. The whole sea tilts like a tray, with a period of about eleven hours. A single great wave crosses the basin end to end, and for someone on the bottom it feels like the world breathing.

At the crest, currents on the plateau floor run hard, sometimes faster than a person can swim. Loose gravel skitters and rolls. Ribbonwood lies flat. Silt clouds pour through the valleys in brown rivers. At the trough, the shallowest summits surface as broad wet flats, and weed beds are exposed to cold air, where they steam faintly in the sunlight. Then the water returns in a rushing, hissing wall a hand's height at first, then a knee's, then a chest's.

Storms add their own chaos on top of this. When a squall line crosses, the surface goes white, and even at twenty meters the water shudders and swings. Internal waves roll along the boundary between colder deep water and warmer surface water. Turbid slumps of sediment slide down the gentle slopes, and every few days somewhere a bed of weed is torn out by the roots and carried away in a mass, to settle somewhere new.

Nothing here is stable except the rock, and the life has made a living from that.

## Fauna

The water is crowded. Life stacks in layers, and every layer is busy.

- **Silver-scaled herring-like fish** move in enormous schools that turn all at once, so a bright wall of them flashes and vanishes. Their flicker signals the seiche's changes long before a human would feel them.
- **Bearded catfish**, as long as a forearm, browse the weed bases with whiskered mouths. Larger ones, pale and heavy, lie in the hollows under the boulders.
- **Giant frogs**, wide and mottled and smooth-skinned, live their whole lives underwater. They hang in the ribbonwood with their legs spread, and at dusk they make a deep, throbbing, honking call.
- **Amphipods and snails** carpet every surface in a fine, ticking, grazing multitude.
- **Diving ducks and cormorants** plunge from above and hunt through the weed forests like torpedoes trailing silver bubbles.
- In the deepest troughs live **slow, dark, heavy-bodied trout** the length of a canoe. They are old and rarely seen, and they hold station in the strongest currents with barely a fin-flick.

## Sound

The sea is loud. Underwater sound carries far and does not fade, so the place is a continuous, layered roar. The surface waves hiss and boom overhead like a permanent surf. Gravel rattles along the bottom in the surges, a dry, crackling rolling. Snapping shrimp-like crustaceans pop by the thousands in the shallows, like fat frying. Frogs honk. Fish knock and grunt. In the deep water, big boulders occasionally shift in the strongest currents with a low, felt thud you receive through your ribs.

And beneath all of it, from many directions, comes the engine sound.

## The Marks of Industry

Human presence is scattered but real, and it is mechanical. Some past or present people found that a high sea in constant motion is a source of enormous power, and they built for it.

On several of the bald summits stand **pump houses and turbine sheds**, squat riveted iron and concrete buildings painted long ago in oxide red, now flaking and lichen-spotted. Steel penstocks descend from them into the water, thick as tree trunks. Inside, when the tide of the seiche is running, generators whine and cough, and thin smoke rises from small stacks where crews burn dried weed to stay warm. Power lines sag from island to island on tilted pylons, and the wind sings in the wires.

Across the plateau floor, iron **cable stanchions** march in lines under the water, furred with weed, where a tram-like hauling system once dragged cut ribbonwood between the beds. A few flat-bottomed **harvesting barges**, diesel-driven and rust-streaked, chug across the surface, dragging steel combs through the shallows and heaping the dripping green into their holds. Their engine notes thud down through the water, and the fish have learned to ignore them.

On the floor of one valley lies a rusting **dredge**, half-sunk in gravel, its bucket-ladder raised like the neck of a stranded animal. Weed grows through its gears. Frogs live in its boiler. A few kilometers away, a solitary **anchored lamp-buoy** blinks red at night for ships that no longer come.

There are no towns. The workers sleep in the pump houses, on the islands, or aboard the barges, and they think of themselves less as inhabitants than as people holding a post.

## The Feel of It

The whole place is old in a way that makes the motion seem younger and more frantic. The hills have not changed in a hundred million years. The water over them never stays the same for an hour. A boulder that has not moved since the Cretaceous sits in a current that strips and rebuilds the weed around it every week. The gulls scream, the frogs honk, the engines thud, and the sun comes down in fractured gold onto a floor of stone so ancient it has forgotten how to break.

At the end of the day, as the seiche gathers for another slow lean eastward, the whole sea leans with it. The ribbonwood bows, the fish turn, and the lamp-buoy swings on its chain, a single red light on top of the world.
