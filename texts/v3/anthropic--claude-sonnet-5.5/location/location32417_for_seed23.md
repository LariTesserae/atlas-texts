# The Hush Basin of Ilmere Sett

Above the last treeline, where the mountain has been bitten out in a shallow bowl, there is a town about the width of a long bowshot. The rim stands at nearly four thousand paces above the sea-fog. Everything inside it is wet, cold, and lit almost past bearing.

## The light

The sun here is not stronger, only more numerous. Every rock face is sheathed in a moving film of meltwater, and every film throws the sun back. Walls that are dark basalt at their core become sheets of shifting white gold. Cloud sits in a lid just above the rim, thin as milk in a bowl, and it spreads the glare so that shadows are pale and blue and nearly absent. People wear slit visors of carved horn, or bands of soot under the eyes. Newcomers walk with their hands out because the brightness comes from below as much as above. The ground shines, the walls shine, and the air, thick with condensing mist that never quite becomes fog, shines with a fine glitter of suspended droplets. Small rainbows sit in doorways and are taken as ordinary. When the wind drops they hang for an hour, and children learn to read them like clocks.

## The ground that breathes

The basin floor is not rock. It is a raft of peat, packed gravel, and old ice a few body-lengths thick, floating on a buried lake fed from the glaciers behind the rim. Every few hours the lake below swells or sinks, driven by some inner tide the townsfolk call the Heave, and the whole floor lifts or settles by the height of a standing person. Its movements are slow and total. Seams open and close. Puddles slide across the town like thoughts. Springs that were ankle-deep at dawn spout waist-high by noon and are gone by dusk.

Nothing here can be built to stay, so everything is built to move.

## The town

The houses are sledge-lodges: low, thick-walled boxes of fitted stone slabs and turf, lashed to timber skids that are precious because wood must be hauled up from below. Each rests on rollers of peeled pole or greased bone. When the Heave turns, a house is dragged a few paces by its household with ropes of twisted sinew and yak-hair. So the streets are not fixed paths. They are the gaps between houses, and the gaps are always being renegotiated.

The density is astonishing. Roofs touch. Walls share ropes. Stairways of flat stones climb from one roof to the next, and the town has a second town on top of itself, a roof-level city of drying racks, smoke-holes, coracle hulls set upside down as sheds, and cisterns of hollowed rock. Bridges of plank and hide span every gap that will exist at some point in the day. People cross them with the sideways, sure-footed shuffle of those who have learned that the ground has moods.

Tools are simple and beautiful: obsidian and flint blades, antler picks, stone mauls, awls of bone, and a little cold-hammered native copper worked into needles, pins, and the bright rings that mark rank. There is no smelting. There is not enough fuel, only dried dung, peat, and rendered fat, and the fires are small, rationed, and tended like animals. Cooking happens communally in steam-pits where hot spring water runs through stone-lined trenches, and the smell of boiled barley and sour milk drifts across the roofs.

## The rock that weeps

The cliffs enclosing the basin are old, fluted, and deeply worn. The water has had ten thousand years to say what it wanted. Long vertical runnels, smooth as the inside of a shell, run from rim to floor. Potholes big enough to sleep in pock the lower walls, and many are lived in. Ledges have been eaten into stepped terraces. The stone is everywhere dark, glossy, and sweating, and the weeping is regular: each pale runnel gives a thin, steady sheet of water, and in the deep cracks small hanging gardens of moss and liverwort hold beads the size of eyes.

This is the only real green. Vegetation is sparse: cushion plants like clenched green fists, pale lichens in orange and bone, thread-thin sedges along the springs, a few dwarf willows kept alive in the town's shelter and cropped for bark and withies. The people treat every plant as a neighbor with a name.

## Fauna

Life is what makes the place crowded. The cold water is thick with animals: translucent salamanders long as a forearm, their hearts visible as red points, packed so densely in the warm-spring pools that they knot into living mats. Fat white-bellied waders stalk the shallows. Clouds of pale midges rise off the water at midday and drift as slow bright veils.

The walls hold the birds. Thousands of small snow-grey, black-masked birds nest in the potholes and ledge-cracks, sharing cliffs with human households. They come and go in drifting streams. Between them scurry pikas, voles, and squat furred things like marmots wearing too much coat, all of them raiding the food stores and curled up in the warm hollows by the steam-pits. The town has never tried to keep them out. Each house has a low ledge for the birds and a small plate for the whistle-rats, and if a household forgets, the animals remember.

## The strangeness

A dozen small oddnesses, each accepted:

The water on the rock faces sometimes runs *upward*, in slow silver threads that climb the runnels for the space of a few breaths and then fall back. The people say the mountain is drinking. They do not stand under those threads.

The salamander mats, when the Heave is at its highest, all turn to face the same direction at once, and everyone in the town knows without looking which way the next shift will move the houses.

At certain hours the reflected suns on the wet rock stop being reflections. They hold their shape when the real sun goes behind cloud, small round afterimages lingering on the wall like coins, and for a while the cliffs are studded with dozens of steady lights that cast no warmth. Birds roost inside them.

## The Hush

The most important rule is silence, and it is real, not cultural. The town is nearly soundless. The wet air smothers sound, and the peat floor drinks it. Footsteps are soft. Ropes slide with a whisper. Speech is low and brief, helped by finger-signs and by cords of knotted sinew that people carry, tie, and pass like messages. The birds, uncannily, are quiet too, communicating in dry clicks and wingbeats.

Loud noise is dangerous. A shout, a struck stone, a dropped maul rings on the runnelled cliffs and sets the weeping into spate. The rock releases its held water in sudden sheets, and the Heave quickens with it. The old people say the mountain is a full vessel and the town lives in its lip. So the streets are quiet even at their most frantic, when a whole quarter is being hauled sideways by fifty people pulling in unison to the tap of a single bone clapper. Turbulence goes on constantly: houses shuttling, floor lifting, water leaping from cracks, birds streaming, ropes snapping taut and going slack. It all happens in a hush like a held breath, with the dripping of the rocks as the only continuous sound, a fine, endless ticking, as if the whole basin were a clock made of water.

At night, if there is a night, because the cloud-lid glows for hours after sunset, the town lies still and pale, and one can hear that ticking from the rim to the floor, a thousand small drops on a thousand ledges, and under it, very faintly, the slow lift of the ground.
