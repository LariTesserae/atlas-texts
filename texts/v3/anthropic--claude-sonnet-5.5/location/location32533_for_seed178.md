# The Braid

Arriving here is mostly a matter of being struck by sound, before your eyes have anything to report.

It is the dark half of the year, or the dark half of a longer year than any calendar you keep. The sky is a low lid of slate cloud with a faint bruised glow along one horizon, and that glow is the only reliable direction. Beneath it lies a plain about two days' walk across, flat in the way new ground is flat, and everything on it is moving.

## The ground

The land is young. Eleven or twelve winters ago, by the reckoning of the people who keep count, a glacier the size of a small country let go of this basin and withdrew north, leaving a wide floor of gravel, silt, and rock flour the color of wet slate. The ground has not settled. Rivers braid across it in dozens of threads, each one milky grey-green, each one changing course every few days. A channel you crossed at midday may be dry cobble by evening, while a new one has cut a fresh, raw-edged trench through a bank you'd thought was solid. The banks are undercut and sheer. Peaty overhangs the color of old coffee drop into the current in slabs the size of doors, and each fall makes a flat, heavy *whump* you feel in your sternum before you hear it.

There are no old landforms. Everything has a sharp edge: gravel terraces with faces like cut cake, kettle ponds with rims so crisp they look stamped, eskers running as narrow, straight-backed ridges of sorted stone, hummocks of boulder clay still holding the scratch marks of the ice. Erratic boulders sit on pedestals of their own shadow-protected ice, slowly lowering themselves as the pedestal melts, then tipping. You hear those too, a grinding crack somewhere out in the dark.

The water is everywhere but never still. It weeps out of every cut bank in threads. It stands in the footprints of animals. Walk anywhere off the gravel and the moss compresses under you and the print fills with cold, clear water, tea-brown at the edges. The water table is a hand's depth below the surface almost everywhere, and in the low places it is the surface.

## The cold and the wet

It is just above freezing in the day, a few degrees below at what passes for night. Because it hovers there, nothing stays frozen and nothing stays thawed. Rain comes as slush, then as sleet, then as fat, slow drops that hit like thrown gravel. Then the cloud lowers and the world becomes fog that beads on every surface, and the beads run together and drip. The air is at all times saturated, cold, and mineral, tasting of wet stone and crushed green things and, faintly, of something like hot copper that comes and goes with the wind.

Frost forms in the fog on windless hours, growing hair-thin needles from every twig and blade. When the wind returns, it all comes off at once with a sound like a great crowd tearing paper.

## The green

Despite the dark and the cold, the plain is lush, dense, overlapping, and almost violent in its growth. This makes sense only when you learn how.

The plants here are dark, almost black-green, some purple-black, some the deep red of liver. They are built for scraps of light. Sedges stand shoulder-high in ranks. Willow and dwarf birch have grown into thickets of tangled, stooped bodies, their stems bent by the wind into hooks and arches you must crawl through. Under them the moss is layered three or four kinds deep, cushion over mat over feather over slick liverwort, and every layer holds water. Horsetails, ferns with fronds as broad as a table, and a rank, spongy, waist-high herb with leaves like flat hands cover the wet ground between the thickets. Nothing has room. Roots lie on roots. Seedlings sprout on the backs of fallen trunks, and those trunks are already furred so thickly that they've become long green animals lying in the undergrowth.

The reason for the growth is the ground itself. The rock flour left by the glacier is a rich, raw feed of freshly ground minerals, and the meltwater carries them up into the roots continuously. And there is warmth in it: the first surprise underfoot, in patches, is that the silt is faintly warm, like a body. Under the plain, at irregular depths, are structures, and they are doing something to the ground. More on those below.

Light, such as it is, comes from three sources. There is the dull horizon glow. There is the ordinary night sky when the cloud tears, with stars hard and close and a green shimmer of aurora that no one bothers to look at anymore. And there is a low, steady, cold bioluminescence in the wet places. Fungus threads glow pale in the rotting logs, a blue-white algae bloom stains the kettle ponds and lights up like breath where something swims, and the ground beetles carry a little lamp on the tail that they flash in long pulses. On a still hour the whole thicket has a scatter of these small cold lights, all at knee height and below, like a starfield laid on the floor.

## The sound

You should know the sound first, because it is the dominant fact of the place, and it is not a metaphor. It is deafening. It is a pressure. People who live here speak in hand signs, or with their mouths pressed to another's ear, or they don't speak.

The chorus is mostly frogs. Small dark frogs with bulbous throat sacs, in the millions, filling every pond and seep and flooded footprint. They call in interlocking layers: a high, glassy trill, a mid-range rattle, a low pulsing *bonk* like a struck hollow pipe. The layers phase in and out, creating enormous slow waves of loudness that roll across the plain from horizon to horizon. Close to a big pond it is like standing inside a struck bell. Insects join them, midges in clouds so thick they hum as a single tone, and a kind of cricket that drums on the underside of leaves.

Under the chorus is water. The braided rivers are loud because they are choked with sediment and roll gravel along their beds; the sound is a continuous hiss and clatter, like a long train of stones being poured onto sheet metal. Every cut bank collapses. Every ice pedestal cracks. Hail, when it comes, hits the broad leaves like a drum-corps.

Over that, from the ground, comes the third layer, which is not natural: a low, constant tone, somewhere near the bottom of hearing, that you feel in your teeth and in the water in your boots. It comes from the structures. Different areas hum at different pitches, and where two fields overlap you get a slow beating, a throb, like a heart, at once alarming and calming. Standing water rings with it in concentric ripples that never quite settle.

When all three layers peak together, the sound is a solid thing. Birds flying overhead can be seen but not heard. Voices carry nowhere. The frogs are so loud in the wet dark that the surface of the ponds appears to shiver, a fine spray dancing at the top of the water.

## What is under the plain

The builders were not here for the glacier, and they were long gone before the ice withdrew. What they left, what the ice buried, what has now begun to reemerge is the tech of the place: vast, quiet, older than anyone's records, and functioning.

Most of it is invisible. It sits in the ground as mats, lattices, and long spines of grey-white material that is neither metal nor ceramic nor stone, and that reads as slightly warm to the hand. It is grown, or was, in sheets that run for kilometers under the silt. Where the rivers have cut down through the gravel, you can see the edges: a pale seam a hand thick, sometimes with fine channels in it, sometimes with a slow, faint pulse of light passing along it like blood in a vein. These seams are the source of the warmth and the hum. They pump heat and dissolved minerals upward, and they seem to be doing something to the water chemistry, buffering it, seeding it. Lush green is what happens when you fertilize a fresh delta with a machine that never stops.

The thing about it is that it is *responsive*. The people who live here say the plain has weather of its own, and they mean the machines. When a river swings too far and threatens to drown a warm zone, the seams underneath shift heat and the bank slumps the other way. Channels are steered. Ponds are cut where they are needed. Nobody sees this happen. They only see that the rivers of the Braid, though chaotic, never quite leave the plain and never quite flood the same place twice. The whole floor is an engine for keeping itself at the edge of stability, turbulent on purpose, so that new ground is always being made.

Where the machines reach the surface, you find what remain of the built world:

- **Standing pylons**, tall, slender, greyish, tapering to a point, each about three times a person's height, leaning slightly. They are scattered a half-day's walk apart. Their surfaces bead with water and sweat a cold light. Touch one and the hum jumps through your arm. The frogs gather thickest around them.
- **Bowl basins**, shallow round depressions a hundred paces across, lined in smooth pale stuff, always full of clear still water that does not freeze even when everything else does. Steam curls off them. The blue algae glow brightest there. People camp on the rims.
- **A single tower**, or the upper stump of one, rising from a hummock in the middle of the eastern braid. Its lower stories are buried; its upper stories have been scoured to a glass-smooth finish by the wind and grit. A door-shaped opening at head height gives onto a chamber that is always dry and always warm, and which hums in a pitch that makes your ribs vibrate. Someone has been living in it for years. There is smoke from a small fire, and nets hung from the ceiling.
- **Human work**, scattered and small: a few turf-and-driftwood huts on stilts, a fish weir of woven willow, a boardwalk of split logs across a quaking bog, cairns marking where the ground can be trusted. Plank bridges lashed together get washed out and rebuilt. Nothing lasts a whole season, and nothing needs to.

## The animals

Life is abundant, and it crowds. The plain teems.

Beyond the frogs are the birds: waders in huge dark flocks probing the mud, geese that arrive and depart in moving walls, small brown things that live in the sedge and never show themselves. Owls and something like a night-hawk hunt the insect clouds by the cold lights. Voles and lemmings tunnel everywhere under the moss, so the ground is hollow and gives way in places. Their predators, stoats and foxes with pale winter coats and fat rounded bodies, appear as bright flickers at the edge of the glow. Larger, in the willow, are the elk, low-slung, dark, wide-hooved, with antlers laden with moss; they wade the shallows and stand in the warm basins at night, steaming, motionless, and utterly indifferent to the roar around them.

In the rivers there are fish, run after run of them, silver and thick, forcing their way upstream over the shifting gravel, so thick in places that you can see the water bulge. Seals have followed them inland, unlikely and sleek, hauled out on the bars.

The animals have adapted to the din. The birds sing in ranges above the chorus. The elk communicate in slow body postures. The frogs have learned to synchronize their bursts of calling in ways that leave gaps through which other animals can slip. Even the predators hunt by vibration and sight rather than hearing, tilting their heads to feel the ground.

## Turbulence

Nothing here is finished. That is the rule of the place. A day on the Braid contains:

- a bank collapse that opens a new channel and drains a pond in twenty minutes, stranding fish and bringing every heron and gull for miles;
- a squall of sleet that comes across the plain like a wall and passes in a quarter hour, leaving every twig glazed;
- a surge from upstream, meltwater rushing down a dry channel in a brown-grey wave with a lip of foam, arriving without warning under a clear patch of sky;
- a brief lit hour, when the cloud parts and the aurora comes down in green curtains that reflect off ten thousand ponds at once, doubling the sky;
- the glow of the pylons brightening in a slow wave that passes across the plain over several minutes, and the hum rising, and the frogs rising with it, until the whole world is a single roaring note, and then dropping away to a lower drone.

People here read these events as one reads a face. They watch the pylons and the frogs. When the hum shifts in a particular way, they move to high ground, because it means the machines are about to redirect the water and they won't be gentle about it. They move fast and without speaking, because there is no speaking, and they carry their children on their shoulders. When it is over the plain is a slightly different plain, with a new ridge and a new lake and a river in an unfamiliar bed, and they get out their ropes and stakes and begin to mark the new safe paths.

## At the edge

If you walk north, toward the dull glow, the ground rises slowly into the terminal moraine, bare and grey and rigid, and beyond it is the ice, a blue-white cliff in the dark, calving in booming reports that the frogs cannot drown. South, the plain falls to a tidal marsh and then the sea, black and flat and huge, where the rivers all end in a delta that grows a few meters a year.

But the center is the place. Stand on the rim of a warm basin at the low point of the night, when the aurora is out and the mist is rising, the pylons are pulsing, the frogs are at full throat, the river is grinding its stones, and the elk are standing in the steaming water with their moss-hung antlers, and you will feel the whole plain pushing up through the soles of your feet: green, black, wet, cold, alive, loud past all thought, and being built, this very hour, out of its own destruction.
