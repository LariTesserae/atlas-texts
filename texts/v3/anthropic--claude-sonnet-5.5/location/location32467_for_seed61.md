# The Drum of Halloway Deep

Nothing here can be seen from above. There is a fissure in a hillside, the width of a shoulder and the height of a door, and behind it a stair of wet steps that turns back on itself eleven times. By the ninth turn the daylight is gone. By the eleventh you can hear the city before you see it, and it sounds like being inside a struck bell.

## The Chamber

The city occupies a single hollow in limestone, roughly the volume of a cathedral nave stood on end. It is narrow, perhaps forty paces across at its widest, and nearly a hundred deep from the vaulted ceiling to the black pool at the bottom. Everything is built against its walls and hung across its gap. Nothing is built on open ground, because there is none. The floor is water.

The rock is the color of old tallow, streaked with rust, green-black, and a bruised violet where some mineral seeps. Its surface is flowstone, layer over layer of dissolved stone laid down drop by drop across a very long time. Curtains, ribs, and frozen cascades have drawn every wall into folds. Erosion has done its slow work here in reverse and in forward: acid water rounded every edge, then set the roundness in glaze. Nothing is sharp. Even the tool marks of the builders have been softened under a skin of clear calcite, so that chisel scars look like the ripples of a thumb pressed into butter.

## The Dwellings

The houses are stacked in terraces up the walls, several hundred of them, so close they share sides. They are made of cut blocks of the chamber's own soft rock, mortared with a paste of clay and crushed calcite that hardens to the same stone. After long years the joins have healed. A wall here is not built so much as agreed upon by the mineral. Doorways are low and rounded. Lintels are single slabs, pale and translucent at their thin edges, and where a lamp is held behind one it glows like horn.

Connecting the terraces are ladders of braided fiber rope, stairs cut into the wall, and narrow plank bridges lashed across the void. Some of the planks are ancient, black and fossil-hard, their wood turned partly to stone, so they ring when stepped on. The copper is simple: hammered sheet, riveted bowls, thin wire, pins, and bells. It has all gone green, and the green has run down the walls in long stains beneath every fixture, so the whole city is streaked like a beard.

Density is the defining feeling. Houses press against houses. Rope crosses rope. Basins, jars, and hanging vessels fill every ledge. You walk with your shoulders turned. Lamplight crowds into every gap, and there are no wide views, only layered ones: a stair, a rail, a row of pots, a doorway, another stair, a hanging shape in the dark.

## The Water

Water is what the city is for. It is everywhere, but only in certain forms.

From the ceiling, thousands of drips fall. They fall from the tips of stone straws and cones, from the hems of the flowstone curtains, from tufts of mineral like frozen lace. The rate of each one is fixed, and has been for longer than the oldest house. A drip that falls once every seven breaths has fallen once every seven breaths for as long as anyone has counted, and the counting is old. Nothing here rises or falls with rain or season. The water is not weather. It is a mechanism.

The walls weep in sheets where the flowstone is thin, and each sheet has its channel, and each channel is caught: in carved troughs, clay gutters, copper spouts, bowls, and rows of tall unglazed jars. The whole city is plumbed by gravity and patience. Water goes from the high jars to the low ones by clay pipes sealed with pitch and calcite, passing through kitchens, dye-vats, and washing stones, until it reaches the cistern at the bottom, a black pool perfectly still except where the drips break it into rings.

The air is saturated. It beads on eyelashes and on the copper. Cloth never dries, only equalizes. Everything is cool and slightly slick, and everyone wears wool, oiled leather, and waxed felt, and smells of lanolin and wet stone.

## The Sound

The loudness is the city's second architecture. Thousands of drips strike thousands of surfaces, and the chamber gathers them. Stone basins give low, round knocks. Copper bowls ring. Clay jars hum. Shallow trays tick and rattle. Where a drip lands in a full vessel it makes a soft, deep *plup*, and the water below answers it with a hollow, long-tailed *ooom* from the pool. All of this happens at once and never stops.

The result is not chaos but a thick, roaring rain of pitched sound, a kind of cataract of tiny notes. Speech has to be shaped to pass through it. The inhabitants talk in low, short syllables and have developed a whole vocabulary of hand signs and taps. They rap knuckles on the stone to speak across the gap, each house having its own knock. A stranger's first hour is spent unable to think.

Then they learn that the sound is a map. Each vessel has a pitch, and each pitch has a place. A blind person could walk the entire city by ear, and some do. At the bottom, where the great drips hit the pool, the sound is a slow, heavy pulse: the Drum. Above it everything else keeps rough time to it, without ever quite agreeing. It is the largest and oldest sound, and the city's houses are tuned to it the way a body is tuned to a heartbeat.

Some vessels are tuned deliberately. Bells of hammered copper hang beneath particular drips so that the falling water strikes them, and these are the city's clocks, its calendar, and its records. A certain bell sounding at a certain interval means *the second meal*. Another means *a birth in the lower terraces*. Another means nothing at all and is simply loved.

## Light

It is dim, always. There are tallow lamps, small, in niches, giving amber light that the wet walls smear into long reflections. And there is a pale fungus, cultivated on damp ledges in mats like grey felt, which gives off a faint cold green-white glow, strong enough only to reveal a hand held near it. The people keep the mats in rows along the stairs the way one keeps candles in a corridor. Between the two lights, the chamber is a series of warm pockets and cold green ones, and the deep gaps between them are black.

The ceiling can never be seen. It is a dark that the sound goes up into and comes back from changed.

## The Rules of the Place

Nothing changes. This is the world's central fact, and it has become the city's law.

The mineral water deposits stone at a constant rate, and the people scrape it back at a constant rate, and the two are so exactly balanced that the chamber has looked the same for a span nobody can measure. Doorways stay the width they are because a keeper on every terrace scrapes them on a set day. Stairs stay steps. The drips stay drips. If the balance slips, and it can, a channel seals over, a house begins to close like an eyelid, and the city treats this as a grave event.

A custom follows from this. Objects that are *finished with* are not thrown away. They are set beneath a slow drip in the Sealing Terraces, and the water coats them over, year by year, in glass-clear stone. A cup, a comb, a child's toy, a pair of shoes, a bell that has cracked. These sit in rows, calcified and gently beautiful, like fruit in ice. The dead are not buried here. Their belongings are sealed, and their names are given to particular drips, so that a person is remembered as a sound falling at a certain interval onto a certain stone.

## Life

The fauna is rare and stays out of the way. There are pale, blind fish in the bottom pool, slender as fingers, the only thing the people fish and the only meat most of them eat, and they are eaten rarely. There are white crickets on the upper walls with antennae twice the length of their bodies, whose ticking is lost in the general roar. Once in a great while, a bat comes down the fissure, circles the chamber once in evident despair, and leaves. Nobody remembers it as a good omen or a bad one, only as an event.

There are no plants. Food is fish, mushrooms grown on rotted felt in the dark galleries, and a chalky flour ground from a pale tuber that arrives, sacked, from the surface by way of the eleven turns, traded for copper and salt. The city's people are a small, quiet, strong-handed population, a few hundred, who look almost embarrassed to be so loud.

## What Is Unusual

If you stay long enough, you notice that the drips are not random. Over the whole chamber, many thousands of them, there is a pattern. Certain intervals recur. The overall texture of falling water breathes: a swell, a thinning, a swell, on a cycle of some hundreds of heartbeats. It does not vary by a moment. The people say the chamber is *keeping a slow song*, and that the first builders did not so much build here as arrive when the song was already being sung, and cut their houses to fit its rests.

Whether this is the natural geometry of a limestone plumbing system or something else, no one on any terrace has ever said. They only say that if the song stopped, they would hear it in their teeth. And when you climb back up the eleven turns toward the thin daylight, the sound follows for a long way behind you, growing fainter, until you notice you are keeping time to it with your feet.
