# The Kettle at Sull

You find it by sound before anything else. The lowland around it is flat black basalt, new enough that the flow ropes still hold their glassy skin, and the heat comes up through your boot soles. The air is thick and tastes of coins. Then, across a mile of nothing, there is a roar like a river dropping over a weir, though there is no river. The noise comes out of a crack in the ground.

The crack is a shoulder-wide fissure, its lips sharp enough to open a palm. It runs down at a slant into a pocket about sixty paces across. The pocket is a collapsed bubble in the lava, roofed on three sides by overhanging plates of the flow. From above, you would walk past it. From inside, the sky is a torn strip of white, and the world is the size of a large hall.

## The floor

The floor is a shallow basin of standing water, never deeper than a shin except in the middle. The water is the temperature of a bath left too long, and it is faintly milky with dissolved silica. Everything it touches gets a coat. The rims of the pools have grown into low scalloped terraces, each one a lip of cream and rust-orange stone, with a hand's breadth of water held behind it. The terraces are new. Their edges are still crisp and thin as eggshell, and they break under a heel with a small clean *tick*. Nothing here has had time to round off. The basalt walls still show the drips and folds of the moment they cooled, and where the roof sagged, it hangs in glossy stalactites of lava, black and beaded with condensation.

Everything sweats. The ceiling rains a fine, continuous drizzle, because steam from the pools hits the cooler rock overhead and comes back down. It falls all day without clouds. Walls weep in threads that braid down into the basin. Standing there is like being inside a lung. Your shirt is wet within minutes, from above and below and from the inside.

## The works

Someone came here, long ago, and saw what the noise meant.

Near the east wall, where the loudest vent breathes, stands a small steam works. It is a single iron shed on stilts of riveted girder, with a corrugated roof rusted to lace. A pipe as thick as a barrel comes out of a bored hole in the rock. It is wrapped in rotted lagging, and mineral crust has grown over it in pale knuckled layers, so that it looks less like a pipe than a fossil spine. It feeds a turbine inside the shed, and the turbine is still turning.

Nobody tends it. The bearings have run on mineral-laden water and steam for longer than the pocket's terraces have been growing, and they have been sealed by their own scale. The turbine gives off a low, wavering howl, an almost musical note, and it is the second voice in the room. The first is the vent itself, a wide, rough hiss like frying on a scale of geology. Over them both come the smaller sounds: the drip from the roof, the tick and pop of cooling stone, and a gargling from the mud pots on the far side, where gray paste heaves up in slow domes and bursts.

The sound is loud enough that you feel it in your sternum and back teeth. Speech is pointless. Anyone who worked here must have used gestures, chalk on slates, or bells.

The turbine drives a shaft that runs through the wall to a dynamo, and the dynamo feeds a copper cable, green as pond scum, that snakes up the fissure toward the surface. Where it goes, no one has said. Maybe it once fed a distant station. Maybe it now feeds nothing and the current bleeds into the ground. But one lamp still hangs from a bracket over the shed door, a bare filament in a cracked glass bulb, glowing a dim, steady amber that flickers in time with the turbine's howl. It has been lit for as long as anyone can say, and it lights nothing that needs light.

## The other marks

There are three other structures, scattered thinly:

- A **ladder** of bolted angle-iron climbs the north wall to a ledge, and stops. The rungs above the ledge were never installed, or were taken.
- A **tank**, a riveted cylinder on its side, half sunk in a terrace. It has been filled by the pool's own growth: the stone has crept over it in cream tongues until it is more sculpture than tank. Rap it and it rings with a dull, waterlogged note.
- A **row of stakes**, seven of them, iron pins driven into the terraces at regular intervals, each wearing a numbered tag stamped in a hand nobody reads now. They were markers for measuring. The stone has grown up around four of them, and around the newest it is still growing, so a person could measure the pocket's growth by the burial of the stakes. Number one is entirely sealed and appears only as a rusty stain in the rock.

## What lives here

No animal comes here. Nothing crawls on the walls or skims the water. The heat and the acidity are part of it, but there is also the noise, which seems to have emptied the place the way a closed door empties a corridor.

Life does exist, but it is small and flat. The pools are lined with microbial mats in colors that shouldn't sit so close together: a vivid orange near the vents, then yellow-green, then a bruised olive, then a rind of brown at the cool edges, all arranged in concentric bands like a drawn target. In the seepage zones along the walls grow a few stubborn plants, a rush-like reed with jointed gray-green stalks, and a scatter of low ferns whose fronds are leathery and hairless, glossy with condensation. Perhaps fifteen individuals in the whole pocket. Each stands alone on its own patch of gravel, with a whole pace of bare mineral around it.

## The odd rule

The strange thing is the standing waves. When the turbine holds its highest note, and it does so for a few minutes at a time, in a slow cycle you learn to anticipate, the surface of the shallow pools freezes into patterns. The water shivers into fine concentric ripples, and then into hexagonal cells, each one trembling. The pattern holds until the pitch slides away. The mats respond too: the orange rings seem to brighten, as if the microbes lean into the vibration. It might be only the light shifting on wet surfaces. But the ferns nearest the shed have grown in a slightly different way from the rest. Their fronds curve toward the turbine, and they are always the ones that have lasted.

## Time here

Nothing happens quickly. The terraces thicken by a fingernail's width over a year. The mud pots relocate a few inches each decade. The vent's roar rises and falls over cycles longer than a human attention span, louder in the wet months, when the ground drinks more, and slightly thinner in the dry. The drizzle from the ceiling never stops, but it thickens in the afternoon, when the strip of sky overhead brightens and the roof cools less than the floor.

If you stay long enough, you may notice that the rust on the shed is being slowly overtaken by pale stone. In another few centuries the works will be a rounded cream lump with a pipe-shaped ridge and a faint hum inside it. The turbine will probably still be turning. The lamp, maybe, will still be lit. And the pocket will go on filling with its own sound, hidden in the black plain, a room of steam and stone where a machine has become one more feature of the geology, doing its patient work for no one.
