# The Held Terrace

Begin with the dark. It is not the darkness of a closed room. It is the darkness of a great depth, where the eye keeps inventing things to do. Purple blooms drift across the retina and fade. Then you learn to see by what little there is.

There is a little. Along the edges of the terrace, at intervals of about a hundred paces, stand thin pillars of pale material that give off no light. They are places where the dark is thinner, as if the water there had been filtered of some quality of blackness. Near one, you can make out the back of your own hand, gray and without color. Two paces away, you can't.

## The Water

The water is very cold and very clear, and it does not move. Nothing drifts, and no silt rises from a footfall, because silt does not rise. Disturb it and it leaves and returns along the same path, as if reeled back. There are no currents, no thermoclines, and no gradient of any kind. Every liter is the same temperature as every other. Your body's warmth leaves you at a steady rate and never meets anything warmer.

The water is not the sea. It sits on a summit, on a high shelf of a mountain whose flanks fall away into a dry, thin-aired world of bare stone you will never see from here. Something holds it. Where the terrace ends, the water ends, at a wall that is not a wall. Swim toward the boundary and your progress slows, not from resistance but from a growing reluctance. The direction stops feeling like a direction. You turn around without having decided to. Small fragments of stone lie along the boundary, thrown or fallen from above, every one of them stopped at the same height as though set on a shelf.

## The Ground

The terrace is young rock, fresh and sharp. It is black volcanic glass fractured into blades and plates, its edges still keen enough to open a glove. Nothing has weathered it. You gradually understand that nothing here can weather it. Erosion needs motion, and there is none. The stone is not recently made. It has simply never been touched by change, so it remains at the moment of its cooling, glittering faintly where the pillars' thin dark touches it.

There is no soil, no moss, no film of life on any surface. The glass is bare, and the bareness is total and old.

## The City

On the terrace, small enough to walk across in an hour, stands a city. It is built to the same rule as everything else.

There are perhaps two hundred structures, spaced widely, each in its own field of empty glass. The spaces between them are wider than the buildings are tall. Whoever laid it out valued the emptiness as a material. The streets are not paved. They are lines, faint seams where the glass has been fused smooth, running between structures in curves that never meet at corners. The seams meet in gentle tangents, so that walking them feels like being handed from one hand to the next.

The buildings are tall, narrow, and faceted, sheathed in a gray-green substance that is neither metal nor ceramic. It is cold to the touch, colder than the water. Some walls have a faint grain, like wood, but the grain runs in three directions at once. Look at one long enough and you will feel it trying to resolve into a pattern, the way a word repeated loses its meaning and then regains a different one.

The doors are round and open. Nothing inside has been disturbed because nothing inside has ever been anywhere else. Rooms contain arrangements of low platforms and standing rings, precisely placed. The rings are large enough to walk through, and if you do, nothing happens, except that afterward the water around you feels very slightly more still, if that is possible. You suspect you have just passed through part of the machinery.

## What the City Is For

No one is home, and you come to believe no one is meant to be. The city is not a habitation. It is a mechanism, and its function is to hold the water in this state: motionless, uniform, unchanging. The buildings are its organs. The pillars at the rim are its senses, the thin dark places, each one subtracting from the water the small disturbances that would otherwise creep in from the boundary. The city is an enormous, patient act of not-happening.

Why, you cannot say. Perhaps something is stored here, something that would spoil if the water moved. Perhaps the stillness is the thing being made. You find no inscription, only, at the center, a plaza of smooth glass with one standing ring larger than the others. Its interior is not water. It is a clear, denser stillness that your hand enters with difficulty, and when withdrawn feels as if it belongs to a slightly older person.

## Sound

The place is mostly quiet, but quiet with a texture. Your own movements produce sound that travels strangely. A tap on the glass returns not as an echo but as a slightly changed version of itself, lower and slower, arriving from the direction you did not strike. Occasionally, at intervals that never vary, there is a single low tone, felt in the sternum more than heard, lasting about as long as an exhalation. It is the only rhythm here. You count it and find it exactly regular, and after a while you notice that your own pulse has begun to match it.

## Life

Fauna is rare. There are no plants, no fungi, nothing that grows. But in perhaps a dozen places, at long distances apart, you find animals.

They are pale, narrow-bodied, about the length of a forearm, with too many fins arranged along the flanks in a pattern that seems like a script. They hold position in the water, motionless, facing the nearest pillar, each in a place that appears chosen. They do not respond to light, since there is none, or to touch. Approach and one will turn slowly, its body aligned to the vector between you and the pillar, and stay that way until you leave. You do not learn what they eat. You do not learn whether they are alive in the way you are. They may be part of the mechanism, or the mechanism's tenants, or its last remaining product. Their stillness is so complete that you begin to think of them as instruments for reading the water, and yourself as another one, briefly.

## What Happens To You

You will not be harmed. This is the strangest part. The place is not hostile. It is indifferent in the way a held breath is. But it is very difficult to be here and remain in a state of change. Your thoughts slow. Your sense of having arrived somewhere and being about to leave loosens. You find yourself standing between two buildings, uncertain how long you have been there, and the uncertainty does not trouble you.

The one clear signal that time still runs is the tone, the low exhalation in the chest. So you count it, and you leave by counting it, walking the fused seam to the rim at the pace of the tone, until the reluctance builds in your legs and you turn, not having decided to, toward the surface you can't see, and rise through water that does not part for you and does not resist, water that closes behind you exactly as it was.

Below, the city holds. It has not noticed you. It has never noticed anything. That is its work.
