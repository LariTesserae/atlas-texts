# The Ringing Flat

The land has been worn down for so long that nothing is left to wear. Whatever mountains stood here were ground to gravel, the gravel to silt, and the silt carried off. What remains is a floor: a vast, shallow basin of grey-green bedrock laid in plates, tilted a degree or two, and stained rust along every joint. The horizon is a clean line in every direction. Here and there it is broken by a knuckle of harder stone, the stump of something enormous, rounded and lichen-pale, rising no higher than a house.

Water is everywhere and almost never deep. It stands in sheets a finger thick over the stone. It seeps from the seams between plates and weeps down the faces of the knuckles in long dark stains. It pools in shallow dishes the rock has dissolved for itself. Step on a wet plate and it sighs. Press a palm to bare ground and the moisture rises to meet it. There are no channels, because the slope is too gentle to make them. The water moves as a slow, braided film, sliding toward some low place too far away to see.

## Light and air

The sky is a lid of low cloud the color of pewter, lit unevenly from within. There is no sun, only a brighter smear that travels the overcast through the day and leaves the world in a dusk that never gets darker or lighter. Mist collects in the hollows and beads on everything. The air is cool and tastes of iron and wet slate. Breath shows faintly. Skin stays damp.

In the dimness the ground gives off its own light. The films of mat that coat the wet stones glow a faint green-white, brightest where something has disturbed them. A footstep leaves a trail of pale prints that fade over a minute. From a distance the flat looks scattered with breath-fogged windows, each one a pool lit from underneath.

## What lives here

There are no plants. Nothing has rooted here in a very long time, if anything ever did. The base of life is the mat: slick, layered films of microbes and felted fungus in ochre, oxblood, and bruise-violet, living on the iron and sulfur that leak from the stone. It grows in rings around every seep, with the youngest, brightest edge facing outward. Everything else feeds on the mat, or on what feeds on it.

The animals are abundant, and they are the reason the place is impossible to be quiet in.

**Bellwrights** are the loudest. They are squat, plated, mud-colored creatures the size of a loaf of bread, something between a toad and a crab. Each has a hollow, drum-tight shell on its back. They wedge themselves into the rock's dissolved dishes, which act as resonating bowls, and boom. One is a bark. Ten thousand make a pressure you feel in the teeth and the sternum. The chorus rolls across the flat in slow waves, swelling and collapsing in patterns that seem almost like speech. Each pool tunes itself to the depth of its bowl, so the whole plain is a vast, uneven instrument.

**Clatterers** are long-legged waders, pale and jointed, that feed along the mat's edge. They strike their hard beaks against the stone in rapid volleys, a sound like hail on a tin roof, to flush the small grazers from the film. Flocks of them move in loose lines, and the noise moves with them.

**Skirl-eels** live in the seams and the thin water above the bedrock. They are thin as cord and about as long as an arm, and they whistle through slits along their flanks. The sound is high and continuous, a thin needle of noise threaded through the low booming.

Together the three make a sound that is not really a sound but a medium, like weather. Standing in it is like standing in surf. Conversation is impossible past arm's length. Thought itself has to shout.

The predators are the exceptions. **Hushers** are broad, flat, low-slung animals, gray and eyeless, that glide over the wet stone on a film of their own slime. They make no sound at all, and their bodies seem to swallow it. A patch of ground where the din has gone dead is a patch to walk around. Everything on the flat, animal or person, has learned to read silence as teeth.

## The rules the place keeps

The bedrock rings. Strike a plate of the pale banded stone with another stone and it answers, sometimes for a long time, at a pitch set by its thickness. The bellwright choruses lock onto these tones and drift toward them, so the loudest ground is always over the thinnest, most resonant plates. The animals crowd there in dense living amphitheaters. Elsewhere the flat is sparse, with great stretches of wet stone and only the low hiss of the skirl-eels.

There is a slow tide with no moon. Every so often, at intervals no one has quite worked out, the water table rises through the stone. The sheets thicken to ankle depth, the mat blazes, and the chorus changes key and doubles in volume. Then it recedes over several days, leaving the plates slick and the pools full and a fine scum of new mat on everything. The people say the flat is breathing. From the pattern of the seeps, it may be.

## The people

Those who live here are few and scattered, small bands moving from knuckle to knuckle. They have no metal and little need of it. They work flint and the hard banded stone, bone, sinew, and hide. They felt the tougher fungal skins into cloth. Their tools are hand-made, precise, and old in pattern: scrapers, awls, hooked spears for clatterers, flat-bladed knives for cutting the mat.

Their structures are low and few. Ring-walls of stacked stone, waist high, roofed with hide stretched over rib frames. Cairns mark good water and the edges of husher ground. On the dry crown of a knuckle you might find a single hut, shut and tended, with a hearth of dried mat-cake smoldering inside. Between the huts, some plates have been carved with long rows of shallow cups and grooves. These are struck with a stone in set rhythms, and the people use them to send messages across the flat, since a voice is useless. The ringing stone carries a clear, dry knock through the roar. A conversation between two camps is a slow, stubborn tapping that takes hours to cross the distance.

The people wear plugs of wax and felt in their ears, and speak with their hands and faces. They read the mist and the glow of the pools. They know when the tide is turning because the chorus lowers a half tone before the water comes, and they can tell where the hushers are by where the noise has holes.

## The feeling of it

It is a place that has finished changing and is now only lived in. The rock has nothing left to lose. The water goes on moving through it, the mat goes on glowing, and the vast, uneven choir goes on singing in its bowls under a gray sky. Nothing here is silent except the things that will eat you, and so the people of the flat find a kind of peace in the roar. To them the noise means the world is still full of life.
