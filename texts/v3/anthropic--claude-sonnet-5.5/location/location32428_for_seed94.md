# The Sodden Shelf

The land is a plateau tilted slightly toward the west. It is so wide that its weather is generated on it rather than arriving from elsewhere. From the eastern rim to where it breaks into the lowland fog is perhaps nine hundred kilometers of the same drowned green. It sits at the height where air begins to feel thin and sweet, and it stays cool all year. The mornings are the temperature of a cellar. The afternoons reach only the warmth of a hand held over a cup.

## Ground

There is no bare ground. The rock is old, dark, folded gneiss and slate, worn into long rounded backs like herds of sleeping animals, and every back is upholstered. First comes a skin of crustose lichen in slate blue and oxblood. Over it comes cushion moss as deep as a forearm, then liverworts in overlapping green scales, then filmy ferns growing in the moss, then small orchids growing in the ferns. Roots go down into last century's dead leaves, and those sit on the century before. In places the living surface floats on a spongy mat a body-length thick, and walking on it feels like walking on a mattress laid over water. Where the mat is thin, the rock underneath weeps. Water seeps from every seam and bedding plane in a continuous fine sheen, beading on the mosses and running in threads too small to hear.

The valleys are shallow, wide, and braided. Each one holds a marsh of standing water the color of strong tea, dotted with tussocks and hummocks and lanes of open black water. Between the marshes rise the ridges, and on the ridges the forest stands so close that trunks touch.

## The Forest

The trees are tall, slow, and narrow: southern-beech-like, with small stiff leaves, leaning slightly with the tilt of the shelf. Their bark is invisible under moss, so each trunk looks like a green column with a faint tree-shaped hint inside. Branches carry gardens. There are bromeliad-like rosettes holding pools, and in each pool live tadpoles, water beetles, and a translucent snail. Hanging beards of lichen reach two meters. The canopy is layered five or six times, and at each layer the light is a different green. At the bottom the light is the dim gold-olive of the inside of a bottle.

Nothing has room. Saplings grow on fallen logs, and the logs are so thick with the next generation that they never touch soil. Vines cross vines. Fungi stand out from the trunks in tiers of pale shelves. Under them, smaller fungi grow on the shelves. Space is so full that death is the main way anything gets a foothold. A tree falls, and within a season the trunk is a ridge of a thousand seedlings.

## Water and Weather

The shelf makes its own days, and they are regular enough to set a clock by.

Before dawn, cold air lies in the valleys and the marshes smoke. By early morning the smoke has thickened into a fog bank that fills every low place to the same level, so the ridgetops stand out of a white sea like islands with dark shaggy coats. Around mid-morning the sun warms the fog from above. It lifts, sags, tears, and climbs the slopes in slow rolling sheets, and it drips off every leaf so that the forest seems to be raining while the sky is clear.

By noon the fog has become cloud, a flat pearl ceiling a few hundred meters up. In the afternoon the ceiling darkens and lowers, and rain comes, not violent but deliberate: fat, cold, steady drops that continue for an hour or two. Every rosette overflows. Every rock sheet thickens into a film. The marshes rise a hand's width. Then the rain thins, the cloud breaks along the western edge, and long horizontal light comes through the trees, gold on green, with steam rising off everything.

On rare days a much larger system crosses the shelf. It is a wall of gray hundreds of kilometers wide, and it brings a three-day rain that swells the braided channels until they merge into one glittering sheet, and the ridges become archipelagos.

## The Engines

There are almost no made things here, only a few. In the marsh valley nearest the eastern rim lie the remains of an industrial episode, possibly a mining venture, possibly a survey, long since given up. Nothing stands but a boiler the size of a small house, lying on its side. It is orange-black with rust and furred with moss. Fern trees grow out of its firebox, and a narrow-gauge rail runs from it fifty meters into the peat and then disappears, its sleepers gone soft as sponge. A cast-iron wheel lies half-swallowed beside it. This is the entire human trace. Visitors, if there were any, would find it moving because it is so small against so much.

The engines that matter are the shelf's own. Water this abundant and ground this old have made a natural industry.

**Bog-iron works.** In the acid marsh water, iron-fixing microbes work in bright orange mats. Over centuries they precipitate iron into crusts and nodules, rusty brown pans below the peat, which in places have hardened into slabs. The marsh edges glow ochre where the seep water meets air and oxidizes. It is a slow foundry with no fire.

**Siphon springs.** In the limestone pockets on the western slope, underground reservoirs fill and then empty through natural siphons. The pools rise slowly, hold, and then drain in a long gulp over ten minutes before beginning again. The rhythm is about forty minutes. Whole terraces of mossy basins fill and drain in sequence, like the strokes of a slow piston. Small fish are stranded and released with each cycle, and herons have learned the timing.

**Steam vents.** Where the crust is thin there are warm seeps. The air above them carries a low fog that condenses in the cold, and the ground around them is bright green in a ring, sometimes with a gently thudding rhythm audible through the moss as the water underneath boils and collapses. These are the closest thing the shelf has to a boiler house, and the fauna use them the way people use stoves.

## Life

The fauna are abundant and mostly small, secretive, and quiet. Sound dies quickly in moss and fog, and the animals have adapted. They communicate through low sounds, touch, scent, and slow flashes of color.

Wading birds with long, pale, slightly upturned bills move through the marsh channels, hunting by touch. Stag-antlered deer as small as dogs browse the ridge understory, their coats dark and damp, their footfalls padded. There are otter-like animals with dense fur in the water lanes, and slow, heavy, ground-dwelling rodents that tunnel through the peat and leave long raised tracks across the moss like veins under skin. The frogs are numerous, and in the rosettes they are jewel-colored and no bigger than a thumbnail. Enormous slugs, banana-yellow and forearm-long, graze the fungal shelves. Above them, spiders string sheets of silk across the gaps between branches, and each sheet is beaded with droplets so that on foggy mornings the forest is strung with a thousand glass hammocks.

Predation is constant and mostly silent: a marten-like hunter dropping from a limb, a heron's single strike, a pit-lurking beetle larva under a moss cushion. Wounded prey is seldom heard. What one notices is sudden absence. A brief tremor in the ferns, and something that was there is gone.

Herds are rare. What crosses the shelf in numbers are the migrating cranes. Twice a year they pass over in long, unhurried lines, and their calls, the loudest sound the place has, come down through the fog as a thin, distant, rusty creaking, like an old hinge turning far away.

## Sound

Mostly, the shelf is quiet. There is dripping, which never stops: a million small taps at different pitches on leaf, water, and moss. Now and then a gurgle from the siphon terraces, or the low hush of rain arriving through the canopy a minute before it reaches the ground. Wind is a soft movement in the topmost leaves, felt more than heard. Between these sounds the silence is thick and padded, and it seems to belong to the moss.

## Feel

Nothing in this place is in a hurry, and nothing is finished. Rock becomes soil becomes tree becomes rock-hard iron crust becomes soil again, a cycle so old it has settled into balance, with every space taken and every edge softened. Even the rusted boiler is being slowly turned into something else, and in another thousand years it will be a low orange mound with a tree on top.
