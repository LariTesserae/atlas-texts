# The Boiler Shelf

Four thousand eight hundred meters up, where the map should show ice, there is a green room in the mountain.

It is a shelf about the size of a village square, a few hundred paces across, cut into the flank of a young volcanic massif. The rock around it is still raw. Andesite columns stand in ranks with their edges unsoftened, the color of wet slate where they are dark and of rust and mustard where the steam has worked on them. Screes of fresh shards lie under every wall, and they ring like crockery when stepped on. Nothing here has had time to become round.

The shelf is warm because the ground is. Heat comes up through cracks in the floor, and the air over it stays near the temperature of a greenhouse while the ridge above wears snow. Thin, cold air pours down over the rim and meets the rising steam, and the meeting makes a permanent ceiling of cloud about thirty meters up. It is lit from within and gives a flat, pearly, even daylight. Above it the sky is nearly black-blue. Below it everything drips.

## Water

Water is the first thing, and it is everywhere at once. Weeping walls line the back of the shelf, where meltwater seeps through the columns and beads on every joint. The beads gather into threads and the threads into a dozen small falls that never dry. The floor is a mosaic of shallow terraces, each rimmed with a lip of pale sinter, each holding a few inches of milky blue-green water that steams faintly. The terrace lips grow a finger's width in a lifetime. Someone standing at the edge can watch the surface tremble as the deposit slowly adds itself.

Underfoot the ground is sponge: moss, then black peat, then water, then hot stone. Pressing a boot down brings up a warm brown seep.

## Green

The vegetation is packed tight. Tree ferns with trunks as thick as a thigh rise from the terraces, and their crowns interlock. Their fronds drip in a steady tick onto the broad leaves below. Beneath them are giant rosette plants, waist-high, silver-furred and cabbage-shaped, a high-mountain lineage that found a place with no frost. Bromeliads clot every fork of every branch, each cup holding its own pond. Moss covers everything else in layers: emerald on the rocks, orange around the vents, a liver-red felt on the pipes. Orchids the size of a fingernail hang in the wet air, and liverworts glisten on the column faces. Nearly every surface is another organism's ground.

There is a rule of this garden that the visitor learns by looking. Distance from the vents sorts everything into rings. Closest to the steam, only pale crusts of bacteria and yellow sulfur grow. Then comes red moss, then ferns, then the silver rosettes at the cooler edges, and finally, at the rim, bare cold rock again. The whole shelf is a target, and the bull's-eye is a machine.

## The Works

The machine is a geothermal steam station, one of the old kind, built when someone decided that a mountain's breath ought to be put to work. It is a squat brick-and-iron hall with a corrugated roof gone to lace, blackened with mineral, and furred over with moss so thoroughly that from the rim it reads as a knoll. Fat riveted pipes come out of the ground in three places, bridge the terraces on cast-iron trestles, and converge on the hall. The pipes are wrapped in fibrous insulation that has become a garden of its own, with ferns rooted in the lagging and stalactites of sinter hanging from every flange.

The turbine inside still runs. Nobody remembers when it was last tended. The governor is rusted into a setting that neither speeds it nor stops it. It turns at a low, patient speed, and the generator it drives sends current out along a line of porcelain insulators and copper wire that ends, after forty meters, in a post with nothing on it. The current goes nowhere, or nowhere anyone has found. In the humid air the post crackles softly and shows a faint blue at its tip on dark evenings.

Scattered elsewhere are other remains: a catwalk of grating half swallowed by moss, a winch house at the cliff edge with its cable still running out over the drop, a rail bogie rusting in a terrace pool and slowly being plated with white sinter, a worker's hut with one wall gone and a cast-iron stove holding a fern. There are a few other things, a gauge with a fogged face, a valve wheel the size of a table. They are separate and small, and the green has taken most of them.

## Sound

The place is loud, and the noise is the shelf's real architecture. The vents scream on a high thin note like kettles the size of houses. The turbine hums a low fifth beneath them. The pipes, expanding and cooling, knock, clang, and tick in irregular chords. A dozen waterfalls hiss into pools, and the pools gurgle and pop where gas rises through them. Fronds patter. All of it is trapped by the cloud ceiling and the curved wall behind, and it swells into a roar that fills the chest.

The weird part is the rim. The basin is shaped so that sound hardly leaves it. Climbing the last scree to the lip, a person hears the roar drop away over the space of a single step, as if a door had shut, and stands in cold, windy, nearly total silence with the whole racket audible only as a faint vibration in the boots. Turning back and stepping down, the noise returns like a wall. The old workers are said to have used this: a shout at the rim could not be heard below, so they kept a bell on a rope that was rung by hand.

## Life

Animals are few and mostly small. Frogs, black with a yellow saddle, sit in the warmest terraces and call in the gaps between vent shrieks, a sound too tiny to reach anyone farther than a few meters. Springtails and pale beetles work the moss. A single species of dark, short-winged bird, a kind of rail, picks along the edges of the pools and does not seem to hear the noise at all. Once in a long while a condor passes over the cloud lid, and its shadow crosses the pearly ceiling like a slow thought.

## Time

Change here is slow, and it is mostly deposition. The sinter lips rise. The moss on the roof thickens by a hair a year. A vent that screamed in one decade has quietly gone to a sigh, and a new crack has opened twelve paces off, and the rings of color are redrawing themselves around it, ferns advancing over red moss over yellow crust. In a century the works will be a mound of rust and stone, and the turbine, if it is still turning, will be turning inside a hill.

For now the shelf holds. At dusk the cloud ceiling dims from pearl to lead, the post with nothing on it lights its small blue tip, and the roar goes on under it, patient and enormous, in a green room the mountain kept warm.
