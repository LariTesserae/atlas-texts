# The Bright Pocket

You come into it on your belly, through a crack the width of a shoulder, and the heat arrives before the light does. It is a wet heat, close to the temperature of blood and rising. It settles on the skin like a hand, and breathing through the mouth is easier than through the nose. Then the crack lets go of you and you slide out onto a ledge, and the light is everywhere.

## The Room

The chamber is perhaps forty paces across and, at its highest, three times a person's height. It is a bubble in the rock, a place where gas or molten stone once pushed outward and then left. The rock is young. Nothing here has had time to be worn down. The walls are dark basalt, still sharp-edged where they fractured, still showing the shine of the moment they cooled. In places the surface is a skin of black glass thin enough to flake under a fingernail. Ridges of frozen flow cross the floor in ropes and folds, each fold crisp enough to cut a palm. Nothing has softened these edges. Water has begun on them, but only begun.

The floor slopes toward the center, where a shallow basin holds a pool the color of weak tea. The rest is broken stone, pale mineral crust, and long stretches of bare glossy rock.

## The Light

The light comes from above, and it is the strangest ordinary thing here.

From the roof hang a few dozen filaments, each thinner than a hair, each impossibly straight until it isn't. They descend from pinholes in the stone and don't glow so much as carry. At the top each is a white point. Along its length it stays nearly invisible, a faint scratch on the air. At the bottom it opens into a soft, wide bloom of daylight, as if a slice of noon had been threaded through a needle and let out here. Each bloom is bright enough to make you squint, and the light has the clean white of high-sun surface light, not the orange of fire or the green of decay.

Nobody knows how they work. The filaments bend around corners in the rock without loss. They pass through solid stone as though it were water. Where two cross they do not interfere. Touched, they are cool, which is absurd in this heat, and they hum faintly against the fingertip, a note just below hearing. Whether something made them or the rock grew them is not settled. They have no maker's marks and no junctions, and nothing about them is arranged for a purpose you can name. They are simply there, as ordinary in this place as stalactites, and the things that live here treat them like weather.

Because the blooms hang at different heights and strengths, the chamber has a pattern of bright islands with deep, sharp-edged shadow between them. You can stand in full light and, three paces on, be in a dusk where the only illumination is what the wet walls throw back.

## Water

Everything is wet, and the wetness moves.

The air is saturated. Every cool surface beads. The roof weeps constantly in slow, heavy drops, each carrying a fine grit of dissolved mineral. Where the drops land repeatedly, thin cones of white and yellow crust rise a finger's height, so small they look like a scattering of teeth. The walls glisten. Any rock a hand touches leaves a print that fills with condensation and vanishes over a slow count of thirty.

The pool in the basin is fed from below by a seep. Every few minutes it rises, lips over the low rim on the north side, and sends a sheet of water a hand deep across the floor, warm as bath water and smelling faintly of struck matches. The sheet flows into the ropes and folds of the basalt, fills each hollow, and drains through a fissure in the south wall with a long gurgling sigh. Then it is a pool again, lower, clearer, until the seep builds again.

Twice in what would be an hour above, the chamber rains. The heat rises off the basin as steam, climbs to the cold ceiling, and comes back as fat lukewarm drops falling in a hissing curtain for a minute or two. The light-filaments turn it into a shimmer of falling sparks. Then it stops, and the room drips for a long time.

## The Living Things

There are no plants. Nothing here photosynthesizes, despite all the light, and anything that tried would drown in steam or cook. What lives here does so differently.

**The mats.** Across the wettest stretches of basalt lie thin films, some slick as varnish, some furred. Near the pool they are rust-orange and mustard yellow. Farther out they are cream and gray, and at the seep's edge there is a band of vivid green-blue that looks painted on. They are microbes and molds and things between. They drink heat and minerals and whatever the water carries. Where the light-blooms are strongest the mats grow in slow swirled patterns, thickening toward the brightness, though no one could say if they feed on it or merely tolerate it. The mats spread visibly over days. A fresh clean patch of rock, exposed when a piece of glaze flakes away, is colonized within a week by a fine bloom of pink.

**The pale threads.** In shadowed cracks grow fungal hairs, white and translucent, some as long as a forearm. They lean toward the drips. When a large drop strikes one it whips and recovers. In thick clumps they look like frozen hair, and when the steam surges they lift, all together, as if breathing.

**The beads.** In the warm shallows crawl animals no larger than a grain of rice, pale and armored, with no eyes to speak of. They graze the mat edges in slow numbers, dozens to a hand's breadth in the good places and none at all across the rest. When the sheet-flood comes they cling, and when it drains they resume, unbothered.

**The strider.** The one creature big enough to notice is rare, maybe a handful in the whole chamber. It is a long-legged, colorless arthropod the length of a finger, and it walks the edge of the pool on the surface film, feeding on the beads and the drowned. It moves in stop-and-go bursts, freezing under the bright blooms and dashing across the dark gaps between. Something larger sometimes hunts it. A translucent, muscular worm as thick as a thumb waits with its head raised from a crack, barely visible, and takes striders that pass too close. The strike is fast enough that you see only the strider missing.

Between these presences the space is empty. Whole stretches of floor show nothing but glassy rock and drops. Life here comes in clusters, islands of mat and thread and bead, with bare stone between.

## Sound

The chamber is mostly quiet, and the quiet has texture. There is the slow drip, irregular, each drop a small tick or a soft slap depending on where it lands. There is the pool's low bubbling from the seep. There is the deep, gentle sigh when the sheet drains through the fissure, and the hiss of steam finding cracks. During the rain the room fills with a fine crisp rushing, and afterward the silence feels enlarged.

Under everything, if you hold still, is the light-threads' hum, so faint you may take it for your own pulse. When many are in your field of sight it seems to gather, one soft chord behind the drops.

Now and then the rock ticks and pings as it cools or heats, a small sharp crack, like a knuckle. It is the young stone still settling, and the sound has a nervous quality. Once in a long while something deeper shifts, a low bump felt in the feet more than heard. The drops all tremble, the strider freezes, the pool rings in concentric circles. Then it passes and the room resumes.

## The Rules of the Place

- **Heat has a gradient.** It is worst by the basin and slightly milder near the walls and the crack you came through. Everything living is arranged along it, mats in the hottest wet, threads in the cooler dark.
- **Light does not warm.** The blooms are bright as noon but give off no heat. Standing under one is a cool relief, and the striders and beads gather at their edges to rest.
- **Water keeps time.** The seep-pulse, the flood, the drain, the rain: the whole room breathes on a rhythm of several minutes, and the living things have arranged themselves around it. Nothing wanders far from the floodline when the seep begins to build.
- **Nothing is finished.** Fresh rock is being wetted, colonized, crusted, cut. In a thousand years the ropes and folds will be rounded, the black glaze gone under color, the cones grown into pillars. Now everything is sharp, damp, and beginning.

## Leaving

You lie in the shadow of a bloom with your cheek against cool wet stone, and the strider stops on the pool's edge a body-length from your hand. Its legs dimple the water. The light falls on both of you the same. The seep starts to build. You can feel it before you see it, a tightening in the air and a slow rise in the pool's tea-colored surface. Somewhere above, through rock and root and unnumbered meters of earth, the other end of each bright thread is open to the sky, and the sky is sending its noon down here to a room no one has come to and no one built, where the heat is patient, the water keeps moving, and small pale things graze in the glare.
