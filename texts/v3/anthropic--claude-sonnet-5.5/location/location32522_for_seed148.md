# The Understory of Harrow Wick

The town is small enough to walk across in the time it takes a candle to gutter, if the town allows it to be crossed. It sits at the bottom of a shallow basin, barely above the level of the water table. Below it the ground is still being finished.

## The Ground

The bedrock here is not old. It is a grey-green mudstone that has only recently surfaced from below, and it is still soft in places, cool and faintly tacky, like a scab that has not decided to be a scar. Where you can see it, in the cut banks and under the collapsed pavements, it is ribbed and shrink-cracked. Some of it is still wet with the water of its own making. The first gullies have begun. They are rills no wider than a finger, drawing patient dark lines down the slopes and finding the same routes again after each rain, as if rehearsing for canyons.

The basin does not have a horizon. It has a green lip, and beyond the lip there is only more dark.

## The Green

Everything is plant. This is not decoration on the town. The plant is the town's flesh, and the stone and iron are the bones the plant found and used.

Ferns as tall as chimney stacks hang their fronds into the streets and form a roof. Beneath them, moss sits in cushions a body-length deep. It has swallowed the cobbles, the kerbs, the tram rails, and the bases of the lamp-posts, and it rounds every corner into a slope. Whole terraces of brick houses have been taken up by a woody creeper with leaves like wet black leather. Its stems are as thick as a thigh and run along the rooflines in cables. Where the creeper has pulled the roofs apart, the gaps have been filled with a pale, spongy tissue that bulges and holds rain, forming domes and vaults. Some of the domes are translucent and gently lit from within.

Underneath the growth, the plant has hollowed itself. There is very little in this place per cubic measure. The green is a rind, and inside it are enormous empty rooms. There are nave-like spaces between the terraces where the creeper roofed over a whole street and left it empty, with only a floor of moss and a slow drip from above. A walker may cross three hundred paces of vaulted dark and meet nothing but the sound of their own breathing, and then a single object, standing alone and precisely placed: a bath, a tea urn, a child's boot with a fern growing from it.

## The Machinery

The town was a mill town, and the machines are still working. But they no longer work for anyone.

In the engine house, a great beam engine stands with its brass gone soft and green. Its flywheel turns, one revolution in the time it takes to hold and release a long breath. It is not driven by steam. A root the width of a mooring post has grown into the crankcase, and it swells with the damp and shrinks in the cool. Each cycle pushes the piston a little farther, and the pawls catch and click. The whole machine breathes with the plant that has taken it over.

Belts of living fibre still run along the ceiling of the weaving shed. The looms clack on their own, slowly, weaving a coarse green cloth from the mosses that grow up through the heddles. The cloth accumulates on the beam in heavy rolls that no one will collect. The gasworks' retort house holds a pale flame behind a grate, a blue pilot-light living on some marsh-gas the peat produces. It is the brightest thing in the basin, and it is not very bright. Every other light is the dim cold green of lichen, or the warm smear of a snail-trail when the snails have eaten something luminous.

The chimneys smoke. The smoke is spores.

## The Water

Water is a rumour that keeps proving true. There are no lakes, and the rain is thin and irregular. But condensation is constant. The cool air lets go of its water on every leaf tip and every iron rail, and each leaf, gutter, and downpipe has its own small weather. Water gathers, runs a few feet, and hides again in the moss.

There is one canal, half choked, that still holds a long black thread of standing water. Pale eels move through it. Above it the plant has closed over so completely that the canal runs through a tunnel, and the only sounds down there are the eels turning and the drip of the ferns above.

Twice in what passes for a day, a rain falls from the ground upward. Beads lift from the moss like a held breath let go. They rise slowly, catch what little light there is, and vanish into the canopy. The townsfolk who are not there would have called this the Lifting. It is the plants' way of drinking back what they gave.

## The Life

Life is everywhere, and very little of it is large. The fauna is abundant in the way rot is abundant: in numbers, in layers, in busy small motion.

Snails the size of fists graze the walls and leave silver roads. Pale moths drift through the vaults in slow clouds, and their wings make a sound like fingers turning pages. Bats hang in rows from the creeper cables, and every so often a whole row drops and flows away as one shape. Voles and shrews run the tunnels in the moss, and above them, lizards with translucent skin sit motionless on the brickwork. Millipedes move in strings along the gutters. Something with many legs and a soft, damp body lives in the boilers and comes out only when the furnace-light flickers.

There are predators, and they are patient. A long-bodied pale weasel-thing hunts the vole roads. Spiders string nets across whole doorways, and their silk holds dew so densely that the webs look like beaded curtains. The hunting takes place at the same slow pace as the engine. Nothing hurries. A chase, when there is one, is startling.

## The Breakdown

This is where the place stops being merely overgrown and abandoned.

Harrow Wick does not hold together. The streets do not keep their order. If you walk down Tanner's Row and turn left at the chapel, you may come out at the chapel again, seen from behind, with its door where its apse should be. Or you come out somewhere the map does not have. Distances are the least reliable thing. A plaza that took forty steps to cross will take four hundred on the way back, and no one will have moved.

Interiors are larger than exteriors, and sometimes smaller. A cottage door might open onto the vaulted dark of a cathedral-sized room, empty, floored with moss, one chair in the centre. The next door on the same street might open onto a wall.

Some things exist in two places. The shift whistle, a long flat note from the mill, is heard in the east and the west at once, though the sound of it is always slightly late, arriving a moment after the steam has already stopped. Shadows can lead their objects. A lamp-post's shadow lies across the moss a few moments before you reach the post, and lies there as though waiting.

Counting is not safe. If you count the windows of a terrace, the total changes with each attempt. If you count the eels in the canal, you will find one fewer each time, until you are told there are none. When you look back, there are dozens.

Gravity sags near the chimneys. Loose things fall sideways, toward the stacks, and hang for a moment against the brickwork before letting go. Moths sometimes settle on nothing at all, on a place in the air, and the place holds them.

And sometimes the town simply rebuilds itself. A whole terrace will be where it was the last time you looked, and then, when you glance away and back, it has been replaced by a slightly different terrace, its ivy grown in a different pattern, its doors in different places, and a sense that this version has always been here and the earlier one was the mistake.

## Sound

Mostly quiet. That is the rule the place seems to keep best. The drip. The click of the pawl on the flywheel. Snails feeding, a sound like someone tearing very fine paper. Moth wings. Once in a long while the whistle, in two places, a beat apart, and after it the silence closes in tighter, as though the sound had been a thing pulled out of the ground and the ground is filling in.

## What Remains

If anyone were here, they would notice that the place is patient. It is still being made. The rock is unfinished, the gullies are just beginning, the plants are still deciding what the machines are for. The town is neither a ruin nor a settlement. It is a thing in the middle of becoming something, with all its layers showing at once: raw stone, iron, green, hollow, dark, and the small warm lives moving through it. Nothing here has finished happening, including the parts of it that already happened.
