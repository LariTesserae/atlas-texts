# The Sill of Basins

You arrive from below by a stair that has no top you can see. It is cut into a slope of pale rock, each tread a hand-width deeper than a foot needs, worn hollow at the center only by water, not by feet. Nothing here has been walked much. The edges of the steps are still sharp enough to catch a sleeve.

Then the slope ends and the plateau opens: a high tableland, thin-aired and white with light, running out in every direction until it goes soft at the edges with distance. Every part of it has been made.

## The ground

There is no soil. Where a thing like soil should be, there is pavement of tamped lime and crushed shell-colored grit, laid in courses and scored with shallow grooves like the lines in a palm. The grooves run downhill, all of them, and they are all wet. A film of water moves in each, a finger deep, clear enough that the pale floor shows through as if the channel were empty. The water is cold and tastes of chalk and something faintly like struck flint.

Rising from this pavement, and packed so close that the alleys between them are a shoulder's width or less, are the **cells**. Each is a squat, rounded chamber of stacked stone and clay block, open-topped or roofed with slabs corbeled inward until they meet. They touch and share walls. They climb over each other in tiers, so that the whole tableland is one continuous honeycomb, stepping up and down across the swells of the land. From a high place it looks like a frozen foam, or the inside of a sponge that has been fossilized and then enlarged.

Nearly every cell holds a basin. Some are shallow bowls, some deep wells, some long troughs cut into the floor. Every one is brimming, or filling, or slowly draining into the next through a notched lip or a clay-lined spout. Water moves through the whole city from cell to cell by gravity alone, downward and outward, in a cascade so slow and so intricate that you can stand in one room and watch a single bead travel, over an hour, along a spout, across a lintel, down a wall, and into a pool that will not be its last.

## The making

The tools are visible everywhere, because the walls are recorded in them. Every stone was dressed by pounding with harder stone: the faces show a fine pocked texture, thousands of tiny craters in overlapping rows. Blocks were split along lines of drilled and wedged holes. The holes are still there, half-round grooves down the sides of the walls like the marks of huge fingernails. Clay was shaped by palm and thumb, and the prints remain, so a wall may carry a hundred hands pressed into it. They are all a little different, and each is sealed under a faint glaze of mineral.

There is a little metal: soft, greenish native copper, beaten into pins that hold slabs together, and into thin bright-edged wedges left in a few niches beside the basins, where someone set them down and did not return for them. The copper has begun to bloom pale blue-green where the water touches it. Otherwise it's all stone, clay, and the water's own work.

That work is the strange part. The water is heavy with dissolved lime, and wherever it runs, it builds. Every lip and spout wears a lace of new travertine, cream and ivory and pale rose, thickening by a hair each season. The builders knew this and used it. They set clay dams and wicks and hanging cords of twisted mineral fiber to coax the deposit into shape, so that some walls were not laid so much as *persuaded*. You find rooms whose curved ceilings are single sheets of frozen flow, ribbed like the inside of a shell, and pillars that were once drips. The line between what hands made and what water made is not a line. It is a gradient, and you cross it a dozen times in an afternoon without noticing.

Because all of this is so recent, or so continuously renewed, nothing is weathered. The corners are crisp. The tool-marks are clean. The rims of the basins are so freshly grown that they're translucent at the edge, and the light passes into them and comes out slightly warm.

## Light and air

The light is the first thing you'd tell someone about. At this height the sky is a hard, pale blue that goes almost white toward the horizon, and the sun is a fierce small coin in it. Everything is pale (plaster, lime, travertine, water over white floors), so the light doesn't fall so much as *fill*. Shadows are soft-edged and blue, pooled in the cell doorways. Standing in an alley, you are lit from below by the glare off the channels, and ripple-patterns of reflected sun slide slowly across every wall, a moving net of gold on cream. It is difficult to look at anything for long without squinting, and difficult to look away.

The air is cool and very clean, with a mineral dampness in it. Mornings, a low cloud settles on the tableland and condenses on everything; the whole city beads, then runs. The builders shaped for this too. Tall, ridged, unroofed slabs stand at the windward edges of the plateau, angled to catch the mist, each streaming into a collecting channel that feeds the highest cells. By noon the fog has burned off and the slabs stand dry and glittering, and by evening they are beginning to darken with the first returning damp.

## Sounds

It is mostly quiet, but never silent. There is dripping, from everywhere and at every pitch: fat slow drops into deep wells that answer with a low round note, quick ticks on shallow stone, the thin continuous whisper of water sheeting down a wall. Since the cells are open to the sky and shaped like bowls, the sounds pool and ring. Occasionally a sealed jar or a hollow pillar catches the wind at the plateau's edge and hums a single note, low and unhurried, that persists for a minute and fades. There is no voice, no footfall, no wing. Your own steps sound enormous.

## Life, or its absence

Nothing grows here except a faint slick of green-black film in the deepest, wettest channels: a smear of the simplest living thing, not really a plant, that gives the shadowed water a tint. And in places, on the north sides of walls, a dull orange crust spreads at a pace measured in decades. That is all. There are no animals. No insect crosses the pavement, no bird sits on the copper-blue lintels. The water is perfectly still wherever it isn't falling.

Whoever made this city is not in it. Or they are in it only as handprints, as the worn spot on a doorstep, as a wedge of copper set in a niche at the height of a hand. There is no sign of disaster; the place doesn't feel abandoned so much as *left running*, like a great slow instrument that keeps playing after the players have gone to rest.

## The rules of the place

- **Water goes down, always, and in order.** No cell is dry, and none overflows. The system is balanced so that every basin holds a level a little below its lip. Fill one by hand, and within the hour the room below is a fraction fuller.
- **The stairs are the only dry path.** Every stair is cut with a slight outward tilt so that water runs off it. The builders kept a path for themselves, and it is the only thing in the city that doesn't participate.
- **Everything is growing, slightly.** If you press your thumb to a fresh rim and return in a year, the print will be under a skin of stone. The city is not finished. It will not be finished. It is, in the slowest sense, still being built by the thing it was built to catch.

You might sit in one of the upper cells at noon, feet near a basin the color of milk-glass, and watch a single line of light travel across a wall, a slow bright net on cream. Behind you, somewhere, a jar hums its long low note. Beneath you, in cell after cell after cell down the slope, the same water is passing through, unhurried, laying down a little more of the world.
