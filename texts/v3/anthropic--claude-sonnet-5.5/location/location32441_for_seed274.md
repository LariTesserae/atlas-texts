# The Tufa Close

You come down into it by a crack in the limestone rim, sideways, with your pack held in front of you. The crack lets out into a bowl about a hundred paces across and not much deeper than a house is tall. The bowl is full.

It is full of stone standing close together. Columns of pale tufa rise from the floor, each thick as a waist or a thigh or a wrist, packed so tightly that you move by turning your shoulders and reading gaps. Some have grown together at the hip and share a base, and some lean into one another and have fused into arches too low to stand under. Between the columns are shelves, lips, and small terraced basins like cupped hands, each holding a finger's depth of water and each overflowing into the one below. There is almost no bare floor. Wherever your foot might go, the ground is a basin, a rim, or a wet ledge with a skin of water sliding over it.

## Water

The water is warm. It is not hot, but warm like a body, like a dish left near a hearth. It comes up from below through seams in the floor and gives off a thin steam that never becomes fog. The air holds exactly as much as it can, so every surface beads and every bead runs. The columns weep from their shoulders. The ceiling of the sky, a low grey lid of cloud seen through the open top of the bowl, seems to sweat back down at you.

Everything is the color of things that water has been working for a long time: cream, bone, the yellow of old candle wax, a greenish white in the shadows, rust-orange where iron rides in the flow. The stone is soft-edged and lobed, like cooled fat or melted wax. Deposit and dissolution have reached a truce here. The water lays down as much lime as it takes away, so the shapes do not grow and do not shrink. They have looked like this longer than anyone has been counting.

## Light

The light is dim and even. It has no direction, because it falls from a grey sky through rising vapor and bounces from a thousand wet, pale surfaces. Shadows are shallow and soft. In the deeper gaps between columns it is dusk at noon, and the water in the small basins holds a dull pewter shine. Looking up, you can see the ring of the rim, dark limestone with a fringe of dripping, and above it the unchanging cloud.

## What lives here

Almost nothing green. On the shaded undersides of the ledges there is a slick film, olive and faintly luminous where it thickens. It is a mat of something simpler than a plant, and it smears if you touch it. In the warmest basins, near the seams, it grows in orange and dull pink rings, like stains left by cups. That is the whole botany of the place.

The fauna is rare and small. Pale snails the size of lentils graze the film, and you might find eleven in an hour. There are tiny white crustaceans in the lowest pools that flick away from your shadow. Once, perhaps, you would see a grey wader with a long bill standing motionless in a channel at the far edge, and it would not fly, only watch you. The absence of birdsong is complete.

## Sound

It is mostly quiet, but the quiet is made of water. Thousands of drips fall at slightly different intervals into basins of slightly different depth, so there is a pitched, ticking, glassy texture with no melody, though for a moment you may think you hear one. Underneath it is a low continuous trickle where the terraces overflow. When you stop moving, the sound settles around you like a fine rain that never wets you further. The same drip falls at the same pitch from the same tip, every time you return.

## The human marks

Someone lived with this, or often visited, and they were not clever with metal but were patient with stone.

- **Stepping stones and cut lips.** A path of flat river cobbles, carried in from somewhere else, is set through the densest part, so a person can cross without breaking the rims. Where a rim had to be broken, it was notched cleanly with a chisel-stone and the notch has since healed with a smooth new lip of lime.
- **Bowls under drips.** In several places, small unglazed clay bowls have been set to catch a particular drip. Some are full and some have been swallowed: a crust of tufa has grown over their rims and around their bellies until they are part of the ledge. One is only a curved gleam under a skin of stone.
- **A house of blocks.** In the southern corner stands a single hut, waist-high walls of cut tufa blocks with a corbeled roof, each course leaning inward until the last slab closes it. It has a doorway you must crawl through. Inside, the floor is dry. This is the only dry place in the Close, and it feels like a held breath. There is a sleeping shelf, a hearth ring with no soot newer than ancient, and a niche with a single green-crusted copper awl laid in it as if just put down.
- **Tallies.** On the flat faces of a few columns, rows of short scratches are cut into the stone, grouped in fives, and then the scratches are covered by a thin glaze of new mineral. The counts stop where the glaze thickens. Nobody knows what was counted.
- **Handprints.** Near the crack you came through, a dozen hands have been pressed into soft wet lime long ago, and the lime hardened around them. They are small. They are pale and perfect and always cool.

## The rules of the place

Nothing dries. Nothing burns except in the hut. A dropped thing will be found, in a season or a century, lightly furred with stone. The temperature does not change between the day you arrive and the day you leave, and the light does not either. The water level in the basins stands where it stood. If you sat and watched for a whole day you would see the drips continue and the steam rise and nothing else happen, and this is not stillness through lack of life, but the stillness of a thing in perfect balance, where each small motion is exactly answered.

When you leave, you go back through the crack sideways, and the warmth comes off you slowly, and the outside air feels thin and dry and much too large.
