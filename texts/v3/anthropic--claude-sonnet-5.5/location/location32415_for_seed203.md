# The Sunk Meadow

The land here fell in, and everything that lives in it has been climbing back toward the light ever since.

The fall was recent. You can tell from the fault walls, which are still sharp, with raw shelves of pale limestone and rust-colored clay stepping down in terraces as tall as cathedrals. Nothing has rounded them yet. Where the rock is bare it is bare in clean planes with fresh fracture-glitter, and water beads on it and runs in threads. But bare rock is rare. The plants have been at the walls for a few hundred years, and in most places they have already hidden the wound.

## The Floor

Nothing here is soil. The floor is a mat of living and half-living plant, sixty or eighty feet thick in places, made of moss, root-felt, and the compressed brown ghosts of earlier moss. It has a give to it, like walking on a mattress, and each step sinks a hand's depth and returns with a slow wet exhale. The top layer is pale green shading to yellow-white, furred with frost in the morning and beaded with water by noon. Below the top the mat is dark and warmer, faintly steaming where a crack lets the heat of its slow rot escape.

The mat is not flat. It heaves. Growth in one season shoves it into pressure ridges. In another it slumps into basins where cold meltwater gathers in black pools with floating carpets of duckweed-like green. Sinkholes open without warning, and in their throats you can see the mat's layers like the rings of a cut trunk, going down into dark.

Standing on it you feel how young the place is. The plants are still improvising. Species are laid over one another in unsettled bands. Cushions of hard blue-green moss sit next to drifts of soft ferns that have no business growing on the same ground. Everything is colonizing everything else.

## The Light

It is very bright. The fault-bounded basin is open to the sky along its whole length, and the sky is enormous, a pale white overcast, evenly luminous, without a shadow to be found. The light comes down and is thrown back up from the wet pale moss, so you stand inside brightness. Your eyes ache. The people who live here wear slit-visors of bark or bone, and squint even so.

Above the floor, at the height of a distant hill, hangs the canopy. It is not a forest canopy. It is a single tangled sheet of vine and leaf-web strung between the two opposing cliff-edges, miles across, thin as a cloth in places and thick as a roof in others. Light passes through it, tinted green in some reaches and gold in others, and where it has torn there are windows of plain white sky. The sheet is always growing and always failing. It sags under its own wet weight, and when a stretch of it can hold no more, it comes down.

## The Falls

This is the turbulence. The world is quiet, but it is not still.

A leaf-fall begins with a tearing sound, a long soft ripping like enormous cloth, and then a shadow appears on the floor, the only shadow you will see all day, and it grows. A sheet of canopy the size of a field peels loose and descends slowly, folding as it comes, shedding water and beetles and seed-fluff and old bird's-nest tangles. It takes a full minute to land. When it does the mat beneath it shudders in a wave you can see, a ripple crossing the moss for a quarter mile, and then the sound arrives, a deep wet *whump* that you feel in your teeth. Afterward the fallen sheet lies across the floor in a green heap, already sprouting, and the sky over it is bare.

There are usually several falls a day somewhere in the basin. Between them the air does other violent things. Cold air sinks off the high rim and pours down the walls in invisible rivers, and where they meet the warmer breath of the mat they make fog. The fog rolls in banks, thick and white, and behind it come squalls of fine cold drizzle formed against the underside of the canopy and shaken down. Sometimes the whole basin clouds over for an hour, and then a downdraft tears it open and the light crashes back in. In autumn, spore storms lift off the fern-drifts in yellow-brown clouds that hang and turn like slow smoke, and the mat behind them is left frosted with new life.

Cold holds all of it. The air is a few degrees above freezing at midday and below at dawn. Breath hangs. Each leaf carries a rim of ice in the mornings, and every drop of the drizzle is one degree from becoming a bead of sleet.

## Sound

Mostly quiet. The moss swallows footsteps and voices. What you hear is dripping, near and far, a huge irregular ticking as the whole ceiling sheds water. Under that is a low hollow note when the wind crosses the mouths of the sinkholes. Sometimes a distant tearing. Sometimes a bird, though very rarely.

## What Lives Here

Life is scarce for its size, a few presences across miles.

Pale beetles the color of tallow work the moss-roots, thumb-sized, slow, and unafraid. Mat-grazers, low gray animals like long-bodied hares with wide flat feet, move in twos and threes across the cushions, browsing with their eyes half shut against the glare. Above the falls, sometimes, a white-winged thing circles, catching the insects the tearing canopy shakes loose. It is the only bird, and it does not sing.

Nothing large hunts here. What kills is the ground itself: the sinkhole, the fall, the cold.

## The Marks of People

The people who come here are few and have little. You may find a cairn of split limestone bound with braided fiber, set on a firm ridge to mark a safe path across the mat. You may find a hut on stilts of hollow stalk, lashed and thatched with fern, its door facing the wall so the wind will not flood it. It is always empty when you arrive, though there are ashes in the hearth-stones and a hand-adzed bowl left upside down to keep off the drizzle. They snare the grazers with fiber loops. They cut spore-cake from the fern-drifts and dry it on racks in the thin light. Their tools are bone, chipped flint from the cliff-shelves, and the odd flake of black glass from somewhere else.

They travel by the lean of the canopy vines, which always bend toward the widest gap of sky, and by the color of the moss, which says how thick the mat is beneath a step. Every path is temporary. A path that was good in spring may have been swallowed by a fall or split by a sinkhole by summer, and so the cairns have to be moved and the routes reknotted constantly. Nothing they build is meant to last longer than the next collapse.

## The Rule of the Place

The rule is simple and everything obeys it: *reach the light, and be dropped for reaching.* The moss climbs the walls, the vines climb the moss, the canopy climbs the vines, and the canopy grows too heavy and falls, and the fall feeds the floor, and the floor rises a little. The whole sunken country is slowly filling itself back up toward the sky it lost, one collapse at a time. In a few thousand years there may be no hollow left, only a flat green plain that has forgotten it was ever below.

For now it is a bright, cold, sagging, tearing, dripping world, empty of almost everything but green, with small fires burning in it here and there.
