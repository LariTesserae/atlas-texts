# The Mulch Works at Karrow Bend

## Approach

You come in along a towpath that has become a tunnel. Willow, alder, and a pale-stemmed vine the locals call *ropewort* have closed over the old canal cut, and the path runs through a green throat about the width of two people. The air is cool and stays cool, around nine degrees at midday, and every surface holds a film of moisture that never quite becomes rain. Your sleeves darken. Beads gather on the fine hairs of the leaves and roll down to the drip-tips, and the drips make the only steady sound: a slow, uneven ticking, like a room full of clocks that disagree.

Then the tunnel opens and you are inside the district. It is small, perhaps four hundred paces across, bounded on one side by the canal, on another by a curving railway embankment, and on the rest by a ring of rusted gantries so overgrown they read as hills. Everything inside is town, or was, and everything is plant.

## The ground

There is no soil here in the ordinary sense. The floor of the district is a deep, springy mat of moss, fallen leaf, shredded bark, and root, built up over what must be two centuries. It is knee-soft at the edges and chest-deep in the hollows. The streets survive only as slight linear depressions, where the mat is thinner over cobbles. If you press a boot down, you can feel the stone beneath, and where you lift it the print fills slowly with pale water and then with a creeping fringe of green.

The mat is warm. This is the first strange thing. Kneel and put your palm flat, and the surface is a few degrees above the air, like the flank of a sleeping animal. The heat comes from below, from the slow rot of all that layered matter. Steam lifts off it in thin ribbons wherever the morning is coldest, so the whole district seems to breathe in slow drifts.

## The architecture that is also a plant

Only about a dozen structures stand in the district, and they are far apart. Between them lie wide reaches of mossy flat, dotted with knee-high fern and the slow spiral of a single bramble. The sparseness is deliberate, or has the feel of it. Whatever grows here grows in a few enormous bodies rather than a thousand small ones.

**The Drying Hall** is a long brick shed with its roof gone. Something better has replaced the roof: a single canopy of interlaced ropewort stems, thick as thighs, woven so tightly that it sheds rain. Light comes through green and slightly underwater. The vines have been trained. Nobody trains them now, but the trellis of iron purlins that once held slates still shapes them, so the growth follows the old geometry of the building, ribs and bays and all. The plant is a ghost of the roof it replaced.

**The Bell Tower of the counting-house** has lost its bell but not its shape. A strangler fig, or something like one, has grown down and around the brickwork, and the brick has since weathered and fallen away in places, so the tower is now a hollow lattice of living root in the form of a tower. You can climb inside it. The steps are root. At the top, a platform of interwoven trunks looks over the whole district, and from there you can see the pattern: the town's street plan visible as faint lines of lighter moss, and steam rising in scattered columns from the hot spots.

**The Chimney Row** is five brick stacks standing in a line, each one furred with moss up to about two-thirds of its height and then bare and soot-dark above, as if the growth were still climbing and had not yet reached the top. Small ferns grow in every joint. Water seeps from the mortar and runs down in dark stripes.

## The engine

At the center of the district, in a stone-walled pit open to the sky, is the reason the place is more than a ruin: **the beam engine.**

It is enormous and improbable. A great cast-iron rocking beam, green with verdigris where it was once bronze-fitted and orange-brown elsewhere, pivots on a stone column. At one end it drops into a cylinder housing. At the other it drives down into a pump rod that disappears into a shaft. The whole thing is coated in a slick of moss and lichen, and yet it moves. Once every eleven or twelve seconds, the beam tilts, slowly, with a long exhalation of steam and a deep, wet *thunk* from the pump. Then it rests. Then it tilts back.

The boiler that feeds it is not a furnace. Coils of old copper pipe run out from the engine house and down into the warm mulch, snaking through the mat, buried at the depth where the rot is hottest. Water circulates through them, heated by the decomposing mass of the district, and returns as low-pressure steam. The engine is not fast. It has never been fast. But it has been fed for longer than anyone alive can say, on nothing but the metabolism of leaves.

What does it pump? Water, out of the deep shaft and into a stone channel that runs across the district and returns to the canal. This is the town's one remaining function: keeping the water table low enough that the place does not drown. If the engine stopped, the mat would saturate, the roots rot, the heat fade, and the engine, starved of heat, would never restart. The system is a loop: plants heat the pipes, the pipes drive the engine, the engine drains the ground, the drained ground keeps the plants in the balance they need. Nobody designed it whole. It accreted. It settled.

## Weather and motion

Almost nothing here is still. The place is quiet, but it is active in slow ways, and if you stay an hour you begin to see the many clocks running at once.

- **The mist cycle.** Each morning the warm mat meets the cold air and produces a low fog, knee-deep, that thickens toward the canal. By ten it has lifted into thin cloud, and by noon it is condensing on the underside of the ropewort canopy and dripping back down. It is a small private weather system, and it rains harder inside the Drying Hall than anywhere else.
- **Occasional true rain.** Two or three times a week, real rain arrives from the west, a fine, cold, steady drizzle. The whole district drinks it. The moss swells visibly and brightens from olive to emerald over the span of minutes. The engine's rhythm slows, because cooler water dampens the mat's heat, and then gradually resumes.
- **The ropewort's daily movement.** The tendrils turn. In the afternoon, tips that were pointing at the gap in the canopy slowly swing toward the thin light. You can see it if you fix on one and count. The whole roof seems to lean and settle like a sleeping crowd.
- **Seed and spore.** In the late morning the ferns release spores in visible puffs, a faint yellow haze that drifts and settles on the wet surfaces and becomes, in a few weeks, a new film of green.
- **The slow collapse and repair.** Every so often something gives way: a rotted beam, a brick course, a fig root. It falls with a muffled thump into the mat and is swallowed within a season. Growth immediately begins on the wound. The district is always mid-demolition and mid-construction at once.

## Sound

Mostly quiet, but layered. Under the drip-tick there is the engine's long slow breath, felt as much as heard, a pressure in the sternum every dozen seconds. Somewhere a loose iron plate taps in the wind, irregularly. Water runs in the stone channel with a thin, continuous chatter. When the wind gets into the ropewort canopy there is a dry hush, like a hand drawn over paper, and stems creak against each other at their knots. Cooling metal pings. There are no engines other than the great one, no voices, and almost no birdsong, only now and then one thin call from far off in the alder.

## Life, rare and particular

Fauna is scarce, and what is here seems to have arrived by chance and stayed because it fits.

- **Pale snails**, the size of a thumbnail, cluster on the warm iron in the morning, and are so numerous on the engine beam that its top surface looks pearled.
- **A single heron**, or a bird very like one, stands most days in the stone channel where the pump water comes out warm. It does not seem to fish. It seems to be warming its legs.
- **Springtails and mites** in uncountable numbers make up the real population, the fine machinery of the rot. You see them only as a shimmer when you disturb the mat.
- **A fox**, once, according to the one person who tends the place, crossing the flat in the mist with its head low. It has not been seen again.

That tender is the only regular human presence: someone who comes on foot twice a week to grease the beam pivots with tallow, clear the pump channel of fallen leaf, and check the copper joints. They leave a lantern on a hook in the engine house, unlit in the day, and a logbook whose pages are soft with damp and full of the same repeated entries: *turned; clear; steady.*

## The unusual thing, once you notice it

The plants in the district are all of a single family, or seem to be. Ropewort, the moss, the ferns, the fig-like strangler, the bramble: they share a certain pale under-leaf and a faint smell like warm bread and iron. Botanists who have visited disagree about whether that is coincidence, or convergent adaptation to the heat and the metal, or something else. The mat is rich in dissolved iron from two centuries of rusting machinery, and the plants concentrate it. Break a ropewort stem and the sap runs clear, then rusts orange on the air within a minute. The whole green district is quietly, slowly, turning the old industry into itself: iron into leaf, leaf into heat, heat into motion.

## Evening

At dusk the light is normal and unremarkable, a soft gray-gold that comes in low under the canopy and lights the steam columns from the side. The temperature drops a degree or two. The mist begins to gather at the edges. The snails retreat into the joints of the iron. The engine goes on: *thunk*, rest, *thunk*, rest. The moss, wet and dark, seems to lean in around the stones. Nothing is finished here, and nothing is abandoned. It is a place that has arrived at its own condition and keeps, patiently, in motion, holding it.
