# The Terraced Basin of Hallen Tarn

## The lay of it

The land stands high, around three thousand meters above a sea no one here has seen. It is a broad basin roughly a day's walk across, cut into a plateau that broke open not long ago. The rim is a ring of dark basalt cliffs with their edges still crisp. You can see where the columns split from the parent rock, and pale scars mark the fresh faces. Talus slopes lie at their feet, but the fallen blocks still fit the gaps they came from, like a puzzle someone has only just knocked apart. Lichen has barely started on them.

The floor of the basin is not flat. It steps down in broad shelves, each a few dozen meters lower than the last. Every shelf holds water, sometimes a sheet only ankle-deep over pale gravel, sometimes a still green pool, sometimes a marsh of sedge and moss you could sink to the knee in. Water leaves each shelf by a low lip and falls to the next in thin, quiet veils. The whole basin is a staircase of standing water, descending toward a single dark lake at the center.

## Water

Water is everywhere and mostly unobtrusive. The ground is spongy. Press a boot down on any moss bank and a bright film rises around it. The cliffs weep: from every seam and joint between basalt columns, water beads and slides in threads, and the rock faces are dark and glossy to a certain height and dry pale grey above it. The line is very level, so the whole rim looks as though it had been dipped.

The air is warm and thick. Heat rises from below, since the ground here is not far from something hotter, and the shallow pools hold a bathwater temperature that never varies. Vapor lifts off them in slow, low veils, meets the cooler air above, and hangs as a ceiling of thin cloud a few hundred meters up. It never quite rains and never quite clears. Everything is coated in a fine condensation that gathers on leaf tips and falls in single drops.

## Growth

The vegetation is lush without being crowded, and it grows in patches with open ground between them. On the drier ribs between shelves stand groves of tree ferns, trunks black and fibrous, fronds unrolling in wide green wheels. Thickets of bamboo-like reed rise from the marshes. Dwarf conifers with drooping, dripping branches stand along the rim. Everything else is moss, in deep cushions that are emerald, rust, and a yellow-green almost like light, covering every ledge and fallen block. Liverworts sheet the wet stone in flat, glossy scales.

Nothing flowers in a showy way. The plants here are the wind-pollinated and spore-bearing kinds. Between the groves are wide stretches of short sedge turf, cropped by nothing, growing in perfect even carpets that ripple under the breeze.

There are no animals at all. No insects hover over the pools, no birds cross the cloud, and nothing moves in the water except the water. The pools are clear to the bottom, and the bottoms are clean gravel and algal mats. This absence has a texture. Fallen fronds and needles are broken down slowly and entirely by fungi, so the forest floor is a layered pale mat of threads and fruiting bodies, and the smell is of wet earth and something faintly sweet, like cut fern.

## The city

Across the basin, at wide intervals, stands a city. It is more a scattering of quarters than a single mass, each set on its own shelf or rib of rock and separated from the next by open water and turf. From the rim you can count perhaps nine distinct clusters, each a few dozen structures, with far more empty ground than building between them.

The builders had only stone, timber, fiber, clay, and a little soft metal, and they used the basalt as though it had been offered to them. The columns split naturally into hexagonal and pentagonal prisms of near-uniform size, and the walls are simply these prisms stacked on end or laid on their sides, fitted without mortar. Corbelled roofs of the same stone rise in stepped cones, capped with thatch of reed and sedge gone silver-brown. The buildings are low, round or many-sided, with doorways narrow enough to require turning sideways. Their dark walls glisten, and moss has been allowed to take the north faces but is scraped, or was once, from the south.

Between the quarters run causeways of laid columns, set flat like paving and raised a hand's breadth above the water. Some cross the shelves in long straight lines, and some end in stepping-stones. Where a causeway crosses an overflow lip, a gap has been left beneath it, spanned by one long lintel, so the water can pass without a sound. Small channels are cut into the stone everywhere: gutters, sluices, settling basins, little weirs of set stone that hold each pool at exactly its level. The whole city is a hydraulic instrument, and every part of it is shaped by where the water goes.

The largest quarter stands on a promontory of columns above the central lake, and its heart is a circular plaza, paved and terraced, with tiers of seating stepped down to the water's edge. In the middle is a shallow basin fed by a hidden spring, and its surface is always exactly full and never overflowing. Around it stand tall posts of dark timber, carved in interlocking spirals, each as thick as a man's torso. They are weathered but sound, since the constant damp and the local resins keep the wood.

There are workshops with troughs and hearth-rings, and kilns of fired clay with their vents still stained black. There are racks of wooden looms, their warp threads long rotted or perhaps still tight, strung with a fiber cloth as stiff and pale as paper. There are stone hammers, adzes, and mallets set neatly on shelf-ledges by doorways, their handles lashed with cord. There are jars, thousands of them, in many sizes, stacked in the cool of the cellars. There are no bones anywhere, no hides, no horn, no shell. Every tool and vessel is of stone, wood, plant, clay, or a dull, soft red metal beaten into blades and pins.

Whoever lived here is not here now, and nothing suggests they left in haste. Beds are made, in the sense that the sleeping benches are swept. Hearths are clean. The place has the air of being paused rather than abandoned.

## Sound

It is very quiet. The largest sound is the fine patter of drops from leaf to leaf, and beneath that the low, continuous whisper of water sliding over lips of stone. Wind moves through the reeds with a dry papery hiss, and the tree-fern fronds tick faintly against each other. At the causeway culverts, water makes a soft hollow note, like breath across the mouth of a jar. If you speak, your voice goes out flat across the water and does not come back. In the plaza, with the tiers curving around you, a whisper carries to the far side, but nothing answers.

## Light and time

The light is even and pearl-grey, filtered through the low cloud, bright enough to see color clearly. Shadows are soft and short. The sun is a pale disk that shows through for an hour at midday and then closes over. The moss is startlingly green against the black stone, and the pools reflect the cloud so exactly that the terraces seem to hang between two skies.

Nothing here changes. That is the deepest rule of the place. The pools stand at their marks, the drip lines on the cliffs sit at one level, the vapor rises and condenses at one height, and the moss has grown to a certain thickness and stopped. Weather does not turn. Season does not turn. The cliffs are new, but no further rock falls. The whole basin has settled into a balance of heat from below, water from above, and stone between, and it holds that balance without effort, the way a full cup holds still. A leaf that falls into a pool drifts to the lip, over the veil of water, and down to the next shelf, and eventually to the lake. Nothing else moves, and everything is exactly where the water has put it.
