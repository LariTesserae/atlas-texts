# The Loomed Plateau

Start at the rim, where the ground gives out. The plateau is a raised tableland about three thousand meters up, and its edge is a fresh scarp of black basalt, still sharp enough to cut a palm. The rock is only a few thousand years out of the earth. Rain has barely rounded it. Plants hold the scarp face like a net over a cargo, and the net is the first thing you understand about this country: the plants here don't grow on things, they make things.

Up on top, the ground is not soil. It is a floor of woven root and runner, tight as basketry and glossy with a resin that sheds water in sheets. It is warm underfoot, because the rock beneath is young and still leaks heat. Steam seeps up through the weave in threads and beads on the underside of every leaf. The air is around thirty-eight degrees and thick enough to drink. It is never dry. Sweat and condensation are the same thing here.

## The Spires

The plateau is open, tens of kilometers of it, and the structures stand far apart, with long stretches of glistening floor and standing water between them. That emptiness is part of the design. Each spire has room.

A spire is a hollow tower of fused stems, a hundred meters or more, ribbed like a bundle of reeds that grew together and then thickened into one wall. The wall is a dark, lacquered red-brown. Slots run up the sides where stems haven't quite closed, and the wind plays these slots. Every spire has a different note, and the notes shift as the walls swell with water. Together they make a slow, huge, uneven chord you feel in your sternum. It is loud without ever being a single sound. Under it come the rain, the hiss of steam, and the constant ticking of seed pods.

Inside, a spire is a chamber. It has floors of woven leaf-membrane, stairs that are spiraling ridges of stem, and windows that are iris-shaped valves in the wall. The valves dilate when rain approaches and close afterward, so the tower breathes. Nothing lives in these rooms. They are complete, furnished only with their own architecture: shelves of shelf-fungus, basins of clear water, and hanging curtains of root that filter it. They are built like a home, but no one has ever come home.

## The Cisterns

Between the spires the floor drops into terraced basins, some as small as a courtyard and some as wide as lakes. Their walls are cupped leaves, or something like leaves, thick as a wrist at the rim and curved into bowls. They are stacked in stairways down every slope, each one filling and spilling into the next. Water falls from lip to lip in veils, and the whole hillside roars softly like a crowd.

The water is tea-colored with tannin and warm as a bath. Floating mats of small round leaves cover it, and under them are trailing beards of root a body's length long. Some basins are lit green from below, where translucent leaf-floors let the light through, so you look down through water into a second, submerged plantscape.

## The Bridges and Ropes

Where the plateau is cut by young ravines, vines cross them. The vines start as single cables and thicken into causeways, sometimes wide enough for ten people, though no one walks them. They are braided and tensioned like cables, and they creak under the wind with a sound like ship's rigging. They are also unstable. Many are half-finished, ending in a fan of searching tendrils that sweep slowly through the air. Others snap in the afternoon gusts and coil back like whips, and by evening the stumps are already re-knitting.

## The Weather Engine

The landscape follows a daily cycle. Mornings are bright, steaming, and quiet, or as quiet as it gets. By noon a wall of cloud has piled against the scarp, pushed up from the lowlands, and it breaks over the plateau in storms. The rain doesn't fall so much as arrive, a drumming so heavy the leaves of the cisterns ring like skins. Every valve opens. Every basin fills. Then everything moves.

In the storm the plants build. Runners lengthen visibly, a hand's width in a minute. Stem-walls thicken with a wet creaking sound. Tendrils lash toward the tallest points and lock on. New ribs rise inside old spires. Seed pods, swollen with water, burst with flat cracks like pistol shots, and each one flings seed in a spray across the plateau. Lightning hits the spires constantly, because they are the tallest things around. Where it strikes, a wall chars and splits. By the next dawn, pale green shoots are already crossing the black.

Just as much is destroyed. Whole towers topple when their stems swell past their strength. Terraces burst and drain. Bridges tear loose. The plateau is not being built toward completion. It is in permanent, violent construction and demolition. Nothing has a finished shape. The spire you see now stands where a wet stump stood last week and where a collapsed heap will lie next month.

## Rules of the Place

Things here follow a few hard laws, and you learn them by watching.

**Water decides.** Every structure leans toward the wettest ground and grows fastest in rain. Dry spells make the plateau shrink and harden, and the walls go stiff and brittle.

**Nothing eats.** There are no animals: no insects, no birds, no grazers. Pollen travels by wind and rain-splash. Seeds travel by explosion, by flotation, and by clinging to the runners that carry them. The absence of animal sound is strange. Every noise has a plant or the weather behind it.

**The rot is quick.** What falls is digested within days by mats of pale fungus and slick bacterial films. The remains feed roots that are already reaching for them. A collapsed tower becomes the foundation of the next one.

## Night

After the storms the cloud thins. The steam keeps rising, the basins keep spilling, and the spires keep singing, but lower. Faint phosphorescence collects in the wettest places: at root tips, along the veins of cistern leaves, in the wounds where lightning struck. It is a cold blue-green, and it shows the edges of things without lighting the space between them.

The rock beneath is still warm. The whole plateau feels like something lying down, breathing and still growing. You are standing on the roof of a structure that nothing designed and nothing occupies, and it is already rebuilding itself for tomorrow's rain.
