# The Crown of Old Hush

You arrive by climbing for three days through country that gets barer and thinner and colder. Then you crest the last shoulder and the air turns warm and heavy against your face, smelling of cut resin and animal musk. What you thought was the summit is a rim. Inside it lies a town-sized basin so full of green and noise that your ears take a moment to catch up.

## The Bones

This was once a mountain range, one of the tallest the world ever grew. It has been wearing away for so long that nothing sharp is left. What remains is a broad, rounded table of dark quartzite and iron-stained gneiss, still six thousand meters above the lowlands because the crust beneath it floats high and refuses to sink. The rock has been weathered past ruggedness into smoothness. Domes, knuckles, and low whalebacks stand in the basin like the backs of sleeping animals. Every edge is softened and every crack is filled with red grit and root. There are no cliffs and no scree. Everything the wind and frost could take was taken long ago, and what is left has settled into its final shape.

Warmth comes from below. The old roots of the range still hold heat, and thin seams of it leak through the rock as dry, mineral-scented breath that rises through fissures all over the basin floor. You feel it through your sandals. Stone near the vents is comfortable to sit on. The air above is thin, but it is fat with the smell of growth, and the warmth pools under the canopy and stays.

## The Green

The lushness here does not come from rivers, because there are none. No stream has run on this plateau in a million years. The stone drinks what little rain falls and passes it down into deep fractures. The plants live on other things: the fog that climbs the slopes each dusk and settles in the basin like breath in a cupped hand, and the mineral warmth of the vents.

Everything has learned to catch fog. The dominant growth is a tall, pale-trunked tree the townspeople call *stillwood*. It has long, drooping, comb-toothed leaves that comb moisture out of the air and channel it down grooved trunks into swollen, spongy buttresses at the base. Around each buttress grows a skirt of cushion-plants and hanging moss, then tangled ferns with leathery, waxed fronds, and vines with fat, water-hoarding stems as thick as a wrist. The canopy is dense and low, rarely more than ten or twelve meters. Below it, light arrives as a dim olive-gold dusk, even at midday, sifted through layers of leaves. In the thick parts you can walk a hundred paces and never see the sky.

Nearly all the moisture in this place lives inside living things. Sap, fruit-pulp, gel-filled leaf-cups, and the fluid in animal bodies are the only water anyone drinks. A dropped seed does not sprout in a puddle. It sprouts in the rotting flesh of a fallen buttress, or in the dung of the terrace herds.

## The Town

Under the green sits a settlement so tightly worked into the land that it is hard to say where the town stops and the forest begins. The people call it **Hush**, a name older than their language, and an ironic one, because it is the loudest place for a hundred leagues.

The houses are built from what the mountain gives: rounded quartzite cobbles, mortared with red clay and dung, stacked into thick-walled cells the size of a large tent. Roofs are woven cane and stillwood bark shingle, thatched again with living moss that the residents trim like hair. The cells are stacked and shouldered against one another up the domes of bare rock in stepped terraces. A single house may share three walls with neighbors and rest its floor on another family's roof. Alleys are narrow, crooked stairways. Some are worn into the bedrock itself, hollowed to a shallow trough by countless feet over more generations than anyone has counted.

Gutters and clay channels run along every roofline and every stair. Nothing runs in them except the slow drip of fog-condensate, collecting drop by drop into wide-mouthed clay cisterns at each corner. Children are set to watch them. Each cistern has a little flat stone lid, and each lid is the property of a family.

Tools are simple: stone axes, fire-hardened digging sticks, bone needles, obsidian scrapers traded up from the lowlands, and a few hammered-copper blades treasured like heirlooms. There is a potters' quarter where the vent-heat is used to fire clay without wood. There is a rope-walk where vine-fiber is twisted into cordage forty meters long. There are drying-racks of sliced fruit and strips of smoked lizard, and long tables of woven trays on which a sticky, dark resin is cured for use as glue, waterproofing, and incense.

## The Noise

The sound is enormous. It rises before dawn and does not truly fall until well after dark. Its main voice is a chorus of dry-winged insects in the canopy, a shrill, shimmering, overlapping rasp like a thousand sheets of paper being torn slowly. Beneath it is the clatter of beetles in hollow logs, the ratcheting calls of small gray birds that live in flocks of hundreds, and the bark-and-grunt of the terrace goats as they are driven along the stair-lanes. Above all this come the human sounds: hammering, shouted bargaining, children, and the deep, slow beat of the log-drums that mark the hours.

The drums are made from hollowed stillwood, each with a tuned slit, and they are hung in the branches at intervals throughout the town. When one is struck, the next answers, and the sound crosses the basin in a wave. The town has no clocks, so it lives by these waves, and by the loud dusk-chorus that starts when the fog begins to climb.

## The Life

Animals are everywhere and nearly all of them are small. Geckos the color of wet slate cling to every wall, and at dusk they gather in their hundreds to lick the condensate from the cistern lids. Mites swarm in the moss. Long-bodied centipedes with pale legs hunt in the alleys, and are tolerated because they eat the roaches. Fruit-bats fold themselves into every crevice of the upper terraces. Flocks of gray birds move through the canopy like smoke. Stocky, hornless goats graze the moss roofs and the terrace gardens, and they drink from the leaf-cups and lick the dew from one another's coats.

The people keep bees too, or something close to bees: fat, slow, black-and-amber insects that make a thick, sour, resinous honey and defend their hives by sheer numbers. The hives hang in the stillwoods like dark fruit, and their drone is the deep bass beneath the shrill of the canopy.

Larger predators are rare. A ground-hunting lizard the length of a man's arm sometimes takes a kid goat. Dusk owls, pale and flat-faced, patrol the terrace edges. Nothing else up here is big enough to matter, and the settlement's density leaves little room for anything that is.

## The Rules

The town lives by a few unspoken laws that everyone follows without discussion.

**Nothing is wasted that once held water.** Fallen fruit, dead leaves, and bones are all returned to the moss beds, and the skins of the dead goats are boiled for their last moisture.

**No fire in the green.** Cooking is done in the vent-kitchens, where the stone is already hot, and open flame is banned under the canopy. A single blaze would burn the whole basin.

**Fog is shared.** A tree on the edge of the basin gets the first fog, and the town gets what passes through. Cutting the outermost stillwoods is the worst crime, and it has been punished by exile. Every child learns to say, before they can properly count, "the rim feeds the roofs."

## The Slow

Very little here changes quickly. The rock has not changed noticeably in ten thousand years. The fog comes and goes each day and the vent-breath rises and falls with the slow tides of the crust. A stillwood takes forty years to reach maturity and lives for four hundred. The people mark the seasons by the fruiting of one particular vine, which flowers once every seven years, and the whole town gathers under it at the appointed time. Otherwise the days are much alike, each with the same warm dimness, the same green and gold, the same din. The place has had all the time in the world to reach exactly this arrangement, and it has no need to change.

At night the noise thins to a hum of crickets and the low breath of the vents. The fog rises, and the lamps of the town glow dull orange through the moss, a hundred small windows buried in the green, high above a world that has forgotten this place is here.
