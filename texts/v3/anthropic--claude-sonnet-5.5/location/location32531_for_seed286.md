# The Ironwater Uplands

The land is a plateau four thousand meters above the level of any sea, and it is full of water. A rim of old mountain holds the water in, and the rim is so far away that from the middle of the plateau you would never suspect it. Forty meters above the floor the surface lies flat as poured metal. Thin, cold air sits on it. Sunlight comes down through the water in long, steady, green-white columns, and it has fallen at the same angle for longer than anyone could count.

Nothing here changes. The water has no tide, no season and no storm. Its temperature holds at a clean, sharp cool, the cold of a stone cellar, and the currents run on fixed courses. If you dropped a seed in one channel, you could return in a thousand years and find it had followed the same path to the same place. The stability is not a stillness. It is a balance, and everything in the place is working to keep it.

## The Substrate

The floor is bedrock, but you seldom see it. It is old, rounded and terraced. Long ago, when the water was younger and less certain, it cut the rock into wide shallow steps, and the steps were then bound in place by roots. Now the roots are the ground. They are thick as ship's cables, pale, ribbed and tightly interwoven, and they form a mat meters deep. Everything grows from it or is fed by it.

The plants are not scattered across this mat as individuals. There are only a few of them, and each one is a continent-sized organism, one connected life running for hundreds of kilometers. You know where one ends and the next begins because of the channels. These are lanes of open, clear water a few hundred meters wide, kept clear by currents, and they run between the great growths like avenues between cities. Standing in a channel, you can see a long way, which is rare here. The walls on either side are kelp, and they rise from the floor to within a few meters of the surface, where they spread into a ceiling of flat, overlapping blades.

## The Growths

The growth is built of stipes: dark olive columns as thick as grain silos, set at intervals in ranks. Each stipe ends in a huge bladder, a pale ribbed float the size of a house. The bladders are the plant's machinery. Each one fills slowly with gas from the photosynthetic tissue in its wall, and when the pressure reaches a threshold a valve of dense muscle-like fibre opens and vents. The bubbles rise in a thick silver rope. The bladder collapses, folds, and begins to fill again. A stroke takes about eleven seconds.

Each stipe keeps its own rhythm, but neighbouring stipes fall into step, and over the ages whole forests have locked together. Across ten kilometers of growth the bladders fire in long traveling waves, and the sound of them is the sound of the place. It is a deep pounding thud, slow and heavy, that you feel in your chest and teeth before you hear it. Under it runs a lower groan of fibre under load, and over it the hiss and roar of rising bubbles. To a visitor, it sounds like a foundry working at full capacity or a fleet of engines idling in a hall of iron. It has never stopped, and it has never been quieter or louder than it is now.

## The Industry Without Builders

Nothing here is made. There are no walls, no tools, no artifacts. Yet the plateau is the most industrial place imaginable, because life here has taken up the methods of industry and no one built anything to house them.

Certain plants draw iron and manganese out of the water and concentrate them in their tissues. Once a year by the plants' own count, and always at the same moment, each stipe sheds hard black nodules the size of a fist, cast in shapes as regular as stamped parts. They fall in a steady rain through the green light and pile in ranks on the root mat, where a class of grazers gathers them. Those animals use the nodules as ballast and armor, and when they die the nodules go back to the roots. The material circulates without ever being used up, in a cycle as tight as a machine's.

The channels are conveyor belts. The current runs down their length at a constant, measured pace, carrying plankton and fragments of plant, which flow past filter-feeders fixed in rows along the growth wall like workers at stations. Each row takes what it needs and passes the rest on. Downstream, the water is thinner and clearer, and it enters the next growth as clean feed for the roots.

The electricity is real. Fine filaments in the kelp hold a slight bioelectric potential, and the fish that hunt among them use it. Long, ribbon-bodied fish called wire-eels flow through the stipes, discharging in bright pale stutters that light the water for an instant. The whole undergrowth flickers faintly with these arcs, and the pulses register on your skin as a prickling.

## The Fauna

Life here is abundant, though spread thinly through the vast volume. It gathers at the edges of the growths and in the channels, and the middle of an open lane can be empty for a kilometer.

- **Ballast-grazers** are broad, slow, armored animals, half crab and half tortoise. They plod across the root mat in slow herds, each one weighed down with a carapace studded with black nodules. They graze the mat's surface film in wide, methodical swaths, and their tracks last for years because nothing disturbs them.
- **Vent-shoals** are silver schools that follow the rising bubble columns upward by the thousand, feeding on the oxygen-rich froth. They turn all together in the light, and the flash of them runs through the water like a change of shift.
- **Drum-whales** are the largest animals, the length of a city block, and there are only a few. They drift the channels with mouths open, straining the current. Their voices are the only sound in the place that is not mechanical. They call rarely, a long falling note that cuts under the pounding of the bladders, and it carries for a hundred kilometers.
- **Sheet-rays** lie flat against the underside of the kelp canopy and glide across it, feeding on the epiphytes that grow there.

## The Rules

Several things are true here at all times.

The water is never still. Every current has a course and holds to it.

The light never dims or brightens, and no night comes. The plateau is too high and too even for anything to shift.

No thing is wasted. Every fall of nodules, every shed blade and every dead animal becomes feed for something else within a fixed and short interval. Nothing accumulates and nothing is lost.

Nothing new is made, either. The forms you see are the forms that have always been here, refined over ages until they reached a shape that could not be improved and stopped changing. The plateau is old the way a well-run engine is old, not ruined and not restless, only kept at exactly the state it settled into.

If you swim to the surface and break through into the thin, cold air, the sound drops away at once, and the plateau lies flat and gray under the sky, quiet and empty to the horizon. Below you the thudding goes on.
