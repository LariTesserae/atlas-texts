# The Sweat Terrace

A shelf of new ground hangs on the shoulder of a volcano, three and a half thousand meters up. It is perhaps four hundred paces across, bounded on the uphill side by a wall of columnar basalt and on the downhill side by a lip where the shelf simply ends in cloud. The air at this height should be thin and cold. It is neither. The ground is warm the way a sleeping animal is warm, and the air sits on it thick, close to blood heat, and so wet that a hand held out comes back beaded.

## The rock

The stone is young and has not yet been softened. The columns of the back wall are still hexagonal, with edges you could shave with, and their tops are broken into uneven steps where the cooling cracked them. Between the columns runs black glass, obsidian in glossy seams as wide as a forearm, so fresh that it still holds reflections: green fronds, a bar of white sky, your own face broken into facets.

Everything on the shelf sweats. Water beads along every seam of the columns and gathers into strings that lengthen and drop. Where the drops land, the basalt has been stained a deep rust-orange, and the stain has scarcely begun to cut into the rock. It is only a color, and the erosion is barely a rumor. Water stands in shallow pans in the floor of the shelf, each one clear to the bottom and warm as a bath. Beneath them the ground is always slightly spongy, because the water table is only a hand's depth down and the whole terrace floats on it.

## The green

The vegetation is lush but spaced out, as if each plant had been given room to be looked at. Tree ferns stand twenty feet apart, their trunks furred in russet fiber, their crowns dripping. Between them lie carpets of moss so saturated they are nearly liquid, and where you step, the print fills with water and slowly, over a minute, rises back. Giant rosette plants, waist-high spirals of waxy leaves, hold cupfuls of rainwater in their hearts, and the water in each is a slightly different color: amber, jade, a sour milky blue. Nobody has explained the colors.

There are also the sundews, which grow too large. Their leaf-paddles are as long as a man's arm and glitter with dew that is not dew, and they turn very slowly toward whatever moves. The moss around them is scattered with the dry wings of things that came to drink.

## The needles

Scattered across the terrace, at irregular intervals, stand the rain-keys.

They are slender, pale, and taller than a person, planted in the ground like thin spears or the masts of tiny ships. The material is neither metal nor bone nor ceramic. It is smooth and cool, even in this heat, and it is always dry. Water does not bead on it, slide off it, or dampen it. Drops seem to bend around it a finger's width away. Rest your palm against one and you feel a faint regular pulse, slower than a heartbeat, like something breathing in a very large body far below.

No one built the rain-keys within any span of memory the terrace holds. They have no seams and no joins. Some lean. One has toppled and lies among the ferns, and it has not stained, rusted, or been colonized by moss. Around it the moss grows in a neat ring, leaving a clear margin, as though the plants had been asked to keep away.

## The house

At the far edge of the terrace, near the lip, stands the only real structure: a dwelling shaped like half an egg, about the size of a small room, of some grown gray-white ceramic. It has no corners. The doorway is a simple oval, and the door is a plate of the same pale needle-material, slid aside and left that way.

Inside it is dry and cool, the only dry and cool place on the terrace, and it smells faintly of ozone and stone. The floor is smooth and slightly yielding. It remembers feet: your steps leave shallow prints that fade over several minutes, and older prints, not yours, rise faintly through the surface and sink again, bare and narrow, coming and going. There is a bench shaped out of the wall. There is a shelf holding three shallow bowls, each one always exactly half full of clear water no matter how many times it is drunk from, tipped, or emptied. Whoever lived here, or whatever they were, left the way someone leaves for a short walk.

## The daily turn

The terrace keeps a schedule.

At dawn the fog does not descend but rises out of the pans, thin white columns twisting up from every pool. By early morning the terrace is a room of standing mist, the tree ferns half-dissolved in it, the rain-keys pale on pale.

By midday the heat is heaviest. The fog burns off, the glass seams blaze, and the sundews open fully. The pans steam. It is the loudest hour, though loud only in the terrace's terms: a tick and crackle from the black glass as it expands, a constant patter from the columns.

In the afternoon the rain begins, and it rises. Water lifts from the pans in fine, glittering threads, vertical and slow, as though the pools were being drawn up through straws. Above head height it gathers into a low, flat cloud that presses over the terrace like a lid. The rain-keys hum then, a low sustained note you feel in the teeth before you hear it. When the cloud is dense enough, it lets go, and real rain falls, warm and heavy, back into the pans and onto the moss. It lasts perhaps twenty minutes. Then the cloud thins, the hum stops, and the light comes back gold and wet.

At night the glass seams hold heat and glow faintly, dull red at the edges, or seem to. The fern crowns drip on into the dark.

## What lives here

Not much, and what does moves slowly. There are small translucent frogs, pale enough that their organs show as faint pink threads, who ride the rosette plants and sing one note at dusk. There are lace-winged insects that hover above the pans in the rising-rain hour, drawn up and set down again with the water. Once in a great while a tall gray bird with a bill like a curved needle walks through, picking its way past the sundews with exaggerated care. It never stays. It never looks at the rain-keys.

The terrace is mostly quiet. Dripping is the constant sound, a thousand small percussions at different pitches. Under that, sometimes, the slow pulse of the needles. Nothing else. The silence has a texture, and it seems to be listening to you.

## The rule

There is one rule that the terrace teaches, without words, to anyone who stays. The water here is not passing through. It is being kept. Every drop that leaves the terrace, dripping off the lip into the cloud, is somehow returned by afternoon, and the pans never fall or overflow. The whole shelf is a single slow machine for holding one measure of warm water in perpetual circulation, and everything living on it, the ferns, the frogs, the sundews, you, is a temporary shape the water is taking on its way around.
