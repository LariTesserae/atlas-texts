# The Hollow Meadow of Ouren Tal

## The Ground That Is Alive

The plateau stands so high that the sky above it is nearly black at noon. Long ago it was a mountain range. Ten thousand million years of wind ground it down to a single tilted table of stone, and then the plants took the table apart from within. What you walk on is not rock. It is a mat of living tissue, in places forty meters thick, springy and faintly warm, woven from roots that ate the mountain and turned it into themselves. Beneath it the old stone is lace, hollowed and veined and mostly gone.

The mat is the color of old brass and dark plum. It rises in slow swells like a sea that forgot to move. Where it thins over buried rock you can feel the difference through your boots: a tightening, a drumhead hardness.

No rain has fallen here in longer than anything remembers. The plants don't need it. Their sap is a pale, heavy oil, and the chemistry of their bodies runs on it. Water is a poison to them. A few mornings a year, frost forms on the upper air-currents and drifts down as fine white rime, and the whole meadow flinches. Stems curl, pores clamp, and slick sacs of oil-sweat bead up and wash the frost off. The people who live here call these mornings *the sour days*. They stay indoors and keep their own canteens sealed. They carry their water like something dangerous, because to the land it is.

## What Grows

Everything is plant. Nothing else has ever had a chance.

**Drum-moss** carpets the low ground, a springy pile that thumps back when stepped on, so that any walker leaves a trail of soft booms.

**Reed-organs** grow in slow, scattered stands, each stalk as thick as a column and hollow. Their tops are cut into ragged flutes by wind and by their own growth. When the wind crosses them they sound chords, low and enormous, that you feel in your sternum before you hear them.

**Lanternvine** hangs from anything tall. Its bloom-clusters glow a dim jaundiced amber, and they pulse slowly in unison across whole valleys, a light-tide that rolls with the wind. This is most of the light there is. The sun is small, pale and old, and it is filtered by a permanent high veil of spore-dust. Noon here looks like late dusk somewhere kinder.

**The Standing Elders** are single plants, or single colonies, nobody is certain, whose trunks are the width of towers and whose crowns are flat and wide, canopies spread like plates to catch the thin light. They are spaced far apart, sometimes a day's walk between them. Between them the meadow is open to the horizon, the swells, the amber pulse and the dark sky.

## The Noise

It is never quiet. That is the first rule of the place, and the people say it as a prayer.

The reed-organs drone. The drum-moss booms. Seed-pods the size of skulls burst with cracks like rifle shots and scatter rattling shrapnel. Under everything, deep in the mat, is a grinding, hissing, ticking sound: the growth of roots, the slow shifting of the living ground. Wind arrives as a thousand pitches at once. On a strong day the plateau is loud enough that people talk by touching foreheads and speaking through the bone.

Animals add their voices. Long-throated grazers bellow across the swells in slow call-and-answer. Flocks of small, hard-winged things tick through the air like scattered coins. In the mat itself, blind burrowers chirr in colonies, and the sound rises through your feet.

## The Cities

The built world is sparse, a few settlements across a plateau that could hold hundreds. But each one is a marvel. The people of Ouren Tal don't build with stone or wood. They build with **resonance**.

A city here is a tuned structure. Its towers are grown from Elder-trunk and reed-organ, but they are held up by something else: standing waves of sound, generated by lattices of humming machines the size of fingernails, seeded into every beam and arch. The machines sing a single note continuously, and that note bends the world in ways the old physics has no word for. Bridges span kilometer gaps with nothing under them but a thread of tone. Whole terraces hang in the air, held by chords. Floors thin as leaves carry crowds without bowing.

The corollary is the second rule of the place: **silence is structural failure.** If the singing stops, the city sags. Any place where the tone fades goes soft and sinks toward the meadow. So the cities are loud on purpose. They are cathedrals, instruments and machines at once. Keepers walk the arches with tuning-rods, listening for the flat note, the dying harmonic, the place where the world is beginning to forget how to hold itself up.

At night the cities glow with the amber of lanternvine, threaded through with colder blue lines that seem to be the tone itself, made visible where it is strongest. Nobody knows how. The technology has been running for longer than anyone's records, and the people who tend it are stewards, not authors.

The largest is **Ouren**, a spiral of a hundred terraces hanging above a bowl in the meadow, so loud at its heart that the air trembles visibly. The smallest are lone spires on the open plain, a single Keeper and a few families, singing their one note through the dark.

## Fauna

The animals of the plateau are abundant, though the meadow is so wide they are seldom seen in the open. Most live inside the mat, in vast warrens, and rise only at dusk or when the drum-moss trembles.

**Grazers** wade the meadow in loose herds, tall and slender, with split hooves to spread their weight. They eat by slicing the top of the moss and pressing it against the roof of the mouth in a long, chewing rumble.

**Hollow-hunters** are the predators: low, quick, oil-slick animals that move under the mat like fish under a skin. They feel the boom of grazers' steps through the ground and rise beneath them. The herds move in irregular rhythms, off-beat, to confuse the drum-moss.

**Sparks** are tiny, wingless glowing insects that swarm around the lanternvine and feed on its pulse, and the pulse in turn is partly their doing. Nobody knows which came first.

**The Choir-Birds** roost in the crowns of the Elders, thousands to a tree. At dusk they answer the reed-organs, and the whole plateau rings with a double song, wind and wing, that carries for a hundred kilometers.

## The Slow Processes

The meadow moves on its own time. Every few years a swell rises and rolls across the plateau, a slow tectonic wave of living tissue as the roots reshape themselves. Cities are built with this in mind, their tone-lattices adjusting to shifting ground. Occasionally a swell breaks open a pocket of old stone, and for a season there is a gray scar on the meadow: bare, dry, silent rock, the last remnant of the mountain. People make pilgrimages to it. They stand there in the odd, cold quiet and touch the stone with their palms, and then they go back to the noise, because the noise is where they live.

## The Feel of It

Walk out at dusk from the last city-spire and the sound comes up around you like a tide. The drum-moss booms softly with each step. The reed-organs drone in the distance, and the lanternvine pulse rolls across the swells in slow amber waves. The sky is black and full of stars, and the light on the ground is the color of old honey. Somewhere a herd bellows, answered by another farther off. Behind you the spire hums its one held note, and if you listen closely you can hear the ground itself singing along.

It is an old place, older than anything on it, worn down to its last shape and wrapped in a single living skin. It is loud because it is alive, and alive because it is loud.
