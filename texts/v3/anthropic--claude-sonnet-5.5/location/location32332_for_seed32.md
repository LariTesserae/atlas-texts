# The Chord Room

From outside there is nothing to find. A low basin of pale, dry ground, a few hundred paces of rubble the color of old bone, and along its far edge a scarp no taller than a doorway lintel. The scarp is the top of a reef. Nothing in this basin has been under water for longer than the word *sea* has had meaning. The reef was raised by something else, and it is still here.

Find the seam in the scarp, a crack you would take for weathering, and turn sideways to enter. You will be inside the reef, in a pocket about the size of a small chapel, and it will be very loud.

## What it is made of

Everything here was built, but not by hands. Long ago, when the basin held a warm brine, mat-forming colonies drew silica out of the water and laid it down in tubes. Each colony extended a hollow stalk, then budded another beside it, and another. They did this for a span of time that has no useful number. When the brine went, they stopped. What they left is a mass of glassy tubes packed against each other with no gaps: fused, stacked, interleaved, bundled like straws in a fist.

The whole chamber is tube ends. The walls are honeycombed with mouths from the width of a thumbnail to wider than your shoulders. The floor is a mosaic of sawn-off tube cross-sections, each one a ring, some filled with fine grey silt, some open and dropping into darkness. Even the ceiling is a thicket of mouths angled downward like a cluster of bells. There is no natural substrate anywhere, no bedrock or soil, only the deposit of an ancient industry with no one in it.

The glass is milky, greenish at the thick parts, clear as ice at the thin lips. Erosion has done its long work here. Every edge is rounded. Blown grit and centuries of dry air have polished the tube mouths to a soft gloss and taken the sharp rims down to fine curves. Nothing snags. Everything feels like the inside of a shell.

## Light

It is bright, brighter than the basin outside, because the reef gathers light and passes it along. The tubes are little light-pipes. Sun falls on the scarp top, enters the thin glass, and travels down the tube walls to leak out in the chamber. The whole room glows from inside its own architecture: a pale, even, shadowless white-green, with brighter threads where a straight tube points at the sky and lays a single hard ray across the floor.

These rays are the only things with edges. They lie across the floor in narrow bars, and the silt in the open rings glitters where they touch it. Because the sun does not seem to move in here, or rather because the tubes average its whole arc into one steady glow, the light never shifts. It is noon in the chamber at every hour.

## Sound

The sound is what you will remember, if you can remember anything over it.

Below the reef is a slow seep of dry gas, cool and faintly mineral, rising through fissures in the deep foundation. It has risen at the same rate for as long as the reef has stood. It enters the tube bundles from beneath and passes up through them, and every tube speaks. Long tubes speak low and short tubes high. The gas flows steadily, and each tube found its pitch long ago, so together they sound one enormous chord, never resolving and never changing. It is a wall of sound, a bass you feel in the sternum, a mid-register that seems to press on your eyes, and above that a shimmering glassy shimmer from the thin tubes, like a finger on a wet rim multiplied a thousand times.

Loudness is the rule of the place. Speech is pointless. You cannot hear your own footsteps, though you feel them through the floor as a small dry crunch of silt. The chord has no beat and no melody because nothing here can vary it. The pitches interfere, and in some spots the overtones cancel and leave pockets of relative hush where you can stand with the sound only pressing at your ribs. In others they add up, and your teeth ache. Over time you learn the map of these places by body alone.

## Life

Not much lives here, and nothing moves on its own. No animal has ever come in: no insects, no scuttling things, no droppings, no bones. The cool dry gas is faintly acrid and holds nothing edible.

The only life is a thin, sparse film of pale green-gold crust on the tube lips where the light rays fall, a photosynthetic skin barely thicker than paint. It grows in rings around the bright mouths, so the walls look faintly haloed. In a few of the wider tubes something like tufted grey moss has packed itself into the shaded throats, dry and springy. It survives on the gas's trace moisture and never seems to spread or die. It has been exactly this much for exactly this long. There is no water, and the crust seems to drink the light and the faint mineral breath.

## Rules

Nothing changes. The chord holds, the light holds, and the temperature stays at a cool, constant, faintly stony chill. If you break off a delicate tube lip, it stays broken forever and the chord shifts by one thin note, permanently, for anyone who comes after. Nothing repairs. The reef is finished, in balance, settled into its final form.

Cold air moves up from the floor rings against your ankles, and you can feel the whole structure humming through your bones like a bell that has been struck once and never stopped ringing.

## The strange part

If you stay long enough, you stop hearing it as noise. The chord becomes a kind of silence, the way a held breath becomes a kind of stillness. Eyes closed, you feel inside a single enormous instrument tuned to nothing but itself. The unusual thing is that when you finally leave through the seam and step back into the pale basin, the quiet is so total that you can hear, for a few moments, the same chord faintly in your skull, alone, still sounding.
