# The Sump at Orrel Halt

The basin is small enough to cross in an hour, and it sits so low that the land around it seems to lean in and listen. The rim is a soft, worn lip of grey shale, rounded like the shoulder of a sleeper. Inside it lies a floor of packed cinder and black clay, and on that floor stand eleven buildings.

They are far apart. Between them is open ground the color of wet ash, crossed by raised pipe-runs on iron trestles and by a single narrow-gauge rail that links the buildings in a slow, looping figure. Nothing else marks the ground. There are no roads and no yards. Each building stands alone with a great deal of room around it, as if the settlement had been laid out by someone who wanted to keep each machine from hearing the others. They hear each other anyway.

## The Works

Most of the eleven are industrial in the old, heavy sense. A pump house has brick shoulders and a copper-green cupola. Two foundry sheds have roofs of corrugated iron gone the deep brown-violet of dried blood. A boiler hall is taller than the rest, with a stack that has stopped smoking and has no need to smoke. A long, low shed holds rolling mills. A water tower is shaped like an upturned bell. The last few are things nobody can name any longer: a ribbed drum the size of a chapel, a gantry with no load, a windowless block whose door is always open and whose interior is always dark.

They all run. Belts turn, pistons cycle, flywheels hum in their pits. No one tends them, and nothing seems to be made. The town produces a steady sound, and that seems to be its whole output.

The rust here is finished. It advanced for centuries and then stopped, sealed under a lacquer of dried secretion the color of amber. Iron surfaces look glazed and wet but are hard and faintly warm. The brick has weathered to a soft pink-grey, and every step and sill has been dished by ancient passage into shallow bowls that hold small coins of standing water. Nothing new has been added or lost in living memory. The town is not decaying. It has settled, like sediment.

## The Damp

The air is cool and close, and the moisture in it has nowhere to go. It condenses on every cold surface in beads that swell, gather, and run down. Rain falls rarely and lightly, and it hardly matters, because the works are already wet. Water collects in gutters, drips from trestles, threads down pipe-runs, and pools in the dished stones. From there it is drawn through grated culverts into the pump house, lifted to the bell-shaped tower, released, and given back to the air as steam from vents along the rim. The steam condenses and falls again. The loop is closed and has always been closed. The water level in the basin sits at one line, marked by a band of pale mineral crust on every wall at exactly knee height, and it has never risen above or fallen below it.

## No Green

Nothing that photosynthesizes grows here. The only living surface that is not animal is a felt of grey-white fungus and bacterial skin. It lines the culverts, furs the undersides of pipes, and stipples the north faces of the brick in soft coin-sized rosettes. It smells of cellars and cold pennies. It is the pantry of everything else.

## The Fauna

The animals are not sparse. Eleven buildings, and every one of them is overwhelmed.

The drummers are the most common. They are armored, low-slung creatures the size of terriers, with segmented backs of overlapping plates, jointed like a lobster tail, and blunt heads. They live by the hundreds of thousands in the culverts, foundation voids, and cinder burrows. When they emerge to graze the fungal felt, the ground itself appears to flow, a brown-black tide moving in a single direction, plates clicking. They strike their carapaces against iron to call. The rhythm is nearly, but never quite, in time with the engines.

The hangers are long, pale, boneless-looking animals that cling to the undersides of trestles and rafters in ranks, like drying laundry. Each is as long as an arm. They drink condensation through a fine comb at the mouth, and they hum, a low, wet, sustained vowel. Where they roost thickly, the roof beams seem to be breathing.

Above the basin, from dawn-equivalent to dusk-equivalent and beyond, come the sheet-birds. They are thin, translucent, kite-winged things that pour over the rim in one continuous, curling ribbon and circle the cupola, the stack, and the tower in a great slow gyre. They eat the drifting fluff of spores. They do not seem to land. Their shadows sweep the cinder plain in long moving bands.

None of the animals are afraid of the machinery. They have arranged themselves around it. Drummers nest in the warm lee of flywheel pits. Hangers cluster where pipes leak. The creatures have never been culled or displaced, and never spread beyond what the town's supplies can hold. The populations are fixed, and the numbers do not change.

## The Sound

Nothing is quiet, and it is louder than it needs to be. The floor of the basin is a bowl, and the bowl holds sound the way a cupped hand holds water. The engines give a bass throb that you feel in the sternum before you hear it. Over it lie the shriek of belts, the clank of valves, the hiss of vents, and the ceaseless tick and rattle of drummers on iron, a sound like hail on a tin roof that never ends. Under that, the hangers hum a single sustained note, and the sheet-birds add a high, chiming whistle as air passes through their translucent wings.

These layers do not blend into noise. Standing at the center, you hear that they have settled into a chord, one held so long that the town's whole architecture is tuned to it. The pipes ring in sympathy. The water in the dished stones shivers in concentric rings. A dropped tool would ring on the same pitch as everything else. Visitors describe the silence upon leaving as a kind of pressure.

## The Light

The light is plain and even. It comes through a thin, high, pearl-colored overcast that neither thickens nor breaks, and it casts soft, unhurried shadows. Everything is visible and nothing is dramatic. Beads of water on the iron give off small steady gleams. The amber glaze on the machinery catches the white sky and holds it. The hours do not seem to differ.

## What Is Strange

The strange thing is that it is all so exactly in balance. The engines burn no fuel that anyone can find. The drummers eat only fungus, and the fungus grows only on the drummers' droppings. The hangers drink water that has been through the works. The birds eat spores the hangers release. Each thing in the basin feeds on the leavings of another, and the ring closes, and the ring turns.

Some say the machines are what the animals have made, and some say the animals are what the machines have made. Both may be wrong. If you place your palm flat on the boiler hall wall, you feel the engine's pulse, and beneath it, in quick small counterpoint, a second pulse that belongs to the animals nested in the brick. After a while you cannot tell which is following which.

The town does not change. It does not need you. But the door of the windowless block is always open, and the dark inside is warm and wet and full of quiet, patient movement, and something in the chord seems to lean toward it.
