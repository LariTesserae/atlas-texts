# The Wet Room

## The plateau

The land up here has been worn down for so long that it has stopped being dramatic. The plateau sits high, about twenty-five hundred meters, but you would not know it from the shape of the ground. It is a vast, warm, rolling pavement of pale quartzite and rust-red laterite, rubbed smooth over more time than mountains take to rise and fall. There are no cliffs. Boulders are rounded into loaves and knuckles. Old rivers left shallow braided grooves that hold nothing but grass, and the grass is the dry gold of a place that gets its rain in rumors.

The air is warm and thin and smells of hot stone and resin. Wind moves without hurry. Sparse, wide-crowned trees stand far apart on the red ground, and between them the land is open and almost silent, except for wind in the grass and the ticking of the rock as it cools. This is the outer world of the place: ancient, quiet, and finished.

## The seam

You could cross this plateau a hundred times and miss it. Part of the land has settled along an old fault line, and the fold has closed over into a shallow crease in the quartzite, no wider than a road. It is roofed at intervals by leaning slabs and curtains of vine. The only hint is a sound, a low, continuous, layered noise like a distant stadium or a city heard through a wall. It seems to come from underfoot.

A worn ramp leads down through the crease. Its steps are dished by something's feet and by centuries of water. The rock at the sides is polished and faintly greasy with moisture. The air changes as you descend. The dry resin smell gives way to wet leaf, mud, and something sweetish and mineral, like a struck match dropped in a well. It grows warmer and denser. Then the roof of vines lifts and you are inside.

## The bowl

The hollow is a single room of open sky, about a hundred and fifty meters across. It is a collapsed cavern, or a sink that dissolved and settled in the deep past, and its walls have been softened by erosion into long inward-leaning curves. They are draped in ferns, moss, and hanging orchids so thick the rock underneath is only a suggestion. The floor is a shallow dish of black, wet soil, threaded with rivulets that go nowhere in particular and sink back into the ground.

It is lush, but it is not crowded. That is the first strange thing. The plants do not press against each other. Tree ferns stand well apart on the floor, each with a wide clear circle of moss around it, as if something keeps the spacing. A few big-leaved things, wild banana-like herbs and a twisted fig with roots draped over a slab, hold the bowl's middle. Between them there is bare black mud, open pools, and patches of pale sand. The vegetation is rich in kind and thin in number. Each plant has room to be enormous, and most are.

Light comes down normal and clear through the opening above. At midday it is a warm white column that moves slowly across the floor, and at the edges it turns green through the leaves. Nothing here is dim or gothic. It is bright and damp and close.

## The reeds

The tallest features in the bowl are not plants.

Scattered across the floor, roughly thirty paces apart, stand slender grey-white shafts, each about twelve meters high and thick as a thigh at the base. They taper to points too fine to see clearly against the sky. There are perhaps a hundred and forty of them. They are not metal, not stone, not bone. The surface is smooth and faintly pearled, worn to a soft matte like river glass, and in places it wears a crust of lichen so old it has laid down layers. Whatever they are, they have stood here for as long as the walls have been eroding, and they have been eroded too. Their edges are rounded and their bases pitted. They are worn, not broken.

They hum. Each has a tone, and together they form a slow chord that shifts. Put a hand on one and you feel the note in your wrist, a deep, patient vibration, slightly cool.

They are also doing something. Water beads on the shafts, and the drops climb. They creep upward along the surface against gravity, gathering into a slow silver rope that reaches the fine tip and vanishes. It does not drip and it does not steam. The tips are cold enough to frost the air around them for a hand's breadth. Whatever the reeds are for, they take water out of the air and send it somewhere the bowl cannot see.

## Weather in pulses

Moisture arrives here by appointment, not by rainfall. Every forty minutes or so, the reeds' chord drops half a step and holds. Then mist wells up from the ground, not falling from the sky. It seeps out of the black soil, rises through the fern crowns, and thickens into a warm white fog that fills the bowl to the rim. For about five minutes you can see nothing farther than your arm. The reeds' hum swells inside the fog, water runs fast up every shaft, and droplets slide down the ferns and drum on the leaves.

Then the chord lifts. The fog thins and pulls downward into the mud as if inhaled. The bowl stands clear and dripping in the sun, and for a while it is nearly dry. Ordinary rain sometimes falls too, a warm afternoon squall from the plateau above, but the pulses are the real weather. The whole ecosystem keeps time to them.

## The animals

The bowl is loud because it is packed with animals, and it is the animals, not the plants, that fill it to overwhelming.

The frogs come first. There are tens of thousands of them, in every size from a fingernail to a dinner plate: emerald, mustard, mottled brown, translucent-bellied, with ribs and finger-bones visible in the light. They fill every wet surface, hanging in rows on the ferns and stacked in the pool margins, and their calls layer into a wall of sound with an internal grain, like weaving. Cicadas, bush crickets, and beetles thrum in the leaf litter and canopy. Birds crowd the rim in bright, noisy tiers: small parrots, honeyeaters, a black-and-white fluting thing that sings in three notes only. Gliding lizards and furred, flat-bodied gliders flicker from ledge to ledge. Ants run in braided highways along the walls, and some of the trails are wide enough to hear.

Where there is prey there are predators. Green whip snakes pour along the fern trunks. Mantises the size of a forearm hold still among the orchids. Long-legged herons stalk the pools, and a pale, narrow-winged hawk cuts through the opening above, sending a ripple of silence across the walls that closes again behind it. Small dramas happen constantly. A frog vanishes mid-note. A beetle is dragged under the moss. Something on the far wall shrieks and stops. The bowl churns with feeding and being fed, and it never runs out.

## What is strange about them

The animals keep the reeds' time. When the chord drops before a mist pulse, every frog in the bowl shifts pitch within a breath of the reeds' change, and the whole wall of sound slides down and locks in a new harmony. Birds hold their song at the same moment. Even the insects tune. The chorus is not random noise but a vast, distributed answer to the reeds' tone, and if you listen long enough you hear that it is organized into slow phrases and refrains, with something like a grammar.

Many of the animals carry the reed-material in their bodies. Frogs have glassy, pearl-grey bones that ring faintly when they call. Some beetles have wing-cases of the same matte grey. A heron's leg, when it lifts from the water, shows a thin pale line beneath the skin. It seems the reeds are not just a machine the animals live among. The animals grow into them and take their color, and perhaps they are their upkeep, carrying, feeding, and cleaning, with the life of the bowl serving as the maintenance crew for the engineering.

## Scattered works

Other made things lie around the floor, few and small, all as worn as the reeds.

- A low round house of fitted stone and grey alloy stands near the fig. Its roof is intact and green with moss. It has no door and never had one, only a smooth oval opening at ground level just big enough for a crouching person. Inside are a dry floor, one curved bench, and a faintly glowing seam in the wall that stays warm to the touch. Frogs sleep on the bench.
- A ring of shallow basins, each the width of a table, is cut into a slab near the eastern wall. They fill from below when the fog comes and drain when it lifts. The water in them is very clear, and the bottoms are engraved with fine spirals that seem to move when you stop looking directly at them.
- A single leaning arch of the pearl-grey material, taller than a person, frames nothing. Ferns have grown through it. If you stand under it during a pulse, the fog inside the arch stays perfectly still while the fog outside swirls.

Nothing here looks recent, and nothing looks abandoned. Everything looks settled into a final state and left running.

## Sound

The bowl is one of the loudest places on the plateau and the plateau does not know it. The walls hold the sound. The frog chorus fills the volume like water in a cup, the reed-chord runs beneath it, and the birds shout over the top. Then, at the moment of the pulse, everything falls a half-step and holds, and for five minutes of fog the noise becomes thick and warm and close, a sound you feel in your teeth. When the fog withdraws and the chord lifts, the chorus surges and brightens, as if relieved.

At night, under warm stars, the frogs sing louder. Points of cold blue light wake along the reeds' tips, and the sound grows until the ramp above trembles slightly. On the plateau, a hundred meters away, the grass whispers in the wind, and nothing is heard of the bowl at all.

## What holds the place together

The rule here is simple and old. In a land ground down to almost nothing, one small, warm room keeps a wetter, older weather of its own. The reeds pull water from deep air and the depths, and the animals hold the rhythm and the repairs. The plants grow only as fast as the room allows and stay far apart, because the room has a fixed amount to give and has never wasted it. The whole thing runs on cycles measured in minutes, seasons, and ages. It has been erased at the edges and left whole at the center.

You climb out through the crease into dry wind and gold grass, and the sound dwindles behind you into the rock. You will notice that you are humming a note you didn't choose, and that it is a half-step lower than when you went in.
