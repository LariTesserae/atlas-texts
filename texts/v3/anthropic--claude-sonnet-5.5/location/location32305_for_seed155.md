# The Tableland of Slow Hammers

Begin with the flatness. Somewhere under everything is a range of mountains that was once sharp enough to tear weather open. It has been worn down for so long that only its knuckles remain, rounded granite domes standing a few hundred feet above a plain that is itself nearly two miles above the sea. The plain is not rock or soil. It is plant. Moss, sedge, cushion-heath, liverwort, and dense grey-green lichen have been laying themselves down on this tableland for longer than anyone has kept a count, and the mat is now forty, sixty, in places a hundred feet deep. It is dead below and living above, and it gives underfoot like a mattress. Walking is a kind of slow wading. Each step leaves a print that fills back in over about an hour, shivering as it goes.

The air is cool and thin, and it tastes faintly of iron and of crushed leaves. The light is ordinary and clear, an unfiltered high-country daylight, but the ground drinks so much of it that the whole plateau looks lit from below by dim green. Moisture comes as condensation more than rain. Cloud drags across the domes, stalls on the moss, and leaves every blade beaded. Real rain falls a few times a week, in narrow curtains you can watch wander across the plain for an hour before they reach you or don't.

## The Domes

The granite knuckles wear the plant mat like fur. Where the rock breaks through, it is streaked orange and black with older lichens, and these are the only colours on the plateau that do not move. Everything else shifts. The mat has a slow tide. Wind combs it into long ridges that creep across the plain like waves in thick syrup, and the ridges carry the seeds, spores, and small floating plants with them. Around midday the tide produces a low whispering, billions of dry stalks rubbing.

## The Cities

There are seven, or four, or eleven. The number depends on who is counting and on where the counter stands.

They are sparse. Each one is a dense knot of brick, cast iron, and riveted plate, and between any two lie ten or thirty miles of open, empty, breathing green. From a dome you can see two or three of them at once, each a dark cluster with a plume, floating on the plain like a ship's lights at sea.

Built on a living bog, they should have sunk. They didn't, for two reasons.

**The first is the pumps.** Each city is ringed and threaded with enormous beam engines and later steam-turbine pumps that draw water out of the mat beneath the streets. If the water returns, the mat swells, and a swollen mat swallows a district in a season. So the pumps run day and night, thudding out water through channels of green-crusted brass into the open plain. The sound is the loudest thing on the plateau: a slow, enormous *whump… whump… whump* felt in the sternum long before it is heard, with the hiss and clatter of valves layered over it. In the cities you talk by leaning close. Between them the pulse carries for miles, and the moss trembles very slightly in time.

**The second is the trees**, and they are the reason the cities look the way they do. They are not trees in the usual sense. They are colossal woody sedges, or something related to sedges, with fluted trunks a hundred feet high and roots like flying buttresses that spread and interlace under the mat, forming a rigid living raft. The city founders did not plant them. They found them growing in ancient rings, and they built inside the rings. Now the roots hold up the foundations. The hollow dead trunks of the oldest ones serve as chimneys, still lined with the pale fibre the plant produces, which does not burn well, and this is considered a blessing. Iron girders are bolted straight into living wood and the wood grows around them. Every tram gantry, every water tower, every gasworks sits in a cage of slowly swallowing green.

## Industry

The industry is real and loud. Foundries, rolling mills, and compressor halls run on a fuel the plateau itself provides. Peat is cut in blocks from the mat's lower layers, dried in long racks, and burned. There are also digesters, brick vats of fermenting sedge that give off a marsh gas, burned in mantles and boilers. The smoke is thin and blue and smells sweet. It drifts low across the plain and settles into the moss, which absorbs it, and so the vegetation everywhere is faintly sooted at the roots.

Narrow-gauge railways cross the open ground on floating sleepers. The trains are small, black, and rattling, and they crawl. The track rides the tide of the mat, so it undulates, and the trains rise and fall as they go. Between stations the drivers cut their engines and coast, and the plateau's whisper rushes back in.

## Where Reality Fails

The plateau is old and settled, and something in that long stillness has gone slack. The rules here hold in most places and fail in patches, and the patches are not marked.

- **Sound arrives out of order.** The pump beat in the eastern cities sometimes lands before the piston moves. You watch the great beam rise, and the *whump* has already come and gone, and then it comes again as the beam falls. Engineers keep two sets of timing logs, one observed and one heard, and do not try to reconcile them.
- **Distances disagree.** The road between two cities is nineteen miles by survey, and it takes a day and a half to walk if you leave in the morning, but two hours if you leave at dusk. Returning takes the same time as going, and nobody has explained it. Coachmen simply charge for both.
- **Counting erodes.** Count the chimneys of a city and you will get a different number each time. Count the stacks of peat blocks in a drying rack and the total shrinks as you go. Old workers say you should never count anything aloud on the plateau. They point to things instead.
- **Rain sometimes falls up.** It happens in narrow columns, thirty feet across, in clear air, near the domes. Beads leave the moss and rise, slowly, catching the light, and vanish about a hundred feet up. Some mornings the moss is dry in the middle of a column and drenched in a perfect ring around it.
- **Streets that continue.** In the oldest districts, some alleys go on longer than the buildings allow. You walk past a wall you know is one block long and it is still going. The walls there are quiet, and the pump sound does not enter.
- **Shadows and origins.** Cloud shadows on the plain sometimes move against the wind. Sometimes they arrive before their clouds.

## Life

The fauna is rare and strange. There are pale, long-legged grazers, something like deer but with too many joints in the leg, who move across the mat in ones and twos and never seem to sink. There are moths as broad as a hand, whose wings look like lichen until they open, and who cluster on the warm brick of the foundries at night. There are a few birds. A small dark bird nests in the hollow chimney-trunks and sings a two-note phrase in the pauses of the pumps, and the sound of it, against the industrial din, is one of the few things people in the cities describe as beautiful.

Nothing preys on anything else that anyone has seen. The grazers eat moss. The moths eat the sooty film on the brick. The birds eat the moths. It is possible the balance has been settled so long that no one remembers the other way.

## Motion

The place is not still. The mat tides, the ridges move, the pumps hammer, the clouds drag through, the trains crawl, the smoke lies down. Across it all, the tableland carries its odd slack in reality like the hum of an engine that has run so long that no one hears it anymore. The plain is ancient, the cities are young, and the plants have absorbed both, and they will keep absorbing them as long as the pumps keep pounding. If the pumps ever stop, the plateau will quietly close over the cities, and the plants will not even have to hurry.
