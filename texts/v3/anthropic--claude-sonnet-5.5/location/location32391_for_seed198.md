# The Terraced Works of Hallow Reach

You come in from above, through cloud. At about two thousand meters the cloud thins to a pearl-gray ceiling, and under it a valley opens about thirty kilometers long. You cannot see a single square meter of ground in it.

## The Shape of It

The valley was a glacial trough once, and the bones remain: a U-section, steep flanks, a braided river somewhere at the bottom. Everything above the bones was made. The flanks are stepped into terraces, some a few meters wide and some broad enough to hold a rail yard. Each terrace is walled in dark stone, poured slag-concrete, or riveted plate gone the color of old liver. Above the terraces stand buildings, and above those, more terraces. Where a slope was too steep even for that, cantilevered galleries hang from cables, and the galleries carry their own smaller buildings.

The whole valley is packed. Streets run under buildings, and buildings straddle pipe corridors. Stair-lanes cut through the ground floors of foundries and come out the far side smelling of hot oil and wet iron. There is no vacant lot, no plaza, no shoulder of road. Every open space is a channel for something: water, steam, rail, people, cable, or the slow trolleys that haul ore down and finished ingots up.

The place is old enough to have settled. Stair treads are dished by centuries of feet. Retaining walls have leaned into equilibrium and stopped. Water has found its grooves through the masonry and holds them, staining the stone in long vertical bruises of rust-orange, black, and green. Nothing here is new, even the parts that were built last month. They are built in the old idiom, from salvaged blocks in the old bond patterns, and the weather has already begun to claim them.

## Water

The air is always at the edge of rain. Fog nets, huge sails of steel mesh, are strung across the ridgelines to comb the cloud into gutters, and the gutters feed cisterns, and the cisterns feed the penstocks. Water is the valley's power. It drops through iron pipes as thick as houses, spins turbines in vaulted stone halls, and leaves as tailwater that runs through the lower works, cooling and washing and carrying away, until it reaches the river black and warm.

Water is also simply present. Walls weep. Cornices drip in patient rhythms. Puddles stand in every low tread, holding small inverted copies of the lamps. Stone is cold and slick, and the handrails, wet all day, are worn bright and pitted at the grip points. Steam and fog are hard to tell apart. A vent breathes out a white column, and it is a few seconds before you know whether the cloud has come down or the works have breathed up.

## Green

The vegetation is dense past all reason, and it is as much a part of the works as the rails. Ferns grow from every joint in the masonry. Moss lies over the north faces of the buildings in thick, saturated cushions, deep green shading to nearly black in the shadows and brilliant lime where a lamp catches it. Vines have been trained for generations up the pipe-racks and across gantries, and they drape from the cables in long slow curtains. In many places it is unclear whether the plant is holding up the structure or the structure the plant.

Some of it is cultivated. The upper terraces are planted with tea, cold-hardy brassicas, and a bitter mountain fruit, all in soil that is itself manufactured: composted mill-waste, sieved slag, and river silt, hauled up and laid down in beds between rails. Some of it is not cultivated at all. Trees have taken root in gutters and on roof ledges, small twisted birches and alders, gripping the tile with pale rope roots. Every few years one falls through a roof and is quietly incorporated into the repair.

## Light

It is always dim. Under the cloud ceiling the daylight is a shadowless gray, and inside the tight lanes it fades to something like dusk at noon. So the valley lights itself. Sodium lamps burn amber along every stair-lane. Furnace mouths glow deep orange behind slatted shutters. Green-white arc lights hang over the rail yards and make the fog around them a solid, glowing volume. At night the terraces form a great tilted field of small warm lights, and each is haloed by moisture, so the whole valley looks like a soft galaxy lying on its side.

Light does its best work on wet surfaces. Every pipe, every stone tread, every leaf takes a bright line, and walking a lane is like walking through a sketch of reflected lamps.

## Sound

The valley is mostly quiet, which surprises people who expect a place of industry to clang. The reasons are physical. Moss, wet air, and thick masonry swallow sound. Turbines are housed deep in stone, and the machines are electrical and hydraulic, the sort that hum and thrum rather than pound. What you hear is water: dripping, trickling, the gargle of a downpipe, and beneath everything a low tone from the penstocks, so constant that you only notice it when a valve closes and the tone drops half a step.

Over that, small sounds carry surprisingly far. A boot on an iron tread. A cart wheel on a rail joint. A sluice gate rolling shut with a long groan. A whistle, high and thin, from three terraces up. Now and then a real noise breaks through, a steam release or the shriek of a cutting saw, and it echoes down the valley for a long time before the fog absorbs it.

## Turbulence

The stability is only in the form. Inside the form, everything is in furious motion.

Work never stops. Every hour something is being demolished, rebuilt, re-piped, re-routed. A stair-lane closes behind a curtain of scaffolding and reopens three days later a meter to the left. A whole gallery is unbolted from its cables and lowered by winch to a new level. Cranes work in the fog like grazing animals, and their lamps swing in slow arcs. Sluices open and close on a logic no one person understands, so streets flood ankle-deep and drain within minutes, and people cross the flooded stretches on planks that other people carry ahead of them.

Landslip is a normal event. A retaining wall gives way in the night, and by morning a crew has already begun to lay the replacement, using the fallen blocks. Rockfall, rust-fall, and slumping of the made ground are met with routine, not alarm. The valley was designed for this. Its true structure is a system of continuous repair, so it is never finished and never quite collapses.

Storm-states sweep through weekly. The cloud thickens and drops, and rain comes sideways off the ridges. The whole terrace system sings with running water, every gutter full and every downpipe roaring. The lamps blur. For an hour the penstocks run at full and the tone climbs into a shuddering note you feel in your teeth. Then it passes, the fog lifts a few meters, and the works go on.

## Life

Fauna is rare. There are a few pale swifts that nest in the pipe-galleries and stitch the fog at dusk. Small gray frogs live in the cisterns, and their voices are among the few soft sounds at night. Rats exist but are thin and furtive. Sometimes on the highest terraces a fox, red-brown and wary, crosses a rail line and vanishes into the ferns.

People are there, but the density is one of structure, not of crowds. They appear as lamps moving in the lanes, as silhouettes in lit windows, as a hand on a rail, as a line of workers in oilcloth coats filing up a stair. The valley feels inhabited mainly by its own processes.

## One Rule

In Hallow Reach nothing is abandoned. A stair no one uses is planted. A pipe that carries no water is given to moss. A machine that stops is mined for its parts. A wall that falls is a new wall. The valley takes everything into itself, wet and green and lit amber, and turns it back into the work, and the work goes on beneath the cloud.
