# The Long Grey Table

The plateau is so wide that the horizon curves. Stand still and you begin to sense the planet's roundness in the way distant ridges sink into haze before they reach the sky. Its elevation is not dramatic. There are no peaks to announce it. You know you are high because the air is thin and cold, and because the clouds hang at the level of your chest, where they drag their bellies across the ground.

## Ground

The land is very old and has been worn nearly to its final shape. Mesas have lost their corners. Tors are rounded like knuckles. The valleys are wide dishes with no cutting rivers left in them, only a shallow memory of rivers, paved in gravel that the frost has sorted over ages into pale bands and stripes. Everything is in balance with the cold. Scree slopes no longer slide. Boulders sit in sockets of their own making, half-swallowed by the soil they shed.

The rock is dark iron-stained basalt and paler quartzite, and the two show through as a mottled grey-brown that changes with the light from slate to rust to the colour of wet ash. Where the surface is undisturbed it is a pavement of small stones, polished by centuries of wind and rime until they look almost oiled.

## Water

Water here is a matter of condensation. It rarely rains. Instead the cloud deck lowers and lifts, and every surface collects a fine bead of moisture that gathers, runs, and disappears into the gravel. Mornings bring hoarfrost, then a brief slick wet, then dryness again by mid-afternoon.

The water gathers in pockets. Seeps darken the bases of the mesas, and each one supports a green-black mat of moss the size of a room. Tarns sit in the hollows, still and dim, skinned with thin ice in the hours before what passes for dawn. Thread-streams braid between them for a few hundred paces and then vanish underground. The whole plateau drinks from its own breath.

## Plants

Vegetation is sparse and hugs the ground. Crust lichens paint the rocks in orange, bone-white, and a bruised olive. Cushion plants form hard green domes that hold heat in their centres, and small pale insects crowd inside them. Along the seeps, black moss and low sedge grow in the only continuous green. Nothing stands taller than a knee. Wind would strip it, and the grazers would too.

## The Kilns

Fauna here is overwhelming, though you will not find it crowded. Life is not spread thinly across this land in the way of deer or hares. It is concentrated into a few colossal beings, and the beings are so enormous that everything else lives on, in, and around them.

They are called kilns. Each is the size of a low hill and moves at the speed of a walking person. Their bodies are low and broad, plated in overlapping scutes of dark, mineral-rich horn, and they carry the cold on their backs as rime and hanging frost. Inside each, deep in the barrel of the chest, is an organ that burns. They eat lichen, peat, moss, and gravel, and they feed the mixture into a slow furnace where iron and other metals are separated from stone and carbon. This is a living industry. It has no chimney or foundry, only a body that does the work.

The kilns exhale steam through vents along their spines. Long pale plumes rise, spread flat against the cloud deck, and merge into it. Because of this the herds make weather. Where several kilns graze in the same basin, the air above them thickens into its own stratum of cloud, and their region of the plateau stays damper and a degree or two warmer than the rest. From far away you can find the herds by the cloud that follows them.

What the kilns leave behind is slag. Each footfall presses into the wet gravel and leaves a bead-trail of dark metallic pellets, some no larger than seeds, some the size of fists. Iron-grey, blue-black, or dull copper-green, they lie in long lines that mark the herd's route for decades afterward. Lichens colonise them within a season, and the pellets change the chemistry of the soil so that the plateau's few plants grow best along the old kiln roads.

## The Life Around Them

Every kiln is surrounded by attendants. Pale beetles work the slag as it cools. Grey-feathered birds ride the backs and pick at the frost. Mites and springtails live in colonies in the folds of the hide. Below the surface, millions of small burrowers feed on the falling waste. When a kiln stops to graze, hundreds of small lives rearrange themselves around it. Each animal is a district, and the district is populated.

Between the kilns lies the emptiness. You can walk for a day across a lichen-crusted pavement and see nothing alive but a single fox-sized scavenger crossing the gravel, its coat the colour of rust. Then the fog thins and one of the great backs rises on the horizon, steaming, and the whole landscape reorganises around it.

## Light

The light is dim and diffuse, and the cloud deck never quite lifts. The sun is a pale coin when visible, and it rides low. Shadows are soft and there are no sharp edges anywhere. Colours are muted to grey, ochre, olive, and slate.

At dusk the kilns carry a faint static glow. They build up charge as they move through dry cold air, and it crawls along the ridges of their armour in thin blue threads. This is the only light on the plateau that does not come from the sky. It flickers across the rime and makes the fog around each animal glow like something lit from within.

## Silence

The plateau is nearly silent. The thin, damp air and the low cloud absorb sound. The kilns make almost no noise, only a deep, slow breathing you feel in the soles of your feet more than hear. A drip from a rock. The faint tick of cooling slag. Sometimes the crunch of gravel under a foot, your own or another's, arriving very small in a very large space.

The animals communicate through the ground. A kiln's step sends a pulse through the gravel that other kilns feel for miles. Whole herds turn together at a signal no human ear would catch, and the movement looks like a slow choreography arranged by nothing visible.

## Time

Change happens slowly. The herds travel a circuit that takes many years, drifting from basin to basin, following the cycle of lichen growth. In their wake the land recovers, and new lichen spreads on the slag, and moss creeps back to the seeps. When the herd returns, decades later, the ground has been restored for them. The plateau's balance is not a static peace but a slow grazing, a very long breath.

Nothing has been built here. The only industry is the kilns', and the only artifacts are the trails of metal they drop behind them, which the land absorbs in time. To stand on the Table is to stand inside a machine that was never assembled, running on cold and fog and patience, and going on without any need for you to understand it.
