# The Cup Under the Ice

The basin sits at the top of the world's patience, a bowl about a mile across, cut into the shoulder of a peak whose summit is still a day's climb above. The rock here is young. A glacier left only a few generations ago and the walls still look freshly broken: pale granodiorite with edges you could cut a palm on, moraine ridges that haven't slumped, boulders perched exactly where the ice dropped them, some balanced so precariously they seem to be waiting for permission. Nothing has had time to round anything off.

The air is thin and cold, just above freezing at noon and hard frost by dusk. But the light is overwhelming. At this height the sky is a deep, nearly violet blue, and the sun is a white weight on the skin even while breath smokes. Every wet surface throws it back. Meltwater films, quartz grains, frost crystals in the shade of stones, all glitter. Shadows are black-edged and exact. Looking at the far wall for long hurts.

## The Green

What surprises everyone who arrives is how green it is.

The floor of the basin is a continuous, uneven carpet of cushion plants, hard domed mounds of tiny leaves packed so tightly they feel like green stone until you press them and they give, faintly, like a sleeping animal's flank. Some are the size of a hat, some the size of a sheep. Their surfaces are studded with minute white flowers that open only in the hour after the fog lifts. Between the mounds runs moss, thick and bright as wet lime, and in the seeps a red-brown sphagnum that squelches underfoot and is icy to the wrist.

Rising out of all this are the rosettes, the basin's tall things. They are thick-stemmed, shaggy plants standing chest high or taller, each crowned with a tight fist of silver-furred leaves. The fur is dense enough to hold beads of moisture like dew on wool. At night the leaves fold inward around the growing heart, and by dawn each rosette is a furred bud rimed with white. Inside, where the leaves overlap, there is a cupped reservoir of liquid water that doesn't freeze. Bees, beetles, and small birds drink from them. In the coldest hour you can put a finger in and find the water is faintly, absurdly warm.

Along the lower rim, where the ground is steadier, grow low thickets of twisted shrubs with bark like peeling paper, their trunks bent all in one direction by the wind that comes down from the summit at night.

## Water

It is damp rather than wet. There is no roaring stream. Water arrives as fog and leaves as seep.

Each morning, around mid-morning, cloud pours up the valley from below and fills the basin to the rim, so the whole place becomes white and close and dripping. It doesn't rain so much as condense: on fur, on moss, on the beards of the animals, on the hairs of your sleeve. Then, sometime after noon, the fog lifts off the ground in slow columns. The columns rise for a while as if the basin were exhaling, and then the sky is clear again and the light returns like a struck bell.

The moisture gathers underground. Little threads of water run beneath the cushions, audible if you kneel with an ear close, and emerge at the low end of the basin in a chain of shallow tarns. These are dark, perfectly clear, and rimmed with a fine white rind of ice each morning. The tarns overflow into a single thin outlet that vanishes into a crack in the rock. Where it goes no one has followed.

## Life

Density of stone and human presence is low, but density of life is not. The basin is quiet the way a full room can be quiet, with everyone breathing.

Herds of small, long-necked grazers move across the cushion fields, dun-colored with thick white bibs, delicate and unafraid. They walk the same loop every day, down along the tarns in the morning fog, up to the sunny rim at midday, back down to the sheltered lee at dusk. Their trails have cut shallow terraces into the moss, so the whole basin is inscribed with pale contour lines.

Rodents the size of fists burrow into the cushion plants and live inside them. On a still afternoon you can see the mounds twitch. Larger, fat-bodied marmots sit on the boulders and whistle, though the whistle is so high and thin it's almost a sensation more than a sound. Ground-nesting birds, brown and round, run rather than fly. Beetles with metallic backs cross the ice-crust in dozens. Bumblebees, heavy with fur, hum a slow low note over the flowers, the loudest thing in the basin.

Above it all, on the thermals off the sun-struck wall, a pair of huge scavenger birds circle. They have bone-white heads and wingspans wider than a man is tall. Their shadows cross the meadow like slow hands and the grazers do not even look up; they look up only for the second, smaller shadow, the fast one, that belongs to something else. Then the whole herd turns at once. Something that hunts here has learned to strike from the fog's edge, when visibility is worst. Everything alive has learned to fear the moment the fog thins.

## What People Left

The signs of people are few and old, and they are the work of hands without metal.

At the north end stands a low herder's hut, roofless now, its walls fitted from flat stones without mortar, so precisely that the joints hold lichen in unbroken lines. Beside it is a circular corral of stacked rock, the gate stones still leaning where they were dragged aside. Inside the hut's doorway, someone long ago scratched a spiral into a slab with a harder stone, and frost has since widened the groove a little at a time.

Scattered on the rim are cairns. Some are waist-high stacks of seven or nine stones, each smaller than the one beneath. Others are single upright slabs. Around a few of them, small offerings have been placed: a twist of dried grass, a pebble with a hole in it, a fragment of red pottery, a bone whistle. New ones appear now and then, though no one is seen leaving them. The herd tolerates them, and one grazer in particular is often seen nosing at the base of the largest cairn, though it never eats anything there.

## The Odd Thing

The basin has one true strangeness, and you feel it before you can name it. The cushion plants hold warmth. At dusk, after the frost has whitened everything else, the mounds stay green and dark, and if you lay your hand on one it is as warm as a sleeping animal, warmer than the air by a margin the sun alone cannot explain. Steam wisps from the biggest ones at dawn. The animals know it: at night the grazers fold their legs and lie down directly on the cushions, in ranks, breath rising in a single haze, and the herd shines faintly in moonlight like a field of low white clouds.

Herders who passed through here said the basin was breathing, and that the warmth was the mountain's, given up slowly through the roots. Whether this is true or a story told to explain a good place to sleep, no one who has lain there under a sky packed with stars, one cheek against the warm green, has been eager to argue.

## Sound

Very little. The hum of bees, the far whistle of a marmot, the tick of frost letting go of stone. Water under moss. When the wind drops, which it does for long hours, the silence has a texture, a kind of bright pressure in the ears. Then a grazer coughs, a rosette drips, and the sound seems to travel forever across the glittering green, until the fog comes back up the valley and closes it in.
