# The Drowned Shelf

At the top of the world's upper ridge there is a sea that no river feeds and no ocean tide reaches. Rain and hot springs fill it. It lies in a broad, broken plateau of fresh basalt, some sixty kilometers across, at an altitude where the air above it is thin and cold and blue. Beneath the surface the water is as warm as a bath left standing in sun.

## The Floor

The ground is young enough that it still remembers being liquid. Pillow lavas lie in heaps like sacks of grain, each with a glassy skin that has cracked in a pattern of small rectangles. Where the flows ran over each other you find ropes and folds and frozen whorls, sharp enough to cut a hand, unsoftened by any weathering except the slow chemical dulling of the warm water. Columnar joints stand in ranks along the edges of old vents, six-sided and clean, with tops still square. Between them run fissures a few hand-widths across, breathing threads of heated water that shimmer where they meet the cooler lake.

Some of these fissures have built chimneys of white silica, terraced and lipped like frozen cake batter. They grow visibly over a season. Their rims are thin as eggshell and they break under a touch, but the fragments do not fall as debris. They drift down slowly, tumbling, into the drifts of pale sand that fill each hollow.

Nothing is level. The plateau is a jumble of fresh ridges, collapsed lava tubes whose roofs have fallen into skylights, and basins that hold their own pooled colors: rust, jade, a mineral yellow, a bruised violet where mats of heat-loving bacteria carpet the stone. Where the mats are thickest the rock looks upholstered.

## The Water

The lake is deep in places (sixty meters, a hundred in the calderas) but clear, and the light reaches nearly everywhere. The sun arrives as a wobbling white coin far overhead, dropping shafts and curtains that swing slowly over the terrain. Away from the vents the water is cool and glassy. Near them it turns thick and oily, and the far side of a plume bends into a wavering mirage.

Around fifteen meters down there is a boundary. It is a layer where warm, mineral-heavy water lies beneath lighter, clearer water, and it works as a ceiling. Looking up from below, you see it as a silver, rippling sheet with the sky's bright coin blurred behind it. Swim through it and your skin feels a sudden shift, like passing through a curtain of warm breath. Below it, everything has a green-gold cast, as if seen through old glass. Above it, the world is blue and sharp.

## The Forest

Everything that can grow here does, and densely. The lake bed is a thicket, layered from the ground up.

**Reed-trees** are the tall structural plants, thick-stemmed and jointed like bamboo, rising twenty or thirty meters from anchors sunk into cracks in the lava. Each carries gas bladders, pale globes the size of a fist at the nodes, which fill with oxygen through the bright hours. The whole stand leans upward, and at the height of the day the stalks stiffen and hum faintly with buoyancy. In the late afternoon the bladders release, and strings of bright bubbles ascend in slow ropes to the surface. The stand sags and settles. This is the forest's breath, and it takes a full day.

Below the reed-trees are **veil-ferns**, translucent fronds in fans and spirals, coiled like watch springs when young and unfurling across days. Below those are **cushion-mosses** in domes of emerald and rust that trap silt and hold small floating gardens of their own. Then a slick layer of filamentous algae covers everything, in sheets that ripple in the vent currents like combed hair. Between the stems, fine nets of pale threads catch drifting matter. Pink and orange sponge-like colonies squat on the stone between them.

On the fresh glass of the newest lava the first colonizers are the strangest. **Stone-seeds** are small, dark, hard-shelled plants that root directly into unweathered obsidian and dissolve it, leaving perfectly round pits, each with a single green shoot at its center. On the older flows these pits have joined into honeycomb, and the rock there is half hollow and half forest.

## The Strangeness

The plants here keep time with each other. Every few weeks, at no obvious signal, entire hillsides of veil-fern flush from green to a deep, wet red and back over about three days, a slow tide of color that sweeps across the plateau from the vents outward. Nothing seems to trigger it. The reed-trees bloom afterward, not with flowers but with clouds of fine pale spores that hang in the water like snow that has forgotten which way is down. The clouds thicken the light to milk for a day, and then settle.

Some drifts of pale sand slowly rearrange themselves in ripples that have nothing to do with the current. They form long, regular ridges, hand-high, aligned in whorls around the vents, as if the sand were being combed by something patient. Nothing has been seen combing it. It may simply be the water, moving by rules that haven't yet been understood.

Boulders sit in the middle of open basins with wide rings of clear sand around them, as though the plants had agreed to keep their distance. The rings expand a few centimeters each year.

## The Creatures

Life that moves is rare here, and it is mostly slow. Long, pale grazers, eel-like and nearly eyeless, drift among the veil-ferns in twos and threes, tasting the fronds. There are armored, many-legged things the color of the stone they cling to, which you only see when they shift. In the upper water, once a season, a translucent sheet-like animal a body-length wide glides across the light, trailing threads, and for a minute its shadow moves over the whole forest floor like a passing cloud.

Most of the life is small: pinprick swimmers in clouds around the bladders, snails on the reed-stems, threads and mites and filter-feeders working the mats. The forest is dense, but it is mostly quiet with growing.

## The Sound

Mostly there isn't any. What there is comes from the water itself: the ticking of gas bubbles rising through the reed-thickets, a crackle of silica cooling and cracking, and now and then from far down in the caldera a long, low groan, like a door opening slowly in another house. When the bladders release at evening the ticking builds to a rain-like patter and then fades. In between the water is still, and the stillness has weight.

## The Edges

At the rim, the plateau rises into a few sharp peaks that break the surface as black islands. On them, tough, low mosses and mats of ferns cling in the wet spray, and the air is thin, cold, and bright. Beyond the rim the land falls away in cliffs of raw, unweathered rock, down toward a world of oceans and forests so distant that nothing on the Shelf knows of it. Water spills there in a few slow, thin falls that vanish into mist long before they arrive anywhere.

The Shelf is young, and it will not stay as it is. The vents are shifting, the lava is still settling, and the forest is still learning the terrain. But the changes are slow, and to anyone floating in the warm gold light, above the blooming red hillsides and the rising ropes of bubbles, they would seem not to be changes at all, only the long, unhurried business of becoming a place.
