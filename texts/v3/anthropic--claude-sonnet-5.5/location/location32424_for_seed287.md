# The Boiler Sink

You come down into it by a stair that someone cut into the wall of a collapse, and the stair is older than the reason for it. The steps are dished in the middle, worn to bowls, and each bowl holds a skin of warm water that never quite dries. Sixty meters above you the plain is dry scrub and pale dust. Here the air thickens with every step down until it feels chewed. It is a damp heat, close to blood temperature and a little past it, and your shirt gives up in the first minute.

The sink is about two hundred meters across and roughly round, with walls of yellow-grey limestone rotted into fluted curtains. Erosion here is long finished. Nothing is collapsing anymore. The walls have reached the shape they were always going to reach, all soft shoulders and rounded ledges, with weeping seams of white and rust-orange where mineral water has been laying down flowstone for longer than anyone has counted. The floor is not flat. It is a crowded terrain of mounds, terraces, and sinter basins, each one rimmed with a lip of stone as smooth as candle wax and filled with water the color of weak tea or pale jade.

## The Green

The vegetation is dense enough that you move through it as through a series of rooms. Tree ferns stand shoulder to shoulder with trunks furred in brown fiber. Under them grow broad-leaved plants with leaves like dark leather tables, and under those a carpet of liverwort and filmy moss, glistening. Everything drips. Condensation forms on the underside of every leaf, gathers, and lets go, so the whole understory ticks and patters as though a slow rain were falling, though it has not rained here in living memory. The water comes from the air itself, from steam that rises, cools against the upper leaves and the cold lip of the sink, and falls back.

Light is scarce. The opening above is ragged and partly roofed by an overhang, so the sun reaches the floor only as one slanting shaft that crawls across the sink over the day, lighting whatever it touches to a sudden brilliant green. Elsewhere it is dim and amber-green, the color of light through a bottle. The plants have organized themselves around this. The broadest leaves are turned to the shaft's path in ranks, like an audience. Deeper in the shade, pale fleshy things grow that do not photosynthesize at all, and they feed on the rot of the rest.

There is no animal life. No insects hum in the green, no birds nest in the ferns, nothing scuttles in the leaf litter. The lushness has an unattended quality, a garden with no one in it. Spores drift in the shaft of light, and no wing disturbs them.

## The Works

Scattered through the growth are the remains of an industry. Nobody lives here, and the engines are the reason it is so loud.

Pipes come first. Iron pipes as thick as a person's waist run across the floor and up the walls, sleeved in moss and crusted with orange and white. Some have been swallowed by tree ferns, which grow around them and split them along the seams. Others still carry steam. Tapped into the hot fissures at the bottom of the sink, they lead to a scattering of small buildings: a brick engine house with its roof fallen in and a fig growing out of it; a riveted tank on stilts, rusted to lace; a gantry whose top is a bird-less crown of vines; a low shed whose door is a single slab of iron, warm to the touch.

What matters is that some of it still runs. Nobody tends it, and nobody has for a long time. Inside the engine house, under the fig, a great flywheel turns, slowly, on bearings that have been fed by something (mineral seep, or the oil of some plant that has learned the trick) for longer than the wheel's makers could have imagined. Rods rise and fall. A governor spins its brass balls. Belts of woven root and old leather run out through the wall to a line of pumps in the pools, and the pumps beat.

## The Sound

The sound is the first thing and the last. It fills the sink like water fills a jar.

Steam screams from a dozen bleeds and safety valves, each at a different pitch, so that together they form a rough, unresolved chord that shifts as vents open and close. Under this lies the thud of the pumps and the long iron *whump* of the flywheel, once every few seconds. Water gurgles in pipes. Sinter basins boil quietly. Sheets of dripping condensation make a constant hush behind everything. And the plants sing. Certain broad-leaved species have grown their leaf-edges into slits along which steam passes, and they whistle, thin and clear, like a room of kettles. You learn to tell the whistling stands from the silent ones, and to navigate by ear.

You cannot talk easily. You lean close and shout, and your words come out flat and small.

## The Slow Breath

Everything here follows one long cycle, and it is the strangest thing about the place. Over roughly a day, pressure in the deep fissures builds. The vents grow louder and more shrill, the flywheel speeds up, the fern fronds flex and shiver in the hotter draft, and the water in the pools rises by a hand's width. At the peak, the sound is almost solid. Then something gives underground, with a deep exhalation you feel in the soles of your feet more than hear, and the whole sink shudders down into a relative quiet. The vents sigh. The flywheel drags. The pools sink back and leave a fresh ring of pale mineral on their rims.

Over centuries, this breath has laid the layers of sinter, and each breath adds a film thinner than paper. The engines have been drowned in it and dug out by it a hundred times. Lines of it run up their sides like tide marks.

## What It Feels Like

Nothing here is dramatic in its motion. Growth is slow, mineral deposit is slower, and the machines turn at the pace of the breath. The place feels less like a ruin than like a settled arrangement between things that don't need you: iron and fern, steam and stone. You stay long enough to feel the heat in your teeth, and you leave up the worn stair with your ears ringing and the surface air feeling thin and strange and silent.
