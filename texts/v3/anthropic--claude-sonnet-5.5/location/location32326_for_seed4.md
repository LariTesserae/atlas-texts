# The Kept Cleft

You would walk past it a hundred times. On the surface there is only a bog forest, low and black-green, standing in its own reflection. Willow-like scrub, cushions of moss the size of sleeping animals, and cold standing water in every footprint. In one hollow among the birches the moss turns to a lip and the lip turns to a slot barely wide enough for a shoulder. Cold air breathes out of it, smelling of wet stone, crushed stems, and something sweet and slightly rotten, like fruit left in a cellar.

Everything below is inside the slot.

## The Descent

The fissure is young. The ground split within living memory, and the walls still remember it. The limestone is pale and sharp-edged, with the two faces still matching. A tall person could trace a crack on the left wall and find its mirror on the right, like the pages of an opened book. Nothing has had time to round them. Where the rock is bare it cuts, and the people who use the stair keep their palms wrapped in strips of hide.

The stair is a ladder of living roots. The bog forest above sent its roots down into the opening, and they now hang in braided ropes, thick as thighs near the top and fine as hair at the bottom. Over years they have been coaxed into steps and handholds. The rungs are alive. In the cold, the bark is slick with condensation and beaded with drops. The roots flex slightly under your weight and take a moment to spring back.

Halfway down, the last grey daylight gives out. You do not so much enter darkness as find that the dimness has been deepening and is now complete.

## The Chamber

The slot opens into a hollow perhaps two hundred paces across, roofed by a ceiling of living root-mat pierced by the fissure, so the whole space is a pocket under the bog. Nothing here is large, and nothing needs to be. It is a room the size of a village green, held under a lid of plants.

There is almost no light, and what exists is biological. Pale specks glow along the hanging root-hairs, each the size of a grain of rice, a cold green-white that lights nothing but itself. These are the larvae of a gnat, strung along sticky threads. Across the ceiling they make a faint, uneven constellation that shifts when the air moves. Now and then a wet patch of moss gives a smear of blue-green glow where a luminous alga has bloomed, brightest after something has crossed it. A footprint glows for a few breaths, then dims.

The people carry almost no fire. A few tallow lamps the size of thumbnails are kept shuttered in the houses, and one hearth burns in the middle of the settlement under a stone hood. More on that below.

The floor is not soil. It is a floating mat of moss, liverwort, and sedge, a hand's depth of green over a foot of black water, and beneath that the true floor, out of reach. You walk on plant. Between the houses, planks of split reed and bundled stem lie over the mat to spread weight. The whole chamber has a slow give to it, as if walking on the back of something asleep.

Water is everywhere at a low, patient volume. It weeps from the walls in threads and sheets. It drips from the root-ceiling, each drop catching a speck of glow on the way down. It stands in the low places as black glass, and it runs in cut channels, narrow ditches lined with fitted stone that carry it in slow loops through the settlement. The air is close to saturated. Breath fogs. Fur and cloth carry a fine dew of beads. It is barely above freezing, a cold that settles in the joints and doesn't hurt until you stop moving.

## The Town

It is a town, not a camp, and a strange one: a street plan laid out by people who had no room for a street.

Its founders came down the roots after the collapse, looking for shelter from the surface winters. They found a warm, still, wet pocket full of food. Ninety winters later there are perhaps three hundred of them, spread thinly across the chamber. The houses stand well apart, each on its own island of firmer mat, joined by plank-ways. Between clusters are wide stretches of open moss, black pools, and hanging curtains of root. The settlement is a scatter of lit doorways, each far from the next, connected by lines of low glow-lamps made of gnat-thread and jelly in clay bowls.

The buildings are grown as much as built. Pole frames of willow are driven into the mat and left to root, so the walls of a house leaf out in the dark, pale-leaved and thin, and are trimmed and woven each season. Panels of peat-brick are stacked and dried on racks. Roofs are thatched with sedge over rib-bones of the big deer that the surface hunters sometimes drag down. Doorframes are cut from a fresh limestone slab, still sharp-cornered because nobody has time yet to weather it.

The town is layered. The mat rises as it grows and sinks as it floods, so older floors lie beneath newer ones. Oldest houses are now cellars, black-watered and hung with pale roots, used for storing fermented tubers and smoked eels. You can see the first generation's tools down there, flint scrapers and hammered-copper awls, green with verdigris, in mud that never dries.

There is a granary on stilts, a communal smokehouse, a meeting-house with a great living arch of roots braided overhead, a long low workshop where stone is knapped and copper is beaten cold into needles and blades, and a tannery whose smell you can find from the far side. Technology is simple: hand tools, weaving, stone, bone, and a little hammered metal from ore brought down from the surface. There is no wheel. The chamber has nowhere to roll one.

## The Fauna

There are more animals than anything else, by an absurd margin. They are small, and this is the key to the quiet.

The moss floor is alive with springtails so dense that in the glow they look like a flowing grey dust, a fine tide that parts around your foot and closes behind you. Pale isopods the size of a fingernail crawl across every plank. Translucent snails leave silver trails on the walls that the glowing alga follows and lights. Cave crickets with bodies like peeled almonds cling in clusters to the undersides of roots, and they do not chirp. The chamber's crickets have no voice. Voices are expensive in a place where sound carries a long way in still air.

Blind white fish fill the channels, so thickly in the deeper pools that the water seems to move as one thing. Salamanders the color of candle wax sit on the wet stone in hundreds, their gills fluttering like ferns. Spiders hang webs between root-strands, and the webs are seldom empty. The gnats that the spiders hunt hatch in slow constant clouds, drifting up toward the fissure and never reaching it, drawn back by some habit of the air.

Overhead, in the crevices of the ceiling roots, hang bats. Thousands. Their calls are far above human hearing, so the chamber is filled with a screaming that no one hears. When a great many stir at once you feel it only as a faint pressure in the teeth. Their droppings rain slowly onto the mat and feed the plants, which feed the insects, which feed the bats.

The people live among this the way a person lives among their own hair. Slugs are herded in pens of wet cloth. Isopods are raked into baskets and roasted. Fish are taken with woven traps, salamanders for medicine. Nobody swats anything. Every gesture is slow, because a sudden hand sends ripples of springtails skittering in a wave that takes a long while to settle.

## The Plants

The plants are not green, not mostly. Without light they have lost the habit. The root-mat ceiling is the color of old bone and pale lilac. Ferns along the channel banks are colorless and translucent, with veins visible like the ribs of a leaf held to a lamp. What little chlorophyll there is grows in the top layer of the moss below the fissure, where a dim gray finger of daylight reaches for an hour each noon, and there the green is startling, a single bright patch the people tend like a garden.

Most of the plants live on what falls. Their roots drink from the guano and the dead, and the sap that rises in them is sweet. Nectar-like beads gather on the ceiling roots in the cold, and this is what the crickets and gnats and snails come for. It is why the animals are so many. The plants are not wasting the sugar. They are paying it, and the animals are the payment's return, for the plants live on the animals as much as the animals live on them.

## What Moves

Nothing here is still for long, though the pace is slow.

**Roots grow visibly.** In a week, a tendril the length of a finger can find the edge of a plank, slide under it, and lift it. Every morning, which is a matter of habit and not light, people walk their plank-ways and cut back what has crept in.

**Roots lean toward heat.** This is the great law of the place. Roots sense warmth, and they follow it with a patient, fluid hunger. A large fire would draw the whole ceiling down like a hand. So the hearth is small, hooded in stone, fed with dry peat, and watched. The cooking is communal, and every house takes its hot coals in a covered clay pot. Children are taught before they can talk: warmth is a thing you carry carefully. A sleeping house is cold. The people sleep in heaps under sedge-felt and fur, and body heat is measured out with care, because a warm sleeper on the outer wall will wake with roots tangled in their hair.

**The water pulses.** When rain falls on the bog above, nothing happens for three days. Then the walls begin to sweat harder, the channels fill, and the black pools rise a handspan. Sometimes more. The town builds high for this reason, and the stilts have marks cut into them from years of floods. When the water is highest, the fish spill out into the streets and the salamanders climb the walls, and the whole chamber rearranges for a week. The fauna ride the water up. The people ride it out.

**The animals move in shifts.** In what the people call the long evening, the springtails rise up the walls in a slow grey tide, the bats stir and shake loose, and the gnat clouds thicken. In the long morning it reverses. The town keeps time by these tides, since it has no sun.

## The Sound

Mostly it is quiet. Water dripping, nearly constant, in a hundred different notes, the higher ones from the small drops on stone, the low ones from the big drops into pools. The faint dry rustle of countless legs, a sound like rain falling far away. The creak of a root taking weight. A voice, low, from some distant doorway, and then not.

The people speak softly and seldom raise their voices, not out of fear but because sound in the chamber carries far in the still wet air and stirs the bats. A shout could set thousands of them dropping from the ceiling in a shivering fall. So the culture has grown around whispering and touch. They tap out messages on the planks. They hum. Their one instrument is a long reed pipe played so gently that it sounds like wind from another room.

## The Feeling of It

The place is small but does not feel small. Hidden, sparse, and dark, it gives the impression of a great cathedral reduced to the size of a thought. You stand on the floating floor with the pale roots hanging around you, the constellation of gnat-light overhead, the glowing footprints fading behind, and the slow, numberless movement of the small things at your ankles. Somewhere a house door opens a little, and a thread of amber light lies across the moss. A figure crosses it carrying a covered pot of coals, walking carefully and slowly, and the roots above turn, very slightly, to follow.
