# The Boiler Hollow

You would walk past it. From the marsh road it is one more low green swell in a country of low green swells, with the same sedge, the same sheen of standing water between tussocks, and the same haze. The only sign is a sound, which at first you take for a distant works: a pumping, a ticking, a long steam-note held on one pitch. Most people assume it comes from somewhere else and keep walking.

The hollow is about forty paces across. It sits in a fold of the lowland, barely below the water table, so the water is always a hand's depth from everything. The rim is a lip of old limestone, worn so long and so evenly that it has no edges. It is rounded and pocked like a knuckle, and every surface is either moss or wet. The rock weeps. Seams in the stone bead and run in threads, and the threads have cut the same grooves for so long that the grooves have grooves. The water is warm, bath-warm where it touches your hand, and the whole hollow holds a heat that never leaves. The air is thick and sweet and tastes faintly of iron and rotting fruit.

## The Roof and the Floor

Nothing here is bare. The floor is a raft of living mat: sphagnum, root-felt, and the stacked dead of a thousand generations, all bound into a springy skin that floats on black water. Step and it sinks a finger's depth, then pushes back. Under it the water is tea-colored and deep enough to lose an arm in. Over it, the plants go up.

The tall growth is sparse, and the hollow is better for it. Perhaps thirty great plants stand in the whole space, each with room around it like a machine in a hall. They are *stacks*: hollow-stemmed giants, pale green banded with rust, as thick as a thigh at the base and rising twice a man's height. Each ends in a flared, hooded funnel of leaf. Their stems are chambered like bamboo, but the chambers are valved. Marsh gas collects from the rotting mat below and pushes up through the stem, and the warm air in the hood draws it. When enough pressure builds, the valves flutter open and the plant sings.

It is not a pretty song. It is a reedy, brassy blare, like a ship's horn heard through a wall. Thirty stacks are each tuned a little differently by stem length and hood shape, and each fires on its own slow cycle of about nine seconds. The result is a rolling, overlapping chord, a factory floor of breath. Between the blasts there is a ticking. Lesser plants like hollow ferns and knuckled horsetails have tiny bladders that snap open and shut, so the whole understory clicks like cooling metal.

The chord has never changed. The stacks are old and the heat is constant. The gas comes up at the same rate it did before anyone counted, and the note that startles you on entering is the same note that has sounded here since the limestone rounded off.

Over everything, a canopy of broad drooping leaves closes the hollow from above. Some are as wide as tabletops, glossy and dark, and the light that reaches the floor is green-gold, warm and steady, with no drifting shadow because nothing above moves.

## The Machinery of Animals

The fauna is abundant, and it works. That is the strangest thing about the place. Everything in it seems to have a shift.

Along the weeping rock, colonies of armored snails move in single file, each dragging a ribbon of mucus, and the whole procession grazes the algae film in even, parallel sweeps, never overlapping, like a mowed field. Above the water, mayfly-like insects hang in the warm air in fixed ranks, wings whirring at a hum you feel in your teeth. Their bodies are copper-green. They hold their positions for hours, and when a dragonfly cuts through, the ranks close behind it like water.

The frogs are the loud ones. They are small, the color of wet slate, and there are hundreds, each on a private station. A frog sits in a crook of moss and does one thing: it pulses a hard, mechanical *chk-chk-chk*, like a ratchet being wound. Their calls interlock into a rhythm as dense and regular as a loom. Under the stacks' horns, under the clicking plants, the frogs add a grinding bed of texture. The hollow is loud enough that you cannot hear your own footsteps, and after a while you stop noticing that you can't.

In the black water live fish that hum. They are long, eyeless, and faintly silver, and each carries an electric organ along its flank. They lay a low field into the water, and in the dark of the deepest pools you can see tiny blue-white sparks jump between the fish and the roots. The roots glow faintly where the current touches them. When you drag a hand through the water, you get a fizzing tingle, not painful, like a mild static. The current runs from fish to root to fungus, and the mat is threaded with pale conductive strands that carry it. Nothing here has ever needed a wire.

At the base of the stacks there are burrows in the moss, and out of them come beetles the size of a thumbnail with wing cases like polished bronze. They carry beads of resin from the stack-stems and pack it into the cracks in the limestone. It looks like maintenance, and in a sense it is: the beetles seal leaks, and the sealed stone holds the heat. If you follow one, you will see it return to the same crack it sealed yesterday, where the resin is smooth and amber and older than you.

## Rules of the Place

The hollow keeps a few laws, none of them written down.

**Heat stays.** The limestone bowl and the thick mat hold warmth like a banked furnace. Fog never fully forms, because the air is always a degree too warm to give up its water, so the hollow hangs in a permanent hovering damp.

**Nothing new arrives.** Seeds fall, insects hatch, frogs age out, and each is replaced by another of the same kind in the same place. The population is a standing wave. Over a thousand years the count of stacks has stayed near thirty, because the gas can feed no more and no fewer.

**The sound is the structure.** Because the chord is so constant, the animals have built their lives around it. The frogs tune to the stacks, the beetles work to the pulse, and the mayflies hold their ranks at the interval between blasts. If the horns stopped, the whole ecology would lose its clock. But they have never stopped.

## Leaving

If you climb out over the rim, the sound drops away behind you within a few paces. The limestone soaks it up and the surrounding sedge muffles the rest. On the marsh road, a hundred steps on, there is only a faint pulse, a ticking, and a long steam-note that you might take for a distant works.

You will likely not turn around.

Below the moss, in the warm dark, the hollow keeps running.
