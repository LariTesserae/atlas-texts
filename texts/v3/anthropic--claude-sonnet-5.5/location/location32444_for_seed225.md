# The Bowl of Lit Salt

You come to it by walking across ordinary, flat, brown country and then not being able to walk any farther. The ground ends in a lip of pale rock, and below it the world continues, only lower. The basin is about two hundred paces across and sunk perhaps sixty feet, roughly round, with walls that lean out at the top and in at the bottom, like a bowl someone pressed a thumb into and then left for ten thousand years.

## Walls

The walls are limestone gone chalky, laced with veins of gypsum and salt. They are pale, almost the color of the inside of a shell, and they hold light instead of absorbing it. At midday the sun drops straight in and bounces from wall to wall until the floor is lit from every direction. Nothing there casts a proper shadow. You stand in a soft glow that has no source, your feet ringed by only a faint gray smudge. Your eyes ache a little and keep wanting to squint at something that isn't there.

The rock has been worked by water so long that it has settled into its shape. There are no fresh breaks and no rubble slopes, just rounded ledges, scalloped hollows, and long vertical flutings like drapery. Each fluting is the path of some ancient trickle that has since found other routes. The weathering is finished, or nearly. The bowl is what it has decided to be, and the changes now are slower than seasons and smaller than a hand.

## The Weeping Band

Damp is the one thing the basin has, and it has it in a strange way. The air below the rim is warm and still, and the rock is cool from the deep water behind it. Each night moisture condenses on the north wall. By dawn a dark, glistening band a few yards tall shines across the pale stone. As the sun climbs, the band retreats up the wall and thins, then is gone by mid-morning. The next night it comes again. Over the year the band drifts slowly around the circumference, following the tilt of the sun, so that on any given day only one arc of the wall is wet.

This is the clock of the place, and everything alive keeps to it.

## Floor

The floor is a hard, cream-colored pan of clay skinned with salt. It is nearly empty. Across two hundred paces you might count a dozen features: a low hummock of crusted mud, a fallen slab the size of a door, a stunted tree, and near the center a shallow pool, more a wet stain than a body of water. The pool is barely ankle deep and shaped like a kidney. Its rim is scalloped with pink and white salt terraces no taller than a fingernail. The water is warm as blood and tastes of iron and old coins.

The salt on the pan grows into polygons, big plates a stride across with upturned edges, cracked in patterns that repeat exactly from one end of the floor to the other. Walking on them makes a small dry sound, *tick*, like a knuckle on a wooden bowl.

## Growing Things

Vegetation is sparse and stubborn. On the damp arc of the wall, in cracks and on ledges, grow low cushions of grayish-green succulent, each a tight dome the size of a loaf. They open their leaves only while the band is wet and close them into stones as it dries. Between the cushions is a thin fur of black-green algal crust that comes alive in the early hours. It turns a startling emerald for perhaps forty minutes, then fades back to soot.

The one tree is a twisted, silvery thing rooted at the edge of the pool. It is old and low, its trunk as thick as a thigh but no taller than a person. Its leaves are narrow and coated in salt crystals, and they chime very faintly when the air moves. Its roots have cracked the pan around it into a star.

## The Abundant Ones

For so empty a place, the basin is full of animals. They just prefer to be elsewhere than the middle of the floor.

The walls are inhabited. Pale snails with thin, translucent shells graze the wet band, thousands upon thousands of them. They travel upward with the retreating damp, then seal themselves to the stone with a rim of mucus and hang there through the bright hours like beads on a wall. From the floor the cliff seems studded with pearls. At night they descend and feed again. Their trails, worn over centuries, have etched faint silver lines in the rock, a pattern like a huge, slow fingerprint.

Small swifts nest in the pockets higher up, a dark cloud that pours out over the rim at dawn and returns at dusk. Lizards the color of the stone hunt the snails. They are so exactly matched to the wall that you see them only when they move, and even then you may take them for a flicker in your eye. There are also beetles with mirrored backs, which fold their wing cases against the glare and walk across the salt in straight lines as if pulled by a thread.

## The Strange Part

The snails do something. At the height of noon, when the wall is dry and brilliant, they begin to turn. Slowly, in a wave that starts on one side and passes around the circle over an hour or so, each snail rotates its shell a quarter-turn on its mucus seal, so that the opening faces the sun. The whole wall glints, and the light goes out from it in a coordinated pulse. Afterward the birds behave differently for a while, circling low, silent, as though listening to something.

No one has shown what the pulse is for. The lizards seem to fear it. The salt tree rings a little louder in that hour, though there is no wind.

## Made Things

There are a few signs that people have been here, though few and small. In the wall beside the pool is a hollow ground smooth as a bowl, with a hand-sized stone lying in it, a grinding place. Near the fallen slab are three cupped rocks set beneath the drips of an old seep, their edges worn to satin. They were arranged by hands, but there is no wall, no path, and no ring of stones, only the quiet reuse of what the place already offered. Sometimes you find a scraper of chipped flint or a bone awl in a niche, tucked back as if to be found again. Whoever left them took what they needed and did not stay.

## Sound

Mostly quiet. What you hear is small: the *tick* of salt underfoot, the faint chime of the tree, the rustle of a lizard, the soft, endless drip from the seep into the stone cups. The drips fall at slightly different intervals into cups of slightly different depth, so the pattern never quite repeats. Once a day, in the late afternoon, wind crosses the rim and the whole bowl sounds one low breath, a note like blowing across the mouth of a jar. It lasts a few seconds. After it the silence feels larger.

## Slow Turnings

Once a year, sometimes less, clouds bank up against the horizon and rain falls. For a day the pan turns to a sheet of mirror-water an inch deep, the walls stream, and the snails lift their shells like tiny lanterns. Then the sun returns and the bowl slowly gives the water back to the sky. The salt regrows its polygons a little differently each time, and over the years the pool shifts a hand's width toward the east wall.

Nothing hurries here. The place is weathered, warm, and bright, and it keeps its own patient count of days.
