# The Struck Basin

You are underwater, and the water is very clear.

It is a lake, though "lake" undersells it. It sits in a bowl of new rock nearly four kilometers above any sea, and it is the whole world here. Eighty meters overhead the surface is a wrinkled sheet of pewter and white, lit from above by a sun that has crossed a lot of thin air to reach it. The light comes down in slow shafts and comes down *hard*. Nothing here is murky, because nothing has had time to make it so. You can see the far wall, six hundred meters off, as a dark blue-black edge in the distance.

## The Walls

The basin formed recently, by geological reckoning. Something beneath it emptied and the roof fell in, and the fall left no soft edges. The walls are stacked columns of dark basalt and pale, glassy rhyolite, broken along clean planes. Corners are still corners. Fracture faces glint where they haven't yet grown their skin. The rock is pale at the fresh breaks and rust-black where the water has begun to stain it, and where ledges have collapsed, the talus lies in angular heaps exactly as it landed. Sunlight lands on the shallow terraces and shows every conchoidal scar.

Nothing green grows anywhere. The light is plentiful, but nothing here has learned to want it. The walls are colonized instead by mats of pale, cold-loving microbes, orange and cream and the gray of wet ash. They spread in slow tongues across the stone, following the seeps.

## The Floor, and the Engines

Down where the light thins to a steady blue, the floor is crowded with chimneys.

They are not built. Hot mineral water rises through cracks in the lakebed, meets the cold, and drops its load, and over decades the load stacks into towers. Some are as thin as a wrist, and some are the girth of a barn. They stand shoulder to shoulder in a dense stand, and their bases merge into terraces and shelves. The chimneys are white, yellow, and a deep oxblood, stained by iron and sulfur. Their tops are ragged and always warm.

What makes the place strange is that they work like machines.

Many of the tall chimneys have grown flaps of mineral near their crowns, thin curved plates hinged on a ridge of crystal. Vent water builds pressure beneath a plate until it lifts and releases a burst of hot fluid with a *clack*. The plate falls, seats, and the pressure begins to build again. A hundred chimneys do this, each at its own period, and the water carries the sound to you as a hammering that fills your chest: thock, thock-thock, a deep tick, a wet bang. Seen from the far terrace, the field is a factory floor. Steam rises in pulses, the beat is layered, and there is a thick rhythm under everything, though no one designed any of it.

The rhythms are not random. Neighboring chimneys share plumbing beneath the floor, and the pulses pull one another into step, slowly, over months. Across the field you can watch waves of synchrony creep along the rows. One section beats like a single great piston, then loses phase and dissolves into chatter, then gathers again. The basin has a slow tide of sound, and the sound is loud enough that the water hums in your teeth.

## The Production

The chimneys also produce, in quantity. Dissolved metals meeting cold oxygenated water precipitate at once, and each vent plume sheds a constant, glittering snow of pyrite flakes, iron-oxide dust, and thin sulfur films. It falls in long curtains, catching the light, and settles on the terraces in layers. Where the sediment lies thickest it has fused into bright metallic sheets that curl at the edges like peeling foil. The floor is a slow, continuous manufacture of mineral plate, and it never stops.

## The Crowd

Animals live here in a density that makes the water feel packed.

**Ratchet worms** line the chimney flanks in fur-thick colonies, each a pale tube as long as a finger with a red plume at its mouth. They feed on the microbial mats and retract when the flaps fire. A whole slope of them snaps back in a rolling wave, a fraction of a second behind each bang. Because they respond to the machine, they seem part of it.

**Lantern shoals** fill the mid-water: silver-bodied fish the length of a hand, in schools so vast they shade the floor beneath them. They turn all at once, and the flash of the turn is visible from two hundred meters. They eat the mineral snow's bacterial film, mouthing the falling flakes as they drop. Their gill covers are faintly metallic, dusted with iron.

**Bell-grazers** are translucent, soft-bodied discs the size of dinner plates, pulsing lazily among the chimney tops. They harvest rising heat, tilting into warm plumes and coasting out again. Their bodies hold a cluster of small hard grains, like gears in a watch case, which they rotate to tune their buoyancy. Hundreds drift above the field like a slow mobile.

**Drum-crabs** are heavy, flat-shelled, with one oversize claw each. They stand on the warm terraces and strike the mineral plates with the big claw, deliberately, at intervals. Whether this is territorial, courtship, or simply feeding on the flakes they knock loose, no one is around to say. Their taps sit inside the chimney rhythm like a second, smaller machine.

**The long ones** are rarer. Slender, eel-shaped, four meters or more, they move through the chimney forest in loose spirals, brushing their flanks along the warm stone. Their bodies are ringed with faint bands of blue light. When they pass, the ratchet worms flinch in a line that traces the animal's path like a finger drawn across a screen.

## Rules of the Place

- **Temperature is layered.** Water at the chimney crowns is nearly hot. Ten meters up it is cool, and at the surface it is bitterly cold. Every animal lives on a gradient and sorts itself by preference, so the crowd is stacked in bands.
- **The water turns over slowly.** In the cold season the surface chills and sinks. A long, gentle overturn moves down the basin over weeks and stirs the pale sediment into a glittering haze. Then the water settles and clears. This is the only slow tide the basin has, and the animals shift their lives around it.
- **Sound rules the geography.** Sound carries in the dense, still water, and the animals steer by it. A worm colony far from any flap is a starved colony. A fish that strays beyond earshot of the field will turn back.
- **Nothing is finished.** The rock is new, the chimneys grow a few centimeters a year, and the flaps are always cracking and regrowing. Over the years the field will rearrange itself, and the rhythm that greets you now will not be the rhythm of a decade from now.

## What You Notice Last

Looking up from the floor, you see the whole basin at once: the chimney forest steaming in slow pulses, the shoals turning like sheets of foil, the bell-grazers hanging in the light, and the wall behind it all, angular and unweathered, going up into blue. The surface above is a pale, trembling ceiling. Above it is thin air and a hard sun, and a wind you will never feel.

Down here it is loud, bright, and crowded, and everything moves to the same slow, hammering pulse, which nobody built and nobody conducts. The pulse simply runs, the way a heart does.
