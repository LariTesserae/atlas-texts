# The Tabletop of Laminae

The plateau is a high place, though nothing up here knows it. Beneath it the seamount falls away for three thousand meters into black water. Above it lie a hundred and eighty meters of cold sea, and above that a surface that is only a rumor of brightness. The light that reaches the top is the gray-blue of old slate. It has no direction. Shadows pool under things without being cast by anything.

Everything you can stand on, or hover over, was made by something alive.

## The Floor

The plateau is a single sheet of secreted stone, laid down layer over layer across some unreckonable span. Cut a section and you would see laminae thin as paper, ivory, ochre, a dull violet, each one the residue of a season, a bloom, a catastrophe. The builders' ancestors began it, and their descendants keep adding to it. Time has planed the whole surface nearly flat, like a table sanded for a million years. The old ridges and vaults are worn to low swells. Shallow basins, wide as fields, hold drifts of pale silt that the currents comb into long, feathering dunes.

Almost nothing rises more than a few body-lengths. This is not a landscape of towers but of remnants. Here and there the stumps of huge older structures stand as blunt, fluted stubs. Their cores are hollow, their walls scalloped by ten thousand generations of grazing mouths, so that they look less like ruins than like teeth.

Between them lies wide, empty pavement, cold to the touch and slick with a film of gray-green bacterial felt. This is the nearest thing to a plant on the plateau: a thin, patient mat that feeds on the seeps of chemical-rich water leaking from hairline cracks. The felt is not forest or meadow. It is a stain that happens to be alive.

## The Water

The water is 2 degrees, clear as glass in the still moments and never still for long.

The plateau sits in the path of great slow currents, and twice a day the whole sea above it leans one way and then the other. When it turns, the change is not gentle. A slack minute, then a shudder runs through the water, and a wall of silt lifts off the dunes and rolls across the table in a brown, boiling front. Internal waves, invisible swells riding the boundary between colder and warmer layers, break against the plateau's edge like surf against a cliff. They fling the water upward in sheets and fill the twilight with tumbling grit. In an hour the dunes are rearranged and the basins are refilled. Whole reaches of the floor are buried and others scoured bare.

Sometimes a storm passes overhead, far above, and its pressure comes down in pulses. The water breathes in and out against the skin. Loose shells rattle across the pavement, and the colonies clamp shut in unison, a sound like a thousand small doors.

## The Builders

The builders are sparse. That is the surprise, given how much they have made. You may travel a long way over bare stone and see nothing living but felt and drifting flecks. Then a cluster appears, and it is dense as a hive.

**The wrights** are pale tube-dwellers, each a soft, ribboned body no longer than a forearm, extruding a chimney of hardened mucus and mineral. They grow in stands, and their tubes fuse as they lengthen into ragged colonnades and thickets a few meters high. When the current turns, their crowns open in fans of translucent threads to sieve the water. The tubes are the plateau's only real verticals, and they are always half-ruined. The same surges that feed the wrights also snap them by the hundreds.

**The sheet-weavers** are flat, mottled things that creep across the pavement laying down membranes of slime that cure into thin, brittle roofs. Under these roofs whole galleries form, low and lightless, tunnels of pale glass propped on wright-stumps. Small fish and pale crustaceans shelter there. Then the surge comes, the roofs crack and lift like ice on a river, and the weavers begin again.

**The unmakers** are the big grazers, slow bulk-bodied animals the size of draft horses, armored in overlapping plates, with rasping mouths along their undersides. They move over the works and eat them. The stone goes into them and comes out as a fine pale mud, extruded in ribbons. The wrights and weavers use that mud as raw material. So the plateau is a circulation: build, gnaw, excrete, rebuild. Nothing is finished, and no structure is more than a few generations old, though the floor beneath them all is ancient beyond counting.

Around these larger lives swarm the small ones. There are clouds of amphipods like blown ash, translucent shrimp, and eel-things that thread through the tubes like needles through cloth. Shoals of eyeless silver fish turn together with a sound like a tearing sheet. Above the plateau in the twilight drift jellies, ribbed and slow, trailing a faint cold fire.

## Light

At the top of the water the gray is dim but constant. Down in the galleries and hollows, the animals supply their own. Lures and glands flicker in slow blue-green pulses. In the moments after a surge, when silt is thick in the water, every suspended particle catches these glimmers, and the plateau seems briefly to be a field of low, drifting stars.

## Sound

The plateau is mostly quiet, and the quiet is textured. There is the low hum of water moving through wright-stands, each colonnade tuned by the length of its tubes, so that on a steady current the plateau gives off a faint, uneven chord, a slow organ played by no one. There is the tick and rasp of the unmakers feeding, and the crackle of shrimp in the tunnels. And every so often, from the deep water beyond the edge, comes a single long groan, a note so low you feel it in the sternum before you hear it. Nothing on the plateau has ever been seen to make it. All the animals go still, and then resume.

## The Rules

The plateau has a few laws you learn by watching:

- **Nothing built here stands through a full turning of the seasons.** The surges see to it.
- **Nothing built here is lost.** All of it is eaten, and what is eaten is made again.
- **The floor is the only constant.** It is not built anymore, only maintained, and the animals seem to steer around its oldest seams as though they knew.
- **The groan is followed by a great surge within the day.** Whatever makes it, the animals treat it as a warning, and they brace.

At the edge, the pavement ends in a lip of fractured stone. Past it is nothing, just the dark and the falling and the cold, wide sea. From there you can look back across the tabletop, gray and scarred and trembling, all of it made by hands that were never hands. A cloud of silt is rising far away, a wall the color of tea, coming slowly across the stone toward the small pale colonnades that are about to be broken and begun again.
