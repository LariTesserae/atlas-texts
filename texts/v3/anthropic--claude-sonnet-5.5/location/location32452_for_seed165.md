# The Held Note

The plateau sits so high that the sky above it is the color of a bruise at midday, a violet so dark the brightest stars are sometimes visible beside the sun. The sun is there, low and swollen and the color of weak tea, but it gives light the way a lamp gives light through a wet sheet. Everything is legible and nothing is sharp. Shadows are soft-edged smears. The light has been at this angle for as long as the place has existed, so the dawn-colored haze on the eastern faces of the rocks has never been followed by anything else.

## Ground

The tableland is broad enough that walking across it is measured in days, and it is not flat. Long, low swells of gray-brown scree roll away in every direction, worn round by a very long patience. Nothing here is jagged. The peaks that stand above the plateau, shouldering up another few hundred meters, have the softened look of old teeth. Their ridgelines have slumped into gentle saddles. The whole region has reached the shape it was going to reach and has stayed there.

The rock is a dark, fine-grained stone, slate-blue where wet and ash-colored where dry, and it splits into flat plates that ring when stepped on. Between the swells lie pans of frost-sorted gravel arranged in rings and polygons, each stone graded by size from a pale core of pebbles to a dark border of cobbles, as if the ground had been combed. The patterns are perfectly regular and have never been disturbed.

## Water

The air is thin, cold, and damp. Fog does not drift here. It *lies*, a flat white lid at about knee height in the hollows and shoulder height on the rises, with an upper surface so level and constant that you can walk with your head above it and watch the peaks stand out over a white floor, or crouch below it and lose the world. It never lifts and never thickens.

Everything beneath it is beaded. Rime furs the windward side of every stone in fine white needles, always the same length. Seeps weep from the bases of the slopes and gather in black tarns rimmed with brown moss. The tarns are still and cold, and the surfaces are skinned with a thin, clear ice that never quite thickens and never quite melts.

One thin river runs through the plateau, and it is wrong. It rises out of bare gravel with no spring, no seep, no seam in the rock, simply a channel a hand deep that begins to be a stream between one step and the next. It runs for about four kilometers, clear and fast over slate cobbles, then ends the same way, flowing into flat gravel with no sink and no pool. The gravel at the end is dry. The gravel at the start is dry. Nobody has found where the water was before or where it goes after. The river is one of the few things here that moves, and it moves the same way at every hour.

## Sound

The plateau is loud, and this is the first thing it does to a visitor.

A single tone fills the open space, a low, wide, sustained note somewhere between an organ pipe and a struck bell, sounding from no direction. The wind starts it: air pulled across the mouths of ten thousand hollow stones, the fluted vents in the scree, the slots in the cliffs. But the wind is not steady, and the note is. It has not wavered in pitch or volume in all the time anyone or anything has listened. Stand in the fog and it seems to press on the chest. Climb above the fog and it seems to come up from below, through the soles of the feet.

Over the note lie smaller sounds, and they are also constant. The river's chatter arrives clearly at distances where it should be inaudible and is silent when you stand beside it, so that you have to kneel and watch the water to be sure it is running. Drips from the rime fall on stone in irregular rhythms, but the rhythm is always the same, and after a day you could hum it. Occasionally there is a crack like a rifle shot from far off, a stone splitting in the frost, and then the same crack again, and again, at the same interval, from the same place, though nothing is left there to split.

## What is wrong

The rules of the place have come loose and settled that way.

Distance does not agree with itself. A cairn visible on the next swell takes an hour to reach one day and forty minutes another, though the days are identical and the walking the same. This is not because time or space has changed. Both have simply learned to give different answers to the same question, and the answers are stable. The hour and the forty minutes are both always the case.

Shadows do not agree either. Near the tarns, a standing stone may throw one shadow east toward the sun's dim direction and a second, fainter one to the north, and both are cast by the same light. Neither shadow is illusion or reflection. They are two true shadows of a single object in a single light, and the stone accepts this.

Sometimes a tarn can be seen from two sides at once, from one bank and from the far bank, though you are standing in only one place. The far view is the one you would have if you were standing there, and it includes you, small, in the dim light, looking back. When you walk around the tarn, the figure walks around toward where you were, and you never meet. Someone else's footprints are in the frost on the far bank, and they match yours.

Stones near certain ridges fall slowly, taking a full minute to drop a body's length, and rise again. This is not a pattern of rising and falling. It is a stone that falls upward and downward at once, and the frost polygons beneath it are worn in a perfect circle.

None of this changes. That is the strangest part. The impossible things are as settled as the erosion, as old as the fog's lid, and the note that hums beneath them all. It is a broken world in equilibrium, a fracture that has healed over and become the ground.

## Life

Vegetation clings to the sheltered sides of stones: cushion plants like tight green fists, crusts of orange and gray lichen, and pale hair-thin grasses in the lees. The cushion plants are centuries old and grow a fraction of a millimeter a year. Around the seeps the moss is thick and rust-brown, and in it live springtails and pale mites that glow faintly under the dim sun. A cushion plant is seldom a hand's breadth from another.

Animals are rare. A small hare-like creature with blue-gray fur and enormous ears lives in the scree, and it is deaf, or has learned to be. It does not react to the note or to the cracking stones. It grazes the cushions in the fog and vanishes down its burrows when a shadow crosses it, whichever shadow. A large dark bird with a wingspan wider than a person sometimes circles above the fog line, never landing, never calling. Those who have watched it say it circles the same circuit, always in the same time.

## Marks

The built things are few, old, and partial. Cairns of flat slate plates stand at odd intervals, each stacked with care and topped with a single white quartz stone. They may mark a route, though no route between them is visible. Here and there are low curved walls of fitted stone, waist-high, forming half-rooms open to the fog. In one basin stands a single small hut with a roof of slate slabs and a doorway facing the sun. Inside are a stone bench, a hearth of black-scorched rock, and a floor swept clean. There is no dust, and no one has been seen to sweep it. Above the door a row of scratched tally marks is carved into the lintel. Count them twice and you will get two different numbers, and both will be correct.

Whoever built these things did so with hands and stone alone, and left. The note still runs through the hut's chimney gap, a little lower in pitch than outside, as if the house were humming along.

## Standing in it

To stand on the plateau is to be inside a long, held breath. The light does not change. The fog does not lift. The cold has the same edge at every hour, the river keeps its four kilometers, and the note fills the space between the peaks. Nothing is sad, and nothing is waiting. The place has become exactly what it is, contradictions and all, and has held that state so long that the contradictions have become its ordinary laws.
