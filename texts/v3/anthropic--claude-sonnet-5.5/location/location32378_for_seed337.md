# The Settling

The plain here dropped. Nobody alive saw it happen, but the grandmothers' grandmothers told of a night when the ground below the old grazing country sank a hundred and fifty paces, all at once, in a single long groan. What remains is a basin about a day's walk across. Its rim is a wall of raw pale stone, and its floor is a city.

The rock is still new. The cliffs of the rim show fresh fracture faces, sharp-edged and unweathered, the color of bone and wet slate. Nothing has had time to round them. Where the old ground tore, ledges hang at angles no long-standing landscape would allow, and talus slopes lie in loose, glittering fans that still shift and tick in the afternoons as they settle. There is no soil to speak of, only crushed rock, grit, and the pale silt that the seeps bring down. Every surface has an edge.

## Water

Water is the maker of this place. It weeps from the rim, from a hundred hairline fractures at the contact where the old aquifer was cut open. It comes in threads, films, and slow sheets, never in a torrent. The water is cold and heavy with dissolved lime, and wherever it slows it leaves a little of itself behind. Ridges of travertine grow in the channels. Terraced pools build their own rims, lip by lip, each holding a shallow skin of water the color of milky jade. Curtains of flowstone thicken down the cliffs a finger's width every few years.

The people of the basin learned early to work with this. They weave willow and reed into frames and set them in the seeps. In a season the frame is a crust, in five years a wall. Their houses are half made and half grown. A family's dwelling is a core of stacked stone and fired brick, sheathed in lime deposit that they coax with diverted trickles and scrape back with bone tools where it would close a doorway. Nothing stays the size it was built. Doors narrow, window slots seal, stairs gain a stair's worth of thickness in a lifetime. Every household keeps a scraping-day each spring, when the whole street works at opening what the year closed.

Rain comes now and then, gray and fine, falling into the bowl and mostly running off the roofs into the channels. Fog is more common. It pools in the basin each dawn, cold air sliding down the rim and holding there, and the city wakes inside a white ceiling that the sun burns off by midmorning. Condensation beads on everything. Metal is always faintly wet. Wool never entirely dries.

## The City

Density is the rule. The city has no plazas to speak of, only places where a building failed and hasn't been replaced yet. Houses lean on houses, roofs of slate and hide-stretched-over-pole carry the foundations of the next tier, and the lanes between are barely a shoulder wide, often roofed over so that a walker passes through long tunnels of lime-crusted timber and stone with the sky showing only in slits. At the lowest level the lanes are also drains, with a thin stream running down the middle over stepped travertine. At the higher levels are catwalks of lashed poles, ladders worn smooth, and rope handrails black with hands.

Perhaps forty thousand people live here, and they take up every usable level surface. The terraces climb the lower rim in tiers, each ledge fitted end to end with dwellings, workshops, granaries, and cisterns. Even the slopes of loose scree have been pinned with retaining walls and built onto.

Their technology is hand-scale. They have copper and a little bronze, stone hammers and wedges, fire-hardened digging sticks, kilns, looms, and pottery wheels turned by foot. They cut the fresh rock by heating and cracking it, and they pry it along its still-sharp joints. Wheels are rare, since the lanes forbid them. Loads travel on backs, on poles between two carriers, and on rope slings down slick chutes. Craft is inherited and specialized: the scrapers, the frame-weavers, the water-readers who walk the rim at dawn and judge which seeps are rising and which are failing.

## Growth and Ground

The land is still becoming. Fresh cracks open in the floor now and then, and the water-readers mark them with red cord. Some go dry within a season. Others start to seep, and a new lime-fan begins. Now and then a small slump takes a terrace edge, and the city absorbs it without much talk, rebuilding upward or sideways. Wall-cracks are patched with lime paste and ash. Because the whole basin is still adjusting to its own collapse, there is a low, irregular ticking in the stone at night, like a house cooling, which the people find comforting.

The dead are laid in the flowstone seeps on the western rim, wrapped in cloth and reed, and the water covers them in a few decades with a translucent, glazed crust. Families walk that stretch of cliff and touch the shapes.

## Living Things

Vegetation is sparse and specialized. Green is confined to wet places: cushions of moss in the seeps, ferns in shadowed crevices, filmy algae on the flowstone in shades of rust and emerald. Lichens cover the older walls in orange and grey. The city grows what it can on roofs and ledges: flat beds of tubers in imported soil, trays of mushrooms in damp cellars, a few crooked fig and juniper trees kept alive on the upper terraces and treated as heirlooms. Wood is precious. It comes down from the rim country in bundles, and the smell of a woodfire is a mark of status.

Animals are few. Swifts and rock doves nest in the cliff crevices and stream out over the fog at dawn. A small number of goats are kept on the upper ledges, tethered, and bring their own thin bells. Pale salamanders live in the cisterns and are protected, since they signal clean water. A few thin dogs slip through the lanes. At dusk, bats leave the higher caves in a slow ribbon. Beyond these, the basin is nearly empty of wild life. It is too new, and the ecosystem has not yet filled its shape.

## Sound and Light

Ordinary daylight reaches the floor from midmorning to mid-afternoon, flat and clear, bouncing off pale stone and lime. In the evening the rim throws long shadow early, and lamps of fat and reed light the lanes one by one, small yellow points climbing the terraces.

The city is quiet for its size. Muffled by damp and by thick walls, its sound is the drip of water into cisterns, the fine scrape of bone on lime, a chisel's tap, wet sandals on stone, low talk behind shutters, and the goat bells far overhead. Loud noise is rare and slightly rude. Over everything runs the sound of water finding its way down, unhurried, adding a little to the world with every drop.
