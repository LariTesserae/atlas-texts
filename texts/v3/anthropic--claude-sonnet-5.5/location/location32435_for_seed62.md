# The Cordillera of Standing Wires

## The Ground

The land was pushed up recently. The plateau sits about three thousand meters above a sea nobody here has seen, and the uplift is still fresh enough that the ground has not learned to be gentle. Fault scarps run across the highland in long, clean lines, some as tall as a house, their faces still showing the pale, freshly broken rock of the day they slipped. The rock is a fine-grained grey-green volcanic stone that splits into plates and blades. Ridgelines are serrated. Gullies are narrow, steep-walled, and V-shaped, cut by only a few thousand seasons of water. Nothing has had time to round off.

Between the scarps the land opens into broad benches and shallow basins, each several kilometers across, stepped down like a staircase for something enormous. You can stand on one bench and see three more below you, and the ridges of another range, blue and hard-edged, on the horizon. The scale is open but never empty. Everything you can see is textured.

## The Green

The plateau is covered in plants so densely that the rock shows only along the scarps and in the stream cuts. This is a high, cool, bright country, and the vegetation has answered it with a layered, upholstered abundance.

At the top are the rosette trees, thick-trunked and slow, their stems wrapped in a skirt of their own dead leaves. Each carries a crown of silver-furred blades like a hundred cupped hands, and the fur is so fine that the light stays caught in it. Some stand three times a person's height. They grow in loose groves, close enough that the crowns almost touch.

Below them is a shrub tier of tough, small-leaved bushes with waxy undersides, red-brown in new growth. Below that are cushion plants, domes of tight green like stone dressed in velvet, some wide as tables. They are so firm you could sit on one. In their crevices grow mosses in a dozen colors: lime, rust, bruised purple, a sharp orange that seems lit from within. Lichens hang from every branch in long grey-green beards. Every surface holds a second surface, and every gap holds something smaller.

The air is thin, and the light is nearly unfiltered. It is bright to the point of hardness, with a blue-white clarity that gives every leaf edge a pin-sharp outline. Shadows are cold and deep-colored. The plants have adapted with pigment, wax, and fur, and so the whole landscape glitters faintly, as if frosted, even at midday when the temperature is a mild, cool ten degrees.

## Water

There is little rain, and it falls as a few brief showers of fine, cold droplets in the afternoons, more mist than storm. The plateau is lush anyway, because it drinks from the air.

Each night, cold air drains off the high ridges and pools in the basins. Moisture condenses on everything. By dawn every blade, wire, and spider strand is beaded. Through the morning a low bank of cloud, a slow white tide, rises from the lower country and spreads across the benches, then thins and burns off toward noon. The tide is the rhythm of the place. Anything that can catch it does: fur, wax, cupped leaves, the beards of lichen.

Small streams run in the gullies, clear and cold, fed by drip and seep rather than by rivers. They gather in still, dark tarns, some no bigger than a room, held in basins of broken rock and ringed by cushion plants. The water in them is so clear it looks like empty space.

## The Machines

Scattered across this country is the old industry, and it is the strangest thing on the plateau.

It was built for the fog. Long ago, engineers strung the benches with **condenser lattices**: ranks of tall steel pylons carrying fine wire screens that catch the cloud tide and, by electric charge, pull its droplets down into collecting troughs. The system is electrified through and through. Transformer houses of dark riveted iron stand at the foot of each pylon line. Copper bus-bars as thick as arms run along the ground on ceramic stands. Above the basins, catenary cables sag between towers in long, patient curves, beaded with dew.

There is no smoke and no engine noise. The works run on static potential and slow ion drift, and they seem to be still running, or to have started running again. The wire screens carry a faint charge. If you hold a hand near one, the hairs on your arm rise and your skin prickles. Drops on the wires do not fall but creep sideways along invisible gradients. A faint blue glow crowns the pylon tips at dusk. It is not sound, and it is not quite light, but a pressure at the edge of feeling.

Other works are scattered among the lattices, each alone:

- **A rectifier hall**, a tall brick-and-iron building with arched windows, its roof sprouting rosette trees, its interior a cathedral of dark cabinets and glass insulators furred with moss. The insulators still hold a charge, and the moss on them grows in tiny branching lightning patterns.
- **A foundry stack** on a bench by itself, a fat brick chimney with lichen lines running up it like tidemarks. It has no building anymore, only a floor of cracked slag glazed green.
- **A narrow-gauge rail spur** that runs a few hundred meters from nowhere to nowhere, with a single ore car left on it, so overgrown with cushion plants that it looks like a long green loaf.
- **A winding house** at the lip of a scarp, where a great iron drum still holds a cable running down into a fault crack. Nobody knows what it lowered, or raised.

The built things are far apart. But each is so densely overgrown and so wound into the vegetation that it feels less placed on the land than grown out of it.

## The Strangeness

The plants have learned the wires.

Around each pylon line, the vegetation has grown into alignment with the electric field. Rosette blades tilt toward the conductors like iron filings toward a magnet. Moss grows in the direction of the flow. Whole groves lean in the same direction. From a high point, the benches show great, faint, curving patterns in the plant cover, the field lines of a system that has been quietly running for longer than anyone remembers. The landscape reads like a diagram of its own electricity.

The dew, too, behaves strangely near the works. Drops rise a few centimeters off leaf tips before falling. In the hour before dawn, in the basins with the most wire, a thin haze of charged mist stands in perfectly straight vertical columns, unmoved by wind, like the pillars of a hall.

Also, the plateau makes no sound. This is not merely a matter of stillness. The thin air, the deep upholstery of moss and cushion, and the fog-damp all swallow noise. A dropped stone gives a soft, muffled tick. Your footfalls disappear into the ground. Your own voice sounds small and close, as if spoken into cloth. In the deepest groves, you can hear your pulse.

## Creatures

Life is rare, and it keeps out of the way. Small brown rodents with dense fur move through tunnels in the cushion plants, and you see only the dents they leave in the moss. A few pale-bodied moths cluster on the warm casings of the transformers, wings opening and closing slowly, feeding on something in the residual heat. Once in a while a single dark bird crosses the sky, high, gliding without a wingbeat, and it makes no sound either. In the tarns there is a small translucent crustacean, visible only when the light catches its gut.

Nothing here hunts loudly. The animals seem to have adopted the plateau's manner.

## Time

Change is slow. The fog tide rises and falls on its daily schedule, and the seasons move by degrees, a slight warming and a slight cooling, without much drama. The fault creeps a few millimeters a year, so that a fence line built across it long ago has parted by a hand's width, its wires stretched into a quiet, singing line (felt, not heard). The rosette trees grow perhaps a finger's length per decade. The moss on the insulators advances by a hair each month.

The wires stay charged, the vegetation keeps leaning, and the cloud comes up every morning to be caught. The whole system runs on patience. Nothing is broken, and nothing is finished. The place is a machine and a garden at once, still cooling from the moment of its making, in a light so bright and a silence so complete that walking through it feels like walking through the inside of a held breath.
