# The Terraces of Aun Selle

## The shape of it

The plateau sits about three thousand meters above any sea. It is a broad, tilted shelf of white stone, forty kilometers from its upper lip to its lower edge, and the whole surface is stepped. Each step is a terrace, a shallow basin rimmed by a curved lip of mineral no higher than a knee or a house, depending on where you stand. Water fills each basin to the brim and spills over into the next. The terraces are not the work of any hand. They are travertine, laid down over a hundred thousand years by hot spring water that gives up its dissolved stone wherever it slows, so the mountain has built its own staircase, and it keeps building.

There is no soil anywhere and nothing rooted. The stone is white, cream, and rust-orange, with veins of sulfur yellow near the vents and a green-black at the deep pool edges that is not a plant. From a distance the whole slope looks like frozen milk, or a stack of dishes set out to dry.

The air is hot, near the temperature of blood on a cool day and hotter near the vents. It is also wet in a way that has nothing to do with rain. Every surface either sweats or steams. The sky is a hard white-blue, and the glare off the pale stone comes from below as much as above, so shadows are faint and everything is lit from all sides at once. Travelers wear slit visors of dark glass, and the residents' faces are permanently creased at the eyes.

## The water

The water arrives from below. It rises through faults in the shelf at the upper lip, where the highest vents breathe. Near the source it is nearly too hot to touch and a clear, unnerving blue. As it descends it cools, and the color changes with it. The vent pools are turquoise. Below them the terraces go milky jade, then a wide band of orange and rust where the microbial mats thrive, then pale yellow-green, and at the lowest edge, where the water is barely warm, a smoked gray.

The water is supersaturated. This is the central fact of the place, and everything else accommodates it. The dissolved stone is held in the water in a state of false calm. A disturbance can end that calm: a sharp vibration, a sudden pressure pulse, a dropped tool, a raised voice, a struck bell. Where the disturbance touches, crystal blooms at once. A rime of white needles shoots across the pool in a second or two, spreading in a fan and thickening into a crust that will hold a person's weight by evening. It happens quietly. The only sound is a faint ticking, like sleet on paper, that runs outward and fades.

This is why the terraces are quiet, and why the silence is not a custom but a piece of engineering.

## The silence

Sound is discouraged everywhere on the shelf and forbidden near the working basins. The air is thick and warm and swallows what noise there is, and the wet stone deadens footsteps. Beyond that the world has arranged itself around stillness. Nothing here shouts or calls. The animals have no voices; they communicate by color, posture, and slow rhythmic vibration through the film of the water, which is a risk they seem to have learned to manage, and which goes unspoken in the food chain.

What sound remains is small. Water sheets over the lips of terraces with a sibilant whisper, a sound like a long held breath. Vents exhale with a low soft hiss. The crystal ticks. Drops fall from the undersides of stone ledges, one every few seconds, into pools so still that each ring can be watched from origin to edge. If you stand for a while you begin to hear your own pulse.

## The city

There is a settlement here, and it is large in extent though sparse in form: Aun Selle, a chain of works and dwellings spread thinly along the shelf like knots on a cord. There is no center. There are perhaps forty thousand people, spread over three hundred square kilometers, and the distance between one cluster and the next can be an hour's walk across white stone.

The buildings are made of cut travertine. Because the mountain grows, the builders quarry directly from the newest terrace edges, where the stone is soft and easy to saw and hardens when exposed. They cut it in long pale blocks with wire saws driven by slow electric motors. The buildings are low, thick-walled, and rounded, with deep eaves and few windows. The ground floors are cold and damp; the upper terraces catch a little breeze. Stairs and ramps are everywhere, and the whole town is a slow slope of them.

Because the water never stops depositing, the town is under constant slow burial. A doorway carved this decade will be half-sealed in fifty years. Old quarters near the upper terraces are sunken, their lower stories entombed in banded stone, their roofs now level with the ground. Newer quarters are built above them, so that the city is layered, a stack of eras, and beneath the walkways you can sometimes see lamps or window frames sealed in translucent rock like insects in amber. Some streets are rivers of shallow water over old paving. Some are dry, cracked, chalky floors of dead terraces, where the springs have moved on and the stone has dulled to dust-white and crazed like old glaze.

## The industry

The industry is electrified, precise, and above all gentle. Nothing is allowed to bang. The works are:

**The brine mills.** Long low sheds straddling the mid-terrace channels, where water is drawn off and evaporated in cascading trays of thin glass, leaving salts, carbonates, and sulfur, which are shipped out by cable. The sheds hum at a frequency chosen to be below the threshold that provokes crystallization. Workers describe it as a pressure more than a sound.

**The condenser fields.** Because the air is saturated, water can be harvested from it. Rows of black-glass plates, tilted at the sun, sweat clean fresh water out of the steam and channel it to cisterns. This is the drinking water; the terrace water is undrinkable, heavy with minerals. On the cool afternoons when mist forms, the fields glitter in long ranks, and the whole plateau seems to be crying.

**The stone yards.** Here the travertine is sawn, planed, and polished by slow wire and belt machines lubricated with water, running quietly. Finished blocks are moved on magnetic rails that make no sound, gliding just above their beds. The rails are a marvel of the region; their builders say they were originally developed just so that carts would not clatter.

**The salvage shops.** Older equipment, when it fails, is not scrapped but allowed to be overgrown. Machines left by a channel are entombed by mineral in a year or two, their gears frozen in white lace. Some are deliberately left as foundations. There are engines in the bedrock of Aun Selle that have been built into it, like fossils that once worked.

Power comes from the earth. Steam from the vents drives turbines set in insulated pits, and the plateau is wired with a lattice of thick cable laid on the stone and strung between white pylons. From the high ridge you see the pylons marching down the slope, spaced far apart, glinting.

## Signals

Since voices, bells, and whistles are not used, the town communicates by light. The glare that plagues visitors is put to work: every district has heliograph stations, hand-polished mirrors on pivots, which flash coded messages down the slope from terrace to terrace. Signals are read against the glare by trained watchers who wear no visors, and whose eyes have gone a pale, filmy gray. Lamp signals take over at night, in long amber and green flashes that ripple down the stair-stepped slope like a slow pulse. Households hang small colored flags for family news.

## Life

Fauna is abundant, which surprises visitors given that nothing green grows here. But life doesn't need leaves. The base of the ecology is the microbial mats: layered films of heat-loving organisms, orange, rose, green, and brown, that coat the shallow terraces in bands of color according to the water temperature. They are photosynthetic and chemosynthetic both, feeding on light, sulfur, and dissolved iron. They grow in slick wet carpets and ruffled curtains, and their pigments make the terraces look painted.

Grazing on them are the **plate-eaters**, flat, low, hard-shelled animals the size of dinner plates, pale as the stone, that slide across the mats in herds of thousands. They move with a rolling, muscular glide, and they scrape the mat with rasping mouthparts. At a distance a herd looks like a patch of stone that is slowly moving. They are silent, and when threatened they clamp down and go still, becoming indistinguishable from the rock.

Above the mats hover **veil-flies**, clouds of midge-like insects with wings that beat without sound, since their wings are membranes that flex rather than buzz. They swarm the mat-edges in shimmering sheets, feeding on the film and laying eggs in the warm shallows. The clouds catch the light like drifting mica.

Feeding on the plate-eaters are the **wading stalkers**: tall, thin-legged animals, hairless, pearl-colored, with narrow heads and long jointed necks. They stand for hours in the cooler pools with their bodies perfectly still, then strike, a sudden lunge that ought to shatter the water but doesn't, because they have evolved a way of entering the water so smoothly it barely makes a ripple. This is a trait they share with the people. Both have learned the same lesson.

Beneath the mats, in the warm shallows, live **sheet-lurkers**, flat translucent predators that lie under the skin of the film and take whatever crosses above them. They can only be seen as slight ripples in the colored bands, like thumbprints drifting.

In the hottest vent pools, the ones that scald, nothing large lives. There are red threads of worm-like things, and there is a pale shrimp-like creature that gathers around the vent-mouths in drifts and moves in synchrony, so that the pool seems to breathe. They are the source of the local legend that the mountain has a pulse.

The people keep some of these animals. Plate-eaters are herded down the terraces to graze the mats off machinery in the brine mills, and their shells are used as decorative tiles in doorways. They are also, in small numbers, eaten, cooked in steam pits, and said to taste like sweet mineral butter.

## Strangeness

There are things about the place that don't resolve neatly.

Some terraces grow *upward* in slow spires, thin needles of stone, brittle and white, that rise from the pools like candles. These are called "listening stones." They grow only in places where the water has been silent for many years, and they are believed to be very sensitive; when one is touched or spoken near, it may collapse, and where it falls it can trigger a whole basin to crystallize at once. Old people in Aun Selle whisper that the stones grow toward voices and are drawn to conversations. No one can say why the old people believe this, but children are taught never to whisper near a spire.

Some pools are perfectly clear and perfectly still, and show a sky that is not the one above them. At certain hours a viewer sees a different arrangement of clouds, or a darker sky with stars, or a light where no light should be. Scientists call this a refraction effect off dense warm layers. The locals call these pools "second sky" and place offerings of shell beside them.

Every so often, at intervals no one has quite worked out, the whole shelf goes white in an hour. A vent surge pushes fresh supersaturated water across the terraces, and the mats bleach in advancing lines, and the air fills with a soft fine rain of crystals that fall like snow in the heat. The people call it "the Whitening." Work stops. Everyone goes indoors to the cold ground floors and waits, silent, for the crystal shower to pass, and afterward the mats slowly recolor themselves over days, orange creeping back from the edges.

## Weather and rhythm

The day has a clear shape. Dawn is sharp and clear, the air thin and gold-white, and the terraces steam heavily as the night's cool meets the warm water. By mid-morning the heat is oppressive and the glare is at its worst; the town goes quiet even by its own standards, and people move only in the covered stairways. In the afternoon, mist rises from the upper terraces, gathers into flat-bottomed clouds against the cooler ridge, and by late day releases a warm, brief, heavy rain. It falls in straight lines with almost no wind. Rain makes a sound here that everyone else in the world would find ordinary, and it is the one time the town is loud: a soft, dense drumming on the stone roofs that no one minds, because it is gentle enough not to disturb the pools. People come out and stand in it, faces up.

Evening brings a long amber light that turns the white terraces pink and gold. Night is warm and bright with stars, and the vents glow faintly from the mineral light-organisms in their waters.

## Edges

Downslope, the terraces become ever older and drier. The lowest tiers are cracked plates of gray stone, wide fields of fractured rim and dead basin, with only a few thin channels of water still reaching them. Here the town thins out. There are abandoned works, whole blocks of stone-yards fused in place, cable pylons leaning at angles, the plate-eaters' shells scattered in drifts like broken crockery. The silence is different here, emptier, a silence of absence rather than restraint.

Upslope, past the highest vents, the shelf ends at a black rim of volcanic rock, sharp and dry, where steam pours out of cracks in ragged white columns. Above it the air is thin, and the ridge falls away to a wider, harsher country no one in Aun Selle has much reason to visit. From the rim you can look back down the whole plateau: the stepped white slope, the jeweled bands of color, the thin lines of pylons, the slow flash of mirrors, and the tiny pale herds drifting over the mats like clouds' shadows, all of it lying in the heat and the glare, and none of it making a sound.
