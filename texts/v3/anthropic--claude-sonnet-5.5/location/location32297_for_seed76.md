# The Tableland of Slow Tides

Imagine a plateau so large that walking its width takes a season, and at that height the sky is a darker blue than you expect, the sun hard and white. The land has been here so long that nothing on it is sharp. Old mesas have worn down into long, rounded shoulders. Ridges sag into saddles, and cliffs have collapsed into aprons of gravel that were later smoothed into slopes. The stone is pale, part gypsum, part sandstone, part something chalky and fine-grained, and it has the soft gleam of very old bone. Where it shows through the green, it is polished by wind, fluted and pocked like the inside of a shell.

There is almost no liquid water here. No rivers cut the plateau, and no lakes lie in its hollows. The dry channels are real, but they are ancient, wide and shallow and softened by moss-like growth, and they carry nothing but wind. A traveler could die of thirst in the middle of the greenest country they had ever seen.

## The Breath

Everything here is organized around one event, which happens most mornings.

Below the plateau's edge, on the far lowlands, lies a cloud sea, a floor of white that fills the basins overnight. Around dawn, as the sun warms the rock, the air begins to climb the escarpments. The cloud rises with it, first as threads in the canyons, then as a slow white wall pouring over the rim, and finally as a low fog that drifts across the tableland for an hour or two, sometimes three. It rarely condenses into rain. It touches, and the land takes what it needs.

The soil is rich in salts that pull moisture from the air, pale crusts and grainy blooms that darken and go damp when the fog arrives and pale again by midday. The plants have arranged their lives around this. Most of them hold their water in the cold hours and spend it in the bright ones.

By noon the fog has burned off, the air is clear enough to show mountains a hundred kilometers away, and the light falls almost without shadow-softness. The green stands in the glare, and the day is cool.

## The Green

The vegetation is lush in the way a tapestry is lush, dense in texture and varied in form, though not tall. Most of it is under the height of a person, and it grows in hummocks and drifts and slow rivers of color.

**Cushion-mounds** are the dominant form: domes of tight, blue-green rosettes, some a hand across and some the size of a house, packed so densely you could lie on them. Their surfaces are covered in fine, silvered hairs that catch the fog as beads and channel it inward. In the bright hours the hairs glitter. The oldest mounds are thousands of years old, and their centers have died and hollowed into dark, sheltered bowls where other things root.

**Comb-reeds** stand in stiff fields, thin stalks the height of a shoulder, each ending in a fan of hair-fine filaments angled into the prevailing fog. When the Breath arrives, the fans darken with moisture and the whole field shifts color, from dusty gray-green to a wet, deep emerald, in a wave that travels across the hillside a few seconds ahead of the fog itself. Watching from a ridge, you see the color arrive before the cloud does.

**Sheet-lichens and crust-mats** cover the bare pale rock in orange, rust, and a strange lilac. On the flat pavements they form large rings, slowly widening outward over centuries, and the ring patterns overlap and interfere like ripples that stopped mid-motion.

**Lantern-trees** are the rarest and strangest plants, and the only tall ones. They rise from the deep saddles where old soil has gathered, with trunks smooth and pale as birch, crowns flat and wide, and leaves that are thick, cupped, and vertical. The leaves turn slowly through the day to keep their edges to the sun, so that at noon the whole crown seems to be looking away from you. At dusk the cups tip upward and collect the first frost, and by morning each one holds a small pale coin of ice.

Between all of these there are blanket-meadows of a fine, springy grass-like plant that is neither grass nor moss, in olive and amber and a bruised violet that shows at the tips in autumn. The meadows ripple in the wind, and the ripple takes the same shapes as the hills beneath.

## Terrain

The plateau is not flat. It rolls in enormous swells, each one many kilometers long, with the gentleness of a very old sea frozen mid-motion. Rounded domes of exposed stone rise from the green like the backs of half-buried animals. Some are polished so smooth they reflect the sky.

In places the erosion has left tall, strange shapes: stone pillars worn narrow at the waist and wide at the head, standing alone in the meadows like sentinels. They are not tall by the scale of the land, perhaps ten or twenty meters, and each is coated in lichen up to a precise line, a height above which nothing grows. That line is the usual level of the fog.

Along the plateau's margins the land breaks into terraced escarpments, long stepped slopes where different bands of rock have weathered at different rates. From the edge you look down onto the cloud sea, and it stretches to the horizon, lit gold or white or pink depending on the hour. Islands of dark peaks stand out of it. Sometimes a second, higher layer of cloud rides above, and you are between them, in a corridor of clear, bright air.

## Weather of Its Own

The tableland is large enough to make its own climate. Air rises over its broad heated surface in the afternoons and draws winds inward from every direction. These winds meet somewhere in the middle, in a long convergence line that wanders from day to day, marked by drifting seed-fluff, pale dust, and the slow spinning of small dust-devils, which here are gentle, columns of pollen and salt-glitter that rise and spiral and dissolve without hurry.

Dry lightning sometimes walks the far interior in the late season, silent from a distance, with pale threads between high, flat-bottomed clouds that never rain. Fires do not follow; the mats are too damp with salt-held water to burn. What follows instead is a sweet, sharp smell, like struck flint and crushed herbs, that drifts for kilometers.

Nights are cold and clear. The temperature drops close to freezing, frost feathers over the cushion-mounds, and the sky is thick with stars, thick enough to cast faint shadows. The frost is the second source of water, and the lantern-trees' cups fill with it. By mid-morning, it's gone.

Autumn brings the **color wave**, in which the violet at the tips of the meadow-plants spreads slowly downward and the whole plateau, over about a month, turns from green to bronze to a deep wine, then back again in the spring. The wave travels from the interior outward, following the drift of the cold.

## Sound

The place is quiet, but not silent. The dominant sound is the wind moving through the comb-reed fields, a dry, papery hiss that rises and falls with the gusts. In the meadows there is a softer noise, a rustle like distant rain that comes from the fine stems brushing each other. When the fog arrives, the sound changes: the papery hiss gives way to a muffled softness, and every step on damp salt-crust makes a quiet crunch.

Seed cases of one small shrub dry and split in the midday heat with tiny, distinct ticks, so that hillsides sound faintly as if they were being tapped by many fingers. Occasionally, high on the pillared stones, wind crosses a hollow and produces a single low, sustained tone, soft as a breath over a bottle. It comes and goes. Nobody, it seems, is meant to hear it.

There is no birdsong and no running water. In the stillness after the fog, you can hear your own pulse.

## Life That Moves

Animals are rare, and you may cross a whole day's distance without seeing one. What lives here is adapted to the long dry hours and the short wet ones.

**Stilt-grazers** are the largest: tall, narrow-bodied animals with long legs and pale, dust-colored hides, moving in loose groups of five or six across the meadows. They graze the cushion-mounds' edges and walk slowly. At the arrival of the Breath they stop, lift their heads, and lick the fog-beads from their own forelegs and from each other's flanks. They never seem hurried, and they are not often afraid.

**Veil-moths** emerge only in the fog. They are large and gray, with wings like folded paper, and they drift among the comb-reeds pollinating the fans as they go. When the fog burns off they vanish. Nobody quite knows where they spend the bright hours, though some are found dormant in the hollowed centers of the oldest cushion-mounds, pressed into the dead heart like pages in a book.

**The stalker**, a long, low, patient predator, gray-furred and slow-blinking, lives in the saddles among the lantern-trees. It hunts the stilt-grazers' young, but rarely, and it can go weeks without a meal. It is more often seen sleeping on the warm stone domes than hunting. When it moves it does so in short, exact bursts, then stops as if it had forgotten.

Smaller things live in the mats and mounds, including beetles that harvest fog-beads on their raised backs and tip them into their mouths, and spiders that spin nets of nearly invisible thread across the hollows to catch moisture as well as prey. Their webs, in the morning, are strings of light.

## What Makes It Strange

Three things set this place apart from ordinary high country.

The first is the **synchrony**. Nearly everything alive here keeps time with the Breath. Before the fog arrives, an hour before, the cushion-mounds tighten and the lantern-cups tilt and the stilt-grazers turn their heads toward the edge. The change is subtle and unexplained. Something in the air, or in the ground itself, tells them.

The second is the **memory in the stone**. The pale rock is banded, and the bands, seen in cliff faces, show thousands of thin, regular layers, each one a year of fog and salt. In some places the layers curve in slow patterns that look, from a distance, almost like handwriting. The plateau has been writing itself down for a very long time.

The third is the **stillness of scale**. The place is so large, and so slow to change, that standing on it you get a peculiar feeling of being inside something patient. The processes here are active, with fog rising, plants shifting color, and animals moving, but they are all gentle, cyclical, and unhurried. Nothing is being built, nothing is being lost. The land has reached a state it can keep.

Late in the day the sun drops toward the cloud sea and lights it from within, a great glowing floor of amber. The cushion-mounds turn copper, the pillars throw shadows a kilometer long, and the frost begins to form in the lantern-cups. Then the light thins to violet, the first stars come out, and the plateau grows quiet in the cold, waiting to be watered by the morning.
