# The Ossuary Plateau

The land here was finished a very long time ago. What remains is the stub of a mountain range that wore itself down over more years than the rock has names for. It now stands as a broad upland almost three thousand meters above the sea, and it is nearly flat. The old ridges survive only as long, low swells, like the backs of sleeping animals. Between them the ground runs off toward a horizon that keeps receding as you walk.

The rock underneath is pale granite, rotted so deeply that in most places it has become a coarse, glittering gravel, quartz and feldspar in grains the size of lentils. Here and there the rot failed, and hard knuckles of bare stone push up through the gravel as rounded domes, boulder heaps, and balanced stones. Every edge on them has been softened to the curve of a river cobble, though no river made them. Nothing is sharp anywhere. The whole country has the smoothness of a thing that has stopped being shaped.

## The Living Floor

You cannot see the ground for the animals.

The gravel is roofed by a living crust of grey-brown, coin-sized, shell-backed creatures called plate-grazers. They sit shoulder to shoulder in scales, in overlapping layers two or three deep, like slates on a roof. Each has a ridged, faintly concave carapace that channels moisture toward its mouth. Together they cover the plateau in a slowly rippling armor. When you step, they don't scatter. They clamp down and go stone-still, and the pressure of your foot passes through the layers with a soft crackling, like walking on dry cereal. A few seconds after you lift your boot the crust unclenches and the dents fill in.

Under the plate-grazers lives a second population, pale, blind, many-legged things that feed on the grazers' droppings and moulted shells. They move in the dark, damp seam between crust and gravel. Under those live threads of fungus, and under the fungus is the substrate itself: centuries of shell, bone, and casing, cemented into a chalky, layered soil. It gives underfoot the way old plaster does.

Above the crust there is a third stratum, and this one is the strange one. Tall, hollow, pale-stemmed animals stand in the crust like reeds. Each is a colony of tiny creatures fused into a tube as long as your forearm, open at the top, swaying almost imperceptibly. They are not plants, though they occupy the plants' place. They filter the air. On still afternoons they lean slightly toward the wetter side of the sky, and the whole meadow of them tilts together, a slow agreement among thousands.

The density is total. Wherever you look closely, at a crack, a crevice, the underside of a stone, you find more life than surface: crusts on crusts, wings folded on shells, tiny bright-eyed things nested in the vacated bodies of larger ones. Nothing is empty. The place is not noisy with it, though. The fauna here has settled into a patience that reads as hush.

## Water

Rain is rare. It comes perhaps a dozen times a year, in warm, straight-down showers that last an hour and are gone. The real water arrives in the evening as fog.

As the day's heat begins to fade, a slow bank of mist climbs the plateau's edge from the lowland forests far below. It doesn't blow in. It simply thickens out of the air, first as a haze in the hollows, then as a low white tide that rises to about knee height on the plate-grazer crust and to head height in the deeper depressions. It is warm fog, close to blood-temperature, and it smells of wet stone and something faintly sweet, like crushed almonds. It beads on everything. Within an hour every carapace is dripping, and the ridges on each shell draw the water toward mouths that open in the dark like tiny, patient hands.

By dawn the fog has burned off, and the plateau glitters, then steams, then dries to a matte grey. This is the daily cycle, and it is the pulse of the country.

In the lowest basins, where the old bedrock is dished, a few standing pools persist: shallow, tea-coloured, rimmed with pale crust. Nothing in them is free-swimming. The water is thick with a green-black felt of algae, and the felt is grazed by the plate-grazers, who queue at the pool edges by the hundreds, in rings, each ring a little more crowded than the last.

## Sparse Green

Vegetation exists but does not lead. Small, stubby, bottle-shaped succulents grow in cracks in the granite domes, each the size of a fist, grey-green and waxy, fat with stored fog. A rosette plant, ragged and leathery, clings to the sheltered sides of boulders. Orange and yellow lichens spread on the sun-facing rock in slow, concentric maps, some of which are older than any nearby structure.

A few trees stand alone in the hollows: gnarled, wind-shaped, no taller than a person, with bark like cracked leather. Their trunks are so completely covered in animals that they look furred. Each bears a slow, dense weight of shells and pale tubes, and the branches bow under it. Some trees have died beneath the load and stand as armatures of life, hosting more animals than they ever hosted leaves.

## The Marks

People or something like them were here once, or perhaps still are, in ways that leave no trace but the marks.

Scattered across the plateau are small stone structures, few and far between. There are roofless huts of piled granite, each one round and no bigger than a small room, the stones stacked without mortar and fitted so closely that no gravel shows between them. They are entirely occupied now. Their floors are heaped with shells and shed casings, and their walls are furred with the pale tubes. Nothing in them is disturbed by your approach.

There are cairns on the higher swells, each a knee-high pile with a single flat stone laid across the top. Some are freshly clean, as though lately handled. Others are buried so deeply in the crust that only the capstone shows, like a table in a flood.

In one place a line of stones, set upright at intervals of about a dozen paces, runs straight across the swells for several kilometers before it vanishes under the crust. Whether it is a path, a boundary, or a calendar, no evidence remains. The stones are worn smooth at chest height, on their downhill sides only, as if generations of animals had rubbed there. Or hands.

Near the center of the plateau stands a single slab taller than a person, grooved from top to bottom by countless fine parallel lines. On its leeward face the grooves are filled with a bright, glassy resin that the plate-grazers apparently secrete there and nowhere else. On foggy evenings the slab glows very faintly, a low amber, and the grazers gather at its foot in a dense, silent semicircle.

## Sound

The plateau is mostly quiet. The sounds that exist are small and dry and come from close by. There is the crackle of the crust under any weight, and the soft ticking of shells settling as the day cools. There is a faint, constant rasp, like distant sandpaper, that is the sound of ten million mouths scraping fog-film off stone. On the calmest evenings a low hum rises from the colonial tubes as air moves across their open tops, a chord that changes pitch as the fog thickens and fades as it burns away.

Rarely, and only at dusk, something deep in the substrate makes a single hollow knock, like a fist on a wooden door. It is answered a long moment later from somewhere far off. Nobody is sure what makes it, and nothing seems to react.

## The Slow Rules

Everything here is slow, and it is dangerous to be fast. A quick movement makes the entire crust go rigid around you, and the rigid crust can pinch, tearing skin and boots. A person who walks smoothly and evenly passes through it like a ship through a calm sea. Those who hurry find the ground closing on their ankles.

The plateau is rising. The granite underneath finished eroding long ago, but the living layers keep building. Each generation of shells falls, is compressed, and becomes floor for the next. Measured over centuries, the surface climbs a few finger-widths, so slowly that the stone huts have been swallowed to their lintels and the older cairns have vanished entirely. The ground of this country is not rock. It is the accumulated remains of everything that has ever lived here, and it is still adding to itself.

At a great enough distance, the tilt of the tubes, the ring of grazers around the pools, the glowing slab, and the daily fog all seem to be parts of a single, very slow sentence, one the land has been writing since before the mountains wore down and will still be writing long after anyone who reads it has gone.
