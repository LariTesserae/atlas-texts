# The Throat of Kessarine

Above the last cloud, where the air has thinned to a cold, mineral-tasting whisper, a mountain range is still being pushed upward. One of its shoulders split open in living memory. Where it split there is now a hollow about the size of a village square, a fresh wound in the summit ridge. Locals call it the Throat, because everything in it seems to be swallowing or singing.

## Shape and Stone

The Throat is a crooked bowl roughly two hundred paces across, walled by black basalt that cooled into columns and was then cracked apart along its joints. The columns are hexagonal and pentagonal, some as thick as a barrel, some as thin as an arm. Their broken ends are still knife-edged, with no lichen rounding them and no dust softening the corners. Fallen columns lie in stacked drifts on the floor like spilled cordwood, and the gaps between them are the true architecture of the place: a dense, layered warren of dark slots, tubes, and chambers, running down through the scree for the height of several men.

Overhead, a slab of the ridge leans across two-thirds of the opening and shuts out most of the sky. What remains is a crescent slot of thin, star-hard blue by day and black by night. The light that reaches the floor is the color of old pewter, and in the deeper crevices it is nothing at all. Most of the Throat is in darkness. Anyone here learns it with their hands and ears.

The rock is cold enough to ache through leather. Its surface is faintly slick with condensation, and in the lower pockets a skin of black water gathers, never more than a hand deep. Meltwater from the ice cap above comes in thin threads and slow drips along the overhang. It falls in the dark, and you hear it long before you find it.

## Water

The water here is intermittent and patient. Some days there is only the damp: breath-fog on the columns and a rime that beads and vanishes. Now and then a thin cold rain falls, or sleet slides sideways through the slot. Most of the wet arrives as condensation. Warm air rising from vents in the deep rubble meets the chilled ceiling stone, and the whole overhang sweats. The drops fall in irregular rhythms, each landing on a different note depending on what it strikes: a tin ping on a hollow column, a wet slap on moss, a deep *tock* into a sump.

Four tarns sit in the floor, none larger than a threshing floor. They are black, cold, and very clear, ringed with a pale crust of mineral. Each has a slightly different taste: iron, chalk, a faint sourness like unripe fruit, and one that tastes of nothing and numbs the tongue.

## Growth

Vegetation is sparse and stubborn. Orange and grey crust lichens cling to the sun-facing edges of the slot. Down in the damp there are cushions of dark moss, tight as fists, and a wiry grass that grows in single tufts from cracks and looks like something combed. The one conspicuous plant is a pale, waxy rosette the width of a plate. It grows only in the seep-lines, and its leaves are faintly translucent. Each rosette shelters a small ecosystem of its own.

None of the plants make food enough to explain the crowd that lives here. The mountain feeds the Throat from outside. Wind lifts insects, seeds, pollen, and dust from the lowlands, carries them up the slope, and drops them into the still air of the bowl like snow. What falls is caught, collected, and eaten by something.

## The Fauna

The Throat is overwhelmingly, obscenely alive. In the dark it is almost too alive. Every surface belongs to somebody.

**The chorus-beetles** are the most numerous: black, wedge-bodied, the size of a thumbnail, wedged in the joints between columns so thickly that the rock looks upholstered. Each one has a ridged plate on its back that it rubs against the stone. Together they sound like a vast, low grinding, a hundred thousand tiny files. The pitch drifts with the temperature. In the cold hours it sinks to a growl you feel in your sternum. In the warmer hours it climbs to a hiss like frying fat.

**The lanternwrens** are small, round birds with almost no tail, feathered in grey and rust. They nest in the tubes between columns, hundreds of them. They are loud, with piercing, whistling calls that ring and multiply in the stone pipes. Their throat feathers carry a faint blue-green luminescence, probably from something they eat. In the deepest dark, a bird calling looks like a small cold lamp flaring and dimming with each note. At dusk the whole bowl is speckled with these pulses, like sparks in a chimney.

**Glass-crawlers** are pale, translucent amphipod-like creatures that swarm in the tarns and the seep-runnels. They are so thick in the water that a dipped hand comes up coated. Their swimming makes a soft continuous rustle, like paper being shuffled.

**Rope-moths** are grey, slow, and large, with wings like frayed cloth. They hang in the hundreds from the underside of the overhang, and when they let go in clouds their wingbeats push the air in soft, silent pulses against the face.

**The stilts** are the great predators of the place: pale, long-legged, wingless creatures, the size of a hunting dog, half-lizard and half-heron in their look. They walk the tops of the fallen columns on thin spread toes, heads low, listening. They hunt by sound, and the din should make that impossible. They manage it by matching the chorus, moving only when the beetles are loudest, and freezing dead still in the pauses. A stilt at rest is nearly invisible: a slender pale shape among the pale mineral crusts.

Everything is packed close. Beetles overlap beetles, wrens sit wing to wing in their tubes, and mites crowd on the moths. In the crevices, life is stacked in layers, and a torch thrust into a gap reveals seven or eight kinds of creature within an arm's length, all utterly still until the light touches them. Then the whole wall ripples.

## Sound

The Throat is loud, and the loudness has structure. There is the grind of the beetles, the whistle-pulse of the wrens, the trickle and tock of water, and under everything a deep hollow note. It is the wind crossing the mouth of the ridge and setting the columns humming, like a finger drawn around a glass rim. When the wind shifts, the note slides. Sometimes two columns find a chord, and for a few breaths every creature in the bowl falls silent, as if listening. Then the noise crashes back.

Speech is hard here. People who visit learn to communicate with taps on the arm or by pressing their brows together and speaking directly into the bone.

## Strangeness

Three things make the Throat feel wrong to anyone who spends a night there.

First, the chorus keeps time. Once each day, in the last hour before the sky-slot brightens, every creature in the bowl falls silent at once: beetles, wrens, all of them. The silence lasts about as long as it takes to draw a slow breath, and then the sound returns in a single swell. Nobody knows what cue they follow. The old collectors say the mountain itself breathes out at that moment, and that a thin draught of warm air rises from the vents. The instruments that might confirm this do not exist.

Second, the stone remembers sound. On certain columns, a shout returns not as an echo but as a delayed and altered version, lower and slower. It comes back after a count of five, as though the rock had thought about it first.

Third, the lanternwrens' glow is not random. In the dark, the pulses pass across the bowl in slow waves, from one nesting tube to the next, hours long. Someone watching from a ledge can see a great pale ripple traverse the dark and disappear over the far wall. It might be display, or warning, or something older that nobody has a word for.

## The Human Trace

There are no buildings here and no made paths. But people have come. In one dry crack near the north wall lies a coil of twisted grass rope, stiff with age. Farther down is a bone hook, bound with sinew to a stick, used to fish beetles out of their joints or drag glass-crawlers from the tarns. Some of the columns bear faint scratched tallies, the scratches of obsidian flakes and stone points, not writing but counting. A dozen small black flakes lie around a flat stone where someone once knapped tools. The visitors are seasonal gatherers who come for the rosette-plant, the beetle-oil, and the wren feathers, and they leave almost nothing behind. The Throat absorbs them as it absorbs everything.

## Time

Change here is slow. The overhang is thickening with ice, a hair's breadth a year. The cracks between columns are opening just perceptibly, and a few chambers have collapsed within the span of a single grandmother's memory. The tarns rise and fall over seasons, not days. The vents warm and cool on cycles longer than a human generation. A place so young is still deciding what it will be, and it decides gradually, in the dark, under a roof of ice, inside a din of ten million small lives.
