# The Wet Loft

Near the summit of a mountain that has not finished rising, there is a room. From outside you would find only a seam in black rock, a crack you could cover with two hands. Inside, the seam opens into a space about the size of a granary, and the space is full.

## The Shell

The walls are fitted stone, each block dressed with flint and antler-pick, and the tool marks are still on them: crisp, pale, and scalloped, with every chisel-bite sharp enough to cut a thumb. Nothing here has been rained on by the sky, or worn by wind or feet. The stone has been *placed*, not weathered. The air smells of fresh-cut rock, like the inside of a split cobble, mixed with the green smell of crushed stems.

The floor is stepped into terraces, each a hand-span or an arm's length high, and each held by a retaining wall of mortarless stone. There are eleven levels in a space you could cross in twenty paces, and they interlock like the cells of a wasp nest. A ladder of lashed poles runs through the middle. Its rungs are tied with wet cord, and the knots are all the same knot.

## Heat and Water

The mountain is warm here. Somewhere below, the rock is not finished cooling, and a vent breathes up through the floor of the lowest terrace with a long, uneven roar, like a giant blowing across a jug. The stone is hot to the palm. The air is hot and thick, and the room sits at an altitude where a person should be gasping in the cold. Instead you gasp because of the heat, in air that is thin and wet at the same time. It is a contradiction the place seems comfortable with.

Water is everywhere near the surface. The ceiling, a vault of corbelled slabs, sweats constantly. Beads gather on the underside, swell, and fall in a hundred separate rhythms. Bamboo gutters, split and lashed, catch the drips and carry them along the walls to clay cisterns, and from the cisterns to the terraces through channels the width of a finger. Every channel is in use. Every channel is *slightly* leaking, and the leaks are how the ferns are watered.

## The Green

The vegetation is lush to the point of argument. Ferns crowd the terraces in fired-clay pots, and the pots are stacked so thickly they form a second wall. Vines have been trained up frames of lashed reed. Wide-leaved plants with waxy undersides crowd against each other, their leaf-tips dripping. Moss furs every surface that isn't sharp stone, and here is the first wrongness: the moss is *old*. It has the thick, layered, ancient look of a hundred years of slow growth, yet it grows over blocks whose chisel-marks are unworn. The stone is new and the moss is ancient, and neither seems troubled by the other.

The stairs between terraces are worn into shallow dishes, smoothed by generations of feet. But the smoothing has tool marks in it. Someone carved the wear by hand, hollow by hollow, to make the steps look walked. Nobody has walked them.

## Light

It is dim. A pale shaft comes down through a sky-hole in the vault, no wider than a dinner bowl, and the sky in it is a dark violet even at midday, a thin high sky with a star or two showing. The shaft moves across the terraces through the day like the hand of a clock. Otherwise the room is lit by fat-lamps in niches (shallow clay dishes with reed wicks, burning with a low orange flame), by patches of lichen that glow a sour green, and by the red seam of the vent below, which lights the undersides of the ferns from beneath.

## Sound

The room is loud. There is the vent's drone, the ceaseless plinking and sheeting of water, and the rattle of bamboo pipes as they fill and gulp and empty. Under these is a third sound: hammering. Mallet on chisel, chisel on stone, an unhurried tock-tock-tock from somewhere just out of sight. You never find the hammerer. It comes from behind the walls, or above the vault, or from a block of stone you are standing beside.

## The Rules

Here, reality is unreliable, and the unreliability follows patterns you begin to learn.

**The hammering comes first.** The sound of a stone being set arrives before the stone. Listen to the tock-tock-tock, wait, and a course of blocks that was not there will be there, dry and fitted, a little dust still settling. The wall you leaned on an hour ago is now a different wall. Some part of the room is always under construction, and the construction is always ahead of you.

**The room does not agree with itself.** Pace it from end to end and you get twenty steps. Pace it again and you get thirty-one. Lay a cord across and mark it, and the mark has moved. The eleven terraces are sometimes nine, sometimes fourteen. The builders do not seem to mind.

**Rain moves wrongly.** Drops fall from the ceiling as they should. But in the far corners, low down, drops rise from the floor and gather on the vault, and in the shaft of light some drops hang for a long time, turning, before deciding which way to go.

**Ropes have too many ends.** A cord tied off on one terrace comes out of a knot on another. Follow it and you find that it has three ends, or one, or none, and is a loop tied around nothing.

**Things are finished before they are begun.** The clay pots come already fired, already planted, already overgrown. Somewhere in the packed rows, a pot is being shaped on a wheel. It is full of fern. It is also empty and wet and rotating.

## The Weather Inside

There is a cycle. At the hour when the shaft of light hits the lowest terrace, the vent surges, and steam boils up through the ferns until the whole room is a white roar. Water condenses on every surface at once and the ceiling rains: a real downpour, warm, brief, and heavy. Then the shaft slides on, the steam thins, and the room drips for hours.

At night the mountain's cold arrives through the sky-hole like a poured liquid. Frost rimes the fern-tips at the top of the room while the stone below stays hot enough to sting. For a while the room holds both seasons, with frost above and steam below, and the plants shiver in a gradient.

## The Living

Fauna is rare. There are pale tree-frogs the color of unfired clay, no bigger than a thumbnail, and their calls are the thin whistling of a reed pipe. They eat the small moths that drink from the weeping stone. Once in a long while a larger thing crosses the far end of a terrace: a slow, low-shouldered shape, quite possibly a person, quite possibly carrying a mallet, always moving away. The vines, for their part, are patient predators. They strangle the lashed scaffolding and take the poles apart, and the builders keep replacing them. This may be the whole economy of the place: wood grown, wood lashed, wood eaten, wood grown.

## What It Is Becoming

Nothing here is finished. That is the deepest fact of it. The room is being built while it is being lived in, and it is being lived in while it is being built, and its age is being installed a little at a time, moss and wear and darkness with the rest. The mountain has not stopped rising beneath it, and each hour the stone is pushed a fraction higher toward that violet sky. When the seam finally closes, or opens, the Wet Loft will have always been here.
