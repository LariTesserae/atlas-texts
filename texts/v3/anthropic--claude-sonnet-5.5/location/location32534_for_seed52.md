# The Lowfold

The land here is new enough that it hasn't finished deciding its shape. A long basin, barely above the level of the sea that must lie somewhere to the south, opened in the crust not many thousands of years ago. Its edges are still sharp. The basalt scarps stand in clean, stepped faces, dark blue-grey where they are wet and rust-orange where they aren't. Their columns are unblunted, and each hexagonal joint keeps its original corner. Rain has only begun to work on them. Where it has, it leaves thin white threads of mineral down the cliffs, like the first lines of a drawing.

The basin floor is a mosaic of young ground: black sand flats, low domes of cooled lava split by cracks, and shallow fissures where warmth still comes up from below. The air is warm, the warmth of a held breath, and it smells of wet stone, crushed green, and something faintly metallic and sweet that you stop noticing after an hour and then notice again all at once.

## The green

Everything that can grow here does. Fern-trees and broad-leaved climbers crowd the fissures, which act as long greenhouses, each a ribbon of dense vegetation running through bare rock. The leaves are large and dark and hold water in their cups. Moss covers every north-facing surface in a deep, pressed velvet. On the open flats the growth is lower and stranger: mats of silver-green succulents that open at night, and tall reed-like things that lean all in one direction, toward the nearest tower, as if listening.

The forest is lush but not continuous. Between the green ribbons lie wide spaces of black grit and open sky, so the whole basin looks like a dark cloth with green threads worked through it and a few bright knots.

## The dimness

It is never fully bright here. A high, thin veil of cloud holds over the Lowfold, and beneath it the day is the color of pewter, like the hour after sunset that never gets on with becoming night. Shadows are soft and have no clear edges. Colors sit deep and a little underexposed: the greens go nearly black, and the rust of the cliffs glows like an ember.

Part of the dimness is the city's doing.

## The city

The settlement has no name that carries beyond itself, and it isn't clear it is a settlement in the way that word implies. It is spread across the basin in a wide, loose lattice. There are hundreds of structures, but each stands a quarter-mile or more from its neighbors, and between them run causeways, aqueducts, and long shallow stairs that descend into the fissures and come out somewhere unexpected.

The structures are made of a material the eye slides across. It is matte and slightly warm, and it drinks light. A wall of it in full daylight looks like a hole cut in the world, the exact grey-black of the inside of a closed eye. In the dimness of the Lowfold this means the towers are not seen so much as noticed, as absences with edges. Only at the end of the day does the material give back what it has taken. For an hour or so the towers glow faintly from within, a low amber that starts at the base and climbs, and the whole basin becomes a scatter of slow lanterns across black ground.

The towers are tall, narrow, and slightly tilted, and the tilts are not random. Walk far enough and you see that they all lean a few degrees toward some point in the basin's center, where there is no tower. There is only a round, level plain of fused glass, perfectly clean, without a single plant. Nothing grows on it and no rain pools on it. Rain that falls there simply isn't there a moment later.

Between the towers are lower structures: arcades, cisterns, bridges that span nothing, and ribbed domes half sunk in the black sand. Everything is jointless and seamless, and it looks less built than *settled*, as if the buildings had precipitated out of the air, or agreed to be here. Ivy has climbed some of them and found nothing to grip. The vines hang a hand's breadth off the surface, held there by something, in a stiff and patient tension.

## Water

The Lowfold is damp rather than wet. Actual rain is occasional, a warm heavy shower that comes up from the south on some afternoons, fills the fissures with a rushing brown sound, and is gone within the hour.

The real water comes at night. As the ground cools, the city's surfaces go cold before the air does, colder than any stone would. Dew beads on them at once, thickens, and runs down grooves cut for exactly that purpose. By morning every tower has a fringe of falling threads at its base. These gather into channels, the channels into small clear canals, and the canals feed the black pools that sit in the lower fissures, deep round pools, still as poured ink, ringed with pale ferns.

The water in the pools is very cold, and it is deeper than it should be. Drop a pebble in and you hear it strike nothing for a long time.

## Sound

Mostly quiet. What sound there is comes at low intensity and from a distance: dripping, the slow clicking of cooling rock, the papery shifting of the reed fields as they lean. Sometimes a wind moves across the basin and the towers answer it, not with whistling but with a single soft tone, always a slightly different pitch, as if each tower were tuned to a different question. When two towers happen to sound together, the air between them feels thick for a moment, and the hair lifts on your arms.

Occasionally, deep and long, the ground itself hums. It is a geological sound, the crust still settling, and it is answered by the towers, which go silent, all at once, for the length of the hum.

## The rules of the place

Some things you learn quickly.

Distances are honest only when you walk them alone. Two people crossing the same causeway will reach the far end at different times, and the one who was looking back will always arrive later.

The pavement is faintly warm where someone has recently walked, and the warmth fades over about an hour. The oldest paths, the ones that have been walked constantly for who knows how long, are warm all the time, and they lead only to the central plain of glass.

Nothing here is ever repaired that you can see, yet nothing is broken. A cracked step, if you leave it and come back at dusk, has closed along its seam with a faint white line, like a healed scar.

## Life

Animals are rare and mostly small. Pale moths as broad as a palm feed on the amber glow of the towers at dusk and fly in slow spirals. Something with long, jointed legs picks through the shallows of the black pools, motionless for minutes, then very fast. You might once, from a great distance, see a herd of tall grey grazers crossing the basin in single file through the reed fields. They stop when you stop, and they never come near the towers.

The fauna you *don't* see is the more persistent presence. Something tends the causeways. Leaves are cleared from the drains, and the succulent mats are trimmed at their edges to a hand's width from the stone. It works in the dim hours, unseen. Once in a long while you may catch, at the corner of your vision, a low ripple in the air over the path, like heat over a road. It moves along the ground in a slow, deliberate line and vanishes at the doorway of a tower.

## Motion

The place is not static. It is slow but continuously active, on several clocks at once. The fissures are widening by a finger's breadth a year, and the vegetation follows them, filling each new gap within a season. The reed fields drift, generation by generation, like tides. The mists rise at dawn from the warm vents, the dew falls at night, and the afternoon rains come and go. Above all of it the towers lean, tone, glow, and wait, and the ground beneath them, young and unfinished, goes on quietly making itself.

At the edge of the glass plain, if you stand there at the hour when the towers are lit, you can see all of them at once, leaning in like an audience. The plain is completely dark. In it, very far down and not a reflection, something is slowly turning.
