# The Loom Heights

## Approach

At this altitude the air is thin and cold, and it carries more sound than air should be able to hold. The plateau is fourteen thousand paces above whatever sea once existed. The sky is the indigo of a deep well, with a sun in it the color of a banked coal. It gives light the way a memory does, without heat and without much direction. Shadows lean the wrong way in the afternoons. Nobody has ever proved this, but the cairns seem to agree.

The land here is not finished. It is being made, and you can hear it being made. That is the first fact and the last one, because the noise is everywhere and you cannot get around it.

## The Sound

It is not a roar, though "roar" is the nearest word. It is a wall of layered tones, the low grinding of plates, a high glassy singing, and a stuttering in between like a great many doors being opened and shut. It comes from the **Joins**, long seams where the mountain is stitched to itself. Along the Joins, rock rises from below in slow ribbons, and as it rises it *sounds*, a pitch for each fracture and a chord for each ridge. Standing within a mile of a major Join is like standing inside a struck bell. Your teeth hum and your sternum becomes an instrument. Thought comes in small pieces, if it comes.

The people who came here, and someone did, stuffed their ears with felted plant fiber and walked in pairs, one hand on the other's shoulder. Handfuls of these plugs lie wherever the walking got too hard, gray and compressed into little bullets, with a few sun-bleached cords beside them. The cords are knotted in a code of touch. The knots were meant to be read by fingers, since speech was impossible.

## The Ground

Everything is raw. The rock has no soil on it and no weathering, and nothing has rounded it. Faces are sharp as struck flint, and fractures run in clean planes of black basalt, rust-red breccia, and a pale green stone with the sheen of wet cloth that is not wet. Scree has hardly begun. Where the ground breaks, the pieces stay near where they broke, like a dropped plate.

The terrain rises in terraces. Each is a wide, tilted shelf of new stone, miles across, with a lip that drops away into a lower shelf. Some drops are a few body-lengths and some are the height of a cathedral. The shelves tilt slightly toward the Joins, as if the whole land were leaning in to listen.

The unfinished parts are the strangest. In places the rock is still *deciding*. Here a slab is half basalt and half a grainy, layered thing like compressed ash, with no boundary between them, only a gradient that smears over the width of a hand. A ridge may be sharp for a hundred paces and then relax into a smooth swell like poured wax, holding the print of whatever last leaned on it. You can leave a footprint in stone here if you stand still long enough and the ground is in that mood.

## The Rule of the Quiet

Sound holds this world together.

Where the noise is loudest, the mountains are hard, the distances honest, and a stone stays the shape it was when you looked away. In the pockets of relative quiet, the **hush-basins** sheltered by the ridges' folds, things begin to fail. Nothing violent happens. Reality goes slack.

In a hush-basin, a slope may keep rising when you walk across it, though your legs say the ground is flat. A boulder may be on your left when you look and behind you when you don't. You may count forty paces to a cairn and then, counting again, find eleven. Edges lose their agreement with faces. Colors detach from the objects they belong to and drift a little, so the red of a rock hangs a hand's breadth to one side of it. A shadow may pool where no thing stands.

The old travelers knew this and treated silence as the danger. Their cairns cluster along the loud routes, and they built almost nothing in the hush-basins. What they built there is left tilted or half-swallowed, stones set in courses that do not agree with each other, a doorway framing nothing you can find from the other side.

If you stay too long in a hush-basin, you feel the world stop being obliged to make sense. The cure is to walk toward the noise, and the noise feels like relief, like a hand on your back.

## The Growth

The vegetation is lush. It should not be, since there is almost no water, only rime frost at dawn and a few seeps of thick, mineral-heavy brine that beads on the pale green stone like resin. But the plants do not live on water the way plants elsewhere do. They drink the vibration.

The tall ones are **fluteweeds**, hollow-stemmed reeds twice the height of a person in dense stands, blue-green shading to violet at the tips, each stem fluted and ribbed like a pipe. Their leaves are thin membranes, stretched taut like drumheads. In the sound they tremble, and the trembling is their feeding. Each stand hums a soft counter-tone against the Joins, and the fluteweed groves are the only place where the din feels *shaped*, almost like harmony, almost kind.

Between them grow:

- **Cushion moss**, deep and springy, in domes as broad as a table, olive and rust and a wet-looking black. It grows in pressure-lines, in fine concentric ripples following the wavefronts of the sound. Read the moss and you can tell where the nearest Join lies.
- **Ribbon fern**, long flat fronds that lie along the ground pointing toward the loudest source. Whole hillsides are combed in one direction like fur.
- **Glass lichen**, translucent crusts on the new rock that glow a faint cold green in the dim, enough to read your own hands by, barely.
- **Bell-thorn**, a low woody shrub with hard little seed-capsules that ring softly when the wind moves them. It rings *in tune* with the Joins. Someone once strung its capsules on cord, and those strings hang in some of the lean-tos, still faintly sounding.

Everything is dense in the loud places and thins to nothing at the edge of a hush-basin. Plants do not grow well in the silences. A few stand there as gray, brittle stalks, upright and unrotted, because nothing consumes them.

There are no animals. No insect crosses the moss, no bird holds the thin sky, and nothing burrows. The lushness is uninhabited, like a garden laid out for guests who never arrived. The only movement other than the plants and the slow shifting of stone is the drift of frost-smoke across the terraces in the cold hours.

## The Marks of Hands

The built world is scattered and sparse. Here are the fragments of it.

**Cairns.** Stacked flat stones as high as a shoulder, each with a fist-sized black stone at the top. They mark routes between the hush-basins. Some have been pushed askew by the rising ground, since the land moves beneath them, and now lean like tired figures.

**Lean-tos.** Low shelters of fluteweed stalks lashed with bark cord to a frame of split stone, roofed with moss mats. They are small, made for one or two, and set in the lee of ridges where the sound is deflected a little. Inside are hearthless floors, since there is no fuel that burns, only fluteweed that hums. Sometimes a stack of ear-plugs sits in a niche, offered, waiting.

**Tools.** Knapped stone hammers, scrapers of green stone with edges still sharp enough to skin your thumb, stone wedges for splitting stalks, and bone-less handles of twisted fiber. It is all hand-craft and simple. The scrapers are worn on one side only, as if used by a single patient person over a very long time.

**The Listening Stones.** On a few high shelves, tall slabs have been set upright and carefully aligned, each drilled with a single hole. If you press an ear against the hole, and you have to be brave with the din, you hear the Join nearest to it *alone*, as one thin pure note with the roar filtered out. Something like awe, or worship, or just attention, clearly went into this. Nothing tells you what they concluded.

No one lives here now. The last cords are not dusty, because nothing here makes dust yet, but they are faded, and their knots have begun to loosen in the cold.

## Slow Motion

Nothing happens quickly. The plateau rises a finger's width in a year, and the Joins slowly migrate. A note that was low a lifetime ago is higher now. The moss ripples shift by inches across decades. The fluteweed groves creep toward new sources and abandon old ones, leaving ghost-stands of gray stalks behind. In another few thousand years the loud places will be elsewhere, the hush-basins will fill with sound and firm up, and new silences will open where the sound has gone. The world is a slow argument between noise and quiet, and noise is winning, which is why the land is being built at all.

## At the Edge of the Light

Late in the long dim day the sun lowers into a band of thin violet haze, and the glass lichen brightens against the darkening stone. The fluteweed hums as a single chord across the whole terrace, deep and unhurried, as if the mountain had briefly agreed with itself. In this hour a traveler who has stopped at a listening stone might feel the ground beneath them completed for a moment, rock that has finished deciding.

Then a Join shifts somewhere below, and a new tone climbs through the chord. Far off, in a hush-basin you can't see, a cairn quietly loses its argument with the geometry around it. The frost-smoke drifts, the cords loosen a little more, and the noise goes on making the world, one struck note at a time.
