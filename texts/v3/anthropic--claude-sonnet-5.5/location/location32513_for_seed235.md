# The Chordal City of Ashuvane

## The Height

The city stands on a tableland nearly seven kilometers above where any sea might once have been. At that height the air should be thin and killing cold. It is neither. The air is thick, close to bathwater in temperature, and each breath tastes of warm iron and crushed leaves. The heat comes up from below. The plateau is a single slab of ancient volcanic rock, and the deep warmth of it seeps through every crack as vapor. Here the world's usual gradient is reversed. The higher you climb, the hotter and wetter it gets. The valleys far below, when the cloud shifts enough to show them, are pale and dry and dead.

The cloud floor is not rain-cloud. It is a bright, flat, unmoving sheet at the foot of the escarpments, and it has lain there so long that dust has settled on its upper surface in a faint gray-gold bloom. Nothing falls from it. Water travels the other way in this country. Moisture climbs. It wicks up the rock, up the stems, up the columns of the city, and beads at the tips of everything pointing skyward, where it hangs in a permanent fine drizzle that never quite falls and never quite lifts. Everything drips upward-then-down in a slow loop that has not altered in living memory, because there is no living memory here. Nothing lives here that remembers.

## The City

It is not built in any sense a mason would recognize. It is accreted. Mineral water, saturated and scalding, has flowed over the same surfaces for so many ages that it has laid down travertine and tufa in pale, layered, ribbed forms: towers, arcades, stepped terraces, stairways with treads worn to shallow dishes that hold still green water. Streets are channels. Squares are basins ringed by rims like the lips of enormous shells. Walls lean together overhead until they nearly touch, and in the last hand's-width between them, lattices of root and vine have knit across to close the gap. You walk in a green-lit dimness, in a city that is half stone, half plant, and whose two halves have been so long entangled that neither could be lifted out without the other.

The towers are hollow. That matters more than anything else about them.

The architecture is dense in the way of a coral head or a termite mound: cell within cell, gallery beneath gallery, balconies fused to the balconies opposite, so that the whole thing seems to be one structure that happens to have ten thousand doorways. There are no doors in the true sense, only apertures. Some are as narrow as a ribcage. Some open on stairways that spiral down into warm dark and come out, after a long descent, at a window high on a different tower. The city is a wrinkled, layered, near-touching mass with no empty middle. Even its plazas are crowded with stone fruit: knobbed stalagmitic pillars growing up from the basin floors, each wrapped in a sleeve of moss.

Nothing was designed here. Nothing was ever built by hand. Yet it has the grammar of a city, avenues that widen toward focal basins, districts that repeat their motifs, a hierarchy of scale from alley to boulevard. It is as if the water learned what a city was by dreaming of one and then made it, patiently, from lime.

## The Green

The vegetation is lush to the point of violence. Ferns as tall as houses arch out of the terrace-lips. Tree-ferns and giant epiphytic aroids clamp the towers with leaves the size of doors, dark and glossy, and every leaf tip carries a drop that is always just about to fall. Mosses lie over the stone in thick emerald pelts, gold where a slant of light reaches them, black-green in the cracks. Strangler figs have poured down the tower flanks like frozen green wax, their roots fused into buttresses and archways that the stone has since partly swallowed. Orchids in every shade from bone to bruise-purple hang in the dimmer galleries. Pale, translucent fungi shelve out from the wet walls in tiers.

There is no animal life. No insect crosses the light, no bird calls. This is one of the strangest facts of the place, given how much there is to eat and inhabit. The leaves are unbitten. The flowers, many of them large, fragrant, and elaborately shaped, open for no visitor and set seed by other means, drifting spore-clouds or the constant upward mist. The plants seem to have forgotten that anything else was ever supposed to exist. It is a full ecology of one kingdom, and its completeness is what makes it uncanny.

## The Sound

The sound is deafening, and it is not noise. It is a chord.

Wind, steam, and water move continuously through the hollow towers, and every tower is a pipe of a different length and bore. Together they sound a single vast harmony, so loud that at the city's edge you feel it in the sternum before you hear it, a pressure like standing beside a waterfall the size of a mountain. Deeper in, it is beyond loudness. It becomes a medium. You do not hear it so much as sit inside it, the way a fish sits inside water. Thick low tones from the great towers underlie mid-register hummings from the terraces and, above those, shimmering reedy overtones from the smallest fluted chimneys, pitched at the edge of pain.

The chord does not change. It has never modulated. Every resonance in the city is locked to every other, tuned over ages by the slow growth of the stone itself, because a tower that sounded a discordant note would have its mineral flow altered by the vibration, and would grow, over centuries, toward consonance. The whole city is a tuned instrument that tuned itself. What you hear is the sound of equilibrium.

## The Breakdown

Here reality frays, quietly and consistently, in ways that have themselves settled into rules.

Sound has position without source. Standing in a basin you may hear a distinct low note from a point in the air a meter in front of your face, where there is nothing. It stays there. Others, in the same place, hear it in the same spot.

Silence has body. In a few alleys there are pockets of true quiet, sharp-edged and about the size of a doorway, and they are solid to the ear the way a pillar is solid to the hand. You can step into one and the chord vanishes so completely that your own pulse booms. Step out and the chord returns like a slap. The pockets do not move. Moss grows around them in a neat ring, as if the plants respect them.

Light lacks origin. The illumination is a diffuse gold-green, bright enough to read by, and it comes through the lattice roofs, but shadows fall in several directions at once, and some fall toward the light. Certain stairways descend and arrive higher than they began. Certain arcades, followed to their end, return you to the arch you entered by, with the same drop hanging at the same leaf tip, though you never turned.

Echoes arrive before their causes. Clap your hands and the return comes first, then the clap, then a third softer answer, and after that the chord swallows it.

None of this changes. That is the last and deepest oddity. The breakdown is not a disturbance in progress. It is a finished thing, a frozen settlement between the laws of the world and whatever pressed against them. The drips fall on schedule. The mist rises at its one rate. The gap-toothed silences stay where they are. The impossible stairways remain impossible in exactly the same manner, so consistently that the impossibility has become a kind of geometry, and you find, after a day inside the roar, that you can navigate by it.

## Edges

Toward the plateau's rim the towers thin and the chord loosens into individual voices, and you can finally hear the separate pipes. Beyond the last terrace the rock drops away to the bright cloud floor, silent and dust-gilded, so quiet by contrast that it seems painted. From there the city looks like a single object: a crown of pale, dripping, green-furred spires, humming one enormous note into a sky that has never answered.
