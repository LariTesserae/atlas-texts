# The Sealed Terraces of Ammereth Deep

Three thousand meters above any sea, a bowl of black water sits in the shoulder of a mountain that has been worn down for so long it no longer has a summit, only a broad, smooth, high shrug of stone. The lake is warm. Heat rises through fissures in the bowl's floor, so the water holds at the temperature of a body at rest. Nothing in it is cold, and nothing in it is bright.

The place you are describing lies at the bottom of that bowl, sixty meters down. It is a settlement, or the memory of one, about the size of a village green: a few hundred paces across, wedged into the crater's throat.

## The Town That Became a Reef

Everything here was made by hands, or by things that had something like hands. There is no natural ground. The bowl floor was paved long ago with fitted cobbles, each one river-round and carried from somewhere else. Over the paving stand terraces, and on the terraces stand houses, or vaults, or hives. The word depends on who is looking. They are low, round-shouldered, corbelled structures of stacked stone and fired clay, with lintels of whale-thick bone. The doorways are narrow ovals, wider than they are tall, made for bodies that move sideways.

The builders had no metal to speak of. There are a few soft copper pins, greened to the color of old moss, and the rest is stone, clay, shell, bone, and cord. Wedges, levers, and the patient rolling of weights did the work. You can still see the tool-marks where the water has not yet finished erasing them: the shallow scallops of a hammerstone, the drag-lines of a pumice rasp.

The water has nearly finished. It is rich with dissolved lime, and over ten thousand generations of slow warm seeping it has coated every surface with travertine, layer on pale layer. Every edge is now rounded. Every corner has been pillowed. The steps of the terraces have melted into ramps, the ramps into slopes, the houses into soft dunes with dark mouths. Streets have narrowed to seams. The whole settlement is fusing into one continuous carbonate body, a reef built by its own drowned architecture. In another hundred thousand years it will be a single smooth boulder with rooms inside, and then, perhaps, a solid one.

The town has almost reached its final form. That is its condition: not ruin and not life, but settling.

## Dark

No light comes down from the surface. Above, sixty meters of black water stand over a black sky. What glimmer exists is made here.

Along the terrace edges grow films of bacteria that glow a dim green-blue, so faint that your eyes must forget the idea of light before they can find it. Where the films are thick they trace the outline of a wall like chalk-dust in a cellar. In the doorways of the inhabited houses, small lamp-creatures cling, pale jelly-bodied things the size of a thumb, pulsing slowly. Whoever lives in these houses tends them, feeds them, moves them from door to door. This is the town's only lighting, and it comes to perhaps the brightness of a sleeping ember seen through closed lids.

Distance does not exist here. You know a thing is near by touch, by the shifting pressure of water against your skin, or by the small ticking sounds it makes.

## The Inhabitants

The fauna is abundant, and dense. Every hollow holds something.

The builders' descendants, or possibly the builders themselves, live here. They are long-armed, soft-bodied, pale animals with the bulk of a large dog, each with eight or nine flexible limbs and a hard, notched beak. They creep along the walls on their fingertips. They carry small tools: a sliver of bone, a shell scraper, a cord of twisted gut. What they do with them is repair, slowly and without hurry. One of them will spend a season resetting a single cobble that a current has nudged. Another will scrape lime-crust from a lintel just enough to keep a doorway open, and no further. The town is sealing itself, and they are gently holding the seal at bay, one door at a time. They do not seem to expect to win. They do it the way one keeps a fire going.

They do not speak, but they touch. When two pass in a lane they lay a limb across each other for a moment, and a slow ripple of color runs down the arm, invisible to any eye but theirs.

Around them, and in the walls, and in the cracks between the stones, are the crowds:

- Blind shrimp in the thousands, translucent, rattling their claws in tiny bursts that blur into a dry static.
- Flat, coin-shaped snails that graze the glowing films and leave clean spirals behind them.
- Pale fish with no eyes and long whiskered chins, hanging head-down in the doorways as if listening.
- Worms in white tufts along the vent-seams, swaying together like a field of grain in an invisible wind.
- Small crabs that wear pieces of the town: a chip of shell, a bead of clay. Some have grown so fond of their armor that it is more mortar than animal.

Nothing here eats a plant, because no plant exists. The whole food web hangs from the bacterial mats and the vent-chemistry beneath them, a dark economy of mineral appetite. What feeds on the mats feeds the shrimp, the shrimp feed the fish, and the fish feed the long-armed keepers, who die in the end into the crumbling lime and become part of the wall.

## Sound

The silence is nearly total. What is there is small: the dry tick-and-scatter of shrimp, the soft *tock* of a beak on stone, the faint hiss of the vents, which you feel more than hear. Now and then the whole town produces a low, slow tone, like a finger drawn along the rim of a glass. It is the warm current moving through the hollow houses, each doorway a different pipe, all of them together sounding one long chord that changes over hours and never resolves.

## Slow Rules

Change here is measured in generations of lime.

Every year, the water breathes. Warmer seasons in the deep vents send up a slight surge, and the glow-films brighten for a few weeks; then they dim again. The keepers time their repairs by it. A door cleared in the bright season stays clear until the next.

Some doorways are already sealed. Behind them lie houses with the last keepers' bodies inside, and their tools, and the lamp-creatures, which live on in the dark for years, glowing to no one, until the lime closes over the last gap and they too become part of the reef. The keepers do not open these. They lay a limb against the smooth wall a moment as they pass, and the color runs down.

Above the town, the black water waits. Below it, the heat keeps rising. In between, a village is turning into stone, at exactly the speed at which it is still being loved.
