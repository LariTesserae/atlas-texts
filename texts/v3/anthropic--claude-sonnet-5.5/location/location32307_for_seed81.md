# The Sinter Terraces of Harrow Vent

## Approach

You come up from the lowlands by a switchback road cut into pale rock, and the air changes as you climb. It should get thinner and colder. Instead it gets thicker and warmer. At four thousand meters the heat is still the heat of a kitchen, a wet, even 38 degrees that has no source you can point to. The mountain is a vent, and the whole plateau breathes out.

The top is a broad caldera shelf, forty kilometers across, ringed by low broken crags. Nothing green grows on it. Cloud sits on it permanently, a low ceiling of steam that the sun comes through as a dull copper coin, so that noon looks like the last hour before dusk. Shadows have no edges. Everything is lit from all directions at once, the way a room is lit by a single frosted window.

## The Ground

The plateau is built of its own deposits. For longer than the city has existed, the water rising through the caldera has been laying down sinter, a pale silica crust, in terraces and rimstone dams and shallow basins. Each terrace holds a skin of water a hand deep, and the terraces step downhill in curved tiers like frozen ripples, hundreds of them, some the size of a table and some the size of a lake.

The colors come from the water, since no plant lives here to supply any. Bacterial mats line the warm basins in bands. Near the vents they are a bleached yellow-white, then orange, then a rust-green, and at the cooler margins a deep liver-brown. Filmy tufts of fungus-like threads hang from the undersides of overhangs. They are gray, slick, and beaded with water. These are the only living surfaces on the plateau that could be called soft.

Every rock weeps. Stone walls, cliff faces, and the undersides of bridges are all wet with slow condensation, and the drip forms long fingers of mineral that hang and thicken. Erosion here is old and has settled into balance. What the water dissolves at one elevation it redeposits a little lower, so the land is always losing its shape and regaining it. Nothing sharp survives. Every edge has been rounded by dissolving, then rounded again by coating.

## The City

The city is called Harrow Vent on the oldest pipe-plates. It covers the shelf in a broad, low, open spread, more like a field of buildings than a town. Between the districts lie hundreds of meters of bare terrace, and the streets are sinter causeways raised on iron piers a meter above the standing water. From any high point you see it laid out like a slow diagram: clusters of stacks and tanks, a long line of cooling towers, a lattice of pipes running to the horizon, and then a great stretch of pale empty ground before the next cluster.

It is an industrial city, and it is built to catch heat. Steam is drawn from the vent field through iron mains as thick as a house, wrapped in blackened cloth and lime. It runs turbines in halls with ribbed brick walls and tall arched windows. The turbines are enormous, slow, greased, and clean, and they turn without pause. Condensers strip the water back out of the steam, and the water is sent down through gutters, sluices, and settling pans to the lower terraces.

Nobody is here. The doors of the foremen's houses stand open, and the rooms inside are furnished: a table, two chairs, a shelf with a tin cup. Everything wears a thin gloss of sinter, a white glaze that has softened the corners of the furniture into the corners of the wall. In the halls, oil is still fed to the bearings by gravity drip-lubricators, and the flywheels still spin. The governors, brass balls on spindles, lift and settle in the machinery's breath. The city has no operators, only mechanisms that answer pressure with valves and valves with pressure.

The output goes nowhere anyone can find. Belts run from turbine shafts through the walls of the halls into the rock, and the rock takes the power. There are shafts and spindles in the sinter that lead down into the caldera and turn, and no one has followed them all the way.

## The Weirdness

Three things are wrong with the place, and you notice them slowly.

**The tide.** Roughly every eleven hours, the vents exhale together. A wall of white steam rolls out from the caldera's center across the streets. It is hot enough to burn skin and thick enough to erase a hand held at arm's length. The city is built for it. Chairs in the houses face the window slots that let the steam pass through. Ribbed louvers close on the windward side. Bell-metal plates set into the causeways ring as the wave passes, low and long, a chord that lasts several minutes. When it clears, every surface is newly beaded, and the terraces are a finger's depth fuller.

**The rain.** Rain falls here, but the storms don't come from outside. The cooling towers exhale plumes that rise, meet the cool layer at the top of the cloud, and fall back as warm rain, thick-dropped and slow, lasting twenty minutes over a single district while the next one stays dry. The city makes its own weather and drinks it. On the oldest plates someone scratched a note that the rain is "returned in the order it was taken," and the sluices are laid out as if it were true.

**The lights.** Dim as it is, the city has light, but it doesn't come from lamps. Glass bulbs the size of melons hang at intersections, filled with a cloudy water in which pale threads drift. They glow a soft green-white, brighter when the tide is coming. Whoever built them didn't wire them. They feed on something in the steam. You never find one dark.

## Life

Fauna is rare and small. Pale, translucent flies hover in the condensation above the warm basins, and their wings tick when the tide comes near. In the drains live blind salamanders the color of wet paper, each one about the length of a finger, sliding through water too hot to hold. On the inner walls of the cooling towers cling sheets of snails with shells like ground glass, grazing the mats. Once in a long while, something larger crosses the far terraces, a tall, thin, gray-legged wader that steps carefully from dam to dam. Whether it is an animal or a maintenance device is a question the city doesn't answer.

## Sound

It is mostly quiet, but not silent. The dominant sound is a low continuous thrum from the turbines, felt in the chest more than heard. Over it comes the water: drips from mineral fingers, thin runnels in the gutters, and the occasional glug of a check valve. Every few minutes, somewhere far off, a relief valve exhales with a long sigh. Metal ticks as it cools and warms. Between the districts, out on the empty terraces, you can hear nothing but your own footsteps on wet sinter and, under it, the ground humming faintly like a struck glass.

## The Rules of the Place

- Nothing stays sharp. Anything left out is coated white within a decade and disappears into the terraces within a century.
- The water always goes downhill, and the city always brings it back up.
- The tide comes on schedule. Those who built here built for it, and anything that is not built for it is gone.
- The light will not go out while the vents are breathing.

At the edge of the plateau, where the crags fall away into the lowland haze, you can turn and look back. The steam ceiling glows faintly from below, from ten thousand small green bulbs, and the city keeps its slow heat, its patient wheels, and its empty rooms with the chairs turned toward the windows, waiting for the next tide.
