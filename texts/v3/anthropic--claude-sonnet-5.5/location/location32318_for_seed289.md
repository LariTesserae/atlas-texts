# The Chord Room

You enter on your belly. The passage is a crack the width of a shoulder, floored with cold clay, and the last three meters of it are already loud before you see anything. The sound presses on your face like a hand. Then the crack opens and you can lift your head.

The room is about the size of a small chapel, but it has no height to speak of. The ceiling is a shallow, sagging dome you could touch by standing on a chair. The floor is uneven, and the whole space feels *unfinished*. The rock here has not yet decided what to be. The walls are raw, freshly fractured limestone and dark shale, still angular, with none of the polish of old caves. There are no flowstone curtains and no smoothed channels. The stone has been opened and left. Water has barely begun to work on it. A few faint scallops in the clay and a single shallow groove mark the earliest attempts at erosion, and they look sketched rather than carved.

## The Light

It is bright, and that is the first strangeness. There is no sun for hundreds of meters, and no fire. The light comes from the walls themselves, from a thin film of pale green-white crust that coats nearly every surface like frost. Under the crust, a living mat of microbes glows steadily and evenly. It is the brightness of an overcast noon, shadowless and without direction. Nothing here has a dark side. Your hand held up has no shade under it. The room looks the way a room looks in a dream where you never wonder about the source of the light.

The glow does not flicker, pulse, or fade. It has held one intensity since before anything in the room was born.

## The Sound

The loudness is the second strangeness. It is a chord, built from thousands of small voices, and it is held. Nothing in the room ever starts or stops it.

The fauna are everywhere. They are pale, blind, armored things about the size of a thumbnail, somewhere between beetle and cricket, with wide ribbed wing-cases that they rub against a ridged plate on their hind legs. Each one produces a single pitch. They sit packed shoulder to shoulder on every surface. They are stacked two and three deep on the walls, layered in drifts in the hollows of the floor, and clustered so thickly on the ceiling that the dome looks upholstered in living gray velvet. The chord they make has a low, grinding root note, a bright open fifth above it, and a shimmering cloud of overtones on top. The room's shape reinforces the chord, and the stone itself hums sympathetically. You feel it in your teeth, in the fluid of your inner ear, in the sockets of your knees.

The rule of the place, and you understand it slowly, is that each animal sings only the note that completes its neighbors' notes. A cricket on the wall listens to those around it and settles on whatever pitch the local chord lacks. If one dies, its neighbors shift slightly to fill the gap, and the chord never wavers. The sound has not changed in any way a human ear could notice, and possibly not for a very long time. If you stayed a day, or a season, you would hear the identical chord. The animals do not sing *to* anything. They *are* the chord, the way stones are a wall.

Newcomers are not born here, exactly. They emerge from a seam in the north wall, pale and soft, and crawl into the lowest gap in the harmony. The population is exact. It never crowds further and never thins.

## Water

It is damp, not wet. The air is cool, perhaps eight degrees, and thick with a fine condensation that beads on every surface and on every wing-case. Everything wears a bloom of tiny droplets that never quite merge and never quite evaporate. Breathing tastes of iron and wet slate.

A single drop falls from a point in the ceiling, from the tip of a small stalactite no longer than a finger. It falls at intervals so regular that after a minute you find yourself counting. It lands in a pool the size of a dinner plate, in the exact center of the room, and the surface trembles in concentric rings that pass across the animals' sounds without disturbing them. The pool has neither risen nor fallen. It drains through a hairline crack in its floor at exactly the rate it fills. Nothing overflows and nothing dries. The water is the only slow, small motion in the room.

## What Grows

Vegetation is sparse. Some flat gray lichens crust the drier ledges and some pale liverwort-like mats grow where the drips have been kind. Everything is sun-starved and leaning toward walls that give off light from all sides equally, so nothing leans at all. The plants grow evenly, flatly, in rosettes.

The one great presence is the root. A tree above, unimaginably far above, sent a single taproot down through the crack in the ceiling. It hangs down the center of the room like a rope as thick as your arm, brown and fibrous, and it ends in a tangle of root hairs that just touch the pool. It is fully alive. Its hairs are fine, white and glistening. It does not grow. It drinks the single drop's worth of water and holds itself perfectly still, and the crickets have covered it entirely so that it hums like a struck pipe.

## Marks

Small structures are scattered through the room, and no one lives here who could have built them, except perhaps the crickets themselves. There are cairns of pebbles stacked two and three high, always in threes, always at the room's edges. There are half a dozen shallow niches worn or chewed into the north wall, each holding a single smooth quartz egg-shaped stone, polished by something. Near the east wall a slab of shale lies across two blocks like a table or a lintel. Under it is a hollow, floored with clean sand, where nothing lives and nothing sings. It is the only quiet place in the room, and the silence there has an edge, as if you had stepped behind a curtain. Sit in it and the chord dims to a tremor in your chest. You can think there. You may find you don't want to leave.

## The Feel of the Place

Nothing here is heading anywhere. The rock is barely formed, the light does not change, the chord does not change, the pool keeps its level, and the root keeps its length. It is a pocket of time held in place by the mutual agreement of thousands of bodies, packed so close there is no room for anything else to happen. The whole room is a single sustained held breath, loud and bright and cold and damp, forever at the moment just before the first real erosion begins.

When you crawl back out through the crack, the sound follows you down the passage for a long way, and then it is gone. You will notice the silence afterward as a pressure. You will notice it for days.
