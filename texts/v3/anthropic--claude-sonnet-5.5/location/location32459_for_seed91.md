# The Sink at Varrick Deep

The ground here fell in, and not long ago. The rim is a raw scarp of pale limestone two hundred meters high, with fracture faces still sharp enough to cut a glove. Lichen has only started to stipple the ledges, in orange and grey coins the size of fingernails. Below the scarp is the floor of the collapse, a basin nearly four kilometers across. Everything down there is green, or the color green turns into when there is too little light and too much cold.

## The Floor

Moss is the substrate. Nothing here is soil in any sense a farmer would recognize. The basin floor is a quilt of living cushions, and under the living top layer are decades of the dead layers that fed it, then peat, then broken rock. A boot sinks to the ankle in some places and to the thigh in others. The mats are sphagnum in rust and olive, haircap moss in stiff dark bristles, and feather mosses in long combed drifts that show where water seeps downslope. Liverworts glaze the wet rocks. Between the cushions run corridors of horsetail, pale and jointed, standing knee-high like small organ pipes.

Trees are few and far apart, which is where the sparseness lies. A dwarf birch, bent and black-barked, stands alone every hundred meters or so. Its lower trunk is buried in moss so deep that it seems to grow out of a green hill. Willows crouch in the damp hollows. The plant mass is nearly total, but it is low and dense, a continuous skin laid over broken ground with only occasional verticals to interrupt it. Where the collapse dropped blocks the size of houses, moss has climbed them from the base and stopped at a line about head-high. It has not had time to reach the tops. Those remain bare, blue-grey, and unweathered.

## The Cold

The air sits just around freezing, and it is never quite still. Rime furs every upward-facing edge in the morning, each blade of horsetail wearing a coat of white needles. It burns off slowly, and only in the patches where light reaches. By afternoon the moss is beaded with condensation rather than rain. Real rain falls only a few times a season, a thin cold drizzle that comes over the rim and hangs. Mostly the water arrives as fog, which forms when warmer air from the plateau spills over the edge and meets the chilled floor. Mist moves through the basin in low sheets, and the moss drinks it directly.

Standing water is rare and small. There are black tarns no bigger than a table, ringed in red sphagnum, and one meltwater stream. That stream matters, because it makes the sound.

## The Sound

It arrives long before anything can be seen, and it is deafening in the literal sense. The stream that runs across the plateau reaches the edge of the collapse and drops into a fissure at the north end. Beneath the fissure, a cave system that predates the collapse channels the water and the air it drags down. Somewhere in the dark the two are forced through a throat of rock. The air comes back up the vent as a continuous bellowing roar, low enough to feel in the sternum and the teeth. It is somewhere between a jet engine and a cataract.

The bowl of the basin holds the roar the way a cupped hand holds water. The walls throw it back, so it comes from everywhere and has no direction. Moss should soak up sound, and it does eat the high frequencies, so that hisses and clicks and voices simply vanish. But the bass gets through untouched. The result is a heavy and strangely soft roar with nothing sharp in it. Conversation is impossible past arm's length, and people who work here use hand signals and lamps. Birds do not sing. If any do, no one will ever know.

## The Works

Eighty years ago somebody decided the peat was worth cutting, and the roar was worth harnessing. The industry that resulted is now scattered and half-abandoned, and it sits in the landscape like something dropped there.

A narrow-gauge rail runs across the basin on a causeway of timber sleepers, now sunk and swallowed on one side. The moss has grown over the rails to form two green ridges with a faint line of rust showing along each crest. A squat locomotive with a tall stack has stood for decades where the line ends, on a siding. It is fully colonized. The cab window is a frame of green, and a birch sapling grows from the firebox.

Near the north end, close to the vent, stands the one building still in use: a corrugated iron generator hall painted dull red, with a steel penstock running from the fissure to a turbine inside. It hums, or would, if hum could be heard beneath the roar. A single lamp burns above the door. A caretaker visits monthly by cable car from the rim, checks the gauges, greases the bearings, and leaves. The turbine sends a little current up the cable to a village on the plateau. That is the whole industrial output now.

Elsewhere there are scattered fragments: a drying rack of rotted timbers, the concrete footing of a pumphouse, an iron wheel half-sunk in a hummock, a stack of stamped brick blocks gone soft with moss. They are far apart and none is large. Each one shows the plants slowly digesting what people left.

## Life and Time

Fauna is rare. There are springtails in the moss by the millions, and small dark beetles. A few mountain hares live in the birch margins and show up as pale shapes in the fog. Ravens sometimes ride the updraft from the vent, hanging in the air with their wings barely moving. They enjoy it, and nobody can hear them.

The light is dim all year. The rim cuts off the low sun for most of the day, and what comes down is grey and even, with shadows so soft they are only a suggestion. Around midday in summer a blade of direct sun slides across the floor for an hour or two, and the whole basin steams.

Change is slow. The moss thickens by millimeters a year. The vent's voice shifts with the meltwater, louder in spring and quieter in the freeze. Frost heave nudges the fractured blocks, and now and then a scarp ledge lets go with a crack no one hears. In another few centuries the rim will soften, the blocks will be buried, and the birches will close into a canopy. For now it is a fresh wound with a thick green bandage, filled with one enormous sound.
