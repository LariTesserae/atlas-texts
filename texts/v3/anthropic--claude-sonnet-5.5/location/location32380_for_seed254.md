# The Sump Quarter

You arrive by going down. A stair of iron grating drops through a square shaft, and with each landing the air gains weight and warmth until it sits on your skin like a damp cloth. At the bottom the sound reaches you all at once, and it stays.

## The shape of it

The place is a few blocks of a town, no more than three or four hundred paces across, built in the floor of an old stone-cut basin and later roofed over by the city above. Brick pump houses, a boiler hall, a row of tenements for the workers who once tended them, a market arcade, and a canal running through the middle like a spine. Everything is packed shoulder to shoulder. Streets are the width of a handcart, and some are only the gap between one wall and the next, choked with growth.

The roof is a vault of old masonry, forty feet up, pierced by a few grated wells that let in a thin green daylight. Most of the light comes from far off and arrives already tired. It falls in slanted columns, and the water-vapor in them makes them solid-looking. Between the columns it is dusk. A few caged bulbs still burn along the main passage, yellow and haloed with mist.

## The green

Nothing here is bare. The plants are not decoration on the town. They have become part of how it stands.

Fig roots as thick as thighs pour down the faces of the tenements, fused with the brickwork until it's unclear which is holding up which. Where a wall has cracked, a root has gone into the crack and become the wall. Balconies are lattices of vine. Stairways have turned into terraces of fern and liverwort, and each step is a shelf of wet moss so thick your foot sinks to the ankle. Old drainpipes are wrapped in creepers that have grown into ropes, and those ropes have been wrapped by finer ones. The tops of the roofs are gardens, and the undersides hang with pale beards of root that drip constantly.

The moss comes in a dozen greens: emerald on the sheltered sides, olive where the vapor is heavy, a yellowed lime where a lamp shines on it. Bracket fungi step up the trunks in tiers, but they are the only fungi, and even they sit in the green like a rumor. Broad-leaved things, taro-like and glossy, crowd the ground in the lower streets, their leaves as big as doors and bowed with condensation. When a drop grows heavy it falls with a small slap on the leaf below, so that every plant is a slow percussion instrument.

## Water

Water is everywhere and in every state short of ice. It seeps from the walls in dark stains and beads on the ironwork. It runs down the brick in threads that have worn shallow grooves over the decades, and the grooves are lined with green slime and then with moss. The canal runs a foot below its stone lip, brown-green and slow, covered from bank to bank in duckweed so uniform that it looks like a lawn until a current parts it. Side channels feed into it through iron sluices, and some of these run hot. The warm outflow from the old boiler hall steams as it falls into the canal, and that is why the air feels tropical.

Puddles stand in every low place, and the puddles have floors of pale silt and rings of tiny plants. The cobbles are slick. Ropes of water fall from the vault's cracks in slender lines and land in the street with a steady patter, the same spot for decades, and the stone under each fall has been dished out into a bowl.

## Iron and brick, aged

Erosion has had its time here, and the town has settled into it. The bricks are softened, their edges rounded like old soap. Mineral crusts, white and cream, have grown down from the vault as flowstone curtains and over the iron as orange-brown scale. Rust has made its own landscapes: blistered fins on the railings, flaking blooms on the boilers, and on every horizontal surface a thin red silt that stains the water in the gutters. Brass fittings have gone a verdigris blue-green that is almost the color of the plants, so that in dim light you can't tell a valve from a leaf.

## The noise

It is loud, and it is loud in layers.

Beneath everything is a deep, continuous drone from the turbine hall, a pump running behind a wall of brick and moss. You feel it in your sternum before you hear it. It drives the water. Over it comes the roar of a spillway at the canal's far end, where the whole flow drops twenty feet through a gate into a lower dark. Spray from that drop hangs in the air of the east streets and puts a fine mist on your face.

Then the mid-sounds: a governor ticking, a chain clanking on a wheel, a valve breathing steam every ninety seconds or so with a long hiss. A transformer hums on a wall, its case furred with lichen. Pipes knock as pressure shifts in them. And under and around all of it is the hiss and patter of drip, thousands of drops on thousands of leaves, a sound like constant applause a long way off.

There is no birdsong, no insect whine, no scuttle. The only living things are green, and their sounds are what the water and machines make of them. If you stand still, you notice that the rustle of a leaf comes only when a drop hits it.

## Slow time

Very little happens quickly. The pumps cycle every few hours, and when they do, the canal rises by a hand's breadth and the duckweed lifts and settles like a breathing chest. Over the years the fig roots crack the pavement, a slab tilting a degree a decade. A stair that was passable within living memory is now a slope of moss. A doorway that once admitted a wagon now admits a stooped person, then no one. The vault seeps its minerals a fraction of a hair per year.

The lamps flicker on a supply that still comes from somewhere, and each bulb has a halo of green where the vapor has caught its light. The whole quarter is more plant than town by now, yet the town still gives it its shape: the straight lines under the tangles, the right angles of the streets, the regular windows through which the leaves lean out.

You stand in the market arcade, where arches frame the canal. A vine as thick as a rope hangs from the keystone, and water runs down it in a bright line into your open palm. It is warm.
