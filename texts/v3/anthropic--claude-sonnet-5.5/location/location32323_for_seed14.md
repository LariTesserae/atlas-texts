# The Grey Floor

Nothing here is empty. The water is full, the ground is full, and the space between them is a matter of opinion.

## The Water

You arrive from above, sinking through a thickening. The upper water is clear enough to see through and carries a last green-blue memory of day. Below that comes the snow: flakes of dead plankton, mucus, fecal grains, and shed scales, all falling at once and slowly, in a density that makes the dark look granular. Your hand held out at arm's length has a fuzz of drifting white on it. Farther out, at a body's length, it's a suggestion. At two lengths it's gone.

The cold is steady and total, just above freezing, and it has been the same for so long that it feels like a property of the water itself. It gets into joints and stays. Breath, if you had any, would ache.

Light is the faintest thing that still counts. The surface is a long way up and gives nothing but a diffuse charcoal-blue that you infer more than see. The rest comes from living things: sparks that fire when something bumps a jelly, a slow blue smear along the flank of a disturbed worm, a green pinprick that vanishes when you turn toward it. When the water is still, the dark is nearly total. When it is not still, and it is almost never still, the whole column flickers with cold sparks as sediment strikes drifting bodies.

## The Ground

The floor is an ancient plain, worn down until it has almost no features left. It was land once, and long before that it was mountains. Every ridge has been ground to a swell you can only detect by which way the silt slides. The horizon, if there were one, would be flat for many kilometers in every direction. The plain is broad and open, and it does not end where the dark starts.

What it is made of is the story. The floor is packed with the dead: shell hash, foraminifera tests, fish ear-stones, the chalky plates of things that no longer have names, compressed into a pale grey ooze that holds a footprint for a minute and then heals. Under the ooze is older ooze. Under that, a stiff clay, the color of wet ash, that has gone through the same cycle for longer than there have been eyes to see it. Where the ooze has been scoured away, you see the clay in terraces a hand tall, smooth as poured plaster, scalloped by currents into shallow spoon shapes.

Scattered through the sediment, packed shoulder to shoulder in some places, are manganese nodules, dark and knobbed, each the size of a fist or a loaf. They grow a few millimeters in a million years. There are so many that in places the floor is a black cobble pavement, and you can walk (if walking is a thing you do) from nodule to nodule without touching the grey between them. They are the slowest things here, and the most numerous. Their surfaces are slightly warm to the hand in a way that cannot be true and is.

## The Storms

The plain is ancient but it is not calm. Deep currents, driven by cold water sinking a continent away, arrive as *benthic storms*: weeks-long events in which the bottom current quickens and the floor lifts into the water. A storm begins as a hiss, a faint rising rush of sediment moving across sediment. Then the near floor goes brown and opaque. Ripples, which had been stationary, begin to crawl. Ridges form and dissolve within an hour. Nodules that have sat for a thousand years are undercut and roll a few centimeters. Clay peels in sheets. The water becomes a slurry, a moving wall of silt as thick as fog, and visibility drops to the length of your own arm.

Out of the storm come turbidity flows: a slump somewhere upslope, a slab of shelf-edge sediment giving way, and a dense cold river of mud runs out across the plain, hugging the bottom, scouring channels a few meters deep that are half-filled again within a season. The plain is flat only because it is constantly being flattened. Erosion and deposition here are not opposites but a single process, all the time, everywhere, and the floor is the momentary sum.

Between storms the fallout returns and the silt settles, and for a while the water clears to something like stillness. Then the far hiss starts again.

## The Sound

Very quiet. Between storms, a hush with texture: the fine tick of grains striking shell, the creak of a nodule shifting, a low thrum from the water itself, too deep to be a note. In storms the hiss rises to a long dry roar that seems to come from inside your chest. Sometimes, far off and uncertain, a whale calls. It arrives as a long tone bent by distance, and it is over before you're sure of it.

## Life

Life here is sparse in kind but crowded in effect. The density is in the small things.

**Mats.** Where the floor is stable for a season, it grows a pale fuzz of bacteria and filamentous fungi, and this is the closest thing to vegetation. There are no plants, no light for them. But there are colonies of *stalked sponges* and glassy, branching, bone-white sponge forests that rise ankle- or knee-high in patches so dense that their stalks touch, each holding out a lattice of silica. They look like something dead until you see the silt gathering in them and being eaten. Around them the current bends, and small animals shelter in the tangle. In a storm the forests bow flat and spring back, and some don't.

**Sediment dwellers.** The ooze is riddled with life: worms in their thousands per square meter, brittle stars packed arm to arm in living carpets, sea cucumbers (pale, translucent, soft as sacks) grazing across the plain in slow herds, each leaving a sinuous trail. Trails cover every unstormed surface, looping and doubling back, a script written and erased. Tiny amphipods, ghost-pale, swarm over any falling carcass in hundreds within an hour.

**Rare bigger things.** Fish are few. A grenadier hangs head-down in the current, tail whipping slowly. A pale eel-like thing nudges through the nodules. A ray-finned scavenger arrives, unseen, at a fallen body from kilometers away, guided by scent. Everything that lives here lives on what falls, and what falls is scarce in the way that matters and abundant in the way that doesn't. The snow never stops, but it is thin food. So the big animals are few, and the small ones stand shoulder to shoulder.

**The tool users.** Here and there on the plain are octopuses. They are small, pale, and slow, with skin the color of raw dough, and they live in the shells of dead bivalves, or under overhangs in the clay. Some of them arrange stones. Around a burrow entrance they stack nodules and shell plates into a low, deliberate ring, and when a storm comes they pull the shell across the entrance like a door and hold it shut with their arms. They carry a half-shell from place to place beneath their bodies, walking on the tips of their arms, a small gray thing hauling its house across the dark. It is not much, but it is the closest thing to craft in the place. Among the rings, if you look, you can sometimes find a nodule flaked to an edge, or a bone splinter jammed into a crevice as a wedge, put there for no reason you can see and clearly on purpose.

## What Was Here Before

The plain was once dry. Under the clay, in places the storms have scoured, you find things that did not fall from above. A tooth, long and curved, mineral-black. A rib of some large grazer, fossilized to the color of the nodules. And, once, half exposed in a terrace edge, a flake of stone: grey flint, one face conchoidally struck, its edge still sharp despite the age, the work of a hand that stood on this ground when it was ground. There is no other trace. No walls, no hearths, nothing built. Just the edge, and the water that came over the land and stayed. Whoever made it is now the same slow sediment as the shells.

The octopuses do not know what it is. But one has wedged a flake like it into the wall of her ring, edge outward, and the silt gathers against it and the storms pass over.

## The Rules

Nothing stays put. Everything is always arriving: snow from above, silt from upslope, cold water from the pole, hunger from every direction. And everything is always being buried. The floor is a slow accounting, and every object on it is a debt not yet settled. The oldest stable thing is the plain itself, the fact of the flatness, the final form the land takes after everything has been worn out of it. Within that flatness everything moves, in the dark, at the speed of settling, and it does not stop.

When the next storm comes you will hear it first, a rising hiss from upcurrent, and the sparks will begin to fly, and the small pale creatures will pull their doors shut. The floor will lift into the water and the water will become the floor. In a season it will be quiet again, and the plain will look exactly as it did before, and it will not be the same plain at all.
