# The Loaf Country

The plateau is the oldest floor left standing. Everything that once stood above it has been ground down and carried away, and what remains is a tableland of quartz sandstone about four thousand meters up. It stretches so far in every direction that the eye gives up before the land does. Erosion has finished its work here. The old towers are rounded stumps, the ridges are low swells, and the cliffs at the margins fall away in walls a kilometer high, streaked black and rust where water has been sliding down them for longer than there have been animals to watch it.

Nothing on top is left to break. What covers the rock is a mat.

## The Mat

The ground is a continuous living skin of moss, liverwort, bromeliad, sedge, and fine wiry shrub. Its roots have knitted together over millions of years into a spongy weave that is in places three or four meters thick. It is dense and springy and honey-colored underneath, where it decays into a dark peat that has never quite been soil. Walking on it feels like walking on a mattress laid over a cellar. Each step sinks a hand's depth, and the sound of the step goes down into the hollows and comes back changed.

The mat is green, but not one green. There are olive hummocks, a red-bronze moss that grows in the wet lows, a silver-gray lichen-crust on the ridges, and pale cushion-plants like sheep's wool that hold a cup of water in their crowns. Bromeliads the size of cartwheels are the only real vertical features. Their leaf-rosettes form standing pools, and each pool is a small ecosystem of its own.

There are no trees, or almost none. In the sheltered hollows a few stunted, gnarled things reach the height of a person, hung with so much moss they read as green shapes, not as trees. The plant world here has gone sideways and downward. It has become the substrate, the architecture, and the soil at once. In the empty regions, the mat is the only thing you can see for hours, rolling out in low swells under an enormous sky.

## Water

Water is scarce in one way and everywhere in another. There are no lakes and few streams. What the plateau has instead is cloud. Most mornings are clear and sharp, and by midday a white bank pushes up from the lowlands and the plateau is swallowed. The mat drinks the fog directly. Every blade beads. Cushions swell. The air tastes like cold metal and rain-on-stone.

Real rain comes in the late afternoon, some days but not all, in heavy short bursts that turn the whole surface to a hiss. The water disappears at once into the mat and reappears hours later, seeping in thin clear threads from its edges. Where the mat has torn away from the rock, in fissures and old collapse-pits called *wells*, the water falls straight down into black quartz shafts and is not seen again for a hundred kilometers. The wells are the plateau's only plumbing. Some are the width of a doorway, some the width of a village. The biggest breathe cold air from their throats, and a stone dropped in one takes seconds to answer.

## Sound

Above all, the plateau is loud.

It's a physical loudness, a pressure you feel in the sternum and the teeth. Frogs live by the hundreds of millions in the bromeliad cups and the wet moss, tiny things, some no bigger than a fingernail. At dusk they begin, and the whole surface rings with a thousand overlapping pitches: clicks, whistles, bell-tones, long electrical trills. Insects come in on top of them, cicada-like drummers and stridulating crickets in layers, so that the sound has a floor, a middle, and a ceiling. Beneath all of it is the mat itself. It is hollow, and it amplifies. The choruses sink into the peat and resonate back up through your boots, and the ground seems to be singing.

There are quiet minutes. They come when a hawk crosses, or when a shadow of cloud passes, and then the whole plateau goes silent in a wave that runs outward for miles, and every animal holds its breath. It never lasts. Somewhere a single frog starts again, and the noise rebuilds from that one point like a fire catching.

## The Overwhelming Life

The fauna is not visible so much as continuous. Every handful of moss turned over is crowded: springtails, mites, beetles with bronze wing-cases, pale spiders, flatworms, translucent shrimp-like things in the water of the leaf-cups. Slugs the size of forearms drag silver trails across the cushions. Lizards with throat-fans the color of embers run the ridges. Hummingbirds sit on the bromeliad spikes, thousands of them, their wings a constant thrum under the frog-noise.

The large animals are few and far apart, and this is where the emptiness lives. A low herd of small, long-haired grazers, something like a cross between a vicuña and a deer, moves in slow lines across the open mat, grazing the sedge tops, and can go a whole day without another large creature in view. Foxlike hunters follow them at a distance. Raptors turn on the thermals off the cliffs, and snakes lie along warm rocks. Between these big presences is nothing: kilometers of green, wind, and sky.

So the place is both empty and overfull. Tall space, scarce large things, and a crush of small life underfoot.

## Motion

Nothing here is still for long. The cloud comes and goes daily. The mat swells with water and shrinks again, and you can hear it creak. Frog choruses rise and fall by the hour, the hummingbirds shift with the flowering, and slow tides of insects move along the hollows. Once a season a great bloom passes across the plateau: the bromeliads all flower within a few days, a red wave rolling from the west, and the air fills with nectar-drinkers and drifting petals.

Now and then a section of mat gives way over a hidden well. There is a soft tearing sound, a slow slump, and a new black mouth opens where none was. The plateau is old, but it is not finished. It is still being unmade from the inside, very slowly.

## The Marks of People

A few people come here, seasonally, and they leave almost nothing. Cairns of flat quartz mark the safe paths across the thin places. Here and there stands a lashed platform of poles and woven fiber, a hunters' blind or a sleeping perch above the wet. Grass ropes span the smaller wells, worn dark by hands. There are fire-rings on the rock outcrops where the mat won't burn, and stone scrapers and bone needles lie half-swallowed in moss. Someone once ground grooves into a flat rock to sharpen tools, and the grooves are now full of green.

The tools are bone, flint, plant fiber, and fire. The people use them lightly and mostly leave. The mat has taken back nearly everything, and in a few more years it will have taken the rest. Past the last cairn, walking west into the fog with the frogs roaring up through your feet, you see how little of you the place needs.
