# The Throat Terrace

The terrace sits on the shoulder of a dead mountain, high enough that the sky above it is a darker blue than it should be. It is no wider than a village: a few hundred paces across, held on three sides by a broken rim of black rock and on the fourth by a drop into cloud. No one could say what lay under it. Nothing you can see is ground. Every surface is something a hand made.

## The Pipes

The terrace is packed with fired clay tubes, set upright, shoulder to shoulder, thousands upon thousands of them. Some are thin as a forearm and some wider than a barrel. The tallest, near the center, stand three times the height of a person. They were coiled by hand, rope of clay upon rope of clay, smoothed with a thumb and a wet stone, then fired in pits that left blackened ovals still visible on the outer skins. Thumbprints survive in the clay. Each tube has a slightly different lean, a slightly different lip, a slightly different wall thickness, and all of them were mortared to their neighbors with a gray-yellow paste that has since gone as hard as the pipes themselves.

Where the tubes ran out, the makers used other things. Between the pipe-clusters are lanes and pockets filled with jars, bowls, pot-shards laid in herringbone, and rammed pavements of crushed brick. The floor of every lane is fired clay. The low walls are stacked unmortared tiles. Even the rim, where the black rock shows, has been chinked and plastered and stepped with clay until the stone is more repair than stone. If any natural substrate remains, it lies beneath meters of made things.

There is a little metal, not much: bands of hammered copper, green-black, wrapped around the tallest pipes to stop their cracking, and a few pegs and rivets. It is early, patient, cold-worked metal, thumb-thick and uneven. Some bands have corroded into crusts of blue-green salt that flake at a touch.

## The Sound

The terrace is deafening, and the sound has no source you can point to.

A hot dry wind rises from vents in the mountain beneath, fissures the builders found and channeled. It does not gust and it does not fall. It has blown at the same pressure for longer than the clay has been weathering. It enters the base of the pipes through buried flues, and each pipe answers with its own note. Because the pipes are packed so tightly and have worn so long, the notes do not sit apart. They have pulled against one another and locked together. What comes out is a single huge sustained chord, low and thick, with a shimmering upper layer like the edge of a struck bell that never fades.

At the outer edge the sound is a wall you lean into. Deeper in, it stops being heard and becomes a condition of the body. Your ribs hum. Your teeth hum. Fine dust jitters on every flat surface in a pattern of standing ripples, tiny ridges fixed in place where the vibration is strongest and smooth where it cancels. The dust has drawn the map of the sound, and the map has never changed. Speech is impossible. Even thought seems to be pitched around the chord. The only usable communication is touch, and perhaps the builders used it: the lanes have worn hand-grooves at shoulder height, polished glassy by palms, running like a rope from entrance to center.

The sound is stable because erosion has tuned it. Over unmeasured ages the wind wore each pipe's lip and bore until every one settled into the one shape that resonates best with its neighbors. Cracked pipes silted up and fell silent. Ill-fitting ones were ground out of tune until they matched or failed. What remains is the equilibrium, the final form, and it has held so long that nothing new can happen to it.

## Light

The light is dim, even at noon. The builders roofed much of the terrace with overlapping clay slabs laid like scales, and where the roofs give out, the tall pipes shade the lanes. Light arrives in thin slits and blades, amber because it passes through the warm dust that hangs everywhere, so that every beam is solid-looking and cut cleanly through the air. The clay itself is many colors, ochre, rust, bone, smoked brown, and there is a soot-dark band at every pipe mouth where the exhale has stained it. Little reflects. The whole place glows faintly like the inside of a kiln that has just gone cool.

At the rim there is a gap in the roofing where the sky can be seen: a hard, thin, violet-blue, with the cloud sea far below, bright and silent, oddly noiseless compared with the terrace.

## Warmth and Dryness

The air is warm, the warmth of the mountain's breath. The clay is warm underfoot and warm to the palm, and near the vent-mouths it is almost too warm to hold. It is utterly dry. Nothing has been wet here for ages; the pipes were fired without slip or glaze and take no stain from any moisture. Salts have crept up out of the vents and bloomed on the pipe rims as white crystalline frost, thin needles of sulfate and chloride that grow no larger than the vibration allows. It is the only thing here that resembles growth, and it is not alive.

Nothing lives. There are no plants, no lichen, no insects in the dust, no birds on the pipes. The noise and the heat would forbid it if nothing else did. Nothing crawls, nothing rots. It is difficult to imagine anything ever dying here either.

## Ancient and Worn

The pipes are old beyond counting. The tallest have lost their tops to slow scouring by wind-borne grit, and stand with smooth, rounded, slightly flared mouths like horns polished by a tongue. The lanes are worn into troughs. The stacked tiles have slumped and fused. Surfaces that were once sharp-edged and patterned now show only faint ghost-ridges: spirals, dot rows, and chevrons pressed in wet clay by the makers and softened by time into something more like the memory of decoration.

Nothing has fallen in a very long time. Whatever was going to collapse already has, and the survivors are those that fit. The terrace has reached the state where further erosion has little left to take. It settled, and it stays.

## The Center

At the middle of the terrace is a low round court, the only open space, perhaps ten paces across. Its floor is a spiral of fitted clay tiles, worn nearly featureless. Around its edge stand seven great pipes, the widest of all, each bound with several copper bands. In the court the chord is at its purest and the dust ripples are at their sharpest: seven concentric rings, fine as fingerprints, unmoving.

Nothing is left there. No bones, no altar, no tool. Whether this was a temple, an instrument, a warning, or a work of pure devotion to a note, the terrace does not say. It only continues, warm and dim and loud, holding the one sound it has held since before anyone remembers, in a place where everything was made by hand and everything has finished changing.
