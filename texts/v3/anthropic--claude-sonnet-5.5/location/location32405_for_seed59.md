# The Wringing

The town sits on a shelf of a mountain at the height where cloud stops being weather and becomes a resident. Everything above it is forest going up into white. Everything below is forest going down into more white. The shelf is no more than four hundred paces across, a tilted bench of old gray andesite, and the town has filled all of it, spilling over the lip in stilted houses that lean out over the drop like birds on a wire.

## Ground

The rock is old and has stopped arguing. Its corners were rounded away long ago and its faces are dished and fluted by ten thousand years of runoff. Water still works at it, but only to keep it as it is. Steps cut into the stone by the first settlers have worn into shallow bowls, each holding a hand's depth of clear water and a slick of green. Walking up the main stair is a sequence of small wet steps, each ringing faintly underfoot.

Nothing here is dry. The rock weeps from seams, from the undersides of ledges, from fern-choked cracks, and the weeping is collected. Channels a thumb wide are carved along every wall and stair edge, leading to gutters, then to troughs, then to cisterns. Below the cisterns, water drops in a series of stone basins down the whole height of the town before it leaps off the shelf as a thin, permanent waterfall, audible from every doorway.

## Plants

The town is not built in a forest so much as inside one that has agreed to be lived in.

Tree ferns stand in every gap, trunks furred with root-fiber, fronds arching over the lanes to form a green ceiling at the height of a second story. Above them are the true trees: gnarled, low, twisted things with trunks you can't see for moss, bromeliads, orchids, filmy ferns, and hanging beards of lichen the color of old brass. Every branch is a garden and every garden has its own smaller gardens. Bromeliad cups the size of washbasins hold standing water with tadpoles in them.

Roofs are thatch of split reed and grass, and they are alive. Moss takes them within a year, then small ferns, then sedums, and the roofs grow thick and soft and sag with rain. A household is judged by how it keeps its roof: cut back enough that the timbers don't rot, left green enough that the thatch holds. On a slope it's difficult to tell which house is the neighbor's roof and which is your own garden.

Walls are stacked stone chinked with clay and moss, and roots run through them so the masonry seems to be held by green fingers. Some houses are built into living trees, floors lashed across low forks, walls of woven cane plastered with clay. Bridges cross the lanes at every height, made of bamboo poles lashed with vine cord, and the vine is replaced before it fails because the whole town knows the sound a failing cord makes.

Everything smells of wet leaf, black soil, crushed stems, woodsmoke that can't quite rise, and a sweetness from some white flower that opens at dusk in the thousands.

## Air and light

It is cool, the sort of cool that never becomes cold: breath faintly visible in the morning, wool worn all day, skin always slightly damp. The light is ordinary daylight, gray-white and diffuse, falling through the canopy in green-tinted shafts wherever the mist thins. Sun arrives in bursts. Twenty minutes of warm glare will steam every roof in town, and then the fog comes back up the valley in a slow wall and the temperature falls three degrees in a breath.

## Animals

The fauna is the town's real population, and the people are a minority in it.

Frogs come first, because the sound does. By dusk there are a dozen species calling: a metallic tink like hammered nails, a rising whistle, a wet rattling trill, a deep hollow bonk that sounds like someone knocking on a barrel. They fill the gutters, the cisterns, the bromeliads, the thatch. They sit on doorposts. People step over them without looking.

Birds are everywhere. Hummingbirds work the flowering vines in the lanes like small angry machines, and their wings make a stitching hum. Flocks of green parakeets tear through in shrieking loops. Wrens in every wall scold in liquid bursts. Toucans and larger fruit-eaters hop through the upper canopy, dropping seed hulls onto the roofs, which patter down all day. At dawn the birdsong is close to solid, and speech has to be pitched through it.

Below that, everything else: rodents in the thatch, small marsupials on the beams at night, bats pouring out from under the eaves at dusk in a dark stream. Ants run in lines as thick as rope along the walls. Beetles the size of thumbs blunder into lamps. Spiders string webs across the lanes each night, and each dawn the children walk ahead with sticks, gathering the webs like laundry. Moths, midges, and mosquitoes rise from the standing water in thick drifting clouds. Households keep smoky fires half for warmth, half for the insects.

The domestic animals are small: guinea pigs kept in floor pens and whistling for greens, dogs with muddy bellies, hens roosting on the lower rafters, a few pack llamas on the upper terraces who spit at everyone. Nothing is quiet. Something is always moving in the walls.

## People and craft

Tools are stone, bone, wood, fired clay, and a little hammered copper, valued and repaired for generations. Knives are obsidian or edge-ground flint. Hoes have wooden hafts with stone blades lashed on with wet rawhide that shrinks tight as it dries. Weaving is the great art: baskets, fish-traps for the stream, rain capes of layered reed, cord of every thickness. Pottery is fired in pit kilns on the one flat dry spot in town, the kiln court, which everyone jealously schedules.

Roughly six hundred people live here. Houses stand shoulder to shoulder, roofs touching, lanes narrow enough that two people carrying loads must turn sideways. The market occupies the widest terrace and is nearly continuous: potatoes and tubers in a dozen colors, dried frog-fat, cane baskets, charcoal, bundles of bark medicine, live fowl in cages, feathers, salt brought up from below by carriers who groan under it. Bargaining is loud, laughter is loud, and it is all tuned to be heard over the water.

## Turbulence

Nothing stays put. Weather turns four times in an afternoon: a hard rain that hammers the roofs into a roar and turns each lane into a stream, then mist, then a rinsed and dazzling minute of sun, then rain again. Each downpour sets the town moving. Shutters bang, people run to unblock gutters with long hooked poles, children plunge into the flooded lanes, and the frogs, thrilled, redouble.

The slope is restless too. Small slides let go after long rains, taking a garden terrace or a corner of a wall. The response is immediate and communal: a horn call, a chain of hands, stone and basket and log dragging to fill the wound. Rebuilding is not treated as misfortune. The town is always being rebuilt, and no wall is older than the last flood, though the stones in it are ancient.

Growth is also a kind of violence here. Left alone a lane closes in six weeks. Vines throw themselves across gaps in a night. A fig seed lodged in a roof-ridge will, in a decade, split the house. The town's work is clearing and cutting, hauling and lashing, all day, every day, against a green tide that isn't hostile and simply doesn't stop.

## Rules

- Water must keep moving. A blocked gutter is a public offense, and anyone who sees one clears it.
- Nothing is left uncovered at dusk, when the mosquitoes come up.
- A bridge is inspected by the person who last crossed it, aloud, so others hear.
- When the horn sounds twice, everyone drops what they are doing and goes to the wall that is falling.

## Night

Evening drops the temperature fast. Lamps of fat and wick are lit in windows and glow amber in the mist, each a soft blur. The frogs reach full chorus, and beneath them the waterfall, the drip from every leaf, the shifting of animals in the thatch, and someone drumming lightly on a hollow log to call a child home. The air is close and cold and smells of smoke and white blossom, and the whole shelf hums, wet and green and loud, like a hive that has learned to hold a mountain.
