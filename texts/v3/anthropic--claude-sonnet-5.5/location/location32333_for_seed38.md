# The Drowned Green Shoulder

The mountains here were once sharp. You can tell only from the way the land is shaped, the way an old man's stoop tells you he was once tall. Across a plateau the size of a small country, at an altitude where the air should be thin and cold, the peaks have been ground into long, rounded swells, and every swell is wrapped in living green. Nothing bare survives above a few places where the granite pushes through like a knuckle under skin. Those knuckles weep constantly, dark threads of water running down the stone into moss that has been drinking from them for longer than the moss has had a name.

## The Mat

The ground is not ground. It is a mat of plant: roots, stems, fallen fronds, and the compacted dead of countless generations. It lies eight, twelve, in places thirty meters thick, and it never touched the rock beneath it. It floats. Under the plateau's wide basins, water has collected in the hollows between the ancient domes, and the vegetation has crept out over it and thickened until it bears the weight of forests. When you walk, the surface gives slightly, then returns. Standing water wells up around your ankles in the low places, tea-brown and warm as a bath left standing. Pressing a hand into the moss releases a clear cold thread from below.

The water beneath rises and falls on a cycle of about seven years. Nobody has counted it, but the trees have. Trunks show rings of collar-scars, bands where the bark has thickened and toughened after being lifted and lowered. In a high-water year the whole forest rides a body's height higher, and paths that were solid become soft channels. In a low year the mat settles, cracks open, and the underwater dark shows through in long seams, breathing cold air and a smell of iron and old leaves.

## The Architecture

There is no single canopy. The forest is built in tiers, and each tier is its own country.

At the bottom is the *floor-garden*: liverworts, filmy ferns, and pale sundew-like carpets in a green so saturated it looks wet even where it is dry. Above it stand the *pillar trees*, thick-boled and fluted, with buttresses that flare into shelves. Their trunks are so hung with epiphytes, ferns, orchids, and strangling figs that the tree itself is hard to find. The plants growing on them have plants growing on them. Above the pillars, at forty or fifty meters, the crowns lock into a roof of overlapping leaves, each as broad as a door and slick with condensation, tilted to shed water in long ropes.

Between tiers, the forest builds its own walls and rooms. Lianas as thick as thighs run from crown to crown and have fused into curtains. Some hang in pleats, some have grown into spiral stairs around a dead trunk, and some have knit into vaulted hollows where a tree fell and the vines closed the gap. You move through a maze of living hallways that no one designed, and the maze rearranges itself over decades.

## Light

The light is dim and green-gold, filtered and refiltered until it has no direction. At midday it is the light of late afternoon. Shafts occasionally break through where a giant has fallen, and they show up like solid objects, full of drifting spores and small insects, and the plants beneath them grow in a hurry, a bright green scar closing over a decade.

At dusk the floor takes over. Fungal threads in the rotting wood glow a faint milk-blue, and certain moss cushions hold a green phosphorescence that fades if you disturb them and returns in an hour. It isn't enough to read by. It is enough to tell where the path is, and it makes the trunks look as if they were breathing out light.

## Weather

The plateau makes its own weather. Warm air rises from the mat all day, carrying more water than it can hold, and each afternoon it builds into a slow, bruised bank of cloud that sits on the domes like a lid. The rain that follows is not violent. It comes in long, steady curtains that last for hours, soft on the upper leaves and loud only where it drips from the second tier onto the third, a diffuse ticking that sounds like a thousand clocks slightly out of sync. At night the cloud descends into the forest itself, and by morning every surface is beaded.

The air is warm, close, and smells of leaf-rot, wet stone, sweet resin, and an undertone of something like crushed mint that comes from a shrub nobody has bothered to name.

## Fauna

The place is crowded with animals, though you see few of them at once. It is a quiet crowding. Most creatures here move slowly and speak seldom.

Long-limbed grazers, pale as driftwood, walk the mat on broad, splayed feet that spread their weight, browsing the floor-garden in herds of six or eight. They lower their heads to the moss and lift them again in unison, as if listening. Above them in the pillar trees live things like sloths but stranger: slow, mossy, with too many joints in their fingers, who spend whole weeks on a single limb. Green algae grows in their fur, and a small pale moth lives only on them.

Snails as long as your forearm leave silver roads across the buttresses. Beetles carry lantern-spots on their backs that pulse when they are frightened. Frogs in the epiphyte cups spend their whole lives in a pool of rainwater forty meters up and never touch the ground. Birds are few and mostly silent, large and dark, with pouched throats, and they call only at dawn: a single hollow note, three beats apart, that carries for kilometers through the wet air and is answered somewhere far off, then again farther, so that the sound seems to travel across the whole plateau.

Otherwise the forest is mostly quiet. There is dripping, a soft settling of the mat, a rustle of something turning over in the leaf-litter, and the occasional deep, slow groan of the floating ground shifting on the water below.

## The Marks

Scattered through this forest are traces of something that is not the forest.

A cairn of river-stones stands in a clearing, each stone the size of a fist, stacked in a cone and greened over so completely that it might be a natural mound, except that the nearest stone is a day's walk away. A ring of six trunks has been trained, long ago, to arch and meet overhead, and it is now a single organism with six roots and one crown, its floor swept clean by animals who come to rest there. Along one ridge, notches in a line of trunks have been stretched by growth into mouths, rows of them at the same height, too regular to be accidents and too old to say who made them.

Deep in one basin, a set of broad stone steps descends into the mat and disappears under the moss. The steps are worn hollow in the middle by feet. Whoever climbed them is gone, and the water has long since claimed the bottom. The forest has not taken them apart. It has simply covered them, gently, as it covers everything, and the grazers sometimes stand on the top step, looking down.

## The Rule of the Place

Nothing here ends. Things are absorbed. A fallen giant becomes a nursery, then a hillock, then a layer in the mat. An animal that dies in the moss is gone in a week and is a flower in a season. Even the stone is being slowly dressed in green. The mountains had their violent youth, and this is their long, warm, wet old age, in which everything they once were is being kept, soft and layered and dimly lit, under the rain.
