# The Drowned Terraces of Ossel Tarn

Four thousand meters up, in a bowl of glacier-scoured granite, there is a lake with a town at the bottom of it. The town is not built on the lakebed so much as grown out of it, and it has been growing for longer than anyone has counted.

## The Basin

The bowl is about a mile across and shaped like a cupped hand. Its walls are old rock, rounded and grooved by ice that left long ago and worn again by the slow work of water. Nothing in the stone is sharp anymore. Ledges have sagged into gentle shelves and cracks have healed over with mineral crust. Where the walls meet the floor there is a soft apron of silt and plant litter, forty feet deep in places and dark as tea.

The water is cold and very clear, the temperature of a cellar, a few degrees above the point where it would turn to ice. Thin mountain sunlight goes down through it in wavering shafts. At the bottom of the deepest part, sixty fathoms down, the light is still the color of weak green glass, enough to read by. Overhead the surface is a hammered pewter ceiling. Wind moves it, and the pattern of that movement slides across everything below as a net of bright lines.

## The Green Mass

Everything is plant. The floor is a mat of stoloned, rooting grasses, dense as felt and a foot thick, and above it the growth rises in tiers.

**The long ropes** are the first layer. These are kelp-like stems as thick as a thigh, anchored in the silt and lifting a hundred feet toward the light. They are olive and amber, with a glossy, slightly rubbery skin. Gas bladders the size of melons sit along their length like knots in a string. The stems lean together in the current, and a slow flowing seems to move through the whole forest even when nothing is happening.

**The lattice plants** are the second layer. They are woody, branching, and fine-leaved, and they grow in strict cross-hatched sheets. Left alone they would sprawl. Here they have been taught, over generations, to grow in flat planes and arches, and the taught shapes have become habit. Shoots that turn the wrong way are simply not fed.

**The moss-carpets** are the third. They coat every stone and every old stem in green fur. Water passing through them makes them stream like hair.

There is almost no bare surface anywhere. Even the dead is plant. Fallen ropes lie across the floor as a pale, slowly decomposing mass, and new shoots come up through them.

## The Town

The settlement is called the Terraces by those who live in it. There are perhaps three hundred structures in the whole basin, but they are spread out. Between clusters lie open meadows of short grass, glades where the current runs fast and clear, and stretches of empty blue where the kelp has been left unplanted.

A **house** here is a hollow made by cultivating a ring of lattice plants, training them into a dome, and letting them thicken over decades. Roofs are woven from split rope-stem and lashed with cord twisted from plant fiber. Some houses are four generations old, with walls thick as a man's arm, dark and hard as horn. Others are new, green and pliant, still being bent into shape. Small stone weights hold the openings closed against silt.

**Streets** are lanes cut through the kelp, kept open by pruning. They are marked with rows of white river stones and by strings of carved wooden floats. There are steps too, worn into the granite along the basin's edge, and a few long stairs of stacked slate leading down from the shallows into the deep town. The oldest steps have hollows in them like the bowls of spoons.

The central **court** is a wide shelf of stone where the lattice plants form an arched vault, a green nave sixty feet high with shafts of light falling through the gaps. It is the nearest thing to a cathedral the place has. People gather beneath it to trade, argue, and tend the great stems.

## The People

The inhabitants are lean and pale, with broad hands and long feet, and they live and work fully in the water. Whether they were once something else, or whether the lake made them, is a matter for their oldest stories and not for this description. They wear woven kelp-cloth, and belts hung with shell tools. Their metal is scarce, a little soft copper and some worked meteoric iron, and most of their edges are flaked stone, ground shell, and fish bone. They use knives, adzes, awls, drop-spindles, and fiber looms weighted with stones. Everything is made by hand and mended by hand.

Their work is gardening and repair. They prune, train, patch, replant, and haul. They tie new stems to old ones with cords that will outlast the person who tied them. A child's first task is to learn which shoot to leave and which to cut, and this is treated as the whole of wisdom.

## Sound

The place is loud, far louder than any dry town, because water carries sound quickly and without softening.

**The gas bladders** pop and creak as they expand and contract with the daylight. In the morning the whole forest fills with a slow crackle, like fire catching, and in the evening with a settling groan.

**Hollow reeds** hang in rows at every crossing, and people strike them with wooden mallets to signal. Each stroke rolls through the water for a mile. There are calls for meals, calls for meetings, calls for a lost child, calls for a death, and calls that only mean *I am here*. The reed-notes overlap and blur into a constant bell-like wash.

**The animals** add their own choruses, and these are the loudest of all.

## Fauna

Life is abundant, as thick in the water as it is in the plant mass.

- **Drummer-fish**, silver and barrel-bodied, gather in shoals of thousands and beat their swim bladders with special muscles. At dusk the whole basin thrums with a low pulsing note that people feel in their teeth and ribs.
- **Clicking shrimp** live in every clump of moss, so that even the quiet glades hiss with a fine, dry crackle.
- **Ribbon eels** wind through the kelp trunks, striped pale and black. They sing when they mate, a thin rising whistle that the townspeople say brings good harvests.
- **Lantern snails** cling to the stems in colonies, each with a faint glowing patch on its shell. In the dim depths they mark the lanes with strings of pale light.
- **Big slow trout**, the length of a man, drift over the meadows and are never hunted. They are counted as members of the community and known by name.
- **Ducks and divers** come through the surface in season, a distant paddling of feet above the ceiling of light.

Herds of small grazing crustaceans move over the floor, keeping the grasses trimmed. Their tracks form patterns in the silt that the gardeners read to know where to prune.

## The Unusual Thing

One feature of the basin is odd, and everyone here accepts it.

At the center of the deepest hollow stands a single **bell-tree**, a plant older than the houses. It has a trunk as wide as a hut, gray and fluted, and its crown is a cluster of great hollow pods that hang like inverted cups. When the current passes through them they resonate with a tone so low that it seems to come from the ground. The tone changes through the year as the water cools and warms. The townspeople tune their reeds to it and mark their seasons by its pitch. They say that if the bell-tree ever falls silent, the lake has stopped remembering the town.

## Change

Things move slowly here, in cycles measured in decades. Ice forms on the surface each winter and lies for months as a white lid, dimming the light to a milky glow. The kelp dies back a little and the drummer-fish go deep and quiet. In spring the lid cracks, groaning and booming, sounds that travel down the whole water column. The ropes send up new growth, and the gardeners begin again.

A house takes forty years to close its dome. A lane takes twenty to fix its course. A child born in the town may see one wall finished in a lifetime. Nobody minds. The place is old, settled, and balanced, worn into a shape it keeps. What changes is the green, and the green changes at the speed of a hand slowly turning.

At day's end the light thins to violet, the lantern snails brighten along the streets, the drummers begin their pulse, and the reeds ring out one long evening note across the water. The bell-tree answers from the deep, and then the whole basin holds a single sound, cold and green and loud, for as long as the light lasts.
