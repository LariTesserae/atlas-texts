# The Weeping Stair

At this altitude the sky is a hard, dark blue overhead and pales to milk at the horizon. The sun is a white coin with no warmth in its face, only brightness. Everything wet throws it back. The whole ridge-country glitters as though the light had been poured on and left to run.

## Ground

The stone is layered quartzite, pale as bread crust, banded with seams of dark basalt that have weathered into rounded, fluted forms. Long ago the mountain stopped being jagged. It has been rained on, frozen, and rained on again for so long that even the spires are soft-shouldered, furrowed like old candles. Deep grooves run down every face, and each groove carries its own thread of water.

Nothing here is dry. The rock weeps from a thousand pores and seams. Water beads at the lip of every overhang and gathers into drops that hang a long moment before falling. Where the seepage has run for ages, it has built **terraces**: stepped, lipped basins of pale mineral crust, each holding a shallow pool. Their colors are milky turquoise, bottle green, and a thin clear grey like breath on glass. The terraces climb the slopes in staircases hundreds of steps long, wall over wall, each rim scalloped and delicate. The mineral builds at a rate you can nearly hear, a faint ticking as the crust thickens.

Between the terraces the spires stand close, so close that in places you turn sideways to pass. They are separated by slots and chimneys and small hanging valleys, each with its own stream, so that walking is like moving through a dense, dripping library of stone.

## Air and Cloud

The air is cool, thin, and clean, with a mineral taste like a coin held on the tongue. Cloud is born here rather than arriving. It condenses out of the wet stone in the warm part of the day, gathers in the slots, and rises in slow columns that catch the sun. You walk in and out of it. In cloud the world shrinks to arm's length and glows white. Out of it, everything is razor sharp and the far ranges stand clear across impossible distances, layer behind layer of blue.

Because the light is so bright, the wet surfaces throw haloes. Small rainbows sit in the spray of every fall, and they are not always where the sun says they should be.

## Sound

The place is loud. Thousands of small waterfalls run all at once, a broad hiss that never resolves into single voices. Under it are the deeper sounds. The mineral crust grows into hollow tubes and horns, and when wind crosses them they boom, moan, and cough at different pitches. Some slots have become natural gongs, and a drop of water falling into them rings a clear bell-note that lingers. Stones shift and clatter in the streambeds. Now and then a slab lets go from a high face and the report rolls around the spires for a long time.

## Life

There is almost no life. No animal crosses the stone and nothing calls. On the wettest walls there is a thin film of rust-orange and violet, a slick living skin that seems more stain than organism. In the deepest pools a fine green filament sways with no visible source of current. That is all. The ecology here is chiefly the water's own: crust growing, ice splitting, minerals moving from high to low and sometimes the reverse.

## Marks

People, or something like them, have been here, though not many and not recently. The remains are scattered and small.

- **Cairns** of flat stones, stacked into leaning columns as tall as a person. They are placed at junctions, always where the path could go two ways. Many have been rebuilt so often that their lower courses are fused with mineral crust.
- **Steps** cut into the rock with stone picks and hammer-dressed edges, worn into shallow dishes. They are shorter than a modern stride.
- **Copper pegs** driven into cracks, now green, marking lines across sheer faces. Some hold the remains of woven cord made of spun mineral fiber, like grey glass wool.
- **A single hut** of corbelled stone, roofed with slate slabs, with a hearth that has no fuel around it to explain it. Its floor is dry. It is the only dry place, and no one can say why.
- **Carved spirals**, tally notches, and rows of small cups cut into flat stone to catch rain. The cups are always full.

The tally marks never agree. Count one row twice and you get two numbers.

## The Rules That Have Failed

Reality here is worn thin, as the stone is worn. It is not hostile, only unreliable, and it fails in consistent ways that the old marks seem to allow for.

**Water sometimes climbs.** In certain grooves, called seams in the cairn-marks, a stream runs downhill for a stretch, then bends and runs *up* the same slope, beading against gravity. It does not seem to notice. It rejoins itself above, so that some streams are loops.

**Pools hold other hours.** Look into a terrace pool and you may see a sky that is not the one above you. It may be dusk, or a sky full of slow stars, or a low orange sun, while overhead it is noon. Reaching in, your hand meets only cold water.

**Sound runs ahead of cause.** The gong-slots ring a moment before the drop falls. If you shout, the echo returns before you finish. In some places the echo returns in someone else's voice, saying something almost like what you said.

**Distance folds.** A spire that looked an hour away is reached in minutes. A cairn you passed is sometimes ahead of you again. The paths agree on one thing: those that climb always arrive lower than they began, and those that descend end higher. Those who cut the steps seem to have accepted this, and every stair is worn smooth in both directions.

**Shadows disagree.** At some pools a shadow falls toward the sun. On certain afternoons the light seems to come from the rock itself, and nothing casts a shadow at all.

## Cycles

The land is active. Each day the cloud builds, breaks, and builds again. At dusk the temperature drops, the seepage slows, and the terrace rims grow a fringe of ice that chimes as it forms. Sudden hail hisses through the spires, and in the following minutes every surface steams. Every so often, with no rhythm anyone has recorded, the whole ridge-country draws a breath. The falls all falter at once, the horns go silent, and the air seems to lean inward. Then it exhales in a roar of water and a swell of stone-note that lasts a full minute, and every cairn shivers.

Above, the mountain goes on rising past what should be its summit, into thin bright air. From the highest terraces the horizon curves visibly, and the shining, weeping, ringing country lies below in every direction, though you have been climbing for what feels like days and, by every stair, arriving lower.
