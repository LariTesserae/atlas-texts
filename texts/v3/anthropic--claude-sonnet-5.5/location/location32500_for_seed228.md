# The Wellwork

You come down into it by a stair that was cut so long ago the steps have become a slope of shallow cups, each holding a palmful of warm water. The air thickens as you descend. At the top it was ordinary morning. By the thirtieth step it is bathwater-warm, tasting faintly of iron and struck flint, and the noise has grown from a murmur to a wall.

The place is a collapse basin about three hundred paces across, sunk into the earth like a thumbprint in dough. The rim is a ragged ring of sky, very far up. Almost none of the light down here comes from that ring.

## The light

The light comes from the Sheets. Across the void hang broad membranes of a clear, faintly greenish material, tilted at angles no wind could hold. They are anchored by hair-fine filaments to hooks in the walls, and none of them sags. They catch the daylight from above and fold it, sliding it edge to edge, so that the whole basin is lit from several directions at once with a flat, shadowless white. You can read the grain of a pebble at forty paces. Where a Sheet passes through a column of mist the light bends into standing arcs, hard-edged rainbows that stay put for minutes and then shatter when a gust arrives.

The Sheets are old. Mineral crust has built up along their anchor lines in stalactite fringes, and the crust has been polished by spray until it looks like part of the cave. Nobody knows whether the Sheets were built to light the basin or whether the basin is just where they ended up. They hum at a pitch you feel in the roots of your teeth.

## The water

Everything is wet, and nothing is at rest.

The floor is a braided flat of shallow channels over pale, scalloped limestone, ankle-deep and warm. It runs in every direction and, where the slope is too gentle to decide, in loops. Ten or eleven Columns rise from it. These are tapering pillars of grey-white stone, wider than a house at the base and pierced by dark bores, and they are the source of the noise. Water climbs them inside. You can see it in the translucent thin places, a bright pulse rising against gravity, and at the top it is thrown outward in great fans that arc across the basin and fall as heavy rain in a different place from where they left.

So the basin has weather of its own. It rains sideways and it rains in ribbons. A cloudburst falls on one quarter while the opposite quarter sits in dazzling mist, and a minute later they trade places. The floor channels carry the returning water back to the Column feet, where it is drawn in with a sucking roar.

Every thirty or forty minutes there is a Turn. The Columns fall silent, one after another like a struck chord dying. For a few breaths the only sound is dripping. Then they start again, often out of order, and the rain re-plans itself. Anything on the floor when the channels reverse is carried a little way and set down again. The pebbles here are sorted into neat drifts by size, the same sizes in the same places, from a long history of Turns.

## The sound

The sound is the strongest fact of the place. It is a roar with structure inside it. The Columns are hollow and tuned, each to a different note. The result is a huge, slowly drifting chord over the rushing, and when the spray fans cross, the chord bends. Under the roar are smaller sounds. There is a constant ticking of drops on the Sheets, and the stone walls give back a hiss like frying. Now and then a filament snaps free of its hook with a crack like a whip, drifts, and reseats itself somewhere new. You will not hear your own voice unless you put your mouth to someone's ear. People who work here speak with their hands and tap patterns on the stone.

## The ground and the walls

Erosion has had a very long time here, and it has finished most of what it wanted to do. The walls are fluted into deep vertical scallops, each one wide as a doorway and smooth as the inside of a shell. Weeping seams of pale flowstone hang like drawn curtains, dripping steadily through all the turbulence. The lower walls carry old tide-lines, dark bands at several heights, from times when the basin held still water. The tide-lines are slightly tilted. This suggests the whole basin has been very slowly warped, or that the Columns once pulled harder.

Fine warm silt gathers in the lee of every obstacle. It is the color of wet ash and slick as soap. Where it dries at the edges into cracked plates, it curls and lifts on the updraft, and so a soft grey snow rises at the margins of the basin while the rain falls in the middle.

## Life

Vegetation is sparse. It grows where the spray is gentle: dark green mats of algae on the flowstone, black-stemmed ferns in wall pockets, and a single pale, leafless tree clinging to a ledge, its roots running down the rock in a hundred wet cords. Cushions of moss hold water like sponges and drip when you press them.

Fauna is rare. There are flat, slate-colored amphibians the length of a forearm that lie in the warm channels with only their eyes above the current. When a Turn comes they all tilt their heads at once, a few seconds before the sound changes. There are also long-legged, colorless birds that stalk the rim of the floor and eat the amphibians when they can. On some days there is a single moth-like thing the size of a hand, translucent and shivering, that rides the updrafts and never seems to land.

## What has been built

Structures are scattered, and most look like part of the erosion.

Near the eastern wall stands a squat, rounded hut fused from a single stone, its doorway worn into a smooth oval by the water that runs through it. There is nothing inside but a bench and a socket in the floor. Something used to sit in the socket.

In the center of the floor is a ring of seven low pylons, waist-high, chipped and glazed over by mineral. They are not part of the Columns' plumbing, and they respond to your presence. When you stand in the ring the roar dims slightly around you and a thin, clear cone of quiet forms. Inside it you can hear your own breathing. It lasts about as long as a held breath, and then the sound floods back.

High on the north wall, well above any reach, is a dish half sunk in the flowstone, tilted toward the rim of sky. It moves. Once an hour it swings a few degrees, and then the Sheets adjust their angles in answer, one by one, in a long ripple of shifting light.

## The rule underneath

This is a machine that has outlived its makers and is now mostly weather. The Columns keep the water in motion, the Sheets keep the light on, and the dish keeps the Sheets aimed. The water has to keep moving, because when it settled in the past the basin silted up and began to close. The turbulence is upkeep. If the Turns were ever to stop, the floor would fill with silt and the walls would slump inward, and the basin would heal over like a wound.

The old builders seem to have understood that a hole has to be worked to stay open. So it roars and rains and flashes, and every forty minutes it goes silent for a moment, as though listening for anything that might be left to listen.
