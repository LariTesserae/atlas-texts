# The Kiln at Ashgrave Col

Near the top of a dead volcanic massif, some two thousand meters up where the air is thin and the stars are hard, there is a hole in the mountain. It is a caldera-chamber about the width of a village square, roofed over by a lid of old lava that failed to collapse. A single shaft, no wider than a doorway, opens in the ceiling. Almost nothing comes through it, not even light, because the shaft bends. The place is kept in near darkness, and it is hot, and it is full.

## The Rock

The walls are basalt, but weathered into something more like bread crust: blistered, folded, and glazed brown-black where ancient gas rose through it. Millennia of heat and dry wind moving through the chamber have rounded every edge. Nothing here is sharp. Ledges sag like melted wax, and the floor is a slope of cobbled clinker that steps down toward the middle in broad terraces, each one hotter than the last. The stone has settled into its own shape and is done changing, except for the slow dust of its surface.

The air is dry enough to crack lips. The temperature at the rim is that of a summer oven left ajar. At the center it rises past what a human body would tolerate for long. The heat comes from the ground, from cracks in the lowest terrace where the mountain's remaining fire breathes out dry gas, mostly carbon dioxide with a thread of sulfur and something sweetish and mineral. There are no pools. The only water is what the salts hold: white and yellow crusts on the walls that pull a film of damp from the air at the cooler hours and give it up again when the heat returns. Those crusts glisten faintly, and everything in the chamber has learned where they are.

## The Dark

The dark is not total. Deep in the floor the fissures glow a dull, sullen red, like the last of a banked fire. That light does not reach the walls. What lights the place is the fauna itself: a cold, greenish-white luminescence that flickers in the bodies of the small things when they are startled, so that light travels across the slopes in slow ripples. Each ripple is a rumor of disturbance passing outward from wherever something died, or fed, or turned. It is the chamber's only weather of light, and it goes through the crowd the way wind goes through a field.

## The Crowd

Everything here is alive, and there is no bare surface. This is the fact that most defines the place. Every ledge, crevice, and sloping shelf is occupied, layered, and pressed against the next occupant. Where the rock is visible, it is because a moving carpet has parted for a moment.

**The Lamplice** are the lowest and most numerous: pale, flat, soft-shelled things the size of a fingernail, packed body to body in sheets over the warm rock. They graze the black film of chemosynthetic slime that coats the stone near the vents, a living varnish fed on gas and mineral, the nearest thing here to a plant. When a lamplouse is frightened it flashes, and the flash passes to its neighbors, and the sheet ripples with green-white light. That is the wave you see traveling across the slopes.

**The Bellows-Beetles** are heavy, domed, the size of a fist, and bronze-backed, with wing cases ridged like corrugated iron. They live in the middle terraces, wedged into pits they have worn in the clinker. They feed on the lamplice and on the constant drift of dead things. Their trick is to hold their spiracles closed for long stretches and then exhale in a single hot huff. Because they do it together, on some rhythm nobody taught them, a slow breathing runs through the whole chamber every few minutes. It is felt more than heard.

**The Threadwalkers** are tall for the place: stilt-legged, long-bodied, gray as dust, moving on legs like drawn wire over the backs of the sleeping crowd without disturbing it. They are the hunters. They do not chase. They stand, sometimes for an hour, and then one leg comes down through a gap and something is lifted out. Their prey does not scream. Very little here has ever screamed.

**The Sextons** are small, blind, and black, and are the reason the place has not drowned in its own dead. They move in lines. They take the husks of the fallen, along with everything that comes down through the shaft (moths, blown seeds, the occasional dead bat, bird-feathers, pollen-dust from the valleys far below), and carry it to the fissures at the center, where the heat dries and cures it. Everything eaten here has been dead for a while, or is about to be.

**The Wall-Mimics** are the strange ones. They are broad, flat, and mottled, pressed into the rock and matching its blistered texture so perfectly that it is a shock when a patch of wall exhales. They grip the vertical faces and hang there for years. Nobody sees them eat. What is known is that lamplice which wander too close to certain "stones" do not come back, and that the stones are slightly warmer than the rock around them.

## The Rules

Life here sorts itself by heat, like sediment. The coolest rim, near the shaft, belongs to the pale, the small, and the new. The mid-terraces belong to the heavy grazers. The hottest ground at the center belongs to nothing living, only to the Sextons, and only in their shifts. The Sextons work in short bursts and retreat, and their black bodies, when they reach the cool ledges, steam very slightly. There is a line on the third terrace, an irregular contour of exactly the right temperature, which almost nothing crosses in either direction. Threadwalkers stalk along it. Lamplice sheets end at it as sharply as a shoreline. Beyond it the ground is too hot for the young and too cool for the fissure-dwellers. It is a no-man's-land, and a scoured, empty stripe runs around the whole chamber there, like a ring left by a vanished tide.

Nothing here hunts by sound. Vibration through the rock is the real language, a constant fine trembling, the way a table trembles when a crowd sits near it. A Threadwalker's footfall is a note. A Sexton line marching is a slow drum. A lamplouse sheet startling is a sudden shiver, quick and thin. If you could put a hand to the wall you would feel a conversation you could never read.

## Structures

Scattered through the chamber are constructions no hand ever planned.

Along the mid-terraces stand the **Beetle-chimneys**: hollow columns as tall as a person, built from chewed pumice paste hardened by the heat into a glassy, slightly translucent mineral. They are ribbed and drooping, like candles left too near a fire. When the beetles breathe, warm air moves up through them and out with a faint, dry, reedy note, the loudest sound in the chamber, and still barely louder than a sigh.

On the eastern wall is a single **honeycomb** of paper-thin cells, three meters across, abandoned. Something built it once, some earlier tenant now gone. The cells are empty and clean. Nothing has settled in them, though everything around them is packed to the edge. No one knows why.

And at the lowest edge of the third terrace, half-buried in clinker, is a **ring of stacked stones**, waist-high and perfectly round, that seems to have been laid by someone. It is not made by any animal in the chamber. Its stones are worn as smooth as the walls. It is the oldest thing in the place, and the crowd has grown around and over it without touching the inner circle, which is bare. The bare floor inside is the only empty ground in the whole chamber, and it is cool.

## The Cycle

Every few hours the fissures pulse. A long, deep exhalation of dry gas rolls up the terraces. The heat rises, the beetles go still, and the lamplice sheets climb toward the rim in a slow tide, flashing green as they go. The Sextons run for the cool ledges. The salt crusts dry and flake. Then the pulse ebbs, cold air sinks slowly down the shaft, and the whole crowd creeps back down to the warm ground again, and the salts begin to sweat.

There is no day or night here, only this tide of heat and its retreat. Above, through the crooked shaft, the mountain's real nights and days pass unseen, and only their leavings come down: a moth, a seed, a feather, one more small dead thing for the Sextons to carry to the fire.

## What It Feels Like

Standing at the rim, you would feel the heat on your face like an open hearth. You would hear almost nothing: a papery rustle, the thin reed-note of the chimneys, the tick of stone cooling and warming. The floor below would seem to be moving, and then you would realize it is not the floor. A green shimmer would cross the slopes, silent and slow, and die at the hot line. Something with wire legs would place one foot down, and hold it there, and wait.

The chamber is not hostile and not welcoming. It is simply full, and finished, and continuing.
