# The Warm Sink

The land above is scrubby limestone plateau, and you would walk past the place without knowing it. Then the ground stops. A crack the width of a road opens, and below it the world continues a hundred and forty feet down, green and steaming, like a lid lifted off a pot.

The sink is a collapsed cavern, perhaps four hundred paces across, and everything in it is shaped by the fact that it is a *below*. Cold air does not enter it. Heat does not leave it. The floor breathes out warmth from somewhere deeper, and the walls sweat it back as a fine continuous condensation. It is hot the way a closed mouth is hot.

## The walls

The walls are the old limestone, but you rarely see stone. Where it shows, it is fluted and scalloped by a very long slow argument with water, ribbed like the inside of a shell, the color of tea-stained bone. Most of it is dressed in moss, then in liverworts over the moss, then in small ferns rooted in the liverworts. Every ledge holds a shelf of soil the width of a hand, and every shelf holds something. Vines drop from the rim in ropes as thick as a wrist. Most never reach the floor; they end in pale, blind tips that sway in the updraft and turn, slowly, toward the seams where the warm air leaks.

Light arrives from the rim as a green-gold haze, already filtered through the hanging vines and the thick canopy beneath them, so that by the time it reaches the ground it is dim as late evening. Shafts of it come through only at midday, and only for a little while. They are visible because the air is full of mist and spores and the drifting scales of moths, and each shaft looks solid enough to lean on.

## The canopy beneath the sky

The floor is a second forest with its own ceiling. Tree ferns and old figs with buttress roots like poured wax reach up thirty or forty feet, and below them the growth thickens into layers: palms, then broad-leaved understory plants with leaves the size of doors, then a knee-deep tangle of ferns, then moss, then the black wet duff. Nothing has room to be alone. Leaves overlap, trunks wear epiphytes like coats, and branches have fused where they rubbed together over centuries. You move by turning your shoulders and ducking, and every pushed leaf leaves a damp print on your skin.

The green is not one green. It runs from near-black in the undergrowth to a translucent yellow where a shaft of light passes through a young frond, and the undersides of some leaves are a dusty violet.

## Water

Water is not plentiful here, but it is everywhere in small amounts. It does not fall as rain from a sky. It gathers on the ceiling of leaves as condensation until the drops grow heavy, then lets go, so that the whole sink has a slow internal weather. Around mid-afternoon, when the warmest air has climbed and met the cooler rim, a fine rain begins inside the canopy and lasts perhaps twenty minutes. It falls unevenly, hard under the fig crowns, hardly at all in the clearings. Then it stops, and the leaves tick and drip for an hour afterward.

There is also one pool, a dark oval in the center, shallow at the edges and dropping to a depth nobody has measured. It is fed from below by a seep that runs slightly warmer than blood. Its surface is skinned with duckweed and bright with floating petals, and now and then a slow ring spreads across it from something rising to breathe. Around the pool the ground is soft and the roots are exposed like knuckles. From the pool a thread of stream runs out across the floor and disappears, without any ceremony, into a crack in the rock. The pool rises a few inches after each afternoon rain and falls again by morning.

## Creatures

The fauna is abundant past reason, and almost none of it is loud. This is the strangeness people notice first: a place this crowded with life ought to roar. But the wet moss and the leaf-mass swallow sound, and over generations the animals here have leaned toward quiet. Frogs communicate by throat-flashes, pale bars of light like winking lanterns, and by drumming on hollow stems too softly to hear more than a few steps away. Birds are small, and they hiss and click rather than sing. What you hear is the rustle of thousands of bodies moving on leaves, the tick of drops, a faint continuous pattering like fingers on paper, and the occasional wet crack of a fig splitting under its own weight.

Life is layered as densely as the plants:

- **On the walls**, cave swiftlets nest in rows in the scallops, thumbprints of mud and spit, and the birds come and go in near silence, dropping into the sink like thrown seeds.
- **In the canopy**, tree frogs the color of wet slate, lizards that run across trunks and hang motionless under leaves, and enormous brown moths with wings marked like open eyes, resting in clusters. Small mammals, something like pale-bellied gliders, cross the gaps overhead in the evening.
- **On the floor**, millipedes as long as a forearm move in slow processions, and leaf-cutter ants run trails you could trip over. Beetles glint. Blind white crickets live in the cracks, and something long and low with a wet gleam works the edge of the pool, a salamander or a very patient snake.
- **At the top of the chain** are the things you rarely see: a spotted cat, lean and low, that lives in the ledges and hunts the gliders at dusk, and a broad pale heron that stands in the pool's shallows so still that moss begins to think of it as furniture.

Predators and prey are both constantly in motion. A day here is a sequence of small deaths and small births: a frog vanishing mid-blink into a snake's jaw, ants stripping a fallen moth to lace in an afternoon, a new fern unrolling in the space where a tree fell. Nothing lingers.

## The unusual thing

The one feature that makes the sink feel like itself and not just a lush hollow is the *lantern moss*. It grows in the deepest crevices, where the sun never reaches, and in the dim it gives off a faint cold blue-green glow, barely more than the memory of light. By itself it would be nothing. But the frogs' throat-flashes match it, and so do the moths' eyespots when they catch it, and in the last hour of dusk the sink fills with a slow, coordinated flicker, points of pale light answering one another across the dark. It is not language exactly. Nobody knows if it counts as anything. But the flashing carries in waves, and the waves always begin on the east wall and cross to the west, as if the sink is exhaling.

## Human traces

People have been here, long ago, and left little. Halfway down the north wall a stair is cut into the stone, each step dished by feet, the lower flights swallowed by roots and moss. On the floor near the pool stands a low ring of stacked stones, waist high, half toppled, with a fig growing out of its middle and splitting it gently apart. Farther in, on a dry flat face of rock under an overhang, are handprints in red ochre, small and large, some overlaid on others, now so faded they read as warm shadows on the stone. A few broken potsherds turn up in the duff, curved and thumb-marked, and the ants carry bits of them around. There are no tools, no metal, nothing that suggests anything but patient hands. The place has absorbed these marks the way it absorbs everything, and they sit in it without feeling out of place.

## The feel of it

To stand here is to be wrapped, held in air the temperature of skin, dripping, breathing spores and green. The heat presses lightly and steadily. The light goes gold, then green, then blue, then the pulse of the lantern moss. Almost nothing makes noise, and yet you have the sense that if the sink were to be quiet, truly quiet, it would be because everything in it had stopped at once to listen to you.

The erosion here is not a story of damage. The stone has been wearing into this shape for so long that it is now more like a body than a ruin: scalloped, smoothed, softly furred. The sink is in balance with itself, always moving and always about the same, like a slow pulse. When the last light leaves the rim, the walls sweat, the drops fall, the frogs light up, and something crosses the dark water with hardly a ripple.
