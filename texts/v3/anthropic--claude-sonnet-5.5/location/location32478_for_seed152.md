# The Skylight Ward

Nothing here has had time to be worn down. The rock is still sharp.

You come to it from above, through a ragged hole in a plain of young basalt, a place where the roof of a lava tube fell in a few decades ago. The plain is black and nearly bare, with a few ferns in the cracks. The hole is perhaps thirty paces across. Below the lip, the world changes temperature. The air that rises out of it is warm, wet, and smells of split wood and cold pennies.

The collapse left a rubble slope of angular blocks, each still holding the crisp edges of its breaking. Some faces are matte and gray. The older surfaces, the tube's original ceiling, are glazed with a thin skin of glassy black that drips in frozen stalactites, finger-length and slightly curved, where the lava sagged before it stiffened. None of them are rounded. Nothing has weathered them. If you run a hand along one it will cut you, faintly, the way paper does.

## The floor

At the bottom is a pocket of ground about the size of a village green, lit from above by the skylight. It is dim even at noon, because the hole is small and the rim is crowded with the leaning fronds of tree ferns and a few thin pioneer trees, so the light arrives green and already spent. In the morning a single blade of sun crosses the floor and moves like a slow hand across a clock. For the rest of the day the light is the color of the inside of a leaf.

The floor is a mix of the tube's original ropy lava, ridged like coiled cable, and a thin soil of ash, leaf-fall, and insect leavings, only a hand deep. It is dark and fine, and it supports a startling amount. Moss lies over the rope-ridges in cushions. Ferns come up in ranks where the soil is thickest. Vines have let themselves down from the rim on long cords, some reaching the floor and rooting, some hanging short and swaying an arm's length above the ground. Around the old cracks, where water seeps, there are broad-leaved plants with translucent stems, pale-veined and beaded with moisture.

## Water

Water is present but never abundant. There is no stream. The rock is warm from somewhere below, so a faint steam breathes from a few fissures in the tube wall, and where that steam meets the cooler air near the skylight it condenses on everything. Leaves are always damp, and drops gather on the tips of the glassy stalactites and fall at unhurried intervals, each landing on a particular leaf or stone that has learned its rhythm. Beneath the driest-looking slab there is often a thumb-deep pool the color of tea.

Rain arrives now and then, in the afternoons: a warm shower that reaches the floor thinned and delayed, first as a rustle in the canopy, then a few fat drops, then a minute or two of steady patter. The whole ward changes for a while. The insects shelter, the frogs come out, and the ash on the ground goes from gray to black and smells sweet. Then it stops, and for an hour afterward the place drips from every edge.

## The city

What makes this pocket a settlement is not anything you or I would build. It is a city of termites, and it is old for a place this young. It began with a single founding pair who dropped from a fig tree on the rim a few years ago and found the warm dark. Now there are perhaps thirty mounds, scattered across the floor with wide empty ground between them.

They are not the gray spires of open savanna. Here they are built of ash, chewed wood, and saliva into tall, narrow, fluted towers the color of dark chocolate, some knee-high, some taller than a person, each leaning slightly toward the light. They have ridged flanks, like folded cloth, that shed the dripping water. The largest stands against the tube wall, and its upper stories have grown around a glassy stalactite, so that the black glass now runs through the mound's body like a nail.

Between the mounds, the termites have made roads. These are shallow, covered galleries, tunnels of the same dark paste laid over the rock, curving for long distances from one mound to the next, and out to the base of each tree fern or fallen log. In places they cross a gap as a bridge, an arch just wide enough for a line of insects, thin enough that morning light comes through it amber. It is a town in the sense that matters: connection, division of work, stored food, walls, ventilation. Its streets are mostly empty of anything you could see, and its life is behind the walls, where the fungus gardens are kept damp and warm in chambers like cupped hands.

If you press an ear to a large mound you hear a dry, continuous whisper, like distant sand being poured. It is the loudest thing in the ward.

## Other residents

The termites feed the rest of the place, directly or not.

- **Small geckos**, pale and translucent-bellied, hunt the mound bases at dusk, their throats visibly pulsing. There may be a dozen in the whole ward, each with a territory of a few square paces.
- **A green-brown frog** the size of a thumbnail lives in the moss and calls only after rain, one soft note, more a click than a song.
- **Mud-dauber wasps** have built their tubular nests in the shelter of the rock overhang on the sunward side. They work in the blade of light, dull metallic blue, fetching mud from the seeps in small rolled pellets.
- **Orb spiders** string webs across the gaps between fern trunks, and their webs are always beaded with condensation, so at certain hours the place is hung with tiny lenses, each showing the skylight upside down.
- **Centipedes**, long and orange-legged, patrol the litter and raid the outer galleries, and are the reason the termites' roads are covered.
- **A single snake**, slender and green, lives in the rim vines and comes down twice a week. The geckos know its schedule better than you do.
- **Bats** roost in the deeper part of the tube, where the roof is intact. They leave at dusk through the skylight, one after another, so quietly that you notice them only as brief gaps in the stars.
- Beetles, small moths, springtails in the wet litter, and cockroaches with the color and sheen of polished mahogany, which clean the leavings of everyone else.

The fauna is abundant but seldom crowded. You could stand for a minute in the middle of the floor and see nothing move, and then realize the moss at your feet is alive with small travelers and a gecko has been watching you from a stalactite the whole time.

## Motion and rules

This is a place in the middle of becoming, and everything in it is doing something slowly.

- Ferns unroll their new fronds in the morning, visibly, if you sit long enough.
- Roots from the rim trees are working into the ceiling cracks, and each year a little more rubble falls. The ward is not stable. Every few months a slab lets go somewhere in the dark of the tube with a single dry clack.
- The termites extend their galleries by a hand's width a week, always toward new wood.
- Moss claims a fresh block of rubble within one wet season. Lichens follow, gray-green crusts on the basalt, and behind them, in a decade, the first soil that is not just ash.
- The fig on the rim drops fruit that the bats and geckos both want. A seedling has started on the floor from one of those falls. If it lives, the ward will eventually be roofed over and darker, and the light-loving ferns will fade. The termites will do well.

The rules are simple. Warmth comes from below and light from above. Water gathers where the two meet. Everything that lives here lives in the seam between them.

## Sound

It is very quiet. When the wind moves the canopy on the rim you hear it as a slow hush overhead, but the air in the pocket barely stirs. What you hear instead is small: the drip from the stalactites; the dry whisper of the mounds; a gecko's claws on a leaf; the click of the frog after rain; the faint tick of the wasps' wings in the blade of sun; and, now and then, a fallen slab somewhere in the dark, with the pause afterward as every creature stops and waits, then resumes.

## Evening

As the skylight dims to a paler green and then to a dark blue-gray, the ferns fold slightly and the geckos come down. The steam is more visible now, rising in thin white threads from the fissures and drifting up toward the hole, where the first stars appear framed by leaves. The bats begin to leave. The mounds are darker than the dark around them, and the covered roads carry their traffic unseen.

You realize that the whole place is about as big as a room, and that it has a city, a climate, a food chain, a future. It is very young, and nothing here has yet had time to be worn smooth.
