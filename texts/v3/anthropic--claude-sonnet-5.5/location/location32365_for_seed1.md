**The Tallow Flats**

The land here was worn down before there were words for wearing. What remains is a basin so old that even its ruin has been ruined. The mountains that once fed it are a few rounded gray knuckles standing out of the mud, smooth as river stones and weeping from every pore. Water beads on them and runs in threads, and the threads braid into the standing water below. The whole plain is a hand's depth above its own groundwater. Press a boot down and the ground gives up a cold brown lens of liquid, then slowly takes it back.

It is cold in the way that wet things are cold, a dampness that gets into the seams of the body. The sky is a low ceiling of pewter cloud, and the light under it never gets past late dusk. Fog condenses on everything and drips off everything. Nothing grows tall. The only plant-like life is a felt of black and rust-colored film over the shallows, a fungal skin that lifts and settles as if breathing.

**The occupation**

Every part of the plain is taken. This is the first thing you understand and the last thing you stop noticing. Between the weeping knuckles there is no bare ground, only bodies. Armored, many-legged grazers move in shoulder-to-shoulder tides, each hauling a dome of fused shell. Long pale things thread through the mud under them, and hard-backed things ride the pale ones. Above, in the fog, wet-winged animals hang in such numbers that they work as a second ceiling, shedding a constant fine rain of grit and droppings.

Nobody built the industry here, but it runs all the same, and it is enormous. It is a production line laid across forty miles of mud, and its workers are animals.

- The **grinders** are broad, low crustaceans with plated mouths like millstones. They drag the silt through themselves in slow rotary strokes and leave it as fine gray flour.
- Downstream, the **washers** stand in braided channels with their fringed limbs spread like sluice gates. They comb the flour through the current, and the heavy red grains sink into their baskets.
- The **smelters** are the strangest. They are fat, warm animals the size of carts, with a gland-furnace under the ribs that glows dull orange through the skin. They swallow the red grains and exhale them as bright, hot beads of iron, which drop hissing into the water.
- The **stackers** are tall, thin, patient things. They gather the beads and pile them in long ridges, and the ridges are always slightly ahead of the tide. The tide takes them, and the plain is rimmed with rust-colored shoals that are also its garbage and its product.

None of this is a structure. The smelters do not live in a building and the stackers do not follow a plan. The line is simply what happens when enough animals are packed close enough, and it has run without pause for longer than the hills have been wearing down.

**The sound**

The Flats are loud. There is no true quiet anywhere, only degrees of noise. The grinders make a deep, continuous grumble, like a mill turning under the floor of the world. The smelters vent through resonant throat-chambers, and when one of them exhales, the sound is exactly a steam whistle, thin and rising. Hundreds do it out of step, so the air is full of long chords that drift and beat against each other. The stackers click their hard limbs together in counted rhythms that ripple down the ridges like signals down a wire.

Over everything there is the hum. Electric organs, a kind of eel-tissue in the mud animals, discharge constantly. The fog above the plain is faintly visible from the sparks, a blue-white flickering that stitches the low cloud from underneath. The air smells of ozone, wet iron, and warm animal. Touch a puddle and your fingers ache and go numb.

Every so often the whole plain changes shift. Nobody signals it, but every whistle and click stops or changes pitch in the same moment. For a few seconds the roar drops to the sound of water and a great many animals breathing. Then the pitch climbs back and the mill starts up again.

**Where the world gives out**

The weirdness is not a decoration. It is a property of the ground, and it gets worse as you go.

The first sign is the sound. Near the seams, you hear a smelter's whistle before it inhales. You hear the click of a stacker's limb a moment before the limb has moved. Cause and consequence are a little out of order here, and the animals seem not to mind.

The second sign is the water. In patches a few dozen paces wide, the rain falls upward, cold beads rising from the mud and vanishing into the fog. Puddles reflect a sky that is not the one overhead. One shows a bright, high, empty blue, another a night with unfamiliar stars, and the animals wade through both without hesitating. Shadows fall toward the dim light, not away from it. In some places the mud stays wet and hot, in others dry and frozen, with no line between them, only a change in the way your feet feel.

The third sign is the count. Whatever you clear away, the plain refills. A slaughter of grinders, a mass die-off, a gap opened by a storm surge: each is closed in moments by animals that were not there before and cannot be traced to anywhere. The population is a constant. The Flats do not hold a number of animals, they hold a fullness, and it is maintained as a law of the place, not a fact about it.

The last sign is distance. Walk toward a gray knuckle of rock and it stays the same size while the ground under you changes, from mud to shell to iron shoals to something with no surface at all, only a depth like the inside of a bell. The seams are where the rendering thins. You can see through the mud there, and through the animals, to a pale gridded nothing that hums at a slightly lower pitch than everything else. It is not an absence. It is what the packed and roaring world is packed *against*.

**The rules, as best they can be told**

- Nothing is idle, and nothing is empty.
- Water is always a hand's depth away, and rises to meet you.
- The line never stops, because it has no beginning to return to.
- What is made is carried off by the tide, and what the tide carries comes back as rust-colored cloud, and the cloud rains it back on the grinders.
- If you stand still long enough, the animals will begin to process you. It is not hostile. You are silt, you are iron, you are warm, and you are in the way of the flow.

Somewhere in the fog a smelter starts to whistle, and its note arrives a second before it draws breath.
