# The Level

The place is a basin about six hundred paces across, and everything in it is plant. The ground is a living mat of sphagnum moss laid over its own dead, which is laid over its own dead, down to a depth nobody has measured. The mat is the terrain, the water table, and the memory of the place all at once. Walk on it and it gives a hand's width under each step and slowly fills your footprint with brown water, which then slowly clears.

## The shape of it

The land here was once mountains. That is hard to believe now. The rim of the basin is a low rounded shoulder of ground, no more than the height of a house above the moss, and the same soft profile continues in every direction for miles. Rain, ice, and wind have taken the old range down to its roots, and the roots are worn smooth. What is left is a wide, shallow dish where the last of the drainage gave up and stood still. Water arrives faster than it can leave, so the dish filled, and the moss followed the water.

The surface is nearly level, but not quite. It rises very slightly toward the center, a barely perceptible dome, because the living moss holds more water than the margins do. The whole bog is a lens of wet plant, slightly convex, like a held breath.

## What is growing

The moss is the ground, in colors that shift over short distances: rust-red, ochre, a green so yellow it looks lit from below, and near the pools a deep wine-brown. It grows in hummocks and hollows a foot or two apart. Threaded through it are the other low things: cranberry vines hardly thicker than thread, sundews holding their red-haired leaves flat against the moss, cotton-grass in white nodding clusters, bog rosemary, and sedges in tussocks that stand up like dropped mops. Lichens crust the few dead twigs. Where the moss is oldest, it has domed up into ridges shoulder-high, and their sides are stitched with liverworts.

There are no trees in the middle. A few stunted pines and birches, sixty years old and knee-high or waist-high, stand scattered at the edges where the peat thins. Their trunks are the width of a wrist. Their roots cannot go down, so the trees go nowhere. Above the mat there is only open air and cool gray-white sky. That is why the place feels empty even though every square inch beneath your boots is alive. All the substance is at your feet, and everything above it is space.

## Water

The water is moderate, not abundant. Nothing floods and nothing dries out. Small pools stand in the hollows, black and perfectly still, some the size of a table and a few as wide as a barn floor. They are the color of strong tea because of the tannins the moss leaches, and they reflect the sky so cleanly that the edge of the moss seems to fold over into a second sky below. The pools have no inlet or outlet. They are windows onto the water table.

Rain comes as a fine drizzle several times a week, seldom hard, and the air holds a constant cool dampness that beads on wool and on the spiderwebs strung between the sedges. Mornings begin with a low mist that lies in the hollows and burns away only to about knee height, so walkers seem to wade. The temperature stays cool all year: a sweater's worth in summer, a coat's worth in winter, and the frost is brief when it comes.

## Sound

The place is loud. Nobody who has been there disputes it, though people expect silence from a bog.

From April until late summer the pools hold frogs, and their chorus rises and falls across the basin in overlapping waves: clicks, low rattles, and a slow dripping trill. Behind that are the insects, a steady high shimmering drone of midges over the wet moss, with the occasional deeper buzz of a bumblebee or hoverfly crossing it. Snipe drum overhead, their tail feathers making a bleating flutter as they dive. Curlews call their long bubbling rising notes. Redshanks scold everything. A colony of black-headed gulls nests on the largest hummock and argues with itself all day. At dusk the frogs get louder rather than quieter, and the whole basin thrums.

The fauna is abundant, but it is small and dispersed. In a whole day you may see only a dozen animals, and hear ten thousand. Dragonflies patrol the pool edges. Pale spiders sit in the sundews. Voles run in the moss on paths they have kept open for centuries. A single fox crosses at intervals, always by the same line, and a heron stands in one of the pools in the morning. The animals are numerous in the way a crowd is numerous when you are inside a fog. The volume is enormous and the visible population sparse.

Beyond the rim there is another sound, low and continuous, and it comes from the century you are standing in. Somewhere over the shoulder of the ground, three or four miles off, there is a rolling mill and a rail yard. Its noise carries in over the flat wet country with no hills to stop it: a deep rhythmic pounding, the long groan of freight couplings taking up slack, and now and then a steam-whistle or a diesel horn. Overhead, a line of high-tension pylons crosses the far edge of the basin on its way from a power station to a city, and in damp air the wires hum at a steady pitch. None of this is inside the bog. Nothing has been built on it, because the moss will not hold a foundation and the ground is worth nothing to anyone. The industry is only a sound and a smudge on the horizon, and the frogs are louder than all of it.

## Rules of the place

The bog obeys a few rules that have not changed in ten thousand years.

**It does not grow up or down.** Moss grows at the top and dies at the bottom at very nearly the same rate. Peat accumulates about a millimeter a year, but the dome has reached the height where its own weight and drainage balance. The surface today is at the same level as it was when your grandfather's grandfather saw it.

**It keeps what falls into it.** The peat is acidic and has almost no oxygen, so nothing rots properly. A leaf that fell in the fourth century looks like one from last autumn. A vole that drowned during some earlier reign still has fur. Old pollen, seeds, and beetle wings lie in layers, so the bog holds a legible record of every summer since the ice left. Nobody digs it. It is too small to matter, and the record simply sits.

**It is stable.** The mist forms, thins, and forms again. The frogs start when the light lengthens and stop when the air cools. The pools stay at the same level in wet years and dry years, since the moss soaks up the surplus and gives it back. There are no floods and no fires, and the hummocks stay where they are. A person who left for thirty years and came back would recognize every tussock.

## The feeling of standing in it

Light is ordinary: soft, diffuse, cloud-filtered, with no hard shadows. Objects have gentle edges. The colors are saturated because of the damp, even though the light is dim. On a clear day the sun makes the sundew hairs flash, and each drop of water on them holds a tiny sun.

You stand on a soft floor that is alive to the bottom. The horizon is a low, worn line, and above it the sky takes up most of what you see. The pools are dark and the frogs are deafening. Beyond them, almost lost in the noise of the marsh, the distant foundry keeps up its pounding. The bog does not respond to it, and it will not respond to whatever the foundry turns into.
