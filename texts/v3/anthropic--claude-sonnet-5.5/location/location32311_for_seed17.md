# The Tablelands of the Struck Bell

## The Shelf

The land is a plateau, and the plateau is under water. It is a table of black basalt the size of a continent, raised so high on its foundations that the sea above it is only forty fathoms deep. On every side it ends in a cliff. The cliffs drop away into blue-black water that has no bottom anyone has measured. Standing at the edge, you look off a wall into a dark that seems to go down forever. Behind you the shelf runs flat to a horizon that is not a line but a fading of clarity, where water finally becomes too much water to see through.

The sea here is almost empty. It is very clear, very cold, and nearly without silt, so sight carries for a kilometer or more. Nothing blooms in it. The water holds no green and no drifting dust, only cold glassy blue, and the ground shows through it as if through air.

## The Ground

The rock is young. Lava came out of the shelf recently enough that the sea has not yet worn it. Pillows of basalt lie in heaps with their glassy rinds intact, black and bright like broken bottles. Sheets of ropy flow crust are frozen in the act of folding. Fracture faces are so sharp they cut the fabric of a glove. Between the flows lie drifts of pale hyaloclastite sand, the shattered glass of lava that met cold water, and the sand is knife-edged too, so that walking on it would sound like walking on crushed shell.

Nothing grows on it. No weed, no frond, no kelp. The only living surface is a thin film of bacterial mat in rust, cream and sulfur-yellow, staining the rock near the vents like spilled paint. Everything else is mineral: black glass, white sand, ochre stain, and over it all the light.

## The Light

Above, the surface is sealed by a lid of clear sea ice, kilometers wide. The sun comes through it as a diffuse, even, milky blue. There are no hard shadows, only soft ones pooled under overhangs. Where the ice has cracked and refrozen, bright seams run across the sky like veins in marble, and light falls down through them in slow pale blades. The pattern never changes. The seams have been in the same places as long as anything has watched them.

The cold is total and steady, just short of freezing. It seeps through anything that isn't made to resist it.

## The Sound

Nothing about this place is silent, and the noise is the first fact and the last.

Cold, dense, clear water carries sound almost without loss, and the shelf's own shape traps it. The sea between the ice and the basalt is a channel, a lid over a floor, and sound that enters it does not leave. It travels the plateau in slow circuits and gets stronger where the paths cross. The sound is not loud so much as total. It fills the chest and the teeth and the hollows behind the eyes. If you were there, you would not hear it so much as be inside it.

Its source is the machines.

## The Works

Scattered across the shelf, often a day's travel apart, are the works. There are perhaps a few dozen. Each is a single riveted hall of iron plate the color of dried blood, standing on footings sunk into the basalt. Each has a tall stack that once vented something and now vents only a wavering thread of warm water. The vents under them are their fuel. The works turn the temperature difference between hot rock and freezing sea into motion.

Inside each hall a great beam engine rocks on a pivot as long as a ship's mast. Its piston drives down into a cylinder, and every seven seconds the whole structure strikes a note like a struck bell the size of a church. That is the source of the deep sound. Between the strokes come the secondary noises: the shriek of steel bearings, the pouring roar of pumped water, the rattle of valve gear, the grinding of flywheels. The beat never stops or changes. The engines were built to run without tending, and they have.

Whoever built them left rails, short lengths of them, running from each hall toward nothing. There are ore carts on some, stacked with cut stone. A crane leans over a dock where no ship has tied up. A stair goes down a cliff face into the deep and disappears. There are no bodies, tools, or doors that were closed behind anyone. The works are unattended and complete.

## The Herds

The fauna is abundant, but the shelf is so big that abundance looks like sparseness.

The commonest animals are the drifters: pale, ribbon-bodied grazers three or four meters long, each with a fringe of soft fins and a wide flat mouth. They graze the bacterial mats and the warm sulfur films and move in herds of thousands, a slow white weather across the black ground. Their herds are kilometers apart. Between them is water so clear that a herd can be seen from ten kilometers away as a faint drifting smoke.

They keep time with the engines. Their tail-beats fall on the seven-second stroke, so a whole herd pulses in unison, the bodies bending and easing together like a field of grain in wind. Young drifters are born near the halls and raised inside the ironwork, where the warm outflow keeps them alive.

Behind the herds come the hunters. They are few, long-bodied and dark, slow and enormous, with mouths hinged like trapdoors. They hunt by feeling the herds' rhythm and waiting for it to falter. Smaller things live in the wreckage: armored crawlers in the boiler rooms, tube-worms clustered on the flywheel housings, pale fish so still they might be part of the machinery.

## The Rules

The world is stable in a strict way. The engines strike on schedule, the herds keep step, and the ice holds its seams. The lava has not moved in living memory, and the sea neither rises nor falls. What looks like motion, the drift of the herds and the sway of the stacks' plumes, is only a repeated pattern. If you came back in a thousand years you would find it exactly as it is.

One law of the place goes unremarked by anything living there: nothing on the shelf is ever quiet, and so nothing on the shelf has ever known that it is loud. The drifters have never heard silence. The hunters have never heard the stroke stop. When you leave, and the sea thins and the sound falls away behind you, what you notice is not how quiet it has become. It is that for the first time you can hear something small, your own pulse, still keeping time with the bell.
