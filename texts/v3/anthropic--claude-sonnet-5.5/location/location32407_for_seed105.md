# The Lantern Shelf

## The lid

Above everything is a lid of ice. This is a highland lake, and the thin air over it is cold enough that the surface skins over even in the warm months. Ice does not reach the bottom, though. Beneath it lies a water-world that runs on for leagues: a drowned plateau, a tableland the old mountains once held up to the sky and that the water later filled. The ice is a milky grey ceiling, bright at noon and dim by afternoon. Light comes through it as a faint, even pallor that fades within the first fifteen fathoms. Below that the light is a memory, and what you see you see by other means.

## The floor

The plateau is old, and it has settled into itself. Erosion has done its slow work and largely finished. Ridges are rounded like knuckles. Basalt shelves step down in broad terraces, each edged with a lip of pale silt that has slumped and re-slumped into soft scalloped curves. The sediment is fine and cold and the color of ash. It lies in drifts, and where the current runs over it, it forms ripples that hold their shape for years.

Between the terraces are wide basins, and some of them steam. Not at the surface. In the dark, mineral springs breathe up warm water, and the water shimmers where it meets the cold, like heat over a road. Rust-colored and yellow crusts collar the vents. The whole plateau is arranged around these warm places, and everything living gathers to them.

## The growth

The vegetation is lush, but it is not what you would expect. Nothing here needs sunlight. The growth feeds on the vents, on dissolved iron and sulfur and the slow warmth, and it has taken every shape a plant might. There are ribbon-forests that stand forty feet tall, each blade dark purple-green and glossy as wet slate, swaying in unison when the current turns. There are low mats of felt-like moss over the basalt, so thick that stepping on it would be like stepping on a mattress. There are hollow reeds in dense stands, each stem pale and jointed like bamboo. There are round, translucent bladders on stalks, full of gas, that hang in clusters like grapes.

The forests are sparse in the large view, since they gather around the vents and leave broad bare stretches of silt and stone between. Inside a stand, though, the growth is so dense that you push through it with your arms up.

It glows. When the water moves, the blade-tips flush a low, cold blue-green, an ember-light that runs in slow waves down the length of a forest and dies out. The moss flickers in patches. In still hours the plateau is nearly black, with only the vents faintly ruddy. When the water stirs, it lights in long soft pulses, as if something were breathing under a blanket.

No animal moves through any of this. There are no fish, no crawling things, no drifting jellies. Nothing grazes the felt and nothing shelters in the blades. The plants have the world entirely to themselves, and they have grown in the way things do when nothing eats them: unhurried, layered and overgrown, thick with their own dead. Old blades pile in brown drifts at the roots and slowly turn to a black paste that feeds the next season.

## The sound

It is loud. Water carries sound faster and farther than air, and this plateau is full of it.

The reeds are hollow, and when the current pushes through a stand they sound like an organ played by someone leaning on all the keys: long, wavering, reedy notes that slide and interfere and beat against each other. Different stands are tuned differently, by the width of their stems, so that the plateau has districts of pitch. One basin drones low, almost below hearing. Another shrills.

The gas bladders swell as they fill and burst with a sharp *tock*, releasing a string of bubbles that rattle upward. In a thick grove this goes on constantly, like rain on a tin roof or a fire of green wood. The vents contribute a deep bubbling churn, and where the seep gas comes up through silt it makes a sound like thick soup at a boil. Far above, the ice groans and cracks. Long booms travel through the water as the sheet flexes in the wind, and sometimes a sharp, pinging whip-crack races across the whole lake from one shore to the other and arrives, long after, as a fading tone.

You feel this sound in your chest and teeth. There is no silence to be had, only quieter districts.

## The overturning

The plateau has weather, though not the kind with wind. The lake stratifies. In calm months the cold heavy water settles into the deepest basins and the warmer vent-water pools above it, and everything is stable. Then something tips. A hard freeze thickens the ice, or a season of meltwater arrives. The layers destabilize and the water begins to move.

This is the Turning. Cold water pours off the plateau's edge in a slow, enormous density current, a waterfall inside the water, a moving slope of dark that takes days to pass. It roars. It drags silt off the terraces in brown veils and lays it down again elsewhere. It bends the ribbon-forests flat in one direction and then, when the counter-flow comes, flat in the other. Reed stands shriek. Bladders burst in thousands, and the whole plateau flashes with blue-green light from one horizon to the other, so bright that the ice overhead briefly shows its cracks like a lit ceiling.

Afterward the water clears. New silt covers the old. The forests spring upright, some torn, some newly rooted where a mat has been carried across bare stone. Everything settles into the changed shape and the ordinary noise resumes.

## The marks

Someone was here, and the traces are few. Someone who used stone and fire, and who worked the plateau before the water was this deep, or who worked in it when the lake was shallower. No one is left to say.

On a terrace above one of the warm basins stands a ring of stacked stones, waist-high, each hand-fitted without mortar. Silt has softened the outline. Inside the ring grows a single dense tuft of glowing moss. Nearby, a line of black wooden posts, thick as a thigh, stands driven into the silt in a row that goes nowhere in particular. They are furred with moss and ribbon-weed, and some lean. Between two of them hangs a length of net or cordage, twisted from long fibers, still faintly taut, still catching drift.

Along a bluff there is a stair. It is cut in the rock in broad, shallow steps worn dish-shaped by many feet, leading down from a shelf toward a basin. At the bottom is a hammered plate of native copper, green now, propped against a stone, with a hole punched through it at the top. In the shallow cup of a small carved stone lamp, whose fat burned away ages ago, luminous moss has taken hold. It gives a small steady glow, as if the plants had learned the lamp's purpose and taken it over.

None of the structures is large. They are widely spaced, the sort of things a few people might build over generations. There are no bones, no middens, no bodies, and nothing else that moves. The marks are patient and tidy and abandoned, and the vegetation treats them as more ground.

## What is strange

The forests answer. When a note sounds in one reed stand, the ribbon-blades nearby lean slightly toward it and glow, and the glow travels outward faster than the current can explain. Sing a low tone into the water and the nearest moss brightens in time with it. The plants seem to hear, or at least to be wired to their own noise. Whether that is a kind of thought is impossible to say. But the pulses of light that run across the plateau in the calm months are not random. They come in slow phrases that repeat, and when they reach the old stone ring, they pause. The moss inside the ring flares once, brighter than anything around it, before the wave moves on.

Nothing here can be sure what the pause is for. The plants have kept it up for longer than the stones have stood.
