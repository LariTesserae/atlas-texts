# The Sump Ward

The place lies at the bottom of a pit, and the pit is the reason for the place.

Long ago the limestone plain gave way and left a bowl about three hundred paces across and forty deep. Its walls are terraced with old ledges and streaked black and ochre where water has run down them for longer than any record. Someone built a district in the floor of that bowl, probably to work the water rather than fight it. Everything that followed was scaled to the pit. There are perhaps thirty structures, spread across a floor that could hold three hundred. Between them are open stretches of wet cobble, black canal, and silt, all patient and unoccupied.

## The Ground and the Water

The floor is never dry, but it is rarely deep. Water stands a hand's breadth under most of the paving and shows in the joints between stones as dark lines. In the low quarters it rises to a skin over the whole street. The limestone walls weep constantly. Each ledge has a beaded line of drips, and each drip has cut a small fluted channel in the rock, so the cliffs look combed. Sheets of flowstone, cream and rust and a green like old copper, have grown over the lower courses of the retaining walls. Some of that flowstone has swallowed the walls' iron tie-rods, leaving only rust-weeping bumps in the stone.

The water has a rhythm nobody set. Every nineteen or twenty days the aquifer beneath the ward seems to breathe in. The canals fill and the streets go under to the ankle. Then it breathes out and the water draws down, leaving the cobbles gleaming and the canal beds ribbed with silt. The old sluice-keepers marked the high phases on a brass gauge bolted to the pump-house wall. The marks run in a long unbroken column, each a little different, and none has ever been quite the same as the last. The gauge is green now, but the numerals can still be read by touch.

## The Buildings

Most of what stands is industrial: brick and iron, all of it settled into itself. There is a pump-house with tall arched windows, a boiler hall, a dynamo shed, a row of low storage vaults, a foundry whose chimney was built to carry smoke up the cliff face and now leans on it like a tired man on a wall, and a few terraces of workers' housing, narrow and tall, with slate roofs gone the color of wet ash. Iron footbridges cross the canals. A single gantry crane stands over the main basin, its hook lowered into the water and left there.

Everything is furred and stained by time. Brick has gone soft at the edges, and its mortar is gone in the lowest courses. Cast-iron columns have swollen into rust-blistered trunks. Roofs have sagged, and some have opened. Erosion here is old and mostly finished. Nothing is collapsing dramatically. Things are simply the shape that decades of water leave them in, and they hold that shape with settled stability, like riverstones.

High overhead, girders span the mouth of the pit in a partial lattice. Some of it is glazed with panes now clouded milky white. The rest is open. The light that falls through is grey and slow and arrives already tired. At midday the ward is the dim of late dusk. At night it is dark except for the lamps.

## The Lamps

The lamps are the first strange thing. Iron standards line the main street, each with a glass globe, and the globes are lit. No wire feeds them, though the dynamo shed is silent and green with verdigris. There is a pale, steady, greenish-white glow, faintly warm, with no flicker. If you open a globe, there is no flame. There are only small colonies of luminous slime-mold, grown into a dense mat on the glass and fed by the damp air. They brighten a little at the high water and dim at the low. The old gas mains still run beneath the street, and some of them seep. Whatever the mold has learned, it lives on these leaks, and it has occupied every lamp in the exact places where light was intended.

Nobody trims them, yet they are never overgrown. The globes are clear at the center and the mat is kept to a neat disc, as if by a keeper who is not seen.

## The Machines

The main engine in the pump-house is still turning, slowly. It is a great beam engine, its walking beam as long as a boat, its flywheel taller than a house. The steam that once drove it is gone. Something else moves it now: a dense water head brought down through old penstocks from a hidden upper cistern that is still fed by the cliff seeps. The beam nods once every eleven minutes or so. Between strokes there is a long pause in which you can hear the whole ward.

The stroke is unhurried. First a hiss and then the long groan of iron taking weight. The beam rocks and the rod slides through its gland. A heavy plunk of water is expelled somewhere below. Then the beam settles and the quiet resumes. It is the only regular mechanical sound in the ward, and the fauna have organized their lives around it.

The other machines are stopped and slowly becoming stone. Drip water carrying dissolved lime falls on them from the ceilings and lintels. A boiler in the boiler hall wears a rind of travertine two fingers thick. A row of lathes in the foundry annex has been dressed in white, smooth calcite, each one a soft ghost of itself with only the handwheels showing as small lumps. Stalactites hang from a gantry. A stalagmite has grown up through a workbench and lifted the vise on its tip. The industry is being converted, one drip at a time, into cave.

## Growth

Vegetation is sparse and specific. Pale liverworts and mosses coat the wet stone near the lamps, where the glow gives them a little light. Ferns with translucent, almost colorless fronds grow in cracks along the cliff ledges. A few sallow-leaved willows have taken root in silt banks along the main canal, thin and leaning, all pointing their crowns toward the open gap in the girders. Their roots run straight down into the water like hair. Fungi are more abundant than any green thing. Shelf fungus in tiers on the timbers, blue-grey mildew in the cellars, and pale stalked caps standing in rows on the housing terraces where the floors have gone soft.

Nothing here is lush. The plants have the look of things doing exactly as much as they need to.

## The Animals

Fauna is the ward's real population. There are more living things here than the sparse architecture would suggest, and they are everywhere once the eye adjusts.

**Swifts and swallows** nest in the girders and along the cliff ledges, thousands of them. They pour out in the pale hours and pour back in at dusk, and in flight their wings make a dry, ticking rustle. Their droppings fall in a slow constant rain. The roofs below have gone white, and the guano supports an entire economy underneath.

**Beetles and springtails** work the guano. **Pale salamanders**, long as a forearm, translucent and pink-gilled, move through the shallows of the canals and the flooded streets and eat the beetles. **Blind white fish** hold in the canals in loose queues.

The strangest are the **tin-shells**, small snails about the size of a thumbnail. They graze the rust and verdigris from the ironwork and take the metal into their shells, so that each one has a different color: copper-green, oxide-red, a blue like a pigeon's neck, a bright bronze. Millions of them cover the ironwork in shifting mosaics. When the beam engine strokes, every tin-shell on the surrounding metal clamps down at once. A ripple of tiny sounds passes over the ironwork like a breath drawn in, and then all of them relax.

The fish also know the engine. At the tail of each interval they gather at the outfall grate, and when the beam nods they dart through the churn. Salamanders wait in the shallows nearby. The swifts, disturbed, lift in a single thin curtain and settle again. The whole ward is fitted into that eleven-minute interval, and each species has its part.

## Sound

The ward is mostly quiet. The default sound is dripping, from ten thousand places, at ten thousand pitches, never quite in unison. It sounds like slow rain in a large house. Underneath is the low sigh of air moving up the cliff through the girders. Now and then a swift ticks, or a fish turns at the surface. Water shifts against brick. Then, at the appointed time, the engine speaks, and after it a quiet slightly deeper than before.

Footsteps carry oddly. The wet cobbles take the sound of a walker and give it back a moment later from the opposite wall, like a second walker a little behind.

## Cool and Slow

It is cool: about the temperature of a cellar, steady through the seasons, a little colder at the low water when the stone breathes out. Breath shows faintly some mornings. Cold condenses on every surface, and the air is heavy with a mineral, coppery, wet-brick smell, with guano and old smoke underneath.

Change here is measured in cycles far longer than a day. The water breathes over weeks. The flowstone thickens over generations, and a hand-width of new stone on the lathes is a lifetime's work. The mold in the lamps slowly moves from one globe to the next along the gas line, so that a lamp that lit a street corner once may go dark, and the corner nine steps further along will come up bright in its place. Nothing hurries. The ward is not dead and not abandoned in any sense the fauna would recognize. It is a machine that has slowly, patiently, passed into the keeping of the things that live in it.

## A Rule of the Place

If you stand at the outfall grate at the end of the eleven minutes, you will see all of it together. The fish gather and the salamanders wait. The swifts are on their ledges, and the tin-shells are still and bright in their thousands on the iron. The lamps burn their green-white. Then the beam nods, and everything answers at once.

Then it settles, and you count again toward the next one.
