# The Vug at Ninth Summit

## The outside, briefly

The mountain is still being born, and it has not learned to hold itself. It stands at an altitude where the sky is a bruised indigo even at noon and the air has too little in it to carry heat or sound. The ridgelines are knife-fresh. Fractured andesite and black glass lie where the last uplift threw them, in slabs the size of houses balanced edge on edge. The scree has no rounding at all. Every stone still has the corners it broke with, and it will cut a glove open.

Nothing is green and nothing is wet. Frost forms only as a fine white breath on the north faces in the hour before sunrise, then sublimes without ever becoming liquid. The wind is enormous and mostly silent. It moves grit and makes a dry hiss, like sand poured onto paper, and beneath that hiss the whole summit ticks and pings as the rock adjusts. The mountain rises a few finger-widths a year. Every few days something lets go, and a slab the size of a barn slides into the cloud deck far below.

At this height you would never find the place unless you were looking for a particular fault line, a seam of pale quartz running diagonally across a cliff of dark rock. Behind one section of that seam the mountain is hollow.

## The vug

Geologists would call it a vug, a cavity left when gas or fluid was trapped in cooling stone. This one is nine hundred meters long, two hundred high at its tallest, and lined from wall to ceiling with crystal. The crystals are not the tidy prisms of a museum. They are ragged smoky quartz and grey-violet fluorite, thick as tree trunks and pointing inward from every surface, so the cavern is a mouth full of teeth. Between the crystal walls, in every gap the rock allowed, someone built a city.

You enter through the Slot. It is a natural fissure that has been roofed over with riveted iron plate and lined with airlock curtains of stitched hide and rubber. The curtains are heavy and cold and smell of graphite and animal. Inside, the pressure is a little higher than outside, kept up by bellows-houses that never stop. The air is thick enough to walk in without the dizziness of the ridge, and it is cool. It is a steady cellar temperature that lets the machines run without cooking anyone. Warm air rises from fumarole vents deep below and drifts up through the town in slow, visible currents.

## The city's shape

The city has no ground plan because the crystals gave it none. Streets go where the crystal allows. Some are trenches cut through quartz clusters with diamond wire. Some are catwalks of riveted lattice slung between crystal shoulders. Some are simply the floor of a grotto, where a path has been worn into the pale dust. Buildings hang from the ceiling crystals like wasp nests and wedge into the gaps between the giant points. They are made of pressed iron sheet, scavenged rail, glass block cast on-site from the mountain's own obsidian, and a brown resinous composite the locals call *pitchboard*. Nothing is painted, because paint is imported and costs more than the buildings. The whole town is the color of rust, soot, and old brass, lit by crystal.

The layers are the main fact of it. At the bottom, in the deepest pits, are the furnaces and the fumarole works, where superheated gas from the mountain's roots is captured, cooled through baffles, and made to turn turbines. Above them are the foundries and drawing-sheds, then the workshops and living quarters. Over those come the market galleries, then the signal-lofts, then the antenna and vent-stacks that pierce the mountain's skin and breathe out into the thin outside. From roof to floor, the vug holds perhaps six full levels of settlement at any point, and the levels lean on one another. A tenement's foundation may be another building's ceiling. A stair may be a girder. Everything is within arm's reach of something else, and the density is almost tender. Nothing has room to be alone.

## Light

The light is dim, and it is beautiful in a poor sort of way. There is no sun in here. The illumination comes from three sources.

First, the crystals themselves. Certain fluorite bands glow a faint violet-blue under the ultraviolet spill from arc lamps, which the citizens leave burning in the deep works. The glow is faint and steady, like the last light on a winter evening.

Second, the furnaces. Their doors open and close on a rhythm, and each opening throws an orange flare that races across the crystals and makes the walls flash like a slow lightning storm.

Third, lamps. These are oil-and-mineral-wick lamps in cages of copper mesh, hung in thousands. From a high walkway they look like a spilled constellation, warm points in the gloom, swaying as the currents move.

Shadows are long and doubled, and there is always a second shadow leaning the other way. Quartz breaks every ray into ghosts, so no one in this city casts only one.

## Sound

It is mostly quiet, which surprises visitors. The machinery is huge, but the air is dense with dust, hides, felt, and moss-colored insulation fungus, and the crystal walls soak up and scatter noise. What you hear is a low tone, felt as much as heard, from the turbines far below. Over it comes the tick of governors, the chuff of bellows, and the soft slap of belts. There is also the small, constant chirring of the animals.

Voices are pitched low. People speak close, and they have a habit of touching a forearm before starting a sentence, since you cannot always tell where a voice comes from. Now and then a gong sounds from the signal-lofts, one long bronze note that rolls around the cavern for a full minute. It warns of a rockfall or a pressure surge, and everyone stops. Then it dies, and the low tone returns.

## The animals

The fauna is abundant, in a place where you would swear nothing could feed. The whole living economy is built on the fact that the mountain leaks food.

**Furnace-mites** are the base of everything. They are a rust-red arthropod the size of a rice grain, and they live by the millions in the warm iron scale of the foundry floors. They eat oxide crust and the mineral salts that effloresce on hot metal, and they produce a waxy, faintly sweet paste which the smelter families harvest as food and lamp-fuel. In the dark pits the floor is a moving carpet of them, a soft, dry crackling that is the loudest natural sound in the city.

**Glass-moths** are pale, wide-winged, and tremulous, with wings that are truly transparent. They feed on the mites and on the salt film that grows on the crystals. They flutter in dense clouds through the shafts of lamp light, and the crystals are always dusted with the powder of their wings.

**Ratchet-lizards** are mid-sized, grey, and slow, and their toes lock into rock like clamps. They hunt moths from the walls and from the undersides of catwalks. They are kept as pest control and, by some families, as pets. Their tails have a trick joint that clicks when they are alarmed, so a startled colony sounds like a hundred tiny ratchets going off in sequence.

**Haulers** are the big ones. They are broad, low, six-legged beasts, something like pack-badgers the size of ponies, with thick bristled hides and a dull patient intelligence. They were bred here over generations for the cramped tunnels and the tolerance of thin air. They pull ore carts and carry loads up the crystal stairs. Drivers ride them like living tractors and talk to them in a quiet, continuous murmur.

Above all this, in the vent-stacks and the high crystal roof, hang the **veil-bats**, fist-sized, silent, dark. In the evening cycle, when the furnace-doors dim, they drop from the roof in one smooth pour, and for a quarter hour the whole city has a second sky of small wings moving through it.

Nothing in the city drinks free water, because none exists. Every animal, and every person, takes its moisture from the fumarole condensers, which drip a thin bitter distillate into brass cisterns, from mite-paste, and from a few chemistries no one quite understands. The bats lick crystal-frost.

## Rules of this world

The people have rules, which they mostly keep without discussing.

*Do not cut the roof crystals.* The big hanging points carry the weight of the whole ceiling, and some hold whole districts. A crystal that seems decorative may be structural. When a crystal cracks, the crack is announced by the gong, and the district below evacuates.

*Keep the bellows fed.* The pressure is life. There is a rota of bellows-watch in every quarter, and to skip a shift is the one crime that gets a person exiled to the outer ridge.

*Water is counted.* Everyone knows their ration. There are no taps, only cisterns with locks, and the keys are worn by the cistern-wardens as badges of office.

*Never whistle near the fumarole vents.* Nobody can explain this one, but it is universally observed. The old people say the vents answer.

## Turbulence

The city is never still, and that is the deepest truth about it. The mountain is young, so the vug is still moving. Every few weeks a tremor shudders through and the lamps swing in unison, thousands of small lights all dipping one way. Crystals sing at odd pitches under strain. Somewhere a catwalk fails. Somewhere a new fissure opens and leaks warm sulfurous vapor, and by nightfall a crowd of smiths has already bolted a manifold onto it and begun to divert the steam into a new engine.

The city is always being rebuilt. Scaffolds cover half its buildings. Districts move: a market that stood on the third gallery last season now hangs from the fifth, dragged up by cranes and pulleys in a single night because the old gallery's crystal support began to fail. Vendors chant and haggle in low voices. Apprentices sprint along girders with tools in their belts. Haulers shoulder through crowds. Furnace flares throw the whole scene into brief high relief, then let it sink back into dimness.

The air currents change too. When the fumarole pressure surges, warm gusts roll through the streets, lamps gutter, moth clouds boil upward, and the veil-bats scatter. When it drops, cold thin air from the Slot pours in, and people pull their hide collars up and wait it out. Twice a year a big surge, called a *heave*, rocks the entire cavern and forces every person to hold on to something for an hour. No one sleeps during a heave. They drink mite-spirit, sing quietly, and listen to the crystals ring like a vast, badly tuned choir.

## The edges

At the far end of the vug, the city thins and gives way to raw crystal. There the walls are pristine: unbroken quartz, clear as ice and violet at the heart, growing in spires that no hand has touched. The lamps do not reach. The animals go there only to die. In that quiet, if you stand still long enough, you can hear the mountain doing what it has always done, slowly pushing itself upward into the thin sky and cracking its own hollows open, with the whole bright, crowded, restless city clinging inside it like a colony of sparks in a seam of coal.
