# The Vault of Black Leaf

You come down by an iron stair bolted into fresh-broken basalt. The rock has not had time to soften. Every edge of the collapse is still a blade, and the fracture faces are the color of wet slate, with a few fine crystals catching what little light there is. Some year not long past, a lava tube lost its roof and dropped a shelf of the world into itself. The chamber below is perhaps two hundred paces across and shaped like a shallow bowl pressed into the earth. Its ceiling is a torn ragged lid, and through the largest break you can see no sky, only the underside of another dark.

## Heat

The heat arrives before anything else. It is not dry. It sits on the skin like a hand, and the air tastes of warm coins and boiled stone. The floor sweats. Water beads on every surface and gathers into threads that run along the cracks toward the low center, where a shallow pool lies steaming and unmoving. It rains here only in the sense that the ceiling weeps: a drop lets go somewhere, falls a long way, and ticks against a leaf. Then, a minute or more later, another one.

## The Growth

The plants are lush and they are black. They are not dead or burnt but a deep glossy black-green, like the inside of a mussel shell, with leaves as broad as doors, cupped and ribbed. They grow in sparse, deliberate stands with bare rock between them, each stand a dark fountain of foliage arching outward from a single crown. Some are tree-ferns with trunks ropy with aerial roots. Some are low rosettes as wide as cart wheels. There are also hanging vines that never touch the ground, thick as an arm and sagging in slow catenaries from the ceiling's broken teeth.

They do not feed on light. Nothing here is bright enough. They lean toward *heat*. Every crown tilts, slightly and in agreement, toward the far wall, where a fissure breathes out hot gas. The leaves are broad because they drink warmth. Touch one and it is almost feverish, and its surface is finely pitted like a sponge. Where two leaves overlap, the lower one is paler and thinner, having been robbed. There are no flowers. Instead, at the tips of the oldest fronds, are clusters of grey bladders that swell and split and release a pale dust. The dust hangs, then drifts upward on the convection, since no wing or foot will ever carry it.

There are no animals at all. No insects tick in the leaf litter, no bat hangs from the ceiling, and nothing has been left behind to eat. The leaf litter is deep, springy, and undisturbed, and it lies in perfect drifts.

## The Flare

At the far wall, where the fissure opens, someone once capped a gas vent with an iron stack and lit it. The flame still burns: a low, dull orange tongue perhaps the height of a person, leaning and slowly folding on itself. It is the brightest thing in the vault, and by it the plants read the world. Its light barely reaches the nearest fronds. The rest is a red-brown dusk in which shapes emerge only as edges of slightly lesser dark.

The flame breathes on a long cycle. Over about an hour it shrinks to a blue bead, almost out, and the whole grove seems to hold itself still. Then pressure builds behind the rock and the flare swells, roars softly for a few minutes, and subsides. The plants have grown to this rhythm. On the swell, the black leaves rotate very slightly on their stalks, a motion you can catch only by looking away and then back.

## The Works

The humans, if they were human, left a station, and it is not entirely dead.

Beside the flare stands a boiler of riveted plate, taller than a house and blistered with rust the color of dried blood. Pipes run from it into a small brick engine-house with its roof half gone. Inside is a single horizontal engine, its flywheel taller than you, still turning. It turns once every few minutes, with a long pale gasp of steam and a tick of its valve gear. Each turn drives a belt to a dynamo furred with verdigris, and from the dynamo a cable of braided copper runs out across the bowl on iron posts.

Along the cable hang five lamps, spaced far apart, each in a cage of wire. They are carbon-filament lamps, glowing the dull orange of a coal seen through a fist. Their light makes small islands. One shows a stand of ferns, each frond edged in amber, and the wet floor under them like hammered copper. Another lights only rock and a rusted handcart on a length of narrow rail. The fourth flickers on the same long tide as the flame, and the fifth has failed and holds a faint red thread of filament that never quite goes out.

The cable has been colonized. Bright filaments of a pale moss follow its length, and the plants nearest it are the largest in the vault. Whether they eat the stray current or only the warmth of the copper, no one recorded.

## Water and Ground

Water mostly collects in the central pool, which is fed by seepage and warmed from below. Its surface stays glassy and nearly black, and it steams in a slow ribbon toward the fissure. The margin is stained orange and white with mineral crust, thin as frost and brittle underfoot. Streamlets braid down from the walls and vanish into the litter, and here and there the rock sweats a soft bead of iron-red slime.

The ground is mostly young rock: glassy, jointed, sharp enough to cut a boot. Only under the plants has it begun to become soil, a dark wet crumb no deeper than a hand.

## Sound

The vault is nearly silent. You hear the drip, the ticking of cooling and warming iron, the sigh of the engine's valve, and the low steady breath of the flare. When a bladder-cluster bursts, it makes a tiny papery pop. Voices, if you use them, go flat and are swallowed at once by the wet leaves.

## What Changes

Everything changes slowly. The leaves creep toward the flame across seasons. The rust eats the boiler a flake at a time. The crust on the pool thickens by the width of a hair. Each year a few more roots find the cable posts and wind around them, and each year the roof lets go another small piece of itself with a distant clatter that echoes long after. Someday the engine will stop, or the plants will close over it. For now the flare swells and dims, the black leaves turn, and the five orange lamps hold their small circles against the dark.
