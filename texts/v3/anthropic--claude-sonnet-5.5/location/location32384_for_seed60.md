# The Governor Fen

You arrive by going down. The land is low to begin with, and then it drops another few meters into a bowl about two hundred paces across. Nothing here can be seen from the rim. The rim is a wall of leaf, and the bowl is full to the brim with green, the way a cup is full of water. The first sign is sound: a **thud** felt in the sternum before it is heard, then a long **hiss** like a giant exhaling through its teeth, then a trailing ticking of a thousand small releases. Then nothing for the space of a slow count of forty. Then the thud again.

That is the engine. It has not stopped in a very long time, and nobody is tending it.

## The ground that isn't

There is no soil to speak of. The floor is a mat of sphagnum, peat, and root, a meter or two thick, floating or nearly floating on warm brown water. It quakes underfoot in slow ripples. Where you step, water wells up around your ankle, tea-colored and body-warm, and smells of iron and crushed stems. The warmth comes from below: the engine's blowdown water, released for generations into the old drainage sump, has made the whole fen a shallow, lukewarm bath. Mist forms every morning and never fully leaves. It thins by midday to a haze that clings to the undersides of leaves and beads there, and the whole place drips. Nothing is dry. Rocks that break the surface, mostly cut stone from foundations, weep in threads along their seams.

Above the mat, the plants take over completely. There is no gap between one plant and the next. Tree ferns stand shoulder to shoulder with their crowns interlaced into a low ceiling. Giant horsetails, thick as a wrist, rise in jointed ranks between them. Between and over everything run vines like cable: braided, furred with aerial roots, each cinched around the next. Where a trunk falls, it does not reach the ground. It lodges in the mass and grows moss, then a seedling garden, then another trunk, and it is still part of the structure. Moving through the fen means going along the pathways the vines and fallen trunks allow, ducking through green tunnels, crawling on bridges of root, and sometimes walking along an iron pipe furred in moss that is the only straight line in the place.

## What was built

The works stood here once: a pumping station meant to drain the marsh for something long forgotten. Its pieces are scattered through the fen, and the plants have made use of each.

**The boiler house** is a brick shell with the roof gone, and a fig has filled it, one tree forming a single room of root and trunk. Through the gaps you can see the cylindrical bulk of the boilers, orange with rust. Their velvet surface is covered in lichen and tiny ferns. A sapling grows from a manhole, its roots pouring down inside the tank like hair.

**The stack** is a chimney leaning slightly, wrapped from foot to lip in a climber with leaves as big as plates. It no longer carries smoke. Now it exhales a thin white breath of mist, and after each thud the vapor puffs from the top in a ring.

**The tanks** are riveted cylinders on stone cradles, half sunk in the peat. Some hold clear black water in which bladderwort floats. Others hold only a dense wet mass of stems. One tank has been tapped from within by a tree and now sprouts a crown of branches from its riveted seams.

**The pipes** are everywhere. Cast-iron mains as thick as a torso run beneath the mat and come up at intervals, sweating, and wear coats of moss so deep they look like sleeping animals. Smaller lines branch from them, and many of these have been *colonized*.

## The strangeness

The engine still runs, and this is not an accident of leftover fuel. Near the center of the bowl, in a shed whose walls have been replaced by living trunks, stands a beam engine: a great walking beam of black iron, a flywheel taller than a person, a rod, a crosshead. The flywheel turns once every eighty seconds or so. It does not turn with any burning behind it. It turns because the plants push it.

Every pipe that enters the shed ends in a manifold, and the manifold's iron has been overgrown, threaded through, and in places *replaced* by a hard, red-brown wood that has grown into the fittings so exactly that you cannot see where metal stops. Sap, warm and pressurized by roots and the thermal seep of the fen, feeds into the cylinder. The piston rod is iron, but the valve gear is half vine, half machinery: tendrils have wrapped the eccentric rods and pulled them in a rhythm the fen has learned. When the pressure in the manifold rises high enough, a bladder-like flap in the wood opens with a wet *clack*. That is the hiss. The sound is loud in the way an engine hall is loud, made larger by the wet air and the way the green mass absorbs the highs and returns only the deep parts.

Above the engine, on its pillar, is a **flyball governor**, the old kind: two iron spheres on arms, swinging outward as the speed rises. One arm is still iron. The other has been replaced by a vine with a swollen, weighted bulb at the tip. The two do not match, but the governor works. Its bulb swings out in the same arc, held to the same rhythm. The strange part is that plants across the fen *lean* to its motion. The tree-fern fronds in the shed tilt as the bulb swings. Farther off, more faintly, the crowns of the horsetails nod a fraction of a second after, as if an old habit of the place ran outward from that one spinning weight.

No one has ever seen it stop. In the deep of the wet season the interval lengthens to two minutes, and the hiss becomes a longer, more strained sigh. In the driest weeks it quickens. That is the only measure of weather the fen keeps.

## Other odd things that grow here

- **Rust-leaf**, a shrub that draws iron from the pipes. Its new leaves come out pale green, then take on an orange bloom over weeks until they are the exact color of the boilers, and fall to leave orange stains on the mat.
- **Heartiron wood.** In the trunks of the oldest figs and alders near the engine, the heartwood is dark red and heavy, with veins of metal threaded through the grain. A fallen limb rings faintly when struck. Over centuries the works have been *entering* the trees; iron becomes wood becomes iron.
- **Whistle-stems**, hollow reeds with a split near the base that sound a thin, high note when steam or air passes. In the mist hour, hundreds of them go at once, and the fen seems to sing in a single ragged tone over the engine's drum.
- **Gauge moss**, in tight domes on the tanks. It swells visibly when the pressure rises and flattens when it falls, and it sits on old brass gauge faces whose glass is long gone, so that the moss has grown into the shape of the dial and its needle-like fruiting bodies point roughly where the needle would.

## Life that moves

Fauna is rare. The fen is too dense and too warm-wet for much, and what lives here does so in the crevices of the machinery. There are pale snails with translucent shells, which graze the rust bloom and leave silver trails. There are beetles, black and armored, who live in the pipes and carry small pieces of moss. There are a few frogs, brown and no bigger than a thumbnail, whose call mimics the tick of cooling metal. Once in a while, at dawn, one heron-like bird, gray and tall, stands motionless on the flywheel's stone housing and lifts each foot in time to the engine, though it is perhaps only shifting its weight.

## Time here

Everything changes, but so slowly you would have to come back in decades. The fig in the boiler house is prying the brick apart a millimeter a year. The stack leans one degree more each generation. The mat rises as peat builds, and the buried pipes go deeper. The forms are old and *established*: erosion has already done its early work and settled into a slow shared shape, in which the brick, the iron, and the wood no longer compete but interlock. The fen has reached a kind of peace with what was left in it.

At the center of the bowl, in the wet, warm, green light that filters down through fern and mist, the beam rocks. The thud comes, the hiss follows, the ticking spreads out through the stems, and the governor's bulb swings wide and returns. The whole packed, dripping, breathing mass of the place leans a little with it, and then waits for the next stroke.
