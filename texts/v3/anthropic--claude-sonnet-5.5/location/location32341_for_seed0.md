# The Clamor Flats

The land here is only a few thousand years old, and it is still being finished. This is a wide basin, low and warm, a day's walk across in any direction and much longer if you try to go straight. It sits at the bottom of a broken country, and the dark is complete except where something living chooses to glow.

## The ground

Underfoot is basalt, black and unweathered, with edges that still cut. Where lava crusted and cracked there are plates the size of doorways, tilted against each other, their upper faces ropy and glassy and their undersides hollow. Walking across them makes a hollow clink that carries far. In older patches, which here means a few centuries, roots have pried the rock into rubble and pulled a black sand of crushed glass and ash into the seams. Nothing is rounded yet. A hand trailed along a boulder comes away scratched. Every ridge has a fresh edge, and every hollow is a crack that opened recently enough that its walls still show clean orange-and-rust mineral staining.

Long fissures score the basin in rough parallels, a body-width to a river-width across. Some are dry, ferny slots. Others breathe warm air, and a few exhale steam in slow, steady columns that stand pale against the dark and lean together when the wind shifts.

## The water

There is almost no standing water and almost no flowing water, and everything is wet anyway. The moisture comes from the steam. Warm breath rises from the fissures, meets the cooler night air, and falls back as fog, then dew, then a fine, constant drip from every leaf tip. There are no rivers. There are seeps, hot pools the size of cooking pots in basalt basins, and a few thumb-deep ponds of rainwater held in the cupped leaves of giant bromeliad-like plants. When real rain comes, it comes as a hammering downpour that lasts twenty minutes and turns every slope to running black glass. Then it stops, and within the hour the fog has closed over the basin again.

Everything is damp to the touch: the rock, the bark, the air, your own sleeves. Nothing ever quite dries.

## The green

The vegetation is dense enough to feel like a second ground laid over the first. It grows in layers.

At the bottom are mosses and liverworts that fill every crack and drink the drip, spongy and electric-green when a light finds them. Above them are ferns with fronds as long as arms, then tree ferns, then thick-trunked cycad-like things with crowns of stiff, glossy leaves. Over those rise the true canopy trees, narrow and tall, with buttressed roots that grip the broken rock like fists. Vines hang between them in curtains and cross-bracing. Epiphytes crowd every branch: orchids, bromeliads, and pale fleshy plants with no name in any language, each holding its own small reservoir.

Because the ground is new and the fissures are warm, the plants grow with a kind of hurry. Saplings crowd a fresh crack within a season. Roots wedge into the rock and split it further, so the vegetation is not just living on the landscape but doing part of the work of breaking it. Lightning-scarred trunks sprout new shoots the same year. Dead wood vanishes into fungus in months.

Some of the plants are unusual. A common one is a knee-high stalk topped with a translucent bulb that swells before each tremor and pulses faintly blue when touched. The creatures of the Flats use it as a warning, and so eventually do people. Another, a low vine with leaves like wet paper, curls tight when a vibration passes through the soil and opens again slowly, so that a wave of curling runs across the forest floor ahead of anything heavy.

## The dark

There is no sun to speak of. A permanent ceiling of cloud and steam holds the basin in a twilight so deep that even at midday the light is a dim, brownish grey, and at night it is absolute. What light exists is made by the place itself.

Fungi glow on rotting logs in cold green. Some of the insects flash in slow amber or hard white. The blue bulbs pulse. In the fissures, the deeper ones, there is a faint red glow from below, the color of a coal seen through a curtain. Where steam rises from those, it is lit from underneath and becomes a ghostly rose column, and the underside of the canopy above blushes with it. Occasionally a vent flares, a little gout of orange sparks and gas, and for a few seconds every trunk in a hundred paces stands out in sharp black-and-copper relief, and every eye in the branches gleams back.

You move through this mostly by touch, sound, and memory.

## The noise

The sound is the first thing and the last thing. It is not background. It is a medium, like the fog.

It begins as a wall of insects, a thousand overlapping frequencies from a high electric whine to a low buzzing drone that you feel in the sternum. Over it come the frogs, which are the true engine of the noise. There are hundreds of species, and they do not call randomly. They call in shifting, roiling waves, each species picking up a rhythm from its neighbors and passing it along, so that pulses of sound travel across the basin like weather. A clacking rattle runs from east to west. A booming, hollow *tonk* answers from the fissures, like mallets on gourds. Whistling trills rise from the canopy. Under it all are the long, low groans of something large in the wet places that nobody has seen clearly.

Above this are the birds and bats and things that are neither. Screeches, cracks, whoops, alarm barks that ricochet from trunk to trunk. Arboreal mammals throw whole tantrums of shrieking at each other from the branches. Somewhere a creature makes a sound like a rusty gate being dragged across a stone floor, over and over, with total confidence.

Then the ground adds its own sounds: the pop and tick of cooling rock, the hiss of vents, the deep grinding shudder of a slab settling. When a tremor comes, the entire chorus stops, not gradually but in one hard cut, as if a hand had closed over the world. In that silence you can hear the drip, the vents, and your own pulse. The silence lasts a few seconds. Then a single frog tries a note, and the whole basin roars back in, louder than before.

Speaking normally is impossible. People here talk with hands, whistles pitched above or below the chorus, and taps on wood or stone.

## The animals

The fauna is overwhelming in the literal sense. Wherever you put your attention, there is too much to attend to.

The air is full of flying things: moths the size of hands, beetles like dropped coins, dragonfly-like hunters with slow, buzzing wings, clouds of gnats that hang in the vent-light like drifting sparks. Bats pour from the fissures at dusk and dawn in streams that take a quarter hour to pass. The trunks are alive with movement, with lizards, snakes, tree-frogs, and long-fingered climbing mammals that pick through the epiphytes for beetles. On the ground, the leaf litter seethes: ants in columns as thick as rope, millipedes as long as forearms, crabs that have wandered in from somewhere and simply stayed, and small, quick, armored mammals that run from rock to rock in the dark.

Larger animals are present but rarely seen. Heavy, low-slung grazers with hides like wet slate push through the ferns along the warm fissures, feeding on the fastest-growing plants. Predators follow them: long-bodied cats with dark coats, and packs of something smaller and louder. Scavenger birds with bare heads wait in the emergents. In the hot pools live pale, blind, many-legged things that come out only when the tremors start, and drink at the edge, and go back.

The animals are not calm. They live in a place where the ground moves and the fog thickens without notice. They are jumpy and quick, and they cluster, flee, and regroup. A panic can run through the forest in seconds, everything heading in one direction, and then stop just as suddenly, leaving a strange hush and a trampled fan of ferns.

## Rules of the place

- **The ground gives warning.** The blue bulbs swell, the paper-leaf vines curl, and the frogs go quiet a heartbeat before a tremor. Anyone who lives here learns to read this as instinctively as breathing.
- **Vents are born and die.** A safe path walked a month ago may now end at a new steaming crack. Old vents seal themselves and go cold, and within a season are choked with moss.
- **Fog is a tide.** It thickens in the small hours and thins slightly toward what would be morning. In its thickest hours, sound is carried strangely: a voice ten paces away may seem to come from behind you, and a distant roar arrives as though it were beside you.
- **Everything is eaten.** Fallen fruit lasts minutes. Bodies last hours. Tools left on the ground grow fungus by morning.

## The people, such as they are

A few people live here, perhaps a couple of hundred in the whole basin, in small groups that move often. They have almost nothing that could be called built. Their camps are hammocks of woven bark cord slung between trunks well above the litter, and hearths of stacked basalt slabs kept lit under overhangs of leaf thatch. They carry obsidian blades knapped from the glassy flows, digging sticks hardened in fire, bone needles, cord nets, gourds sealed with resin for carrying water, and light spears tipped with chipped stone. They tie tally-knots into cords to record the locations of safe paths and new fissures, and they hang these cords, worn smooth by handling, at the entrances to the routes they use. A few marks scratched into bark or stone, arrows and counts and warnings, are the closest thing to writing.

They do not fight the noise. They have learned to hear through it, to listen for the gaps and the changes rather than the sound. To them the Flats is not silent, dark, or dangerous. It is simply loud, and the loudness is information, and they read it all night long without rest.

## Toward morning

Near what would be dawn the sky above the steam turns from black to a smoky, uneven grey. The bats return in their streams. The frog chorus does not fade so much as change, the night species handing over to the day ones in a slow overlapping transfer, like two crowds passing in a narrow street. The vents still glow dully. Dew runs off every leaf in threads. Somewhere, out in the middle of the basin where the ground is newest, a fresh crack opens with a sound like a great branch snapping, and for a moment everything stops, and then everything begins again.
