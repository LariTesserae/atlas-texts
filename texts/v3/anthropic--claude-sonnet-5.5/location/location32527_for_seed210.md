# The Shift-Basin

You arrive over a lip of fresh granite, still sharp enough to cut a palm, and the basin opens below you: a hanging cirque perhaps four hundred paces across, cupped in broken walls that look as if they were cracked open last week. Nothing has had time to round them. Every ledge is a clean edge. Every boulder on the floor still fits the scar it fell from, and you can see the matching wounds high above.

The air is cold and thick with suspended water, not quite fog and not quite rain. The light is dim and pewter-colored, and it comes from no clear direction. It seeps down from the low cloud ceiling and pools in the basin like something poured.

## The Sound

You hear it before you understand it. The basin is loud, a layered industrial roar: the slow *chunk-hiss, chunk-hiss* of a great press, a rattling like looms in a long hall, a bass grinding like ore in a drum. Now and then a whistle blows, a flat steam-note, from somewhere among the ferns.

There is no factory. Look as long as you like and you will find no wall, wheel, chimney, or rail. The sounds are grown.

The press-sound comes from the **bellows-bogs**, wide sheets of sphagnum that breathe. Gas gathers under the moss until a whole raft of it lifts a hand's breadth, then drops and exhales through a ring of rust-colored pores. It happens every nine seconds, and it has always happened every nine seconds. The loom-rattle is a beetle, thumb-sized and copper-backed, that drums its wing cases in interlocking rhythms. Thousands of them sit in the wet bark of the dwarf birches, and their patterns mesh so precisely that the sound seems to have a shuttle passing through it. The grinding is the stones themselves. Great round cobbles sit in worn sockets in the streambed and turn slowly, driven by the current, polishing themselves and their cradles against each other. They have turned for exactly as long as they have been there.

## Water

Everything weeps. The cliffs run with threads of water that cross the black rock and hang in beads from every lip. The water table sits at the surface, so that stepping off the stone puts you ankle-deep in cold, clear seep. Pools lie scattered across the floor, widely spaced, each with a slightly different color: tea-brown, milk-green, a clear one with a floor of white quartz sand that boils gently in place without ever moving the grains.

A waterfall drops from the northern wall in three stages. It is the only one in the basin, and it does not behave. The water leaves the top ledge, falls, and arrives at the bottom pool a moment *before* the sound of it leaves the top. You hear the crash of the impact, and then, a heartbeat later, the soft hush of the water starting to fall. The delay is fixed. Nothing about it has ever been otherwise.

## Growth

The vegetation is lush in a crowding, upholstered way. Moss covers every horizontal surface in cushions the thickness of a forearm, in greens that shade toward yellow and blue-black. Tree ferns stand in loose, distant clusters, each with a wide clearing around it as though the others were keeping their distance. Between them are dwarf rhododendron and knee-high bamboo, and dripping curtains of liverwort on the rock faces.

The spacing is sparse but deliberate. Between one tree fern and the next lie flat, open stretches of wet moss and standing water, and across these you can see a long way through the mist. The emptiness is part of the pattern. Presences here are few and each one is thick with life.

The plants have the same strange relation to time as the waterfall. The tree ferns unfurl their crosiers, but the crosiers are always at the same stage, and each frond is always halfway open. Nothing grows or dies. The basin keeps its inventory constant, and when a frond is eaten, another is already at that stage.

## Fauna

Animals abound, and they gather where the sparse places allow. Around each tree fern is a crowd. There are small deer, no larger than dogs, with long dark lashes. There are grey salamanders that walk upright when they think themselves unobserved. There are hundreds of the copper beetles, and there are birds like slate-colored wrens whose song is a perfect imitation of a steam gauge ticking up to pressure.

Then there are the **foremen**, which are the strangest thing here. They are large, long-legged herons, slow and pale, that stand in the shallows at regular intervals. When a bellows-bog exhales, every heron within earshot lifts one foot, and when the bog drops again, they set it down. No heron has ever been seen to fish. Yet the frogs are fat, the salamanders are fat, and no one is ever hungry. Food simply *is*, in the right amount, in the right place. Small dark fruit hang from the birches at all hours, and they are always ripe.

## The Breakdown

Reality here is stable, but it is not consistent, and the inconsistency is the fixed part.

Shadows fall toward the light source in the eastern half of the basin and away from it in the western half, and a line of ferns marks the seam. If you stand on the seam, your shadow points both ways and holds together without strain.

The stones in the streambed turn, but some turn against the current and some with it, and they do not touch.

One pool reflects the sky as it would look at noon, bright and blue, although the cloud is thick above you. A second pool reflects the basin from underneath, as though the water were a ceiling and you were standing on the far side of it.

If you count the tree ferns, you get eleven. If you count again, you get eleven. If you count them from the rim, you get seven, and both counts are true.

Sometimes the rock weeps something that is not water: a warm, slow, amber drip that smells of machine oil and crushed mint. It falls from a fissure in the eastern wall and collects in a shallow stone bowl that nobody carved, which the cliff simply formed. The bowl is never full and never empty. Animals drink from it and are not harmed.

## What It Is

Stand long enough at the center of the basin and you understand that the roar is not chaos. It is rhythm. The press-bogs, the beetle-looms, the turning stones, and the whistle-wrens all lock into one enormous cycle, a shift that began at some point and has simply been *in progress* ever since. The basin is an engine that makes nothing, or makes only itself, forever, at a steady pitch.

There is no dawn and no dusk. The dim light does not change. The nine-second breath does not skip. You will leave the way you came, over the sharp lip, and the roar will fade behind you into ordinary wind. Somewhere behind you the whistle will blow, exactly on time, and nobody will have been there to answer it.
