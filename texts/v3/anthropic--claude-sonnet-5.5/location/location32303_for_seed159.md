# The Warm Sump

You descend into it rather than arrive. The surface up there is a plain of cracked grey crust, and then it simply stops. The ground breaks along a ragged lip and drops through four hundred feet of stepped, wet, ochre wall into a basin some eleven miles across. Its roof is still mostly on. A shelf of old crust spans overhead, torn open in long fissures and round collapsed windows, and through these the daylight falls in slanting planks, thick with steam. Where the planks land, the floor is bright as noon. Between them it is a warm, pearly dusk. The light bounces off wet mineral and reaches everywhere, so nothing is ever truly dark, only shaded in milk-colored layers.

The air is the first thing that reaches you. It is body-warm and heavy, and it smells of hot pennies, boiled eggs, and bread crust. Everything is beaded with condensation. It doesn't rain, exactly, but the roof weeps constantly. Drops fall from the underside of the crust in ten thousand slow strings, and the whole basin ticks and patters like a room full of clocks.

## The floor that is still being made

Nothing here is finished. The ground is a raw work in progress, made of terraces of travertine and sinter still soft from the springs that laid them. Their edges are scalloped like frozen pie crust, in cream, rust, sulfur-yellow, and a startling copper green. Each terrace holds a shallow pool of hot water the color of milky jade. Water sheets from pool to pool in thin, glassy veils, depositing a film of new stone as it goes. Leave a bone in a rim-pool for a week and it comes out crusted white and fat, like something dipped in sugar.

The rock is soft when it's new. Footprints stay for hours before they harden, and a whole day's traffic leaves the terraces stamped with a script of prints, claw-drags, and belly-slicks that is slowly locked in and buried. In the older ground, cut by a few raw gullies, you can see the layers of earlier days, prints and all, pressed like leaves in a book.

Nothing has had time to erode. Edges are sharp. Spires of new stone, some thin as a finger and some thick as a tower, stand unweathered and dripping. Columns grow visibly toward each other from roof and floor. Where they've nearly met there is a constant tiny drip and a sound like a held note.

## The pulse

Every few hours, the basin *surges*. Deep in the floor, a heat-driven chamber fills and empties, and the springs answer in sequence. First comes a low shudder through your soles and a groan like a ship's timber. Then the terrace pools brim over. Geysers of every size go off across the basin in a rippling wave, from stuttering knee-high spouts to a few white columns that punch up into the daylight planks and come down as warm rain. For twenty minutes the whole floor runs with hot, shallow water, braided and fast, carrying silt and stranded creatures, laying down new skins of stone, cutting new gullies. Then it drains, and the landscape is subtly, freshly different. Rims have moved and pools have merged or split. A causeway that was dry is a stream.

Everything alive here plans around the pulse, and the planning is the loudest, busiest thing in the world.

## What lives here

There are no plants, and no light strong enough to want them. The base of life is felt: mats of microbes and fungus that coat every wet surface in slick, layered carpet, purple-black at the top, rust below, and a bright snot-green where the water is hottest. Where the mats are thick they blister and gas, and the blisters pop with small wet claps. Grazing fauna tear them in strips. Stands of pale fungal club-stalks, waist-high and soft as wet felt, crowd the cooler gullies. They release brown spore-smoke when stepped on, and some have been cropped so evenly they look mown.

Above the mats lives an overwhelming crowd:

- **Shellmoths**, the size of a thumbnail, hang in drifting clouds above the pools. They flash when startled, and the flash runs through the cloud as a rippling silver wave. They are so many that the air near the springs looks grainy.
- **Ladder-crabs**, translucent and long-legged, climb the terrace walls in unbroken files. Where a file is thick enough, the wall seems to be flowing upward.
- **Pale wallowers**, big as cattle and slick as boiled eggs, lie in the warm pools like heaps of dough. They breathe through fringes on their backs and moan when the surge comes, a deep sustained vowel that the whole herd takes up, so that the pulse has a bass line.
- **Glass-eels** braid through every shallow sheet of water, and skimmer-birds without feathers, wet-skinned and reptilian, snatch them in flocks that turn like smoke.
- **Cliff-chitterers**, small furred things in their thousands, live in the wall-holes and scream at one another in rising and falling waves of alarm. Every sound the basin makes is eventually answered by them.

Everything eats something, and much of it eats the dead things that the pulse strands on the terraces. There are no quiet places. Every square yard has three or four kinds of life feeding, mating, fleeing, or shedding. The mats move with the bodies under them. Even the still pools boil quietly with larvae.

## Sound

It is loud, but loud in layers, like standing in the middle of a market. Under everything is the hiss and gurgle of water and the deep wallowers' moan. On top of that comes the clicking of crab-legs, the tick of dripping, the chitterer-chorus, the wet clap of blisters, and the sudden, huge percussion of a geyser going off. The surge builds to a roar that you feel in your ribs. Then comes the silence after the drain, which is never really silent, only thin, with drips and a hundred small voices coming back one at a time.

## The few marks of hands

Something else lives here too, or did. They are scattered, small, and easy to miss in the crowd: cairns of stacked bones, cemented by the sinter into permanent white knuckles. Woven mats of fungal fiber stretched over the gullies as footbridges, sagging and patched, some fresh. Low round huts of baked silt, domed like beehives, set on the highest and driest terraces. Their doorways are wide enough for a crouching person, and they are smoke-black inside from small fires fed on dried wallower fat. Around them are stone-tipped fishing spears, bone hooks, and clay bowls left rim-up to catch the drip. Weirs of woven reed-fungus stand across the eel channels, and their poles are already sheathed in stone.

The builders are rarely seen. When you do glimpse them, they are lean, wet-haired figures moving in single file along the ridgelines, carrying baskets, timing the pulse by the wallowers' moans. They mend their footbridges after every surge and rebuild their weirs after every flood, and they never seem to hurry or complain about it. Some of the huts have been sealed by the rising stone, with their fires still smudged on the ceilings, and they have not been reopened.

## The strangeness

The basin has habits you only notice after a while. At the top of the surge, just before the water breaks, every animal falls silent for perhaps three heartbeats, even the chitterers. Then the whole floor erupts at once, as if a conductor had dropped a hand. The shellmoth clouds synchronize their flashing to the geyser rhythm, so the air pulses silver in time with the ground. And in the deepest gully, where the water runs hottest and the light barely reaches, the mats grow in slow spirals that turn, if you watch them for an hour, very slightly, as if something beneath were stirring a pot.

You leave through one of the windows in the roof, climbing a rope ladder of fungal fiber that some patient hand has left hanging there. Behind you, the basin drips, chatters, and groans, laying another inch of stone over the day.
