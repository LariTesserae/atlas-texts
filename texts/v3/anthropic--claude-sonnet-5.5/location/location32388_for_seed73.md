# The Warm Shelf of Ahn-Sethuret

You climb for three days through cold, and then the cold ends.

The last of it is a wall of scree and old ice at a height where your lungs work like wet paper. The path goes over a lip of black stone, and the air changes on your face, the way a door opens onto a kitchen. It is thick, warm, and smells of rot and flowers and hot iron. You are still higher than most clouds ever get, and below you the world is a floor of white. But here the ground is steaming, and something is screaming in the trees.

---

## The Shelf

The Shelf is a high basin, a broad shallow bowl about forty kilometers across, cupped among peaks that stand another kilometer or two above it. Its floor is a mosaic of terraces, marsh, and low dome-shaped hills, all dropping slowly toward a central chain of lakes. The rim peaks are bare and cold, with snow on their shoulders, and the snow sends meltwater down into the warmth. The basin itself is tropical.

The heat does not come from the sun, though the sun is strong here, in air thin enough to make the sky a deep blue-violet at noon. It comes up from below. The rock is warm to the hand everywhere, like a sleeping animal, and in the low places it is hot enough to make you lift your foot. The bedrock is dark and fine-grained, threaded with veins of a pale, waxy, translucent mineral that geologists from the lowlands have never matched to anything. The veins are warmest. Follow one across a hillside and you can trace it by the line of steam and the line of ferns that grow along it like a green seam.

Nothing here is new. The hills are rounded and soft, the drainage settled into deep, patient channels, the stream beds lined with cobbles worn to the size of eggs. The mountains around the Shelf have had their edges ground down over spans that make the warmth seem more remarkable. Whatever heats this place has been doing it for longer than the valleys have had their current shape.

## Water

Everything is wet.

The warm rock breathes moisture upward, and the cold rim air, sliding down as night wind, catches it. The result is a condensing atmosphere that never quite becomes rain and never quite stops. By midmorning a fine mist stands in the hollows. By afternoon it thickens into a low ceiling that drips. At night it comes down in sheets you can feel as a change of weight on your skin. Real storms are rare, but the Shelf is never dry.

The **weeping rocks** are the signature. Cliff faces and boulders sweat continuously from pores along the pale veins, thin clear water that runs warm and tastes faintly of coins. Below each weeping face lies a stained fan of orange and green algae, and below that a pool. Some pools are the size of bathtubs and some of ballrooms. Many are hot enough to poach an egg, and others are cool where some deeper current reaches them. The animals know which is which. You will not, at first.

Between the terraces the ground goes to marsh: floating mats of sedge and moss over black water, so soft that a step through the crust drops you to the thigh. Water sits a hand's depth under most of the low ground. Where the marsh meets the hills there are long edges of standing reeds taller than a person, rattling constantly in the rising air.

## Growth

It is lush in a compressed and layered way, as if the vegetation had been packed into the basin by hand.

Tree ferns, thick as a man's waist, rise in stands to ten or twelve meters, their fronds dripping. Beneath them are smaller ferns, then mosses, then a floor of liverworts, all wet and glistening. Broadleaf trees with waxy, dark-red new leaves knit the canopy above, and every branch is furred with epiphytes: orchids, bromeliad-like cups holding small pools of their own, and pale strings of lichen hanging like wet hair. The bromeliad cups are full of tadpoles. Tadpoles live in the treetops here, and the frogs breed thirty meters above the ground.

The plants have odd habits. Many grow lopsided, leaning toward the pale veins in the rock, the way plants lean toward light. Roots seek the veins and wrap them. Where a vein runs shallow under a hillside, a single plant species may dominate the line of it, a strip of one kind of leaf running like a ruled stroke across the mixed green. Botanists who have visited say the plants are not drawing heat but something else, and cannot say what.

Higher up the terraces, where it cools a little, the forest thins to giant heath and tussock, and then to a strange belt of hot, wet alpine meadow: yellow flowers and cushion plants growing in soil that steams, under a sky dark enough to show a pale daytime moon.

## Fauna

It is loud with life.

At dawn the chorus begins with frogs, and it is not one sound but a great many overlapping. There are clicks, ratchets, low bass thrums that you feel in your sternum, and whistles rising in glissandi. Birds enter next, with bright rattling calls in the canopy and the heavy, whooping wingbeats of large fruit-eaters crossing the open air. Insects run under all of it, a steady electric shimmer that never stops and rises with the heat of the day. Add the sound of water, running, dripping, and pouring off the terrace edges in a hundred small falls, and the Shelf has a roar like a crowd in a stone room. Conversation at normal volume fails beyond a few paces. People who live here speak with their hands, or close.

The animals are dense and layered. Amphibians are the dominant vertebrates: frogs from the size of a fingernail to that of a dog, salamanders as long as a boat oar, and a blunt-headed climbing caecilian that swims through the mossy soil like a pale eel through water. Reptiles bask on the warm rock, especially on the pylons, of which more below. Small furred things and large winged ones feed on fruit and each other. Fish fill the lakes. There are also mist-swimmers, thumb-sized translucent creatures that drift through the fog layer at dusk in shoals, feeding on the condensed droplets and perhaps on the spores in them. They glow faintly green when disturbed. Swat one and it leaves a streak of light on your palm.

The strangest habit belongs to the animals as a whole. They sing in intervals. Amid the noise of the chorus there are gaps at a regular pace, and in the gaps a low pulse can be heard, from the ground or from the air. The animals fall silent for a beat and then resume, all together, as if listening to something. Every few minutes the whole basin holds its breath and lets it go.

## The Built Things

Few people have ever been here, and the marks they left are sparse. What was here before them is stranger.

Scattered across the Shelf, in no pattern anyone has traced, stand the **pylons**. Each is a slender spire of off-white ceramic, smooth as an eggshell, ten to forty meters tall, rooted in the rock with no visible foundation. They are warm, always. They hum, a note so low it is more felt than heard, and this is the pulse the animals listen for. Lizards lie along them in rows. Vines climb them and die at a certain height, leaving a clean band of bare white at the top. In the lakes, some pylons rise from the water with no ripple at their bases, and the mist near them does not condense.

Nobody knows who raised them or what they do. The reasonable belief, held at the edges of the Shelf, is that they are the reason the basin is warm, that they draw heat from far below and let it seep out through the veins. The reasonable belief also cannot explain why the heat does not fade, why the humming pulses in a slow cycle of a few minutes overlaid on another of a season, or why instruments carried in from the lowlands drift in odd directions near the spires. Compasses lean. Clocks lose small amounts of time, and then, after a day or two, gain it back.

Other remains are humbler. There is a stair cut into the rock beside a waterfall, going nowhere. There is a ring of squared stones in a reed bed, half sunk. There is a small herder's hut with a roof of fern thatch, patched, sometimes lived in by a person who comes up in the dry season with a few animals and does not stay. A line of cairns marks the route from the pass. Someone still replaces the topmost stones.

## Slow Time

Nothing here hurries. The marsh edge creeps a few meters in a decade. The pylons' hum shifts pitch across years. Trees grow in the steady, unstartled way of things that have never met a frost, and fall when they are done, and the ground takes them in a season. The mist thickens and thins on its daily rhythm, and the great pause, when the animals stop and listen, comes as regularly as breath. You could sit for a week and see the same day repeat with the light slightly changed.

At dusk the sky above goes black-blue at the zenith while the fog below stays gold. The mist-swimmers rise in their shoals, glowing where the air moves, and the frogs' second chorus begins. The pylons hum, the rocks weep, and the snow on the rim peaks turns pink, so far above the warm wet floor that it looks like weather from another planet. Then the pause comes, and everything listens, and you find you are listening too.
