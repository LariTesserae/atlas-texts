# The Salt Works at Verrin Flat

The land is barely land. It lies a hand's breadth above the sea and slopes so gently that the tide comes in as a thin, silent sheet, and in the hour after the turn you cannot say where the coast is. The air is at blood heat and so wet that the horizon has no edge. The sky is a low lid of pale, brownish overcast, and the sun is a dull coin behind it, bright enough to cast no shadows. Everything is lit evenly and dimly, as in a room with the blinds drawn.

## Ground

The surface is mud, salt, and old concrete, in that order of abundance. Where the mud has dried it forms a grey-white crust that breaks underfoot into plates, each curled at the edges like a roof tile. Under the crust it is black, warm, and sulfurous. Where water lies in shallow pans, it takes on colors that have nothing to do with the sky. Some pans are milky green, some rust-orange, some a bruised pink from the bacterial mats that grow in the brine. These mats are the nearest thing to vegetation here. They are films and felts and slick ribbons, never rising higher than a thumbnail. Nothing with a stem or leaf has ever taken hold. The soil is too salt, the water too hot, and the ground too often drowned and baked again.

The land has been this way a very long time. The old channels have cut themselves deep and then filled in with silt and cut again, so that the plain is scored with abandoned meanders, each softened into a shallow trough. The few rises are low banks of shell and compacted silt, rounded and smooth, with pale salt weeping from their flanks. Rain, tide, and heat have worked the place into a settled shape that changes only in small ways: a channel shifting, a bank slumping, a pan silting up.

## The Works

Onto this plain, a century or two ago, someone built an industry. Nobody lives in most of it now, but it was built on a scale that assumed a great many people, and it still stands, spread thin across the flat as if the city had been diluted.

Evaporation pans lie in enormous grids, each a kilometer across, their dikes raised and straight and edged with cracked concrete. Between them run the causeways: raised rail beds, some still carrying track, most with the rails sunk in salt scale. Each causeway runs dead straight toward a landmark on the horizon and vanishes into haze before it arrives. The landmarks are pumping houses, squat brick and concrete boxes with tall arched windows, their iron sashes bloomed orange. Farther out, the chemical works rise in clusters: cylindrical tanks, a cracking tower, a run of cooling stacks, and a cluster of chimneys leaning slightly toward the sea. Between clusters there may be four or five kilometers of open pan with nothing on it but a survey post and a line of birds.

The buildings sweat. Every wall streams with condensation, and the concrete is streaked black and green-grey below every seam and sill. Rust runs down the tank sides in long red beards. Inside the pumping houses it drips from the roof trusses into standing water that reflects the windows in perfect, unrippled squares. Salt grows in crusts on the bolts, in fringes on the pipe lagging, and in hard white stalactites from the eaves.

Some of it still works. One pumping station in every five or six turns its great iron wheel, driven by a diesel that thumps at the pace of a slow heart. A signal light still changes at a crossing where no train has run in years. A single stack releases a thread of white steam that climbs straight up and dissolves in the overcast. A small crew, a few dozen people over the whole region, live in a compound of tin-roofed sheds near the main sluice. They keep the gates and the diesels running for reasons that have become mostly habit, and they are almost never seen.

## Fauna

The place belongs to animals, and there are more of them than any other feature suggests.

On the mud flats, fiddler crabs cover the ground so densely that it seems to flow. Each has one oversized claw, and the whole flat appears to be waving slowly in the same direction. Behind the crab bands are mudskippers in their thousands, propped on their fins at the water's edge, and beyond them the snails: tiny cone shells that coat every post, every rail, and every wall to head height. They cluster in the wet seams of the concrete like grey seed. Walk a causeway at low tide and you crush some with every step, and the ground clicks faintly.

Above the pans are the birds. Waders work the shallows in sheets: stilts, avocets, sandpipers, and tens of thousands of small pale shorebirds that lift together, turn, and settle without ever seeming to fly. On the gantries and cracking towers sit the roosters: cormorants in black rows, ibises hunched on every rail, terns along the roof ridges. Rust and salt have been whitened by droppings, so the towers wear guano like snow. Where the brine is rich enough, flamingos stand in pink drifts a kilometer long, heads down, filtering.

The air is alive too. Midges rise from the pans at dusk in slow brown columns, each a hundred meters tall, and the swallows and bats feed on them in the dimness. Brine flies in a dark fur coat the pan edges and lift ahead of your feet in a rolling wave. Under the water, the shrimp and copepods are numerous enough to tint the pans. Every predator has plenty to eat, so there is little drama in the hunting. Herons stalk, a monitor lizard sometimes sinks into the crab crowd and rises with something in its jaws, and a fish eagle takes a mullet from the sluice channel, but these events are small against the great slow abundance.

## Sound

The place is mostly quiet, and the quiet has texture. It is the hush of a hundred thousand small bodies: the tick of crab claws, a faint continuous crackle as the salt crust dries and shifts, the soft rustle of birds resettling. The birds are strangely subdued. Most forage in silence, and the flamingos murmur low among themselves. Now and then a tern rasps or a stilt gives a sharp yip. Far off, one diesel beats its slow beat, and closer there is the steady drip of the sweating buildings, which never stops. When the wheel of a working pump turns, the whole plain seems to hold its breath and then let it go.

## Motion

Nothing here stays as it was. The tide runs twice a day, and the flats change from crawling brown to mirrored silver and back. In the early afternoon the heat builds until the pans shimmer, the distant stacks float above the horizon on inverted copies of themselves, and the air feels like a wet cloth held to the face. By four the clouds drop and thicken, and a storm crosses the plain, often out at sea and visible as a grey curtain, sometimes overhead. The rain is warm and heavy, and it lasts twenty minutes. Every wall runs, every pan is dimpled, and the birds turn their backs to it and sit. Afterward steam rises from the whole world at once, and the light turns the color of weak tea.

Twice a year, on the highest spring tides, the crabs move. They cross the causeways and climb the walls of the works in a dense red-brown tide, and for two nights the rail beds are impassable. The birds follow. For that week the industrial ruins are ringed with feeding, and the plain is loud, though even then not very.

## Evening

At dusk the overcast goes the color of a bruise, and the pans, one by one, take a last flat gleam. The midge columns rise. The bats come out of the brick pumping houses in long streams. Somewhere a diesel coughs and settles. One window in the distant compound lights yellow. The heat does not break. It lingers, damp and dense, over the whole flat land: a warm, quiet, salt-white expanse where a great many small lives go about their business among the patient machinery of a vanished crowd.
