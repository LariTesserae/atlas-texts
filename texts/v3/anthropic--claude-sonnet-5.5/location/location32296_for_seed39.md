# The Foundry Plateau

Nothing here has been made by anyone, and yet the place runs like a works. It is a high, young country still being manufactured, and everything in it is at work.

## The Ground

The plateau sits so high that the sky above it is a darker blue than it should be, nearly ink at noon, with the sun a small hard coin that gives less warmth than it appears to. The air is thin and cold, just above freezing at midday and well below it by night. Breathing is a matter of policy rather than instinct.

The rock is new. Recent uplift has shoved this whole region skyward faster than weather can dull it, so every edge is still an edge. Fault scarps run across the plateau like the seams of freshly welded plate, some of them a few hundred years old, their faces of clean grey-green stone unweathered and slick with seepage. Slabs lean where they were shrugged loose. Boulder fields lie in angular heaps, each block still fitting the shape of the one beside it, as if the mountain had been dropped and not yet swept up. There is almost no soil. Between the blocks is gravel, rock flour, and a pale silt ground by ice into something like flour.

The relief is broad rather than crowded. The plateau opens into basins a day's walk across, ringed by ridges that are raw, toothy, and snow-streaked, with a few summits standing higher yet, hazy and far. Between the high points lie flat gravel floors, wide braided channels, and shallow lakes the color of pewter.

## The Water

Water is everywhere just under the surface of things. The rock weeps: from every fracture, in every shaded face, thin sheets and threads seep out and freeze into glassy curtains at night, then run again by mid-morning. Whole cliffs glisten. Along the scarps, water gathers in ledges and drips in slow ticking rhythms that set the tempo of the quiet.

The valley floors are half marsh, half gravel. Cushion mats of sedge and moss form sodden green islands in the grey, and walking across them is like walking on a saturated sponge, cold water rising around every step. Beneath them, ice lenses lie a hand's depth down and never fully melt. Pools sit in hollows, skinned with ice in the morning and dark and bright by afternoon. Fog forms in the basins at dusk, condensing out of the very ground, and lies in layers, so that ridgelines rise out of it like islands.

## The Machinery

This is where the land shows its industrial character. It works in cycles, loud with pressure and heat, though it uses no engine anyone built.

Along the great fault that bisects the plateau is a line of **geothermal vents**, dozens of them, each with its own schedule. Some hiss steadily like boilers at working pressure. Others hold quiet for an hour, then throw a column of steam and scalding water thirty meters into the freezing air with a sound like a valve giving way. The steam plumes lean in the wind and freeze on everything downwind, so the ground for a hundred meters is furred with white rime that is scoured off and regrown every day. The heavier drops fall as hot rain and crust the rocks in mineral: **terraces of rust-orange, ochre, and sulfur yellow**, stacked in tiers where mineral-rich water spills from basin to basin. They are the plateau's true manufactured product, layer on layer of iron and silica set down in rings. Fresh stone is being made here as you watch, in thin bright films.

Elsewhere, meltwater drains through a hidden plumbing of fractures and reappears far down-slope in **pulsing springs**, gushing and slacking on a slow period of some minutes, like a pump with a stuck governor. Where a glacier meets the plateau's edge, ice advances like a conveyor belt loaded with stone, dropping its cargo of gravel at the terminus in long parallel ridges. Each night the ice groans and cracks. Each day the streams below swell brown with the load of ground-up mountain they carry to the lower world.

Nothing here is finished. Faults tick and shift. A rockfall somewhere on a far ridge sends up a puff of pale dust, and a few seconds later a dry rattling arrives across the distance. The place feels under construction, by a process too large to see, at a pace just fast enough to be noticed.

## The Weather

The weather runs in rounds. Mornings begin clear and brittle, the ice curtains glinting. By late morning, moisture rising from the marshes builds white towers of cloud against the ridges. Afternoons bring sleet or wet snow in gray veils that trail their lower edges across the basins and pass on. The light goes dim and pearly under these ceilings, shadowless, the colors muted to slate, moss, and rust. At evening the cloud tears open in the west and the low sun throws a brief copper light along the wet scarps, so the whole weeping rock face lights up like a sheet of hammered metal. Then the fog comes up, and then the frost.

## The Living

Vegetation is sparse: cushion plants, sedge, lichens in yellow-green and black on every stable stone, tough grasses in the lee of boulders. Trees do not exist. But the fauna are abundant, drawn by the warm vent margins and the wet green pockets, and they crowd the sheltered places while the wide gravel stays empty.

Around the vents and their warm runoff live enormous concentrations of small life. Mats of pink and green microbial growth coat the warm streams. Swarms of flies and springtails, black dust on the snow, gather where the thaw seeps out. Feeding on them are **birds in thousands**: waders picking the marsh, pipits and finches darting between rocks, and rosy-breasted flocks that rise all at once from the terraces in a rattle of wings and settle again. Over the ridges, great scavenging raptors ride the thermals from the vents, unhurried, scanning.

On the gravel floors live **burrowing rodents and pikas**, whose warrens honeycomb every deposit of soft silt, and who whistle sharp alarms when a shadow crosses. Hunting them are foxes and small pale weasels, and higher, a lean, thick-coated cat rarely seen. Herds of heavy, shaggy grazers move across the basins in slow lines, hundreds strong, breath steaming, keeping to the sedge flats and the warm springs. They pass in a low continuous grunting, their hooves breaking the crust of night ice. Behind them come ravens. Wolves shadow the herd's edges, and the herd tightens and shifts in response, a slow wave of alertness running through it. It is a working ecosystem at full stretch, all hunger and thaw and migration, set against a landscape that offers little beyond warmth and water.

## The Sound

Mostly quiet, but layered. A constant faint seep and trickle. The hiss of steam somewhere always. The tick of freezing and thawing stone. Wind, when it comes, whistles thinly through the gaps in the scarps. Then punctuation: the whistle of a pika, the sudden roar of a geyser letting go, the crack of ice deep in a glacier, and the long, low, communal grunt of a herd on the move. Between these events the silence is large and cold and rings faintly, like the inside of a great vessel that has just been struck.

## Rules

Everything wet here is also either freezing or being warmed, and the land lives on that boundary. Nothing stays put: water becomes ice, ice becomes steam, steam becomes stone. The plateau's whole nature is production. Given time, it will wear down, soften, grow soil and trees and quiet. But not yet. For now it is an unfinished thing in full operation, high and cold and gleaming, humming under an inky sky.
