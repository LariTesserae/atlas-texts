# The Bladder of the Mountain

You reach it by a crack you would not have noticed if the crack were not breathing.

It is near the top of a young volcano, well above where the air thins and the sky goes the dark blue of a bruise. The cone is only a few hundred years old. Its flanks are black clinker and glassy scoria, still sharp enough to open a boot, and no lichen has settled on them yet. Wind scours the summit, and the cold up there is a real cold. But along one shelf of fresh lava there is a seam no wider than a shoulder, and out of it comes a slow exhalation of warm, wet air that smells of struck flint, boiled stone, and something faintly sweet, like bread left too long in a closed room.

Go in sideways. The walls are obsidian, unweathered, with edges that keep their first-day sharpness. They are wet, and the wetness runs in threads that seem to hold to the glass rather than fall. Twenty paces in, the seam turns and opens, and the air becomes the temperature of blood, then warmer.

## The pocket

The chamber is small. From wall to wall it is perhaps forty paces, about the size of a village threshing floor, and its roof is low enough at the rim that you would crouch there. Toward the center it rises into a rough dome. It is a gas bubble frozen into the lava as the flow cooled, and it has been kept as a hidden room inside the mountain.

The floor is a shallow basin of standing water, milk-green and body-hot, ankle-deep at the edges and knee-deep at the middle. Water seeps from every wall. It beads on the black glass, gathers, and slides down in sheets. The whole dome weeps, and the air holds so much condensation that your skin is wet within a few breaths. Where the roof has a fine fracture, a thread of daylight comes through and lies across the steam as a pale, solid-looking rod. That is the only light, and it is enough. The chamber is lit like a cloudy afternoon, evenly and without shadow.

Nothing here has been built. No ledge was cut or wall stacked, and no hand has ever set one stone on another. The place is entirely the mountain's own accident.

## The crowd

You will think the walls are moving. They are covered.

The fauna here is so dense that the surface of things is made of it. On the wet glass, in the water, in the mud of the basin, in the air as a fine drifting haze, everywhere is something alive, and most of it is touching something else. There is a felt layer of pale mites, each smaller than a grain of salt, that ripples when the steam shifts. Above them are translucent snails with shells like drops of resin, grazing the bacterial film. There are soft-bodied grubs in strings, fused end to end, dragged along by a leader that does not seem to lead. There are flat crimson worms in the shallows, stacked like coins. There are hopping things with too many joints, and tiny amphibians the color of wet slate, packed so tightly that a step must be placed on a living surface or not at all. Above all of them, on the roof, hang the bats. They are the size of a thumb, hairless, pink-veined, and hung wing to wing over the whole ceiling like a second skin, and they do not sleep and do not fly.

The only plant life is what the mountain allows. There are mats of orange and grey fungus in the seeps, a slime of green-black algae in the warmest water, and a threadwork of white filaments that runs along the cracks and seems to connect one stretch of glass to another. Nothing has leaves. Nothing is rooted deep. The animals eat the mats, eat each other, and eat what falls from the weeping walls: mineral scum, flakes of sulfur, dead things.

None of this is loud. The chamber is nearly silent. You hear the drip of water, an occasional tick as the obsidian shifts in cooling, and the breath of the vent. The animals themselves make almost no noise. They speak by touch, one antenna laid against another, a wing-tip against a wet snout, and the only sound of it is a faint, damp, papery ticking, like fingertips brushed together in another room. It is a great crowd in a hushed cathedral.

## The tools

Then you notice that some of them are carrying things.

On the shelves near the edge, where the glass makes a natural bench, a few of the larger animals are at work. They are palm-sized, pale as candle wax, with long fingers and no eyes, and they squat in the mud and strike flakes from lumps of obsidian with pebbles held in both hands. They are knapping. The flakes are thin and curved, translucent black, and very sharp. Other animals pick them up. Some scrape fungus from the walls with them, and some cut the strings of grubs. Some wrap a flake in a twist of white filament and carry it like a small knife. When the flake dulls, it is dropped into the water and someone else takes another.

They make nothing that stands. They have no shelter, no pile, no cache. The tools live in their hands and are set down and forgotten, and the floor of the basin under the water is paved with a thousand blades in a slow drift, a bed of black glass leaves.

## Where it breaks

This is not a stable place, and the way it fails is part of what it is.

**The count will not hold.** Try to count the bats on the ceiling and you get one number. Count again and you get another, larger. Look away and the gap you left is already filled, though nothing moved to fill it. The chamber seems to keep a constant fullness, as though the place were a vessel that must always be brimming. When an animal dies, its body is gone within a breath. Something new steps out of the wall's wet glass, as if the obsidian had a back side full of them, and takes the vacancy without a sound.

**The animals are not separate kinds.** Watch the snails long enough and you see one grow a second shell, then a third, and the shells turn into a grub's segments, and the grub is a hopping thing with its legs still folding out. Species overlap and blur. An amphibian and a mite occupy the same place, one inside the outline of the other, and neither seems troubled. You may find a pale toolmaker with a bat's wing-membrane between two fingers, holding a flake, unhurried, entirely correct.

**Time runs in layers.** In the same square yard of wall you can see eggs, hatchlings, adults, and desiccated husks, all in the same instant, all in contact. Some of the husks are still moving. Some of the eggs are already old. The animals do not live through time in order. They pass through it like water in a braided stream, and each of them is, at once, several ages.

**Depth is dishonest.** The dome looks about forty paces across and low. But walk toward the center and it does not get nearer. You can walk ten minutes, and the basin's edge is behind you and the middle is still the same distance off, and the steam thickens, and the crowd on the floor thins by no amount at all. Turn back and the seam is three steps behind you. The room is small from outside and is as large as it needs to be from within, and it does not tell you which is true.

**The wall is thin.** In places the obsidian goes clear, and through it you can see the mountain as it was before it rose: a flat plain, a grey sea, the same hot light. You see the same animals crowding there, in the same posture, at some earlier or later time. Sometimes a toolmaker on the far side looks up, blind, and raises a flake toward you, and you find you have raised your hand too.

**The heat has no source.** The vent breathes warm, but the warmth is not in the vent. It is in the crowd. It comes from the sheer press of living bodies, and where the animals are thinnest the air cools, and where they are packed it is unbearable. The chamber is a furnace made of touching.

## The weather of the pocket

Inside this closed room there is still weather. Every few hours the condensation on the roof reaches its limit and the dome rains, a fine warm downpour that beads on the bats without wetting them and sends the mites into a synchronized shiver. The water level rises a hand's breadth, and the blades on the basin floor lift in the current and turn, glittering, and settle in a new arrangement. Then the vent pulses, the steam thickens to a white wall, the thread of daylight goes solid and gold, and the whole chamber goes still for a moment, every animal frozen mid-touch. Then a ticking starts at one wall, spreads, and the crowd flows on.

There are hunters here too. A long, translucent, many-legged thing, thin as a blade of grass, threads through the mites and takes them by the hundred. It is eaten in turn by the snails, which are eaten by the toolmakers, who die and are unmade into the mud and become the mites. The cycle turns, but the number in it never changes. That is the rule of the room, and every process is a way of keeping it.

## Leaving

When you go, the seam is easier to find than it was, and it is colder. You come out into thin air and an enormous sky, on a slope of black gravel that stretches for miles under a sun that has no heat in it. Your clothes steam. Your hands are wet. In your palm, if you are unlucky or lucky, is a single curved flake of obsidian that you do not remember picking up, wrapped in a twist of white thread.

Behind you the crack is closing, or opening. You cannot tell which. The mountain goes on breathing warm air into the cold, and inside it the small, packed, silent world goes on being full.
