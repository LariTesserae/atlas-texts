# The Pot-Wall Well

You come to it by walking across flat ground that gives no warning. There is dry hardpan, a few thorn shrubs, and a low ring of fired brick no higher than your knee. Then the sound reaches you. It is a tide of cooing, clicking, dripping, hammering, and a low many-throated hum. You look over the ring and the ground falls away.

The pit is about sixty paces across and perhaps eighteen deep. It is round, or was once, and the centuries have made it softly lopsided. There is no raw earth anywhere in it. Every surface you can see was shaped, fired, stacked, rammed, plastered, or woven by hands. The walls are terraced in ring after ring of mud brick and fired brick, and set into the brick, thousands upon thousands, are the mouths of clay pots.

## The Wall

The pots are laid on their sides with their necks facing out, like cells in a comb. Some are the size of a fist and some are large enough to crawl into. They are orange, buff, grey-black, and a dull plum where a kiln ran too hot. Most are stained white below the mouth by droppings and dark above it by generations of soot, oil, and rubbing feathers. Each is a nest, a roost, a hive, or a granary. Pigeons and swifts hold the upper tiers, small owls the middle, and bats the deep pots near the bottom where the air is cool. Bees have the sun-warmed east face, where the pots are stoppered with dung and clay and hum on their own private note.

Weathering has rounded everything. The brick edges have gone soft as old soap. Stair treads are dished in the middle from bare feet. Long tongues of salt bloom creep down from the pot mouths and crust the plaster in pale lace. The wall is a record of repairs. A patch of yellow brick lies over red, red lies over grey, and grey lies over something older, a fused vitreous rubble that nobody made deliberately. No stretch of wall is younger than the grandparents of the people who mend it, and none looks quite the same as it did in their memory.

## The Floor

The floor is brick laid in herringbone and worn so smooth that it holds the sky in a dull sheen after rain. It is warm underfoot. The sun reaches the bottom for only a few hours each day, and the brick stores that heat and gives it back all evening. Drains run across it in shallow fired-clay channels. Some are open, some are capped with slabs, and some vanish into the wall through pipes that were once glazed and are now furred with green.

Water is the reason the pit exists, though there is little of it. Rain is rare and rough, and the warm air rarely holds moisture. But at night the deep pit chills, and by dawn the walls are beaded. Every terrace has a lip and every lip drips into a gutter. Gutters join into channels, and channels tip into a chain of basins descending like a staircase of small pools. The last is a round cistern at the center, capped with a wooden lid pierced by a bucket-hole. Condensation is patient work. Each morning the whole pit ticks and drips like a slow clock, and the cistern gains a hand's width.

When real rain comes, perhaps once in several weeks, it is the great event. Water strikes the rim, sheets down every terrace, and finds every gutter at once. The pots ring and the channels roar. The whole pit becomes a drum and a fountain, and the birds go quiet for a few minutes out of something like respect. Then they all begin again, louder, and the brick smells of hot clay and wet dust and old feathers.

## Sound

It is never quiet. The base layer is pigeons, a continuous wet murmuring from perhaps four thousand throats. Above it come the swifts, shrieking in fast loops around the rim, and below it the drip of water in twenty different pitches. On top of that is the work. Somewhere a paddle is slapping wet clay, a bone awl scrapes a pot rim, and a mallet taps a brick into a new course. Children run the terraces with rattles made of dried gourds to keep hawks away. Goats in small brick pens complain, and hens scratch in the gravel of the second ring.

The strangest sound is the wind. When it crosses the rim at the right angle, air enters the necks of the larger pots and they sound. It is not a note but a chord, a deep, slightly out-of-tune organ tone that swells and fades over the whole pit. It can be heard through the walls at night. The builders never planned it. Over generations they learned that certain pot shapes carry certain pitches, and they began to place them on purpose. A young mason's first task is to make a pot that sings a new note and doesn't clash. Sometimes it does clash, and the whole pit winces. Then the pot is removed, or a nesting bird is allowed to fill it with grass and quiet it.

## The Living Things

Vegetation is sparse and always in containers. Fig trees stand in huge half-buried jars along the third terrace, their leaves stiff and dusty. Caper and gourd vines are trained up frames of split reed, and a few bitter herbs grow in cracked pots on the sunny ledges. Moss and pale ferns live only in the damp channels, and they are treated like livestock, thinned and fed. Nothing has roots in true soil. What passes for soil is composted guano, ash, and crushed shell, carried in baskets and renewed every season.

Animals outnumber the people ten to one at least. Pigeons are the economy, giving eggs, meat, and dung. Bats eat the mosquitoes that breed in the basins. Geckos in the plaster eat the moths, small snakes eat the geckos, and the small owls eat nearly everything. A fox-sized wild cat sometimes drops from the rim to hunt, and the whole pit lifts in a roar of wings. Then the birds settle again, and one nest in the wall is empty.

The people are perhaps a hundred and fifty. They are lean, clay-stained, quick on the ledges, and hard to startle. Their tools are fired clay, wood, bone, flint, reed, and a little soft copper, which they beat into pins and hooks. They wear woven grass and undyed wool, with pigeon feathers in their hair as a kind of signature. They keep no written records. Their history is kept in the wall, in which brick style tells which family mended which section in which generation, and they can read it as easily as text.

## Rules of the Place

Nothing here is ever finished. A section left alone for a season begins to slump, so repair is constant and each generation adds to the wall. The pit is slowly getting shallower, because bricks laid on the rim and fill dumped in the bottom are gradually lifting the floor. It is also getting narrower, because each new terrace pushes inward. Some elders say that in a thousand years the well will close over and become a solid warm brick hill full of sealed chambers and singing pots, and that this is what it wants.

Light is plain and steady. It falls from the disk of sky as a slanting column, moves across the brick through the day, and lights each terrace in turn like a slow lamp. At noon the bottom is hot and blazing, and everyone retreats to the shaded pot-rooms behind the walls. At dusk the swifts drop in a spiral, thousands of them, each finding its own pot by feel. The sound builds to its loudest, a great wet rush of wings. Then it drops to the murmur, the drip, and the low tuned breath of the wall, and the well settles in for the night.
