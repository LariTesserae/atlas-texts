# The Weft at Low Kiln

The basin is small enough to cross in the time it takes a pot to cool. It sits below the surrounding land like a dish pressed into a table. From the rim you can see all of it at once, and that is the last time you will be able to. Inside, it is too dense to see across.

Nothing here is ground. The floor is fired brick, laid in herringbone and still faintly warm, as though the kiln beneath it went out an hour ago. The edges of every brick are sharp enough to notice through a sandal. Nothing has weathered. The corners are crisp, the glaze-drips still glossy, the knapped flint in the walls still bright at its fractures. It all looks finished yesterday, and you get the sense that yesterday is always the day it was finished.

## The Grove

The trees are coiled. A trunk is a spiral of clay rope, thumb-pressed and smoothed, rising twice the height of a person and then unraveling into hundreds of branches. Those branches are split cane and twisted bark cord. The leaves are the lush part: broad strips of dyed bast, thatch-reed blades, flattened copper foil beaten by hand until it is thin as a fingernail, and here and there a leaf of scraped hide with the veins drawn on in ochre. They overlap so thickly that the light comes down green-amber and cross-hatched, a warm weave of shadow over the brick.

They are alive, and it takes a while to accept that. In the slow hours the leaves turn toward the light. Cord fibers swell and tighten. Where a coil of clay trunk has cracked, a pale new coil is pressing out of the crack, damp-looking but not wet, laid down by nothing you can see. The grove grows by *addition*. It is never sprouted or budded. It is put on, one turn of the rope at a time, by a process that has no hands and leaves a thumbprint anyway.

The thumbprints are everywhere. Every coil, brick, and knot carries one, and they are not all the same size. Most are ordinary. Some are as wide as a dinner plate, pressed into the flank of a boulder-sized loaf of fired mud. A few are so small you need to kneel and squint to see the whorls. Local wisdom, if that word applies to a place with no locals you can name, holds that a thing lives only as long as its print is legible. Where prints have been rubbed smooth by a passing shoulder, the thing beneath goes gray and brittle within a season. So the paths are narrow and everyone walks sideways.

## What Drinks

There is almost no water. One clay cistern, sealed with pitch, sits in a hollow near the center. It holds a hand's depth of something clear that may be water and may be a kind of thin mineral oil. Nothing drinks from it, and animals go around it.

What the place drinks instead is resin, a thick amber milk that pools in bowls carved into the branch-forks. The whole grove is plumbed with hollow reed veins that carry it slowly from the roots up. Squeezed leaves smell of pine, warm honey, and hot dust. When you put your ear to a trunk you hear it moving, less a flow than a crawl, a sound like someone breathing very carefully in the next room.

## The Fauna

Abundant, and all of it assembled. Birds are made of split quill, sinew, and a clay beak baked hard enough to ring. They perch in rows along the cane-branches and turn their heads in unison, stiffly, like a clock deciding. Beetles are fired-clay shells with flint-chip legs. They scuttle across the bricks, and where they walk the herringbone is scratched into a faint script no one has read. Moths are scraps of pounded bark-cloth with wire-thin antennae. Something like deer, if deer were carved from cedar and strung together with gut, stands in the deeper aisles. When it moves it clacks softly and pauses, and the pauses are the part that is alive.

None of them eat. They tend. The beetles burnish the bricks. The birds pull loose fibers from the leaf-weave and tuck them back in. The cedar deer lean against sagging trunks and hold them up for days. The whole fauna is maintenance, endless and small, and every animal is also a repair to the place it lives in.

## The Breakdown

Somewhere in the middle, the rules of the made and the grown fail together.

A doorway is set into the side of one great trunk, a rectangle of lashed cane with a hide flap. Behind it is not hollow wood but the same grove from the same angle, the same doorway in the same trunk, with a slightly different light. It is a room that opens into itself. You can walk through and come out a few paces from where you started, with your own footprints already pressed into the brick ahead of you, warm.

The axes are stranger. In one glade a stand of shrubs bears stone hand-axes on their twigs. They ripen from gray to a fresh flaked-cream color, and when ripe they drop. Where they land they lie on the brick with their edges still sharp, waiting. If you pick one up, its grip is already worn to fit your hand, and you know without being told that you have used it before. The knowing is not a memory. It sits in the tool, in the polish on the butt, and it seeps up your wrist.

A rope path crosses the low center. You can walk it, and it is also the root of the largest tree, and it is also, at one point, a long thin sentence someone began saying and never finished. Follow it too far and the fibers begin to arrange themselves into your own gait. You start to feel the grove's attention, patient and not unkind, the way a weaver's eye rests on a thread.

## Light, Sound, Time

The light is ordinary, warm midday light that never quite reaches noon or leaves it. Warmth rises off the bricks and comes down through the leaves, so the air is bathed from both sides. It is not hot. It is the temperature of a held bowl.

The place is mostly quiet. What sound there is arrives in small pieces: reed leaves ticking together, the tap of a clay beak, the slow crawl of resin in a trunk, and now and then, from somewhere too far to locate, a knapping sound, one flake struck off a core, then a long wait, then another.

Everything moves on a scale of days and seasons. A new coil takes a week to complete its turn. A leaf takes a month to unfold. The cedar deer cross the whole basin in a year. If you stay long enough, the place will reach for you. The first sign is a faint warmth at the back of your hand, where a thumb-shaped pressure has begun, gently, to make you.
