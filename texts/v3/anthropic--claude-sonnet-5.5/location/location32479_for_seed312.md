# The Stacked Country

## Ground

The mountains here are new, and you can see it in the stone. Nothing has had time to soften. Ridgelines are serrated, and the faces are cleaved flat along fault planes still pale where they broke. Below each cliff lies a skirt of fresh scree, angular shards the color of bread crust, of slate, of pewter, all unrounded and unweathered. Where the rock is freshly exposed it is sometimes banded rust and green, and when you crack a piece open the inside is brighter than the outside, as if the mountain has only just been opened to air.

This is high country, a plateau broken into ranges, a shelf of the world raised so far that the sky above it is a darker blue than it should be. The valleys are not deep by carving but by uplift: steep-sided troughs where the land was shoved skyward on either side, their floors still flat with unsorted gravel and glacial flour. Rivers are young and braided and go where they like across the gravel, and they are cold enough to ache the bones of a hand held in them.

It is cold everywhere. Frost lives in the shadow side of every stone through the whole day, and in the deep months it lives in the sunlight too. The ground under the frost is hard as iron. Breath shows nearly always.

## Scale

It goes on past the point where distance means anything. From a high pass you see ridge behind ridge, each paler and bluer than the last, until they are a rumor of ridge, and on every one of them are the marks of habitation. You could walk for a season and never leave the city.

The scale is large enough that the place makes its own weather. Smoke from ten thousand dung and scrub-root fires rises in the cold still mornings and pools under the inversion layer, a brown-gold lid over a whole basin. Above the largest settlements, clouds form where the warmth of the crowded hearths lifts damp air past its dew point. These are small, persistent, lens-shaped clouds that sit over the city like a hat, so people in the basin can tell from the ridge whether a city is awake. Mist climbs the valleys from the far lowlands each afternoon, thick and slow, and the upper city sits in it as an island sits in a sea. In the evenings it thins and the stars come out hard and countless.

## The City

The city is not built on the land so much as into it. It is one continuous, layered structure of fitted stone, running up valley walls in terraces and staircases, across saddles, along the flanks of ridges, and down into the gravel floors. It has no center and no wall. It has districts, and the districts have names that sound like weather.

The builders have no iron. They have hammerstones, antler wedges, copper chisels soft enough to need constant reshaping, bone awls, obsidian flakes traded from somewhere unimaginably far. What they have in place of metal is patience and the fresh geology. The young stone splits along clean joints, and a skilled mason reads a slab the way a butcher reads a joint of meat, finds the plane, wedges it, waits for the frost or a wet rope of rawhide to do the work. The blocks come out nearly square and are fitted dry, with no mortar. The corners are tight enough that a knife blade won't enter. The houses lean slightly inward for the earthquakes. These come rarely and slowly, a low groan and a tilt rather than a shock, because the ranges are still rising, a finger's width in a lifetime, and the masons account for it. Walls are rebuilt with the shift, and old houses show their history in slightly bent courses of stone.

Density is the defining texture of the place. Houses press against houses. The roof of one is the yard of the next, and the yard of that is the street of the one above. Streets are often just the gaps between roofs, or narrow stair-lanes worn into pale hollows by generations of feet, or covered galleries where the ceilings are the floors of upper dwellings. Doorways are small and low against the heat loss, with hide curtains or wool hangings, and the wall thickness is such that each door is a short tunnel. In the deep cold, whole blocks of houses share walls and hearth-warmth, and the lanes between are stuffed with drying dung cakes, hanging fleeces, ranks of dried tubers, bundles of grass.

Bridges of braided grass rope, renewed every year in ritual, cross the narrower gorges, and beside them the stone bridges the masons are proud of: a single flat slab laid across, or corbelled arches of stacked slate that never quite meet, each course reaching a little further toward the other.

## Water

The place is damp rather than wet, and everything here is a way of collecting moisture.

Little rain falls. It comes in short, cold, sleety showers that skim the ridges in the afternoons. What the city lives on is condensation, fog, and slow melt. On the windward ridges, rows of upright nets of knotted grass and hair stand like a strange fence, and mist beads on them and runs into stone gutters. Every house has a rooftop basin, shallow and lined with clay. Every district has a cistern cut into the rock, capped with a slab, and the sound of dripping in those dark chambers is a kind of prayer. Meltwater comes down from the icefields in narrow channels lined with fitted stone, each one owned and maintained by a lineage, each turned by a small gate of slate. At dawn the channels are skinned with ice, and children walk the lines with wooden mallets, tapping them clear.

Here and there are tarns in the cirques, small and black, absolutely still, ringed with rime. People go to them to be quiet.

## Living Things

Vegetation is thin, and mostly it is not what people planted. Cushion plants form green-gray domes as hard as stone, so dense you can stand on them. Orange and mustard lichens crust every sheltered stone face, and the oldest walls wear them like tapestries. Low wiry shrubs grow in the lee of boulders, their roots burned for fuel. On the sunlit terraces there are patches of cultivation: knuckled tubers, hardy barley, bitter greens, all in thin soil hauled up in baskets and held in place by stone lips. Every field is small, walled, and worth more than the house beside it.

Animals are the abundant life. Herds of long-necked, wool-bearing grazers drift across the high slopes and through the city's upper lanes, and they are half-tame, half-wild, known by ear-notch and dye. Pack animals wait in the lanes with their loads. Small brown rodents are kept in the floors of houses, fed on scraps, and eaten at festivals. Every wall has its nests: swifts, choughs, pale-headed finches in the chinks, ravens on the roofs. Great scavenging birds with wingspans wider than a doorway circle the thermals over the ridges and drop, sometimes, onto the flat funeral roofs. Marmots whistle from the scree. Dogs, lean and dense-coated, sleep in doorways in tangled heaps for warmth. Even in the most crowded lanes there is something with a pulse within arm's reach, and in the deep cold that warmth is counted as part of the architecture.

## Sound

It is mostly quiet, and the quiet is not empty. Cold air and stone and wool damp the noise. What you hear is wind across the cornices, the tick of frost in stone, a wooden bell clonking on a beast, the scrape of a loom's beater, the soft two-note call of someone across a gap, the drip in the cisterns. People here speak low. Shouting on the steep slopes is thought rude, and in some places dangerous, because loose scree on the young slopes will sometimes take a loud voice as a reason to move. Music is flutes of bone, reed pipes, hand drums under blankets. Evening songs are hummed rather than sung, drifting between roofs.

## Light and Change

The light is clean and ordinary and fierce for how thin the air is. Shadows are sharp and blue-cold, and sunlit stone is warm to the touch on a still day, even with frost an inch away in the shade. Dawn arrives on the ridge tops long before the lanes, a slow line of gold descending the walls over an hour.

Change here is slow. The seasons are two: the Long Frost and the Thin Thaw, with barely a shoulder between. The glaciers retreat a few paces in a generation. The mountains rise by a hand's width over a grandmother's life, and every district keeps a notched post in a doorway, marking the height of the ridge against a sight-line, so that they know how much higher the world has become.

Somewhere on the far side of the basin, smoke is rising. A cloud settles over it like a hat. Down in the lane, someone sets a fresh cake of dung into the hearth, a dog shifts against a wall, and the mist starts its slow climb up the valley from the invisible lowlands, as it has every afternoon, as it will.
