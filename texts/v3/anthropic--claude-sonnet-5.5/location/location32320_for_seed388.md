# The Sump at Harrow Weir

The town sits in a bowl barely deeper than a cathedral nave is tall, and it fills that bowl to the rim. From the lip of the bowl you can see all of it at once: about forty buildings, a few hundred paces across, jammed so close that the alleys are seams. From the lip you also hear it, and the sound arrives before the sight does.

## The Sound

It is never quiet. Under everything is the **pump-beat**, a slow, wet, four-part thud from the central engine house, felt in the sternum more than heard. On top of it sit the feed-pipes, which are cast iron and clay and have been tuned by decades of corrosion until each one sounds a different note when water shoves through it. The town hums a rough, unresolved chord. When the flow shifts, the chord slides. Nobody planned this music, and the ear takes a long time to stop trying to resolve it.

There are also the gutters, the weirs, the drip-plates, the belt-slap of the old line-shafts, and the hiss of the condensers. No bird, insect, frog, or rat adds anything. The town has no animals at all, and never had them. The silence between the sounds is not the silence of a forest at rest. It is the absence of any small, living, unmechanical noise. People who come here say it takes a day to notice, and then they can't stop noticing.

## Ground and Water

The floor of the bowl is not soil. It is a black, fibrous mat of old peat and silt, waterlogged to within a hand's depth of the surface. Step off the paved lanes and the ground gives, then weeps around your boot. The lanes are raised on brick causeways, and the brick has gone the color of liver. Every seam glistens.

Water is the town's business. The marsh wants to reclaim the bowl, so the town lifts water out of the ground, runs it down through a series of sluices, mills, dye-vats, and cooling races, and lets it out through a breach in the eastern rim. The marsh seeps back in. Both sides are patient, and the pumping has been going on longer than any record. The town is not fighting the water so much as breathing it.

Rock here is mostly the manufactured kind, such as concrete, slag-block, and fired brick. Long saturation has aged them into something almost geological. Walls have sagged into gentle, buttery curves. Lintels are hollowed by centuries of drip. Ledges hold small standing pools that have etched themselves into rounded basins, each with a rim of pale mineral crust. The stone weeps constantly, in threads and beads. Where it weeps over iron, it leaves long orange stains, and over copper, a green that is nearly luminous in the dimness.

## The Growth

The vegetation is lush to the point of upholstery. Nothing is bare. The dominant plants are old lineages that do well in warm wet shade:

- **Tree ferns** grow out of the courtyards and up through broken roofs, their fronds meeting overhead and roofing the alleys in green.
- **Horsetails** stand in dense ranks along the drainage cuts, jointed and squeaking faintly when the pump-beat shakes them.
- **Liverworts and moss** cover every north-facing and south-facing surface alike, thick as felt on the walls and forming cushions on the pipes.
- **Algal skins** in orange, green, and a dull bruise-purple line the races, and slide off in sheets if the flow is diverted.
- **Pale fungal shelves** grow from the rotting timber of the old conveyors, shedding spores into the fog like slow snow.

Because there are no animals, no leaf has ever been eaten and nothing has ever pollinated anything. The plants spread by spore, by rootstock, and by the water. Every frond is intact and unbitten. The place looks strangely finished, as if no one had ever needed to be afraid of it.

## The Light

Light is dim, and it is mostly not the sun. The tree-fern canopy and the steam hold the day at a permanent greenish dusk. Overhead, a few cracked skylights pass slabs of pale gold that travel across the floor over the course of a day. Most of the illumination comes from the town: rows of low-wattage filament lamps strung along the causeways, each in a brass cage furred with moss. They burn amber and stay dim, powered by a turbine that only ever produces a little. Fog sits around each lamp in a soft, visible globe, and the lanes become a chain of lit spheres with dark between.

## Industry

The buildings are mills and works, all connected. A **felting shed** presses the moss into sheets. A **peat-kiln** bakes the fibrous mat into brick. A **dye works** feeds its runoff, in slow ribbons of madder and indigo, into the outflow. Nobody sees this happen, because the workers move only in the dimmer hours, and they are few. The place is built for hundreds and holds perhaps thirty. It is warm, around the temperature of a breath, and the heat comes from the boilers and from the composting of the town itself.

The **central engine** is the heart, a triple-expansion beam engine housed under a vaulted brick hall. Its flywheel is taller than a house, and its bearings sit in troughs of water that steam faintly. The engine breathes. Its rhythm, timed to the hour, has become the town's clock.

## The Strangeness

Two things do not fit.

First, **the roots are part of the machinery.** Over generations, fern rootstock has grown into the joints of the drainage system, and it now regulates it. Where a pipe would clog, a root mass swells and diverts. Where a sluice would overrun, a bed of horsetail drinks the excess. The engineers long ago stopped pulling the roots out and began building to accommodate them. Governors and valves now carry brass fittings shaped to hold living stems. It is unclear whether the town is maintaining the plants or the plants are maintaining the town.

Second, **the fog moves against the wind.** The condensers along the rim exhale cool mist that doesn't rise or drift the way mist should. It sinks toward the engine house and collects there, thick and low. Sound behaves oddly inside it. A voice spoken in the fog comes back a half-second later, slightly lower in pitch, from the wrong direction. Old residents say the town "keeps" what is said in it. No one has been able to say what happens to it afterward.

## Time

Change here is slow. The bowl settles by about a finger's width per decade, and the causeways are re-laid by inches, one course at a time. The moss advances up a wall at a rate you could measure with a pencil mark and a decade of patience. The pump-beat drifts a fraction slower each year as the bearings wear, and every so often the whole chord of the pipes shifts to a new key. When that happens, the ferns tremble in unison and the fog draws in tighter around the lamps. The town takes a long time to adjust, and then it goes on.
