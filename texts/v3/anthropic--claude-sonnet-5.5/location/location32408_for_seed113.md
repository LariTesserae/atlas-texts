# The Drowned Concord

You arrive by a stair that was cut for someone else's stride. The steps are too shallow and too many, and they descend through rock still raw from its breaking. The walls of the stairwell are sharp-cornered slabs of grey limestone, unrounded, with fracture faces so fresh they glitter where the light finds them. The air thickens as you go down. It is warm and close, and it tastes of wet iron and cut stems.

Then the stair ends at a railless ledge, and the cavity opens below you.

## The Bowl

It is a single chamber, roughly a quarter-mile across, roofed by a ceiling of broken stone that hangs in ragged tiers. The whole space is a collapse, newly exposed. Some seam of the earth gave way, and a city that had been sealed underneath was suddenly under open sky, or as open as a sky gets when it is a thousand feet of rock with one crooked skylight of daylight far off to the east. That shaft lets in a pale, greenish, moving light. Most of the illumination is the city's own.

The floor is a wide shallow basin of black water, ankle-deep in some places and waist-deep in others, and it is never quite still. Through it, and standing above it, are the structures. There are not many. This is an urban place in the way a cathedral is urban: everything in it is a deliberate act, and the emptiness between acts is part of the design. A few dozen towers, spans and terraces are spread across the void, each a long walk from the next.

## The Architecture

The buildings are not stone or steel. They look like something spun and then persuaded. Each tower is a bundle of pale, translucent filaments the thickness of tree trunks, twisted together and glazed with a skin like wet porcelain. Inside them, slow bright fluids climb and descend, threads of amber and cold cyan. This is the light of the place. Where a tower's fluid passes, the surface glows faintly, and the glow drifts upward like breath.

Nobody can say what the fluid is. It behaves like a liquid and then, where it pools at joints, it stands in perfect spheres that hold their shape against gravity for a minute before dissolving into the tower again. There are hanging drops the size of houses beneath the bridges, each a clear lens in which the whole cavern is inverted and slightly wrong. Some show the cavern as it would be with the ceiling intact. Some show it flooded to the roof. None show it as it is now.

Between the towers run causeways of a smoother, darker material that seems to repair itself. Crack one and you can watch the edges seek each other over an hour, with a low creak like a bending branch. Yet the cavern's collapse has not been repaired. The city's healing is patient, slower than the ceiling's falling, and the two processes have settled into a truce along a line of boulders, where new-broken rock lies half-embraced by dark, growing sinew.

## Water

Water is the second architecture. It weeps from the ceiling in a thousand strands, from hair-fine threads to ropes, each catching the light of the towers. The ceiling is a slow rain that never stops. In the far reaches it becomes real downpour, thundering on the basin floor, and in the near reaches it falls as mist that hangs and beads on every surface.

Every eleven hours or so, the basin *tides*. A deep sound starts under the floor, and water pushes up through drains and vents the city built for exactly this. It rises knee-high across the causeways, sheeting silver, and then withdraws with a long sucking roar down the same vents. Some mechanism in the city meters it. The tide is not natural, and yet it has begun to feel natural: the plants have adjusted their lives to it.

## Growth

The vegetation is lush and wildly various, and it has had only a few years since the collapse. It grows thickest in the seams where fluid leaks from the towers. Ferns with fronds wide as doors grow in ranks along the causeways, unrolling and re-rolling with the tide. There are mats of moss that glow a soft blue where the luminous fluid has soaked them. There are pale, tall reeds in the shallows, and pendant vines that lower themselves from ceiling cracks toward the water, some of them a hundred feet long and still growing visibly, tips waving in the drafts like slow hands.

Some plants have learned to use the city. Trees the color of wet slate have sent roots into the filament towers, tapping the amber fluid, and their leaves have turned faintly gold and translucent. The towers do not seem to object. Perhaps they are built to be tapped. Perhaps the city was always meant to be a garden and something inside it is glad.

There are no animals. No insects hum in the fern. Nothing crawls on the moss or swims in the black water. The plants reproduce by spores and floating seed-boats carried on the tide, and the whole cavern has the eerie completeness of a stage set for creatures who never arrived, or who left.

## Sound

The place is loud. The layers:

- The ceiling-rain, a continuous hiss punctuated by heavy drops striking flat stone like knuckles on a table.
- The tide-roar, twice a day, a sound so large it is felt in the sternum.
- The **pipes**. Along the upper terraces are rows of tuned tubes, thin as reeds or wide as chimneys, and the moving air and fluid make them sound. They produce a shifting chord, never resolving, some tones so low they are pressure rather than pitch. Walking beneath them, your teeth ache with harmonics.
- The towers themselves, which *sing*, a thin high glassy tone that rises when their fluid climbs and falls when it descends, so the whole cavern seems to be slowly inhaling and exhaling in overlapping voices.
- Now and then, ceiling stone settling: a crack like a rifle shot, then falling grit, then a long echo that runs around the bowl and returns changed.

## The Rules

A few things you learn quickly.

The light follows the fluid, and the fluid follows the tide. In the hour before the water rises, the towers brighten and every shadow sharpens. In the hour after it recedes, they dim almost to darkness, and only the moss glows.

Do not stand under the hanging spheres when the tide begins. They burst downward all at once, cold and heavy.

The causeways lead you, if you let them. They branch, but they tilt slightly, and the tilt always favors the way toward the center of the bowl.

## The Center

At the center is the only structure not made of filaments. It is a smooth grey disc, perhaps sixty feet across, set flush with the basin floor. The water avoids it. Even at the tide's peak, a dry ring stays clear around it, as if something invisible holds the liquid back. It is silent, the only silent place in the cavern. Ferns grow up to the edge of the ring and stop.

When you stand on it, the noise of the whole city falls away to a distant murmur, and you can hear your own pulse. Above, through the ragged ceiling, the greenish shaft of daylight wavers. Somewhere inside the disc, very faintly, something is counting.
