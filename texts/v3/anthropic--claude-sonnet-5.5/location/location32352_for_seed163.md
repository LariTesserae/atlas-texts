# The Shelf of Loud Water

The plateau is young. Something lifted it recently, in the slow way of continents, and the land has not finished deciding what shape to be. You arrive across its upper surface at about three and a half thousand meters, in air that is cool and thin and almost too clear. The light comes down without haze. Every leaf edge is cut sharp, and the wet surfaces throw the sun back so hard you walk with your eyes half shut.

Almost nothing here is bare. The rock is new, pale grey-green granodiorite and dark slabs of something glassier, and it shows only where the land has broken. Everywhere else is plant. Moss lies over the ridges in cushions a meter and a half thick, so saturated that it behaves like a slow liquid. Step on it and it sinks to your shin and gives back a cold, sweet, tannic water that fills your footprint and then, over an hour, closes it. Cushion plants, sedges, and dwarf bamboo grow into this moss and hold it together. Tree ferns stand in scattered groves, their trunks furred with epiphytes, and those epiphytes carry their own soil, and that soil carries its own seedlings. In places you can find a living column four meters tall with no ground inside it, a stack of plants growing on the rotted bodies of earlier plants. The true ground is far beneath, and nobody has seen it.

## The Terraces

The plateau does not slope. It steps. The uplift left it in great shallow benches, each a few kilometers across, each held up by a scarp of raw rock that plunges to the next. Water covers each bench in a sheet of braided threads through the moss. It has not yet cut proper channels, so it wanders. Then it reaches the edge and falls.

The falls are everywhere. Some are ribbons of twenty meters, and some are hundreds of meters of white rope dropping into blue shadow. The rock behind them is unweathered. The joints are still square, and the faces still show the scars of the fracture that made them. Nothing has rounded them. The streams have had no time to make gorges, so every fall drops straight off a clean lip, and below each one the water has only begun to excavate a plunge basin. Every basin is ringed with fresh boulders, and every boulder is already wearing its first green skin.

## The Sound

The sound has weight. It is not a roar you hear so much as a pressure you stand inside. The many falls overlap into one continuous mass that fills the whole sky-bowl of the plateau: low thunder underneath, a hiss of spray like static across the middle, and high on top the ringing of water striking water, thousands of small bells. Under the moss there is a fourth layer, a gurgle and drum from the hidden channels beneath your boots. The plateau sounds like standing inside a machine.

You cannot speak. You can shout into someone's ear and lose most of it. The people who work here have made their peace with this. Their tools, signs, and habits are all built for silence in the middle of noise: painted paddles held up, colored flags on poles, a hand language of shoulder taps and finger counts. Cairns of white quartz mark the safe boardwalk routes. You feel the world more than you hear it. The boardwalks tremble faintly, and the water in a cup shivers into concentric rings.

## What Was Built

The structures are few and far apart, and each sits alone in the green.

On the third terrace stands a small hydroelectric station, its concrete a stained cream, its roof a low sheet of corrugated iron already furred with moss. Two steel penstocks come down from a intake weir in the stream above, each a meter and a half across and painted red once, now orange with rust and bearded with liverwort. Inside, the turbine hall carries the deepest vibration on the plateau, a hum you feel in your teeth beneath the sound of the falls. It supplies the weather station, a lamp-lit bunkhouse, and the winch line. Two or three people live there in rotation and are rarely seen.

The winch line is the other industry. A cable-way of galvanized steel runs from the fourth terrace out over the scarp. It carries buckets of peat, cut in cold gray bricks from a place where the moss is deepest, and lowers them to a narrow-gauge track far below. Its pulleys shriek, but you cannot hear them. You see the bucket swaying out into the mist, and the cable dipping and rising, and the drum turning by the road. A small diesel engine chatters in a tin shed beside the drum, and its exhaust rises straight up in still air, blue and thin.

Farther out there are traces of older things: a line of iron survey stakes, a stone hut with no roof where a tree fern has taken the doorway, a single rusted boiler lying in the moss like a dead animal, half swallowed. The plateau digests these things slowly. The moss climbs a centimeter a year.

## Water Everywhere, Rain Seldom

It is wet, but the wetness comes less from rain than from the air. Clouds form at the scarp edges where warm air rises up the cliffs, and drift across the benches in low white sheets. In the mornings the plateau is brilliantly clear, and by noon a moving fog has closed over it, glowing with light, dripping from every leaf tip. When it lifts the world is rinsed and shining. Rain falls in short hard bursts most afternoons and is gone in twenty minutes, and it hardly registers against the noise. You see it only as a shivering on the pools and a darkening of the moss.

The rock itself weeps. Along the scarps, seeps stain the faces with dark green lines of algae and hang them with fringes of ferns. Small pools sit in every hollow, each one perfectly clear and rust-brown at the bottom, each with its own tiny reflection of the sky.

## Life

Few animals live here, and most you'll never see. A small dark rodent tunnels through the moss and leaves ridges like mole-runs. A pipit or a ground-tyrant works the sedge tops, calling silently as far as you can tell. Frogs sit in the seeps, and they must signal by throat-flash or by tapping the water, since no voice would carry. They are a bright, polished orange. Once in a while a large bird, dark with a white collar, rides the updraft along the scarp and hangs there without moving its wings, and you watch it for a long time because nothing else up here moves that way. Insects are few and cold-slowed, and mostly you notice them as beads of light on the water.

## Time

Nothing changes quickly, and it all changes. The falls retreat upstream by a fraction of a millimeter a year. The plunge basins deepen. In ten thousand years the terraces will have been carved into a proper valley, and the moss forest will have to find new footing on softer ground. For now the plateau is between shapes. Cloud crosses it, and light crosses it, and the bright water goes on falling, loud beyond all speech, over the new stone into the green.
