# The Ossuary Plateau, or the Long Settling

## The ground

You arrive by climbing for days, and the top does not feel like a top. The mountains that once stood here were worn down over more time than the rock seems able to remember, until only the roots of them remained, a tableland five thousand meters above any sea. The land is nearly level. It rolls in long, low swells like a sheet shaken once and left to fall. Nothing is sharp. Old summits survive as domes you could walk up without using your hands, their shoulders rounded and their faces pitted like the skin of a very old fruit.

The ground is quartzite grit, pale as bone-ash, packed so hard it rings faintly underfoot. Over it lies a scatter of wind-rounded cobbles in rust, ochre, and a grey-green like tarnished copper. Where the cobbles thin, the bedrock shows in wide pavements, fractured into polygons a hand wide, each plate lifted slightly at the edges so the whole surface resembles a dried lake bed. There has been no lake here for longer than lakes have been a thing. Water in any usable sense is gone. What remains is a rime of frost that forms on the north face of every stone before dawn and sublimes by mid-morning without ever becoming wet. Your lips crack. Your tongue tastes of flint.

The air is cool, thin, and clear. The light is ordinary daylight, neither harsh nor dim, so the far distance stays legible for a very long way. The place is fully visible and fully open, and that turns out to be its trouble.

## The Multitude

Every part of the plateau is occupied.

You would expect emptiness. What you find is the densest fauna you could imagine, a population that has filled the land the way sediment fills a basin. The animals stand, crouch, coil, and lean on one another across the horizon. There are grazers with long jointed legs and no obvious mouths. There are low armored things like living paving stones. There are tall, thin, heron-like shapes with feathers of fused mica that catch the light, and things like pale hares with too many ears. Underneath and between them are smaller layers of life: beetle-analogues packed in drifts, lattice-bodied crawlers nested in the hollows of larger bodies, threadlike things strung between the legs of the tall animals like laundry lines.

There is no free ground. You walk along the gaps between bodies, and the gaps close behind you. Animals touch shoulder to flank, and some are stacked, each species resting on the one below in a strict order that seems to have been settled long ago. Stone-shelled things form the base, hare-things sit on their backs, and the herons stand over all. Nobody argues about the arrangement, because the arrangement is final.

They are alive. That is the disturbing part. Their sides move with slow breathing. Their eyes, when they have eyes, follow you, though not with hunger or fear. The attention is more like that of a room full of people who all know a fact about you that you do not.

They do not eat as animals do. Their chemistry is not built on water. Their tissues are a mineral-resin composite, amber-hard on the outside, and inside a slow oil-like fluid that carries salts, sulfur compounds, and dissolved silica. They live on the frost-rime, on the slow exchange between cold stone and thin air, on the tiny differences between the north and south sides of a rock. Their metabolism is so slow that a single breath takes about a minute. They are not starving and they are not thriving. They have reached the equilibrium at which a thing can continue without ever needing to end.

## The rule of running out

Here the place stops being merely strange. The world here is worn out. It has been at this longer than any other place, and it has reached the stage where it is running out of distinct things.

Look at any herd long enough and you will see repetition. Two of the mica herons share the same crooked left wing-joint. Then a dozen do. Then, as your eye adjusts, you realize that across a whole valley the same heron stands in hundreds of places, differing only in small ways, a scuffed feather here, a slightly different tilt of the head there. The hare-things are copies of copies. Some are so degraded that they are hare-shaped only in outline, their interiors reduced to a smooth, grey, eyeless mass. Further out, at the fringes of the herds, there are versions of animals that have lost detail entirely and are only silhouettes, flat like cutouts, standing on their own thin shadows.

Reality here has settled into its last form by copying itself. Nothing is being invented. Nothing new is coming, and what already exists is being repeated at lower and lower resolution, like a story retold until only its outline survives.

Some other things go wrong in ways that resist explanation:

- **Counting fails.** If you count the animals in a hollow, you get a number. Count again and you get the same number, which is uncomfortable, because you know you have skipped some and double-counted others. The total is fixed no matter how you count. The world does not permit disagreement about it.
- **Shadows disagree.** Every animal casts a shadow, but the shadows fall in more than one direction. Some point toward the sun as if pulled by it. Others fall away, correctly. A few fall straight down into the ground and seem to go deeper than the ground allows. None of these seems to bother the animals.
- **Distance is loose.** A herd on the horizon looks the size of a hand. Walk for an hour toward it and it looks the same size, though the ground under you has clearly changed. Then abruptly you are among them, and the tall herons are twice your height, and you cannot say when the scale corrected itself.
- **Size is negotiable.** In the deepest herds, a beetle-thing on a stone is identical, down to its markings, to a much larger animal a few meters away. It is the same creature at a different size. When you look from one to the other, something in your head slips, like a stair that isn't there.
- **The pattern of the herds is a map.** From a high dome you can see that the arrangement of the multitude is not random. The species are laid out in slow concentric bands, dense with the armored kind at the low points, then hare-things, then herons on the rises. It looks less like ecology than like a diagram of an ecology, something drawn to explain how a living world would work, and then made real.

## Sound

The plateau is nearly silent, and this is what makes it feel wrong. A multitude that size should roar. Instead there is a hush like the inside of a held breath.

What you can hear, if you stop walking, is small: the tick of frost releasing from stone in the morning, the dry rustle of mica feathers when a heron shifts, a faint far-off hiss like sand falling through a narrow place, which is the combined breathing of a hundred thousand slow lungs. Sometimes a grazer's leg-joint creaks, once, and every head in the near herd turns a few degrees toward the sound, then turns back. That is the loudest event in a day.

Your footsteps are the only regular noise, and they seem rude.

## Plants

Vegetation is sparse and mostly used up. Pale lichens grow in rings on the cobbles. There are cushions of gray-green moss-like matter in the shelter of larger stones, and some hard, wiry shrubs the height of your knee, with leaves reduced to gray scales. They grow only where the animals allow it. Most of them are already half-covered by the creatures nesting around them, and the plants seem to have come to terms with this. They are eaten, if at all, only by the fine threadlike things that hang between the legs of larger animals.

## The made things

Very few people come here, but some have. Their marks are small and scattered, and every one has been absorbed by the multitude.

- **Cairns:** low stacks of quartzite cobbles, waist-high, on the rounded dome-tops. Each has a thin flat stone laid across its crown. Animals nest around them in tight rings, all facing inward, as if the cairns were altars or just warm.
- **Dry-stone walls:** short, curved segments that once made a windbreak or a pen. Now they are half-buried in the flanks of sleeping armored grazers, so the wall and the animal have merged into a single low ridge.
- **A hut:** one only, on the southern rim of a broad swell. Its walls are fitted stone and its roof was laid with hides and bone rafters, long since fossilized into the same amber-hard material as the animals. Inside is a hearth ring with no ash, a sleeping platform, and a row of hand-made tools laid out neatly on a shelf: flint scrapers, bone awls, a sling of braided sinew, a drill with a chipped stone bit. Nothing is disturbed. Dust lies on everything in a perfectly even layer, and there are no footprints, though the floor is soft with fine grit. The hut is surrounded by exactly one animal of each kind, arranged in a ring at equal intervals, all looking at the door.
- **Carved marks:** on the flat pavements, shallow grooves in spirals and dotted lines, made with a hard stone. Some of them count something. If you follow the marks you find the tally reaches a number and then simply stops, mid-line, as if whoever was counting realized the count was already complete.

The builders' technology was primitive: chipped stone, sinew, bone, some worked native copper in a few small beads and pins. They were not conquered or driven off. From the marks it seems they came, made careful arrangements, and then stayed or left so completely that there is no way to tell which. Some of the hare-things wear their beads. Perhaps they have been given them. Perhaps they were always part of the pattern.

## Stability

Nothing changes here. That is the last rule and the hardest.

There is no weather to speak of. The wind is a steady low push from the west, at the same speed every day. The sun rises, moves, and sets, but the light at every hour feels identical to the same hour yesterday, and yesterday's to the day before. The frost forms and burns off on schedule. The animals breathe in unison and shift in unison and settle again. Nothing has been born in a very long time, and nothing has died. The multitude does not grow, because there is no room. It does not shrink, because whatever fades is replaced by a copy.

You might stay a week and find that you have not aged, that your boots are not worn, and that the frost on your sleeve was there when you arrived. Or you might find that your own footprints, laid down that morning, have already been filled by grit and are indistinguishable from the ground. You feel the place taking you in, not with malice but with the plain, cool indifference of a settled system that has room for one more and will make it fit.

If you stay long enough, you will see the pattern begin to accommodate you. A gap will open near you, just your size. The nearest heron will lean a little to make room. The hare-things will turn their heads. And you will notice, from a high dome in that unchanging light, that somewhere across the plateau, standing among the herons, there is already someone who looks a great deal like you, who has been standing there for a very long time, slightly out of focus, waiting to be counted.
