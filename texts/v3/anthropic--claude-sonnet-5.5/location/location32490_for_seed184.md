# The Pumpworks at Low Vell

The town lies at the bottom of a sinkhole, and the sinkhole is full. Nobody knows how long ago it flooded. Everything in it has already stopped thinking of that as an event.

It occupies a bowl about four hundred paces across. Its rim is a ragged ring of limestone, furred with growth, far enough overhead that the surface is only a paleness, a dim greenish-brass disc that brightens and fades over the day. Beneath it the water is the temperature of a bath left standing. It is thick with dissolved iron and tannin, tea-brown at the edges of sight and clearer green near the rooftops. Below the roofs it darkens toward amber and then to the color of strong coffee in the deep streets.

## The Fill

No space in the town is empty. Where buildings do not stand, plants do, and where plants do not stand, infrastructure runs. Tenement rows lean together across alleys so narrow that the ribbon-weed growing from both sides has braided into a ceiling. Chimneys, forty or fifty of them, stand as masts for a tall, tube-stemmed plant that has grown up their brick shafts and out of the flues. Each is crowned with a slow fringe of pale fronds. Iron catwalks connect the upper stories. Pipes as thick as a person's waist run along the fronts of buildings and disappear into windows. Every pipe has been colonized by pink and ochre sponge, by tunicates like spilled glass beads, and by a stiff red weed that grows only on warm metal.

Vegetation is lush without being wild, because it has been shaped by long coexistence. The vines are the most important plant. They are dark, cable-thick, and finely ridged, and they climb the stone in the exact courses where old electrical conduit once ran. Stripped from a wall, one would show a copper core the vine has been slowly digesting, or perhaps only sheltering. Leaves shaped like open hands grow from them, and they turn all together toward the surface light. In the deepest lanes, where no light reaches, the same vine grows leafless and pale, and it glows faintly at the nodes.

That glow is most of the town's illumination. Between it and the sunk disc above, the light is dim and amber-green, without hard shadows. Objects show as layered silhouettes, each a step darker than the one before, and depth is judged by how much color has drained from a thing.

## The Machines

The industry is still here, and it still works, though not at any pace the builders would recognize.

The town made something, probably cloth or paper or cordage, because the factories are full of looms and rollers and drying frames. They were all driven from a central engine house, a cathedral-scale hall with a vaulted roof of riveted iron ribs. It stands at the lowest point of the bowl. Inside, three great turbines the size of houses hang in their cradles. They are still turning, but the motive force is no longer steam or current. A vent in the sinkhole floor breathes warm mineral water, and the town has grown around that breath. The turbines turn when it pulses, roughly once every eleven hours, and each revolution takes minutes. Belts ease across pulleys. Shafts creak a long way off, and a rank of looms in the next building answers with a single stroke of the shuttle. This is the town's rhythm.

For a few minutes at each pulse, everything leans. The vent pushes a slow warm current up through the streets, the ribbon-weed streams flat in one direction, the fronds on the chimneys bow, and silt lifts from the cobbles in soft brown veils. Then the current subsides and the weed drifts upright again.

The machines have been repaired by no one, or by no one anyone has seen. A gear cracked in the engine hall has been replaced by a lattice of calcified vine, hard as bone and nearly the right shape. A pulley-wheel in a rope-walk is a ring of living coral that has grown into the round of the old one and been worn smooth by the belt. Whether this is coincidence, slow accretion, or intent, the town does not say. The strangeness is that the machines seem to fit better now than they did when they were new.

## Sound

The town is quiet. Water eats sound and carries only some of it. What survives is small and close: the tick of shrimp along an iron rail, the soft chuff of a valve releasing a stream of bubbles, the creak of the turbines. Now and then a long, low note rolls through the whole bowl, when a pulse passes and every pipe in town rings like the mouth of a bottle. It lasts perhaps ten seconds. It is not loud, but you feel it in your teeth and sternum. Between pulses the quiet has texture, a fine granular hiss like sleet on distant glass.

## Fauna

Animals are rare and almost always alone. Pale, long-bodied fish, slow and translucent as candle wax, thread the streets at rooftop level as though following routes. Snails the size of fists graze the window glass, and the glass beneath them is faintly cleaner in curved trails. A few transparent shrimp live in the vine canopies and are heard more than seen.

Something larger sleeps in the flue of the tallest chimney. Its tail hangs out of the top like a length of gray rope, and sometimes it slowly retracts. It comes out at the vent pulse, drifts through the town at the height of the second-story windows, and touches things with its blunt, whiskered face. It touches the vines, the brass door handles, and the gauge faces, one after another. Then it goes back to the chimney. What it is checking, if anything, is unknown.

## The Weathering

Erosion here is settled and old. Brick has softened and rounded at the corners into something like soap. Iron has bloomed rust and then been overgrown by a crust of pink lime, so that every surface is a hard, warm, slightly pitted skin. Cobbles are cupped from centuries of current. Stairs are worn to shallow slopes. Nothing is collapsing. The town has reached the shape it will keep, in equilibrium between what grows and what dissolves, and the decay continues at a pace too slow to register.

## What the Place Knows

There is one more oddity, and it is what makes Low Vell strange rather than merely drowned. The lamps still light. Hundreds of glass lamps hang along the streets and in the windows, their filaments long gone. Inside each, a small, dense gathering of luminous growth clings to the old electrodes. It pulses very slightly in time with the vent, brightening with the current and fading as it ebbs, so the whole town breathes in amber. When the great note rolls through the pipes, every lamp flares at once and then settles, and the fish in the streets stop and hang motionless until the water is still again.

Nothing in the town is dead, exactly, and nothing is quite alive in the way its makers meant. It is a settlement that has been taken up by the water, the plants, the warmth, and the slow engine of the vent, and it keeps going by its own patient logic. Whoever stops here and drifts along its lanes is moving through a machine that has become a garden, or a garden that has learned the shape of a machine.
