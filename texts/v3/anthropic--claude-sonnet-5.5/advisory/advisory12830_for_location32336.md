# Preparing for the Lathe Plain

## The short version

The Lathe Plain is not hostile in a dramatic way. It has no predators worth naming, no poison, and no weather that hits hard. Its danger is that everything about it is quietly incompatible with unprotected human life, and nothing on the plain will warn you. The air won't feel dangerous. The cold won't feel dangerous. Your judgment will go first. Treat this as a high-altitude vacuum-adjacent expedition that happens to have wind.

## 1. Breathing comes first

Nine thousand meters, a near-black zenith, and sound that dies a meter from the mouth all suggest air that is thinner than the altitude alone would justify. Assume the worst and plan to be wrong on the safe side.

- **Do not arrive unprotected.** Without supplemental oxygen you have minutes of useful consciousness, and you won't notice the moment you lose it. Euphoria and confusion come before collapse.
- **Wear a sealed system, not a loose mask.** A full pressure suit or a sealed hardshell with a positive-pressure oxygen rebreather is the right answer. A mountaineering mask alone is marginal at best. Pure oxygen at low ambient pressure is workable, but you need a real regulator and a backup.
- **Carry three times the oxygen you expect to use.** Walking on shifting gravel in thin air burns supply fast, and the plain gives you no landmarks to judge distance by.
- **Skip acclimatization plans.** You cannot acclimatize to this in a week, and the plain doesn't reward those who try. Rely on equipment.
- **Have a redundant, independent emergency supply** you can reach with either hand without help, since a suit cannot be easily reached into.

## 2. Cold, light, and radiation

The cold is steady rather than savage: cellar cold, not arctic. That makes it easy to underestimate. The sun gives almost no warmth, and the deepest hour before dawn is when the frost forms, so temperatures fall well below what the daylight suggests.

- **Insulate for the night minimum, not the noon temperature.** Layered insulation under a windproof, abrasion-resistant shell. Use heated liners or chemical warmers for hands, feet, and the face-seal area.
- **Protect against ultraviolet.** With little atmosphere overhead, the small white sun is harsher than it looks. Use dark, rated eye protection that seals against grit, and cover all skin. Sunburn and snow-blindness-type damage can happen in an hour.
- **Ignore the dark sky's mood.** The saturated blue-black is beautiful and disorienting. Don't look at the sun directly, even when it seems small.

## 3. Wind, grit, and storm fronts

The wind is fast but weak. It can push you a few careful steps, but it will not knock you down like a gale at sea level. Still, it carries abrasive grit, and it never stops sorting the ground.

- **Choose abrasion-rated materials.** Ordinary fabrics will be scoured. Ripstop is not enough. Visors should be replaceable or sacrificial, with a spare and a lens cover.
- **Seal every joint.** Fine grains work into zippers, hinges, and glove cuffs, and there they grind like abrasive paste. Use gaiters, taped seams, and sealed connectors.
- **When a grit wall approaches, turn your back, crouch low, and let it pass.** Don't try to outrun it. Stay in place, protect your visor and air intake, and expect the world to be rearranged around you. Behind the front the pavement will be reassembled.
- **Cover your breathing system's intake and exhaust** with sacrificial filters or shrouds.

## 4. Footing

The pavement is thumbnail-sized stones, wind-polished and sorted, and whole provinces slide downwind slowly. It behaves like walking on ball bearings that happen to be in motion.

- **Wear wide-soled boots with aggressive, flexible tread,** or use the same principle the local animals use: broad, cupped footing that spreads load. Snowshoe-style platforms may help on the loosest bands.
- **Use two trekking poles with wide baskets.**
- **Move slowly and shift weight deliberately.** Test each step.
- **Do not walk in the dark.** You cannot read the ground.
- **Do not assume the ground you started on is where you'll end up.** It moves under you at a slow steady rate.

## 5. Navigation and the vanishing trail

The horizon is a ruled line, the terrain is featureless, and the plain re-sorts itself continuously, so it erases footprints, markers, and cairns. That helps the plain and hurts you.

- **Don't rely on landmarks.** The cairns will be restored precisely, which makes them useless as markers of your own passage.
- **Don't rely on drop-in markers or flags.** They will be buried, shifted, or scattered. If you use beacons, use active, powered ones, and expect them to drift.
- **Use inertial dead reckoning and a multiverse-tolerant beacon,** and carry a passive backup such as a compass and a paper log of headings and times. Unknown physics may affect magnetic devices, so verify against the sun.
- **The filaments are useful but unreliable as guides.** They are spaced a few hundred meters apart in ranks and visible only in the right light. At the wrong angle they vanish, and you can walk into one unawares. They stand in perfect rings of combed stone, so if you see concentric patterns, you're close to one.
- **Do not touch a filament.** Nobody knows how they are powered or what they do. Keep several meters away, and treat the concentric rings as a boundary. Whatever tunes the wind may not tolerate interference, and you should not be the test.
- **Arrive with a fixed extraction plan.** Your portal or return anchor may not be where you left it if it sits on sliding ground. Anchor it to something that does not move (a filament base is not an acceptable anchor). If your return device can be pinned into bedrock, do so, and check its position constantly.

## 6. Communication and silence

Sound doesn't carry. A shout dies close to the mouth. Voice communication across even a few meters will fail without hardware.

- **Use short-range radio between all party members,** with a backup channel.
- **Agree on hand signals** and a system of visible lights for low visibility.
- **Stay within sight and tether in storms.** A rope between people works better than voice.
- **Prepare for isolation.** Much of your experience will be visual and tactile. You will hear your own breathing and equipment more than the world.

## 7. The tone

When the filaments come into tune, a low pressure with no pitch arrives, felt in the sternum and teeth. The stones shiver. It lasts a few seconds.

- **It is not known to be harmful,** but it is unnerving, and it can trigger nausea, panic, or a startle reflex.
- **When you feel it building, stop moving, crouch or sit, and breathe slowly.** Do not run and do not fight it. Let it pass.
- **Secure loose gear.** Vibrating stones can shift cached items.
- **Brief every member beforehand.** The worst reaction is the one nobody expected.

## 8. The built things

- **The glass house** is the only real shelter, and its shape is a product of ten thousand years of wind. Use it if you must, but treat it as a found place, not a base. Enter and exit by the door facing away from the prevailing wind. Don't sweep or disturb the pale sand floor. The air currents that pattern it are part of the site's function. Bring your own heat and oxygen. A shelter that keeps out wind is not a pressurized shelter.
- **The inverted tower** is worth seeing when the sun stands at the right angle, when the grooves throw their slow ripple across the ground. Don't climb the base, don't probe the grooves, and don't attempt to measure its depth.
- **The cairns** are the local courtesy. You may build one, but expect the plain to take it down and put it back. Don't be offended when it does. It's the plain's way of acknowledging you.

## 9. Life

- **Leave the lichen crust alone.** It lives on frost and slow stone-dissolving, in the lee of rocks, and it is easily crushed. Don't step on cushions or domes.
- **Give the long-legged animals distance.** They travel in single file along storm margins, and they are not known to be aggressive, but they are large, and you are a strange thing in their country. Don't approach, don't feed, and don't try to follow them into storm edges. If one stops and stands listening, stop too. Whatever it hears, you may not want to interrupt.
- **Don't collect specimens.** You are in a maintained landscape, and removing pieces may matter in ways you can't know.

## 10. Supplies and the body

- **Water:** Dehydration comes quickly in thin, dry air. Carry insulated water, or hydration inside the suit's thermal layer so it doesn't freeze. Plan for more than you'd expect.
- **Food:** Small, high-calorie, easy-to-eat items that can be consumed through a suit port. Cold will make you hungry and slow.
- **Medical:** Bring supplemental oxygen, a pulse oximeter, warming supplies, eye wash, and treatment for altitude sickness. Know the signs of hypoxia (blue lips, confusion, giddiness) and frostbite. Team members should watch each other, since you won't notice your own decline.
- **Electronics:** Batteries lose capacity in cold, so keep them warm against the body. Seal against fine dust. Bring spare power.

## 11. The mind

The plain is calm, silent, and enormous, and it will work on you.

- **Expect depth-perception problems.** The flatness and ruled horizon make distance hard to judge. Things are farther than they look.
- **Expect time distortion.** The unchanging light and silence make hours vanish.
- **Expect a sense of being attended to.** The plain is maintained, and it feels like it. Nothing here is watching you. The feeling is the plain's scale acting on you, and it isn't a threat. Still, it can wear on a crew. Keep to a routine and talk on the radio regularly.
- **Do not stay longer than planned.** The place is easy to be moved by and easy to lose an afternoon in.

## 12. Etiquette

The plain is finished and never done, and you are a small disturbance in it. Leave nothing you don't intend to be reassembled. Bring out all gear, waste, and broken pieces. Stones you move will be sorted again, but your equipment won't be. Walk lightly, stay unhurried, and let the process go on without you. It does not need an observer, but it will tolerate a respectful one.

## Checklist

- Sealed oxygen system with 3× reserve and redundant emergency supply
- Insulated, abrasion-resistant, sealed suit rated for night cold
- UV-rated sealed eye protection with spares
- Wide-soled footwear and poles
- Short-range radios, tethers, hand signals
- Active drift-tolerant beacons, compass, dead-reckoning log
- Anchored, checked return device
- Water and food accessible from inside the suit
- Warm batteries and dust seals
- Medical kit and pulse oximeters
- Briefing on the tone
- A plan not to touch, take, climb, or follow anything

Go slowly, and bring more air than you think you need.
