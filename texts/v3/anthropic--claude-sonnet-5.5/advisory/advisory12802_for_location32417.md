# Preparing to Visit the Hush Basin of Ilmere Sett

## The short version

You are going somewhere high, wet, blindingly bright, and silent, built on a floor that rises and falls like a sleeping animal. Most of your preparation comes down to five things: **protect your eyes, stay dry and warm, arrive acclimatized, learn to move quietly, and never trust the ground to stay put.** Loudness is the one sin that puts the whole town at risk, so it deserves the most rehearsal.

## Before you go: body and altitude

The rim is a very long climb above the sea-fog, and the town is inside a bowl that traps cold, damp air. Treat this as a high-altitude expedition, not a village stroll.

- **Acclimatize in stages** somewhere lower for several days. Arrive with a slow heart rate and a clear head. Do not rush the last ascent.
- Bring altitude medication if your home world's medicine has it, and know the signs of pulmonary and cerebral edema. There is no local remedy you can rely on, and descent is the only cure. Ask a guide on the lower slopes who can take you down if needed.
- **Get in good physical shape**, especially legs, balance, and core. The floor moves, the roofs are stairways, and you will be shuffling across plank bridges for days.
- Practice **balance on unstable surfaces**: foam pads, wobble boards, a rowboat, a waterbed. The Heave lifts or lowers the floor by a person's height, slowly, so it is less like an earthquake and more like standing on a raft, but it is constant.
- Carry hydration and electrolytes. Cold, dry-feeling air at altitude dehydrates you even though everything around you is wet.

## Light: the most immediate danger

The glare comes from every direction, including below. A hat brim does very little.

- Bring **glacier-grade eye protection with full side and bottom shielding**, and a spare pair. Or plan to buy or trade for the local carved-horn slit visors, which are probably better. Either way, protect your eyes *before* you arrive. Snow-blindness-style damage sets in hours after exposure and is agony.
- Soot under the eyes works, and locals use it, so it is socially normal. Bring a lampblack stick or greasy charcoal.
- **High-SPF sunscreen, applied under the chin, nostrils, ears, and eyelids.** Reflected light hits from below. Lip balm with UV protection, too.
- Walk with your hands slightly out, as the locals say newcomers do. Depth perception is poor when the shadows vanish and the ground shines. Use a light pole, but see the sound notes below.
- At night the cloud-lid glows, so it never gets truly dark. Bring a **sleep mask**.

## Cold and wet

Everything is soaked, constantly. It is cold and damp rather than freezing, which is the classic hypothermia setup.

- **Wool and synthetic base layers, no cotton.** Bring several changes and a way to dry them, which will mostly mean waiting.
- A waterproof shell, but choose it carefully (see Silence below). Rubberized or waxed fabrics are better than stiff, crinkly ones.
- **Waterproof, insulated boots with excellent grip** on wet peat, gravel, and glossy stone. Gaiters. Extra wool socks. Foot care matters, since wet feet in cold air lead to trench foot.
- Waterproof gloves that still let you grip rope. You will be asked to haul.
- A thin emergency bivvy or space blanket in an inner pocket, for falls into cold water.

## Silence: the rule that matters most

The hush is a physical fact in this place. Loud sound sets the cliffs weeping into spate and speeds up the Heave. A shout can put people in danger.

- **Strip your kit of noise.** No velcro, no crinkly nylon, no loose zippers, no clinking carabiners, no rattling trekking poles, no buckles that snap. Tape or wrap anything metal. Test your gear in a quiet room by shaking and walking. If you can hear it, so can the cliffs.
- **Turn off all electronics that beep, buzz, or chime.** Silence phone and watch alarms. Do not bring speakers, whistles, bells, or clapping devices.
- **Learn the finger-signs** ahead of time if any documentation exists, and learn to talk in a low, brief voice. Expect to say very little.
- Learn the basics of **knotted cord messages** too. You'll be handed cords and expected to read or tie them. Practice simple tying, and be humble about reading.
- **Plan a silent emergency system.** You cannot shout for help, and you must not blow a whistle. Agree with your companions on a rope-tug code, hand signals, and a bright cloth to wave. Learn what the locals do (a bone clapper is used for hauling; find out what it means before you touch one).
- Practice **falling, stumbling, and dropping things quietly**. It sounds silly, but dropping a water bottle on stone here is a real mistake. Use lanyards on your gear.
- **Do not wear earplugs.** The drip-ticking of the rocks and the tap of the clapper are your information. You need to hear the town.

## The moving ground

The floor is a raft of peat, gravel, and old ice a few body-lengths thick, floating on a lake. Seams open and close. Springs appear and vanish.

- **Never walk alone on the open floor.** Stay near houses and follow people who know the Heave.
- Ask your host each morning what the day will do: when the lift comes, where the seams will open, which streets are moving.
- Carry a **short throw rope, a pair of ice picks on a neck cord** (for pulling yourself out of water), and a floatation vest if you can fit one under your coat. Falling through a seam into near-freezing glacial water is the main way to die. Ice picks worn on cords are quiet.
- Do not step on the salamander mats, do not lean against the weeping runnels, and do not put your pack down in a low place where a puddle may slide.
- Sleep in a sledge-lodge with your hosts. You will be woken when the house needs to move. Get up fast, quietly, and help.
- Expect motion sickness during the first day or two. Ginger or anti-nausea medication helps.

## The strangeness: what not to do

Follow local custom on the odd phenomena without arguing about it:

- **Never stand under water that runs upward** on the rock. Step back, say nothing, and let it pass. The locals don't, so you shouldn't.
- **Don't touch the afterimage lights** on the wall, and don't disturb the birds roosting in them. They give no warmth and cast no shadows. You can watch, but do so from a distance.
- Watch the salamander mats. When they all turn together, the town will shift. Learn to read them, but also watch what people do, because the townsfolk will already be moving.
- Rainbows in doorways are ordinary. Don't exclaim over them. Children read them as clocks, so ask a child.

## Etiquette, gifts, and trade

This is a low-fuel, low-wood society with no smelting. Your needs will strain it.

- **Bring your own food** for the whole stay, plus a reserve, and pay hosts in extra. Fuel is rationed, so do not expect to cook on their fires. A tiny sealed stove and your own fuel is acceptable if your host agrees, but ask first, and never use a loud one.
- Best gifts: **timber** (a small, well-worked piece of hardwood is precious, since wood must be hauled up), rendered fat, salt, honey, dried fruit, cordage, good needles. Avoid flashy metal. Copper rings mark rank, so don't wear copper jewelry or give it casually.
- Avoid introducing steel or advanced tools as gifts, and don't display gadgets. Keep your stay light-handed.
- **Food and water**: expect boiled barley, sour milk, and steam-pit cooking. Take probiotics and stomach remedies. Boil or filter all water, including from springs. Don't assume the salamander pools are drinkable.
- **Animals get their share.** Every house has a ledge for the birds and a plate for the whistle-rats. Do what your host does. Keep your own food sealed in hard containers, or the pikas will find it. Wash your hands after contact with rodents and droppings.
- Offer to help. Ropes are pulled in unison by whole quarters, and being one more pair of hands is the best courtesy.

## Mental preparation

- The density is astonishing. Expect no privacy, shared walls, and roofs that are also streets. Bring patience and a willingness to be close to strangers.
- The hush is heavier than you think. Many travelers find continuous silence eerie at first, then calming. Sleep and routine help.
- You will feel clumsy and loud, because you will be. Move slowly, apologize with gestures, and be forgiven.
- Bring a small notebook (silent pencil, not a clicking pen) to record what you see and to communicate.

## Packing checklist (condensed)

- Glacier glasses (spares), soot stick, high-SPF sunscreen, lip balm, sleep mask
- Wool and synthetic layers, quiet waterproof shell, insulated waterproof boots, gaiters, rope-friendly gloves
- Ice picks on cord, throw rope, thin flotation vest, bivvy sack, headlamp with a dim red mode
- Altitude medication, rehydration salts, probiotics, anti-nausea tablets, first-aid kit with hypothermia supplies
- Water filter or purification tablets, sealed food reserve, hard food containers
- Gifts: wood, salt, fat, cord, dried fruit
- Notebook and pencil, wrapped or taped metal gear, lanyards on everything

## Leaving

Plan your descent in advance. Weather closes the rim, and the cloud-lid can hold for days. Tell your guide below when to expect you. Leave nothing behind but a good name, and take away all your waste. The mountain is a full vessel, and the town lives in its lip. A polite guest is one who leaves it exactly as full as they found it.
