# Preparing for the Clamor Flats

## The right expectation

The Flats will not feel like a dangerous place at first. It will feel like an assault on your senses. The dark, the roar, the wet, the crowding green and the sharp black rock all press on you at once. The real dangers are quieter: gas pooling in a slot, a new crack under moss, a tremor while you are on a tilted slab, a stampede in fog. Your preparation has two jobs. One is to protect you from the constant abrasion of the place. The other is to keep your judgment intact, because the sensory overload attacks that first.

Plan for a slow, humble visit. The basin is a day across "and much longer if you try to go straight," so budget three to four times whatever your map suggests. Don't trust a map anyway. Any path older than a month may end at a new vent.

## Before you go: the non-negotiables

**Get a guide, or don't go far.** The people who live there read the place all night without rest, and you can't learn that in a week. Make first contact at the edge of the basin, not by wandering in. Arrive with patience and with gifts that don't upset their world. Good choices are salt, needles, cordage, and resin-sealed containers. Steel blades are prized, but they would reshape their tool economy, so think carefully before handing them out. Expect to follow, not lead. Step where they step, stop when they stop, and never move ahead of your guide on a route you don't know.

**Learn the local language of signals before you arrive.** Speech is impossible, so you need a small set of hand signs, low and high whistles pitched outside the chorus, and taps on wood or stone. Rehearse a personal vocabulary with your party: *stop, danger below, gas, follow me, injured, turn back, I am here.* Practice until it is automatic. A jointly held rope with agreed tug codes is worth more than any radio.

**Set your exit before you enter.** Arrival points in a place this young are unreliable. A new crack could open under your anchor, or fungus could swallow your marker in a day. Find out how your return works, and have a backup. Mark the arrival point with something that doesn't rot. Metal stakes with reflective tape will corrode and sprout fungus, so use carved basalt cairns, or better, have your guide's tally-cord system record it. Plan to leave on schedule, because fatigue and sensory strain build up faster than you expect.

## Body protection

**The rock cuts.** Nothing has been rounded off. Bring:
- Tall, stiff-soled, well-gripped boots, with gaiters or snug pant cuffs to keep glass grit out.
- Leather-palmed gloves, replaced when they get damp and torn. Every handhold is a blade.
- Knee and elbow protection, since you will fall or crawl.
- A climbing helmet. Branches fall, epiphytes drop, and tilted slabs shift.
- Sealed eye protection. The ground is crushed glass and ash, and the air carries drip, insects, and grit.

**Everything is wet all the time.** Wear synthetics or wool, never cotton. Bring more socks than seems reasonable, at least a fresh pair for every few hours of walking, and keep a dry set sealed for sleeping. Trench foot and fungal infections are the real threat to a visitor. Treat your feet like equipment: dry them, powder them, inspect them every stop. A lightweight, breathable rain layer is useful. Expect to be damp inside it and stop worrying about that.

**Insects and biting things.** Treat clothing with permethrin before departure. Bring a head net and fine-mesh gloves. Gnat clouds hang in vent-light, moths the size of hands will bat into your face, and ants move in rope-thick columns. Never sit or lean on anything without looking. Snakes and lizards live on the trunks, so watch where your hands go when you climb. Generic antivenom is unlikely to work on this biology, so avoidance is your medicine.

**Unknown biology.** Assume the local microbes, spores, and parasites are unlike anything you have been vaccinated against. Bring a broad medical kit, including wound irrigation, antifungals, antibiotics, and rehydration salts. Accept that you may need to leave early for medical reasons. Wear a respirator (see below) around bat streams and guano. Don't eat, drink, or touch anything unfamiliar. Fallen fruit may look tempting, but it is gone in minutes for a reason.

## The air: gas and steam

This is the most likely thing to kill a careful visitor. Volcanic vents release carbon dioxide, hydrogen sulfide, and sulfur dioxide. CO₂ is heavier than air and pools in dry ferny slots, hollows, and low fissures, especially on still, foggy nights. It is odorless, and it will simply put you to sleep.

- Bring a **personal multi-gas monitor** (CO₂, H₂S, SO₂, oxygen) with a **vibration alarm** and a bright light, since an audible alarm is useless in the chorus. Clip it to your chest and keep spares.
- Wear a half-face respirator with acid-gas and particulate cartridges. This also handles ash, glass dust, and spores. It is a filter, not a supply of air, and it will not help in an oxygen-poor pocket.
- Never enter a fissure, slot, or low hollow, however inviting the fern-hung slot looks. If your guide won't go there, neither will you.
- Steam columns can scald at close range, and the vents that look calm can flare without notice. Give them distance and stay upwind.
- Hot pools the size of cooking pots may be mineral-heavy and much hotter than they look. They are not drinking water, and they are not baths.

## Light and dark

The dark is complete and the light is alive. Bring:
- A headlamp with a **red or dim amber mode**. Use it sparingly. Bright white light ruins your night adaptation, attracts insect clouds, and makes it harder to see the faint glow of fungi, blue bulbs, and vent-light, all of which carry information.
- A backup lamp and spare batteries, sealed in waterproof bags.
- Chemical light sticks in a few colors for marking a route or a hazard. They are the only markers that survive a night.

Let your eyes adapt. After twenty minutes you will begin to see the place: the pale steam columns, the green glow on logs, the rose underlighting of the canopy. A sudden vent flare will give you a few seconds of clear sight. Use them to fix landmarks, then keep moving.

## Sound: protect it, and learn to use it

You will be in a roar that reaches your sternum for the entire visit. Constant exposure damages hearing and wears down your judgment.

- Wear **high-fidelity musician's earplugs** or similar filtered plugs. They lower the loudness without erasing the pattern. The sudden hard silence before a tremor still comes through, and you need to catch it.
- Consider bone-conduction or vibration-based devices for communication and alerts.
- Learn to listen for gaps and changes, not the sound itself. This is what the locals do. A chorus that stops is a warning. A moving wave of a particular call may mean an animal is coming. In thick fog, sound lies: a voice ten paces away can seem to come from behind you, and a distant roar can seem beside you. Don't trust your ears for direction after midnight. Trust your rope, your guide, and your feet.
- Arrange sleep with earplugs and a cover for your head. Expect broken rest and plan your days around it.

## Navigation and movement

- **Move in a line, roped or in touch.** Fog and dark separate people in seconds. Use a light cord or tap-code.
- **Test every step.** Slabs are hollow underneath and tilt. The clink of a hollow plate carries far and means the surface may not hold. Step on solid ridges and crossings, and probe with a stick before you commit your weight.
- **Follow the tally-cords.** They are the map of safe paths and new fissures, worn smooth by handling. Do not remove, move, or retie them. If a cord ends at a fresh steaming crack, believe it.
- **Read the warnings.** Blue bulbs swelling means a tremor is coming. Paper-leaf vines curling in a wave across the floor means something heavy is coming. Frogs going silent means the ground is about to move. Learn these signs until you notice them without thinking.
- **A tremor drill.** The moment the chorus cuts, stop moving. Get low. Get away from tilted slabs, stacked rock, trunk-tops, and fissure edges. Protect your head. Afterward, wait for a single frog before you move, and check the ground and your gas monitor before going on.
- **Stampedes.** When panic runs through the forest, do not run with it and do not try to outrun it. Move sideways out of its path, put a buttressed trunk between you and the flow, and get above the ground if you can. It ends as fast as it begins.
- **Rain.** The twenty-minute downpour turns slopes to running black glass. Get off slopes before it starts, or stay put and wait.

## Camp and supplies

- **Sleep off the ground.** Use a hammock strung well above the litter, with an integrated insect net, as the locals do. Hang it from a large, healthy trunk, not a dead or lightning-scarred one. Check knots before you sleep and after any tremor.
- **Water.** There are no rivers. Collect drip from leaf tips, and from bromeliad cups if your guide says so, with a tarp and a funnel. Filter and chemically treat everything. Carry more than you think you need, since pure water is hard to find.
- **Food.** Everything is eaten quickly. Carry compact, sealed rations. Hang them, and never keep food in your hammock. Expect ants anyway.
- **Fire.** The locals keep hearths of stacked basalt under leaf thatch. Do not try to make your own fire in this damp. Share theirs, or carry a sealed stove and fuel, and use it only in the places your guide chooses.
- **Electronics.** Assume they will fail. Keep essentials in sealed cases with desiccant, and bring analog backups: compass, paper notes, a whistle, cord.
- **Gear rots.** Tools left on the ground grow fungus by morning. Check your kit each dawn. Metal rusts and leather molds, so dry things whenever the chance allows, and expect some equipment to be lost.

## Your mind

The first day will be the hardest. You will be tired, wet, and half-deaf, and every shadow will seem to move. Panic comes from overload, not danger. Have a routine for the moments it hits: stop, breathe, touch the rope, check the gas monitor, watch your guide. Eat and drink on schedule, even if you don't feel like it. Say little, since you can't anyway.

Some practice before you go helps. Spend nights in a loud tropical forest. Learn to tie knots by feel. Learn a few whistle patterns. Practice sleeping with earplugs and a head net. Train your feet for wet, uneven, abrasive ground.

## Manners

You are visiting a place that is still being made, among a few hundred people who have lived through its worst moods. They do not fight the noise, and neither should you. Take up little space. Leave the cords alone, don't waste their water, and don't bring your habits into their camps. If you're allowed to stay a night by a hearth, watch how they listen.

If you do it right, somewhere around the second night the wall of sound will begin to separate into voices, the dark into shapes, and the ground into a pattern of warnings you can read. That is when the Flats stops being an assault. It becomes a place that is telling you things, and you can hear it.
