# Preparing for the Held Terraces of Ossary Vale

## The short version

This is one of the gentler places you can visit. Nothing in it hunts you, starves you, or burns you. Its dangers are mostly physical (the climb, the wet, the thin air) and psychological (a place where time seems not to pass). Prepare for a long, cool, damp, quiet stay in a place that is very hard to leave in your head, even though it is easy to leave on foot.

## The climb

Four thousand steps up a fresh fault-scarp is a serious ascent, and the treads are cut from tuff and basalt with edges that have never been softened. Assume every step is crisp and that wet stone is slick.

- **Footwear:** Boots with a stiff sole and aggressive tread, already broken in. Bring a second pair of soft-soled shoes for the settlements. The locals wear them, and you will be walking on dished causeways and in doorways that clearly matter to people.
- **Trekking poles:** Yes. Your knees will thank you on the descent, which is the harder half.
- **Gloves:** Thin, grippy ones. The rock is sharp enough to cut a palm if you catch yourself on a column edge.
- **Fitness:** Train for sustained stair climbing with a pack. Do not arrive at the base of the stair unfit and hope the scenery carries you.
- **Pacing:** Go up in stages, with a rest at a fixed interval. The air at the top is cool and slightly thin. You are not at a dangerous altitude, but you will notice it if you rush. Drink before you feel thirsty.

Do not bring anything that needs to be carried in loose hands. You will want both free.

## Clothing and the wet

Expect constant cool damp, no sun, no wind worth mentioning, and no seasonal shift to dress for. The temperature is the same on day forty as on day one.

- **Layers of wool or synthetic**, not cotton. Cotton gets wet in the rain columns and stays wet.
- **A real waterproof shell** with a hood. The rain columns are pleasant to walk through once, but you'll want to be able to enter one deliberately, or avoid one, without being soaked for the rest of the day.
- **Spare socks, many pairs.** Foot rot is the most likely ordinary illness.
- **Drybags** for everything that must stay dry: electronics, notebooks, medications.
- **A wool hat and a light insulated layer** for evenings, though "evening" barely exists. The light doesn't change.

The locals wear undyed wool and oiled hide. If you can, dress in muted, natural tones, and avoid anything loud, logo-heavy, or synthetic-bright. You'll stand out anyway, but there's no reason to shout.

## Time, and how to keep hold of it

This is the real preparation. The light never changes, shadows never move, and there are no seasons. Around the third day, you will notice that your body has lost its clock.

- **Bring a mechanical or reliable digital watch and a paper calendar.** Check whether your watch still keeps time when you arrive. If it does, trust it more than your own sense of how long you've been there.
- **Keep a daily log.** Write the date, what you ate, when you slept, and one observation. Reading it back will tell you whether you're drifting.
- **Set a departure date before you climb,** tell someone outside the valley, and treat that date as fixed. Don't decide to "see how it feels." The valley will feel the same every day, and that is the trap.
- **Sleep on a schedule you impose.** Lights out and up at set times. Bring an eye mask and earplugs if you want, though the quiet is deep and the light is soft, so neither is essential.
- **Bring something that changes.** A plant cutting, a candle you burn down a little each night, a jar of bread dough. These give you something to measure against a place that refuses to age.

## Food and water

The valley evidently supports a few thousand people, so it can feed itself, but you shouldn't assume it will feed you.

- **Carry enough food for at least the first week,** and preferably the whole stay. Dense, compact, familiar foods: nuts, dried fruit, hard cheese, oats, jerky, and something with salt.
- **Purify all water,** even the rain columns. Bring a filter and a chemical backup. The columns look clean, but you're an outsider with an outsider's gut.
- **If you're offered food, accept graciously and eat a little.** Refusing hospitality can be a serious insult almost anywhere. Take modest portions, and be aware that your stomach may be slower to adjust than your manners.
- **Don't hunt, fish, trap, or forage.** This is especially important here. The animal counts are held constant, and the locals believe the valley returns what is taken. You do not want to test that belief with a deer, a salamander, or a hawk. You do not know what the "return" looks like, and you would not want to be part of it.

## What to bring, and what to leave

**Bring:**
- A good headlamp and spare batteries, for the windowless lower storeys and any interior spaces.
- A small first-aid kit, including blister care, wound closure, anti-inflammatories, electrolytes, and antibiotics if you can carry them legally.
- A notebook, pencils (which work when wet), and a waterproof pouch for them.
- Small gifts that fit the place: good salt, needles, thread, dried herbs, high-quality wool combs. Practical, quiet, non-perishable, and non-electronic.
- A sturdy pack cover and a light tarp.
- A whistle and a compass or paper map. GPS may be unreliable in a place like this, and the compounds are scattered across a day's walk of basin.

**Leave behind, or keep hidden:**
- Loud devices, speakers, and drones. The valley is quiet as a library. Break that and you will be noticed, and not kindly.
- Anything that leaves residue: plastic packaging, cigarette butts, batteries that leak. Carry everything out.
- Seeds, live plants, insects, or soil. You do not want to introduce a new species or disease to a place that keeps everything the same.
- Weapons. You won't need them, and carrying them says something you probably don't mean.

## Behavior and etiquette

The people are few, quiet, unhurried, and unbothered. They don't seem to be waiting for you or against you. The best conduct is to act like a guest at a very old and very quiet house.

- **Speak softly.** Voices carry.
- **Walk on the causeways.** Don't cut across the meadows or through the moss domes. The moss stops exactly at the rim of the rain columns, and that boundary matters.
- **Don't move, take, or "fix" stones.** They replace blocks with identical blocks from the same quarry face. If you pick one up, you may be interrupting a repair cycle that has been running longer than you can imagine.
- **Don't try to add to the city.** Building anything, even a cairn, is probably rude at best. The city is finished.
- **Ask before entering a compound, and before photographing anything.** Photographs may be unwelcome, especially of shrines and stone markers.
- **Learn a few words of the local speech before you go,** if a guide or an earlier traveler can teach you. The word for seasons, *the going-past of other places*, is worth understanding. It tells you what they think you are.

## The stranger risks

Most of this can't be verified in advance, so treat it as caution rather than fact.

- **You are a difference.** The valley's rule is that nothing gets worse and nothing gets different. Whatever you are, you are a change in the valley. It may tolerate you as it tolerates the "going-past of other places," or it may not. Watch for signs that things around you are subtly correcting themselves: your footprints filling, your cuts healing unusually fast, your possessions returning to their original places. If that starts, leave early.
- **Your own change might be resisted.** If you get ill or hurt, or start to feel oddly *stable*, unusually calm, or reluctant to go, that's a warning sign, not a blessing. Get down the stair.
- **Don't bring anyone who is grieving, fragile, or looking for permanence.** A place with no decay and no hurry can be a deeply seductive refuge. It's a bad place to hide.
- **Don't trust your own sense that a few extra days won't matter.** They won't feel like they matter. That's the problem.

## Leaving

The descent is harder on the knees and easier on the lungs. It also feels like leaving a held breath. Expect:

- **A few days of dislocation** when you get back to a place with weather and moving shadows. Sleep will feel strange and time will seem too fast.
- **A lingering image** of a shadow still falling exactly where you left it. That's normal. Write it down, tell someone, and go on with your life.
- **Wash and check your gear** for spores, seeds, and insects before you cross into any other world. You don't want to carry the valley's moss into a place that isn't ready for it.

If you go prepared, the valley will very likely give you nothing worse than wet socks, sore knees, and a slow, quiet unease that you'll have trouble explaining afterward.
