# Preparing for the Chorus Hollow

## The short version

You are visiting a hot, sealed, gas-venting bowl under a roof of unknown strength. It is full of plants that burst, resin that burns, and sound loud enough to feel in your bones. It is kept by something you will probably never see. None of that is a reason not to go. It is a reason to go slowly, in pairs at minimum, on a schedule set by the forty-minute breath, and with a strong preference for watching over touching.

## 1. Getting in: the salt flat and the seam

The surface is the first hazard, and it looks like nothing.

- **Glare and heat.** A white plain reflects the sun upward as well as down. Bring dark-lensed, side-shielded eye protection, a wide-brimmed hat or a veil, and sun-covering clothing that is loose and pale. Expect sunburn on the underside of your chin and nose.
- **Navigation.** The flat has no landmarks. Do not trust your sense of direction. Bring a beacon or return-anchor for your home reality, and mark your route with cairns or staked flags. Whatever your instruments do in other worlds, assume they may drift here. Bring a physical compass and a paper map you drew yourself.
- **The crooked seam is a sag, not a door.** You are looking for a subtle line where the crust has slumped. Do not walk on it. The roof is thin as horn in places, and from above you cannot tell thick from thin. Probe ahead with a pole, rope up in a harness on a long line anchored to something heavy (buried deadmen, or a vehicle that is not near the seam), and approach the edge on your belly to spread your weight.
- **Descent.** Plan for about forty meters of vertical travel. Take rope, a rated harness, a descender, and a second, independent way to ascend, such as ascenders or a winch with a capable partner on top. Gypsum and salt are soft and unreliable as anchors, so do not rely on the rock itself. Pad your ropes where they cross edges, because salt edges cut.
- **Leave someone up top.** Ideally two people stay at the surface, in shade, with water and a clear plan. The people below will be deafened at intervals, so agree on a signal system that does not depend on voice: rope tugs, light flashes, and a timed check-in tied to the breath cycle.

## 2. The air: your most serious problem

The vents exhale a dense, dry, faintly sweet gas that smells of struck flint and warm resin. Treat it as toxic, asphyxiating, and flammable until proven otherwise.

- **Do not trust your nose.** Many dangerous gases dull or erase your sense of smell. The sweetness and the flint note tell you the air is not normal. They do not tell you whether it is safe.
- **Bring a multi-gas monitor**, with oxygen, carbon monoxide, hydrogen sulfide, carbon dioxide, and combustible-gas sensors at minimum. Bring two, worn by two people, with alarms you can feel as vibration since you may not hear or see them. Test them at the surface, and re-check in the bowl.
- **Supplied or self-contained air is the sensible choice**, especially for a first visit. A filter mask is a gamble because you do not know what the gas is. If you must use filtration, use it only as a backup, with a combined organic-vapor, acid-gas, and P100 particulate cartridge, and accept that it does nothing against low oxygen or carbon monoxide.
- **The gas is dense and it pools.** Low spots, vent-cracks, and flue mouths under the streets are the worst places to linger. Do not crouch or sit in them, and never sleep down there.
- **The pulse matters.** During the roughly forty-minute exhale the gas pours up and concentrations rise near the vents. The inhale draws air in from fissures at the edges, cooler and full of grit. Log readings through at least one full cycle before you let anyone move away from the entry point.
- **Fire and ignition.** Hot resinous air, a hydrocarbon-smelling gas, and tarry sap that beads like pitch make an ignition risk. No open flame, no lighters, no stoves. Use intrinsically safe lights and radios if you can get them. Avoid synthetic fabrics that build static. Wear cotton or wool, or flame-resistant clothing. Ground yourself by touching bare stone before touching gear.

## 3. Heat

The Hollow is as hot as a closed kiln, and the floor radiates.

- The air is dry, so sweat evaporates instantly and you may not feel how much you are losing. Drink on a schedule, not when thirsty. Plan on at least a liter an hour, more if you are working. Carry electrolytes and salt.
- Wear a cooling vest or wet-neck wraps if you have them. Use only sealed, closed containers of water. Do not pour water freely (see section 8).
- Set a hard limit. For a first descent, stay one or two breath cycles (40 to 80 minutes) and then return to the surface to cool for at least as long. Watch each other for confusion, clumsiness, and cessation of sweating. Those are signs to leave immediately.
- Your boots need heat-resistant soles. The flagstones hold the heat, and the lantern-mats are slick.
- The inhale feels like mercy, but it is only a little cooler, and it carries sand. Do not let relief tempt you into staying longer than planned.

## 4. Sound

The Hollow is a huge instrument, and it plays itself.

- **Hearing protection is not optional.** At full exhale, the whole town sounds at once: horns, whistle-stacks, organ-ranks, and reed-tongues, all rising together. Wear rated earplugs under earmuffs when in or near the plaza. Lower-frequency sound passes through plugs, so expect to feel it anyway.
- **Infrasound.** The drum-canes produce a rolling sub-audible pulse you feel in your breastbone. That can cause nausea, unease, blurred vision, and a sense of dread in some people. Know that this is a physical effect, not necessarily a sign that something is wrong, but also do not push through it. Move to a quieter zone if you feel it.
- **Unpredictable noise.** The pods burst with a crack like a snapped board, at random. Startled climbers fall. Keep your feet planted, keep a hand on something solid, and warn your team before you move.
- **The quiet after the exhale.** During the inhale you can suddenly hear the selenite straws humming. If you want to listen to the place at all, this is the time. Take your earmuffs off only then, briefly, and put them back well before the exhale starts. Learn the cycle's early signs, such as fronds beginning to unroll and the first low notes from the stacks.
- **Communication.** Voice will not carry over the chorus. Use hand signals, light signals, and tugs on a shared line.

## 5. The plants

They are beautiful and they are not friendly, exactly.

- **Flue-ferns.** The fronds are door-sized and stiff during the exhale, then ratchet shut in the inhale with a dry rattle. Do not stand among them at the transition. A closing frond could pinch a hand or sleeve. Keep your distance from the vent-stands.
- **Drum-canes.** They are hard and hollow and about twice your height. Do not climb them or lean on them. They are also part of the town's living machinery. Don't cut them.
- **Lantern-mats.** Slick, black-green, and shifting to bronze in the light coins. Assume they are slippery like wet algae. Step around them whenever you can, and never run.
- **Pods and spores.** Bursting pods throw seeds and fine dark spores. Wear a helmet, a face shield or goggles, and a P100-rated particulate filter or supplied air. Spores can be respiratory irritants or worse. Assume they are unknown allergens. Cover exposed skin.
- **Sap.** The black tarry sap is oily, sticky, and flammable. Wear nitrile gloves under heavy leather or heat-resistant gloves. If you get sap on skin, do not scrub it hard. Wipe it with oil-based cleanser, then wash it away outside the Hollow. Do not touch broken stems bare-handed.
- **Decontamination.** When you leave, brush down and bag your outer layer. Do not carry seeds, spores, or sap into other worlds. That is basic multiverse manners, and it may also be a safety issue for your home reality.

## 6. The roof and the structures

- **Falling material.** The stalactites and straws are dry and fragile, and the roof is thin in places. Wear a helmet at all times, chin-strapped. Do not touch the hanging selenite.
- **Roof thinning in the east.** A coin of light there has grown over the years, and the crust is nearly transparent. Do not camp or work beneath it, and keep out of the eastern sector when the sun is high. When it breaks, expect debris, an inrush of sunlight, and a change in the gas balance and the breath. If you are inside when it does, go to the nearest wall or doorway, cover your head, and wait for the debris to settle before moving.
- **Walls and vaults.** The masonry is dry-stacked and worn. It has held for a very long time, but do not test it. Avoid climbing on terraces. Do not go under corbelled vaults if they show new cracks. The stair-treads are dished from wear and will hold water if any ever came, but they are also slippery with sand and salt.
- **Copper.** Green copper sheeting is sharp at the edges and may flake. Gloves.
- **Flues under the streets.** They are open ducts into the vents. Do not put your hands or feet into them, and do not drop gear into them.

## 7. Behavior: what to do and not do

The clearest sign in the Hollow is that it is *kept*. The flues are clear, the reed-tongues are unbroken, and the newest organ-rank is lashed with green cane. Something tends this place, and the worn path suggests it comes and goes.

- **Stay off the path.** The bright, smooth line runs from the western stair through the plaza to the low door in the east. Do not walk on it, camp on it, or set gear on it. Cross it quickly and only when needed, and never leave anything there.
- **Do not play the instruments.** Do not blow into horns, strike the reed-tongues, or clear a flue that seems blocked. You do not know what the tuning means to whoever maintains it, and a change in the airflow could change the whole chorus.
- **Do not take anything.** No cane, no bricks, no seeds, no copper. Leave the place as you found it. Your footprints are the only marks you should make, and the wind will erase those.
- **No fire, no noise-making of your own.** Don't add your voice or your machines to the chorus more than you must.
- **Watch, don't hunt.** Nobody has seen the keeper. If you see something, or see signs of something recently, stay still, keep your hands visible, and be prepared to leave. Do not chase, corner, or photograph it with a flash.
- **Be aware of the feeling of being expected.** Places like this can make you feel like an audience or an intruder. It is a good rule to act like a guest at a service in someone else's temple.

## 8. Water, and why to be careful with it

The Hollow has run on dry heat and gas for a hundred thousand years. Nothing here is wet, and its plants make oil instead of sap. Bringing water in is unavoidable, but:

- Keep your water in sealed bottles or a bladder with a bite valve. Do not spill it, dump it, or wash in it.
- Do not water the plants, or pour water into the flues or on the stone. Moisture could damage the salt-rock and clay, and you do not know how the flora or the instruments will respond.
- Carry your empties out.

## 9. The low door

You will be tempted. Do not go in on a first visit.

The low door in the eastern wall stands open, and the air from it is darker, hotter, and louder, with the smell of resin and flint. It is at the end of the worn path. That combination suggests it is the source of the breath, the keeper's home, or both. It may also have much worse air than the bowl, with more heat and gas and less escape.

If you want to go in eventually:
1. Spend several full visits mapping the cycle from the outside.
2. Take gas readings at the threshold, from a distance, on a line.
3. Go only with supplied air, a tether, and a team at the threshold.
4. Do not enter during the exhale, and be out before the next one.
5. Keep in mind that whoever keeps the Hollow's breath may not welcome interruption. Consider not going in at all.

## 10. Emergency planning

- **If the breath stops**, the Hollow goes silent. That suggests something is badly wrong with the vents or the keeper. Leave immediately. The gas balance may change unpredictably, and the plants will begin to die.
- **If your gas alarm sounds**, go up. Do not stop to collect gear or take measurements.
- **If the roof breaks**, shelter in a doorway, cover your head and airway, and move only once the falling has stopped. Expect blinding light and a change in the sound.
- **If someone collapses from heat or gas**, clip them to the line and haul them up. Get them into shade at the surface, cool them, and assume they may need gas-exposure treatment as well as heat treatment. Have oxygen on the surface team's kit.
- **Have a rendezvous and a hard turnaround time**, set in terms of breath cycles ("out by the third inhale"), not clock time, since clocks may drift.

## 11. Kit checklist

**Descent and surface:** rope (static, two lines), harness, descender, ascenders, anchors and deadmen, probing pole, helmet with chin strap, gloves, sun hat, veil, dark side-shield goggles, cairn and flagging materials, shade tarp, extra water for the surface team, return-anchor or beacon.

**Bowl:** supplied air or best-available respirator (P100 plus OV/AG as backup), two multi-gas monitors, earplugs plus muffs, face shield or goggles, heat-resistant gloves over nitrile, long-sleeved cotton or flame-resistant clothing, heat-resistant boots with thick soles, cooling vest, sealed water (1+ L per hour), electrolytes, first aid kit, oxygen, intrinsically safe lights, signal lights, rope line, paper notebook, pencil, sample bags for decontamination, no lighters or matches.

**Mindset:** patience, quiet, and a willingness to turn back.

## 12. A last note on what it will be like

You will come down through the dull amber light into a warm brown dusk, with coins of gold sliding slowly across the floor. The first exhale will hit before you are ready. The fronds will unroll, the air will shimmer, and every instrument in the town will begin to speak. You will feel it in your teeth. Then the inhale will come, the fronds will click shut, and a strange near-silence will follow, broken only by the patter of seeds and the hum of the straws.

You may feel like an intruder, or like the first audience in a hundred thousand years. Both are probably true. The roof is thinning, and this chorus will not last in its present form. Come prepared, come respectfully, listen carefully, and go up before the next breath.
