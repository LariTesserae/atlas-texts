# Preparing to Visit the Foundry Under the Ridge

## The Short Version

The Foundry is not aggressively dangerous. It has no predators, no traps, and no hostile intelligence. It is dangerous the way a desert and a working machine shop are dangerous, and its rules of time, distance, and cause are loose. Prepare for the heat, the gas, the sharp rock, and the moving parts, and then prepare separately for the fact that you cannot trust your own sense of sequence while you're inside. Keep your visit short, keep records, keep a line to the outside, and touch as little as you can.

---

## 1. Understand What You're Walking Into

- **It is a place still being decided.** The rock is young, the chamber's geometry is provisional, and the walls carry annotations that seem to be instructions for the room itself. You are a guest in a workshop, and nothing in it is a leftover or a ruin. It is all in process.
- **It is not for you.** The output is unclaimed and reabsorbed, and there is no audience and no customer. This is not an insult, but it means nothing in the chamber will help, warn, or notice you. Do not expect to be welcomed or threatened. Expect to be irrelevant, which is its own kind of disorienting.
- **It gets looser the longer you stay.** Plan the visit as a series of short, disciplined entries, not one long exploration. Set a hard limit in advance and stick to it even when everything feels fine. Especially then.

---

## 2. Before You Go: Planning

**Go in a pair or a team of three at most.** You want at least one person outside the crack at all times if possible. A crowd makes the chamber's distance-stretching and timing effects harder to track.

**Set your limits in writing beforehand:**
- Maximum time inside per entry (I'd suggest under an hour by your own clock, whatever the chamber says).
- Water turnaround rule: leave when you have used a third of what you carried in.
- A recall signal, a specific thing that means "leave now, no discussion."

**Acclimatize to altitude.** Ten thousand feet is enough to cause headache, poor judgment, and fatigue, and you will be doing heat labor on top of it. Spend at least a day or two near that elevation before the approach. Poor judgment is the last thing you want to add to a place that already erodes your sense of order.

**Learn to recognize heat illness in yourself and others.** The chamber's heat behaves strangely: it is intense in the air but doesn't register through touch on the stone, so you will not feel the usual cue of hot surfaces warning you. Rely on your body's signs (flushed skin, cessation of sweating, headache, confusion, cramps) and on a partner checking you at fixed intervals.

---

## 3. Gear

### Body and Heat
- **Long-sleeved, loose, light-colored, breathable clothing.** You want to shield skin from sun, sharp rock, and hot mineral salts, while letting sweat evaporate. The air is extremely dry, so evaporation works in your favor if you let it.
- **Wide-brimmed hat or a light head covering.** A hard hat or a cap with a neck flap is even better for the crack, where the walls are close.
- **Cooling neck wrap, cooling towels.** Bring plenty, and expect them to dry out fast.
- **Skip the heavy cooling vest with phase-change packs** unless you can carry many and swap them. They will be exhausted quickly in the chamber.
- **Lip balm, saline eye drops, nasal saline spray.** The dryness will crack lips and dry mucous membranes within an hour or so. This is minor but miserable and distracting.
- **Sunscreen and UV-rated sunglasses.** The ridge is at high altitude with almost no cloud, and the quartz seam delivers hard direct sunlight through the ceiling.

### Feet and Hands
- **Sturdy ankle-supporting boots with thick soles.** The scree is unweathered basalt with every edge intact. It will cut through soft shoes, and a fall onto it hurts.
- **Gaiters** for the ash plain and the scree.
- **Cut-resistant gloves that also insulate somewhat.** The rock is sharp, the ingots and frond-sheets have perfect edges, and some surfaces will be hot regardless of what the air feels like.
- **Knee protection** if you expect to crawl through the narrow crack.

### Breathing
- **A half-face or full-face respirator with combination cartridges for acid gases and ammonia/organic vapors, plus particulate filters.** The plants feed on hot mineral vapor, ammonia, and sulfur salts. Even if the air feels breathable, sulfur compounds and ammonia can damage your lungs and eyes and you may not notice until later.
- **A personal multi-gas monitor** (H₂S, SO₂, NH₃, oxygen at minimum). Bring a backup and cross-check them. The chamber's laxity about cause and order may make instruments lie, so treat a clean reading as "probably fine, still wear the mask."
- **Goggles that seal.** Ammonia and sulfur salts in dry hot air are hard on eyes.
- **Dust masks or a buff for the ash plain**, where wind will lift fine grit. Ash is abrasive and gets into everything.

### Water and Food
- **A lot of water, more than you think.** Plan for several liters per person for the approach and the visit, and a reserve. Use sturdy sealed bottles or a bladder with a bite valve.
- **Electrolyte mix and salty snacks.**
- **Keep all water sealed while inside the chamber.** The plants live on vapor and thin brines, and there is almost no free water. I can't tell you what a spill, a splash of sweat, or a sip of your drink will do to something so carefully balanced. Don't find out. Drink outside or in sealed, controlled sips, and don't pour anything on the floor.
- **Food is optional.** There are no animals and nothing edible. Eat before you go in, not inside.

### Navigation and Records
- **A long, strong line or cord,** anchored outside at a solid point. Distance stretches inside, and the crack turns. A tether is your physical assurance of the way out. Use a line with extra slack, and don't count on its length matching your paces.
- **A paper logbook and pencil or a pen you can rely on, plus a watch and a second, independent timekeeper.** Write entries in strict order as you go, with the time you believe it to be. Sequence will feel unreliable in there, and the only sequence you'll trust afterward is the one on the page.
- **Do not depend on a sundial-like reading of the light bar or shadows.** Shadows lag and sometimes lead.
- **Headlamps and a backup light,** though the chamber is lit, the crack may be dim and your lamp helps in the turn.

### Safety
- **First aid kit** with burn treatment, heavy bleeding control, eyewash, and rehydration salts.
- **Two-way radios or line signals.** Rock and distance distortions may make radios unreliable, so agree on pull signals for the tether.
- **A means of leaving the world you're in.** Whatever your normal multiverse egress is, confirm it works before entering and that it functions from the ridge, not only from inside. Do not rely on a departure method that requires you to be inside the chamber at a specific time.

---

## 4. The Approach

- **Travel across the ash plain early or late in the day** if you can, when it is cooler and the light is easier on the eyes. If you must cross midday, shade and water breaks matter.
- **Expect no shelter, no water, and no aid.** The plain is dead flat and the range is unweathered. Nothing has rounded off the scree, so every slope is unstable and every handhold can shear.
- **Go slowly on the scree.** It lies at its original angle and has never settled. Test each footing. A small slide here means cuts, sprains, or worse.
- **Mind the geology in general.** A young range like this may have gas vents, thermal fissures, or seismic activity. If you feel tremors, smell rotten eggs strongly, or see fresh cracking, retreat.
- **Finding the crack:** it looks like a shadow. Look for warmth radiating from the rock, like a bread oven. Mark the entrance with a cairn or a flag outside on the scree, not on the walls.

---

## 5. Entering the Crack

- **It is shoulder-wide and warm.** Take off or secure bulky packs so you can pass, and go one person at a time.
- **Claustrophobia and heat build up here.** If you feel panic rising, stop, breathe slowly through the respirator, and back out. The turn in the crack disorients direction.
- **Clip the tether on before the turn.**
- **Keep a hand free** and avoid scraping the walls. Do not mark them, scratch them, or leave anything on them. They are still being written.

---

## 6. Conduct Inside

### Physical
- **Move slowly and stay out of the way of the plants.** Their joints move with clean mechanical strokes. A swelling stalk or a turning cam-plant can pinch, crush, or draw threads and fingers along with it. Keep hands, hair, straps, and loose clothing clear of anything moving.
- **Don't touch the products without care.** Ingots come out warm from pressing and edges are perfect. Sheets are razor sharp. Wound copper mineral threads can snag.
- **Watch your footing on glassy rock.** The floor is smooth between plants, and dry heat makes you tired and clumsy.
- **Stay out of the drift** at the low end. It sinks into the floor "like a body into snow." Do not step on it or into it, or set down gear there. Assume that anything on it will be absorbed.

### Temporal and Spatial
- **Don't trust what you see first.** Objects may appear before their cause. If you find an ingot under a straining stalk, don't try to work out whether it came first. Simply note it in your log.
- **Distance is unreliable.** If two stalks that were far apart suddenly stand together, don't try to reason about it in the moment. Stop, look around, check the line, and log it.
- **Never use paces to gauge how far you are from the exit.** Use the tether.
- **If the far end of the chamber seems to take a hundred breaths to reach, turn around.** It may also take one. Neither should feel like something to test. Your only fixed reference is the line and your turnaround time.
- **Stay a consistent rate of speed and pause when confused.** Check your partner before doing anything unusual.

### The Whistle
- **When the flat tone sounds, stop.** Every joint in the chamber pauses, so pause with it: stop moving, keep still for the length of the tone, and resume when the plants do. This is the only communal event and the safest course is to match it. Don't make sudden movements during it. It is not known what pausing is for, and it costs you nothing to join it.

### Not Touching the Annotations
- **Do not add to, alter, or try to complete any of the white notations.** They are strata that formed as instructions, and the rock is built to match them, sometimes before they exist. Writing in a similar notation or adding a mark, even accidentally, could become part of what the room builds. Do not trace them, rub them, or copy them into your log where you could confuse them later. Sketch them if you must, in your own notation, and label the sketch clearly as a record.
- **Do not measure the room in a way that leaves marks.** Use passive tools, not chalk, tape stuck to walls, or scratching.

### Leave No Trace
- **Bring nothing alive.** No pets, no seeds, no pack of unwashed clothing carrying spores. You are the only animal here and it has run for a very long time without any. Sterilize boots and gear as much as you reasonably can.
- **Don't carry out plant material, crystals, or ingots from inside.** I don't know what happens if you interrupt a line of production or remove a finished part. It is possible nothing does. It is also possible that the room will revise around the gap.

---

## 7. Mind and Morale

- **Expect an odd sense of presence.** The rustle of leaves against each other sounds like "a very large room in which someone is thinking quietly." There is no evidence anyone is watching. Don't talk to it, and don't feel foolish if you want to.
- **Expect to feel unnecessary.** The chamber doesn't need or use you. This can be unsettling for people who define their trip by purpose. Have a purpose of your own (observation, survey, record) and hold to it.
- **The lack of time markers will loosen your judgment.** Assign someone the role of timekeeper. That person calls the turnaround whether or not it feels premature.
- **If a partner starts acting strangely,** by repeating things, expressing certainty about something that hasn't happened, or moving as if the room's geometry is different for them, end the visit at once. Don't argue with them about the sequence of events. Guide them out along the line.

---

## 8. Leaving

- **Exit along the tether,** same order as entering, with a headcount at the crack.
- **Rehydrate and cool down outside.** Heat illness often shows up after you leave the hot place, when you relax. Rest in shade and keep drinking.
- **Check for injuries.** You may not have felt cuts, burns, or chemical irritation.
- **Compare logs.** Do this soon, and in writing. Discrepancies between what two people recorded are normal here and worth noting, not resolving.

### The Ingot by the Entrance

You will likely find one, a hand's width from the entrance, cool, heavy, and inscribed with a line that stops mid-measurement. Here is my honest advice, given that nobody knows for certain:

- **Prefer to leave it where it lies.** It was not there when you went in, and it is not clear who or what it is for. It is the only cool thing in the place, which suggests it is not part of the heat and is meant to be noticed, but being noticed is not the same as being offered.
- **If you take it, understand the risk.** The line of figures is unfinished. It "waits for the rest of the mountain to be built to fit it." Carrying it away might leave that measurement dangling, or it might pull the mountain, or the nearest thing that can be built, toward wherever you take it. You might be carrying a specification out of the one place that can fulfill it.
- **If you do take it, don't carry it into a place you care about.** Keep it away from settlements, and don't leave it in a shelter, a workshop, or anywhere that might get "revised" to match. Don't add to the notation, and don't complete the measurement, even if you think you know how it ends.
- **The safest middle path:** photograph it, sketch the notation carefully in your log, and put it back exactly where it was.

---

## 9. Emergency Notes

- **Heat stroke:** get the person out along the line, into shade, and cool them aggressively with water, wet cloth, and fanning. Do this outside, not in the chamber. If confusion or collapse persists, treat as an emergency.
- **Gas exposure:** if you smell strong sulfur or sting in the eyes or throat, move outward immediately, breathe clean air, and flush eyes with saline or clean water.
- **Lost or stretched distance:** stop, breathe, find the line, and follow it. Do not run. Do not trust your sense of how far you are.
- **Trapped or pinched by a plant:** don't force it. The stroke completes on the order of minutes, so wait for release if it's not crushing, and have your partner assist. If it is, use a pry tool and signal.
- **Rockfall or tremor:** retreat at once through the crack. Young ranges shift.

---

## Final Word

The Foundry asks nothing of you and gives nothing back except the sight of work being done with no one to use it. That is a rare thing to witness. Treat it with the manners you'd bring to any working shop: stay out of the way, keep your hands to yourself, don't touch what you don't understand, and leave when your time is up. Bring more water than seems reasonable, a line stronger than you think you need, and a log you trust more than your own memory.
