# Preparing for the Unfinished Shoulder

Think of this as a visit to a mountain range, an industrial city, and a slightly unfinished piece of reality all at once. Most of what will hurt you is ordinary: altitude, cold, wet, sharp stone, soot. The unusual part is subtler, and you prepare for it mostly by being methodical.

## 1. Before you go: your own constants

The loose ground doesn't usually attack you. It makes things *approximate*, and the danger is losing track of what is true. So bring things that are reliably themselves.

- **A plumb line and a small bag of iron filings.** Buy them at home, from a source you trust, and learn to use them before you arrive. The foundry plumb-wardens do this three times a shift, and you should do it whenever you change wards, wake up, or feel that a room is slightly off.
- **A paper notebook and a pencil.** Batteries and screens are poor bets in a place where objects hesitate. Each morning and evening, write down your name, the date by your home reckoning, the number of people in your party, and what you are carrying. Read the last entry before writing the next.
- **A mechanical watch, wound by hand,** and a second, different timekeeper. Distances vary, so travel times will too. What you want to know is whether your own sense of duration is drifting.
- **A physical anchor to home.** A key, a coin, or a photograph: something whose details you know cold. Check it against memory now and then. If it starts to differ, leave.
- **Your exit plan.** Know exactly how you'll leave, and never let your only route depend on soft country. Arrange it so that the last leg back to your departure point is over well-fixed ground, and give yourself a generous margin. Time and distance here can't be trusted to hold still for a tight schedule.

## 2. The body: altitude, cold, and wet

The plateau is high enough that the sun is a pale coin in a plum-dark sky. Treat it as a serious altitude environment, not a scenic hike.

- **Acclimatize.** Spend days at a lower ward before going higher. Consult a doctor about preventive medication before you travel. Bring a small emergency oxygen canister with a mask. Headache, confusion, and clumsiness are ordinary altitude signs, but here they're also easy to mistake for the loose ground, and the reverse. Don't guess. Descend and rest.
- **Cold that is never bitter, but constant.** Because it never feels dangerous, it sneaks up on you. Wear layers: a wool or synthetic base, an insulating mid-layer, and a real waterproof shell. Cotton is a bad idea anywhere here.
- **Wet.** Everything condenses and drips, so pack gear in dry bags and carry spare socks, gloves, and a base layer sealed separately. Expect to be damp constantly and plan for it. Waterproof matches, a small stove, and hot drinks in a good flask matter more than they seem to.
- **Light.** The sun is dim and shadows are soft and doubled, which flattens depth perception. Steps, drops, and cracks are harder to judge. Carry a headlamp *and* a backup, with spare cells kept warm inside your jacket. High-altitude UV can still be significant despite the gloom, so bring tinted eye protection anyway.
- **Noise.** Steam whistles, rolling mills, and echoes that return three or four times mean hearing protection isn't optional. Bring good earplugs, and learn to tell the whistle patterns from the mountain's own booms and rings. Take them out in quiet stretches, though. You *want* to hear the echo-before-cause when it happens.

## 3. Footwear, hands, and the sharp new rock

The stone is fresh, with edges you could shave with.

- **Boots** must be stiff, waterproof, and ankle-supporting, with a very aggressive tread, since wet moss over black rock is slick. Break them in before you go.
- **Cut-resistant gloves** are essential. You will grab rock without thinking. A cut in a cold, damp place heals slowly and gets infected, so carry a serious first aid kit with irrigation, closure strips, and antibiotic ointment, and know how to use them.
- **A helmet.** Rockfalls are common, and the rock hasn't yet weathered into safe rounded shapes.
- **Eye protection** against flakes, especially in cuts and galleries.
- **Trekking poles.** Use them to probe.

## 4. The moss: stay on the tracks

The deep green mats hide fissures. Hikers who leave the tracks "go through to the thigh," and below, the rock is often warm, which may mean heat, vented gas, or both.

- Stay on marked tracks and worked paths. Poles are for probing, always, wherever the ground looks like cushion.
- Rope up on any unfamiliar stretch, with an experienced local if possible.
- Be aware that warm air rising from a fissure isn't necessarily benign. Don't crawl into cracks, and if you feel dizzy, sleepy, or notice an odd smell after a fall, get out and upwind. Consider a small personal gas monitor.
- If someone goes through, don't all rush to the hole. Stop, spread out, and assume the ground near them is also hollow.

## 5. Water, food, and the pools

The round, clear "first pools" with unnaturally orderly gravel are lovely and you should not drink from them. Locals say they're older than the rock around them, and nobody argues, which is a signal to be careful. Whatever they are, they aren't ordinary water.

- Carry your own water and a filter, and use a purifier on any condensate you collect. Mist-water is probably better than pool water, but boil or treat it.
- Bring the bulk of your food from home, sealed. If you eat local food, stick to what the wards' own kitchens serve residents daily, prepared in front of you.
- Do **not** eat anything foraged: moss, lichen, orchids, or the grazers hunted in the open. The fauna is "drifting," and whatever a species is deciding to become may not agree with your digestion.
- Don't touch or taste *fix*. It is a pale oil pumped through everything, poured into foundations and painted along the seams of loose ground. It holds reality together for the locals, and you don't know what it would do in you. Wash your hands if you get any on them, and don't cook with pipe-heated water unless the locals themselves do.
- Handle **lantern-stone** the way you'd handle an unidentified mineral: lightly, briefly, with gloves. Don't keep it in your pocket or your bed. It glows when struck and it's part of the engines and the fix, so it's clearly doing something. A dosimeter is a cheap precaution if you can get a working one.

## 6. Soot, steam, and industry

The wards are full of foundries, rolling mills, hoists, and open pits.

- Wear a good particulate respirator in the ward streets, in mine cuts, and around any foundry. Damp air plus soot plus altitude is hard on lungs.
- Follow the shift whistles. A change in the whistle pattern means the plumb has hung crooked, more fix is being called for, and the shift is filing out. **If everyone around you starts moving in the same direction, move with them, then ask questions.**
- Keep clear of pipe trestles, steam vents, and lifting gear. They're old, damp, and loud, and their operators won't hear you shout.

## 7. Getting around: cable-cars, railways, and paths

Between wards there is emptiness, cloud, and moss. You will spend hours crossing it.

- Travel between wards by the official cable-cars and narrow-gauge railways, not on foot, unless local guides recommend the route.
- Ask about journey times, but don't rely on them. A trip that took an hour on Tuesday may take forty minutes on Wednesday. Pack supplies for a delay *and* for arriving early into a ward with nobody expecting you.
- Carry a pencil map, and take the eraser seriously. Expect to redraw it. Record landmarks and signal-boxes, and note which lamp was burning in each. If a stretch repeats, note that too. The pattern is more informative than the contradiction.
- Never begin a journey late in the afternoon, when the cloud climbs into the ridge folds and visibility drops. Aim to arrive before dusk.
- Do not cross the grey-white lines painted along seams unless you're with someone who knows what's on the far side. They mark where the fix ends and the loose ground begins.

## 8. Loose ground: a practical field guide

You won't always be able to avoid soft country, so be able to recognize it.

- **Sound before cause.** If you hear a whistle, rockfall, or a voice and see no source, treat it as a *forecast*, not a mistake. Step back, get clear of overhangs and rails, and wait. Then you'll learn what it was warning you about.
- **Repeating places.** If you pass the same corner, signal-box, or pool twice, stop. Don't keep walking in the hope you'll break out of it. Go back the way you came, using the notes you've been keeping, and ask a local guide for the marked route out.
- **Objects hesitating.** A dropped tool falling slowly, a cup settling level in stages: these are signs the ground is undecided. Don't put down anything you can't afford to lose, and don't sleep there.
- **The flat green-white glow.** Where the silver light stops at an invisible edge and there's no shadow, *do not step over it.* If you find yourself in that light, retreat toward shadow and don't try to explore.
- **People and counts.** Herds go into soft country and come out in a different order or number, and so might your group. Rope up when passing through it. Call names aloud at regular intervals and count heads before and after every crossing. Agree beforehand on a private phrase, something only your party knows, to confirm identity if someone returns from an odd stretch.
- **Use your plumb.** If it hangs crooked, you're on unfixed ground. Go back to the last place it hung true.

## 9. Lodging and local customs

- Stay in a well-fixed ward with a working plumb-warden. The cheap wards near soft country are full of cheerful, careful people, and they're fine to visit in the daytime, but sleeping there is a different matter.
- **Use both locks.** In the borderland districts, every door has two, because sometimes there are two doors. If your room has one door and your key opens it, good. If you find a second door you didn't see before, don't open it. Lock both, and ask the landlord in the morning.
- The locals note the "small errors" of the place with a clerk's flatness, so don't correct them, argue with them, or make a fuss about strangeness. Don't tell anyone their pools are impossible. It's rude, and it may not be well received.
- Be respectful to the plumb-wardens. Their work keeps the ward true, and interrupting a check is dangerous as well as impolite.
- Bring currency the wards will accept (ask ahead about coin, script, or barter), and dress to blend in with the working clothes: dark, heavy, practical.

## 10. Animals

- The grazers are shoulder-high, dense, and shaggy. They are herd animals with inward-curving horns, so give them room, especially in spring, and never come between an adult and a young one.
- The great scavenger birds with wingspans wider than a tram car are a warning when they circle. **If they hold a pattern over an empty place for days, don't go there.** They may be looking at something the ground hides from you.
- Animals showing "drift," like the extra-jointed leg or the two-shaped beak, aren't to be touched, chased, or photographed at close range. They aren't dangerous because they're strange, but because nobody knows what they are yet.
- Don't take specimens home. Don't feed anything.

## 11. Coming back

- Leave before you're tired. Fatigue, altitude, and the loose ground all erode your confidence in what you remember.
- On the way out, check your notebook against your memory, your party's count against the head-count, your anchor object against your image of it, and your plumb line against the world. If any of these fail, stay in a well-fixed ward and rest until they agree.
- On return, wash all gear thoroughly, isolate anything that came into contact with fix or lantern-stone, and see a doctor for altitude effects. Repeat the plumb test on the first few days at home. If something seems slightly wrong and won't settle, note it in the book and consider returning to have it checked at a well-fixed ward.

## The right attitude

The Unfinished Shoulder is not hostile. It's *incomplete*, and its people have made a workable peace with that, with brooms, whistles, and a great deal of patience. The best visitors adopt the same manner: modest, methodical, unhurried, and a little wry. Take your bearings again and again. Trust your notebook over your feeling. Step back when the echo arrives before the sound. And when the dusk whistles all go up together across the gaps and the furnaces glow like embers set in black stone, stop, listen, and enjoy it. It's one of the more remarkable places you'll see, and it will still be unfinished when you leave.
