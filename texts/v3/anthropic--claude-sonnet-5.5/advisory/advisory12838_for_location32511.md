# Preparing for the Chorus Pocket

## What you're actually walking into

The plateau is a sun-and-glare problem. The descent is an endurance problem. The Pocket itself is a sensory-overload problem, where sound is a physical force acting on your ears, teeth, chest, and eyes. Everything else follows from that.

The place is also not empty in the way it first seems. Thirty deaf caretakers live in a city built to be heard, tending plants that may be listening to something. You are a guest in a room where someone is concentrating. Prepare like a mountaineer, and behave like someone entering a monastery during prayer.

---

## 1. Hearing

This is the main risk. The Tuners are commonly deaf by forty, and they are experts with a lifetime of the best protection they can make. Assume any exposure costs you something, and plan to spend as little of it as possible.

- **Double protect.** Wear high-rated foam plugs, inserted deep and correctly, under heavy over-ear defenders. Add a felted wrap or a wax seal over the top, as the Tuners do. Bring several spare sets, because sweat, dust, and vibration work plugs loose.
- **Remember bone conduction.** The roar reaches the inner ear through the skull. Even perfect ear protection only gets you so far. Wear a snug padded cap or hood and a wrapped headband. Keep your jaw slightly slack, because clenched teeth in that drone will ache and chatter.
- **Budget your exposure.** Treat your ears as having a daily allowance. Spend time in the padded inner rooms, and take breaks in the quieter upper terraces. Do not sit in the loudest cluster of pipes to "get the full experience."
- **Get a baseline.** If your home world has audiometry, get a hearing test before you go and after you leave. Bring ear drops and a plan for tinnitus.
- **Screen yourself.** Do not go if you have inner-ear disease, epilepsy, a heart condition, a pacemaker, or an implant that sound or vibration could affect. Pregnancy and migraines are also good reasons to say no.

## 2. Vibration and the body

You will feel the sound in your sternum, your gut, your sinuses, and your eyes. The description notes blurred vision and loose-feeling teeth. Expect nausea, vertigo, a racing heart, anxiety, and tunnel-like fatigue.

- Eat lightly before descent. Nothing greasy.
- Introduce yourself in stages: a short first visit, a long rest, then longer stays.
- Do not climb, carry loads, or use ladders during the late-morning gust. Sit against a padded wall and wait it out.
- If you feel panicky, put your palms flat on a warm wall and breathe slowly. That is how the Tuners cope, and it does help.
- Never go in alone. Rope up or use a hand-tether on the stairs and in the plant beds, because you cannot call to each other.

## 3. Communication

Shouting is pointless and rude, and radios are only useful if they work at all. Prepare non-acoustic communication:

- Learn a basic touch-and-gesture code with your party before arriving: *stop, go, sick, danger, water, leave, wait*.
- Agree on tap or squeeze signals on the shoulder or collarbone. The Tuners use collarbone touch, so keep it gentle and unobtrusive.
- Bring bright signal cloths. In the light columns they show up well, and the Tuners use them too.
- Bring a slate or waterproof notebook with chalk or a grease pencil, and pre-write simple phrases. Do not assume the Tuners will read your script, but you might learn theirs.
- Approach the Tuners in clear view, in the light, from the front. They will not hear you coming, and startling someone on a high stair is dangerous for both of you.

## 4. Light and the plateau

- **The plateau** is a gypsum and salt pan, pale enough to hurt. Bring dark, side-shielded glacier goggles, a broad hat, a face wrap, and long loose sleeves in light colors. The heat rebounds from below as well as above.
- **The Throat** is marked mainly by its pitch. Note its position well before you descend. Use landmarks, a cairn, or a bright marker, because the pan is featureless and shimmers.
- **The hollow** is bright everywhere, with no dark shadows, and the selenite throws light in bars and coins. That confuses depth perception. On the stair and in the streets, keep goggles on (a lighter tint inside), and watch your footing. Steps are dished into bowls, and gypsum dust makes them slick.
- Bring a headlamp with a red or dim setting for the ascent or descent at dusk or dark. The light-wells go dark at night and the stair does not forgive stumbling.

## 5. Air and dust

- Wear an FFP2/N95-grade mask or better. Gypsum dust lifts in the gusts and hangs glittering in the light.
- The hollow is the lowest ground for a day's walk, and it sits above a hot vent. The air smells sweet, "like baked clay and cut stems," which is pleasant and also potentially a warning. Carry a gas monitor (CO₂, H₂S, and if possible a general multi-gas unit). Check it on the way down and again at the bottom. Do not sleep in a low spot where vent gas could pool.
- Bring saline eyewash and a dust cover for cameras and instruments.

## 6. Water and food

The Pocket has essentially no drinkable water.

- **Carry all your own.** Plan for the plateau heat, the hour-long descent, your stay, and the climb out. That is more than you think: budget at least 5 to 6 liters per person per day of activity, plus a reserve.
- Add electrolytes and salt. Dry heat and stress will pull them out of you.
- **Do not drink the clear oil** and do not eat any of the plants. It is the plants' lifeblood, the Tuners carry it in jars for their own purposes, and you don't know its chemistry.
- Do not drink the dawn vent-fog condensate, either.
- Bring dense, non-perishable food you can eat without much chewing. A mouth full of dust and loose-feeling teeth is not a good combination with hard crackers.
- If you want to give a gift to the Tuners, clean water in sealed clay or waxed containers, beeswax, felt, and unspun fibre are useful and nondisruptive. Avoid giving iron, machinery, or anything that changes what they can do. Do not offer sound-makers of any kind.

## 7. Gear (and what to leave behind)

**Bring:**
- Sturdy, soft-soled boots with good grip (dust makes the steps slippery, and you want quiet footfalls)
- Gloves for handling rough stairs and rope
- A short rope or tether, and a modest climbing harness for the stair if you're unsteady
- A first-aid kit with burn care, eye care, and rehydration supplies
- Padding and cloth wrap for every hard object on your body (buckles, carabiners, flasks) so nothing clinks
- A solid-state, non-acoustic way to trigger your return. If your anchor, beacon, or portal depends on a chime, a spoken phrase, a tuning fork, or a hum, it is useless here, and worse, it is a foreign sound in a place tuned to precision.

**Leave behind:**
- Speakers, whistles, alarms, instruments, recordings, and phones with ringtones. Do not hum, sing, or whistle either.
- Spinning hard drives and delicate vibration-sensitive electronics. Loud low-frequency sound can corrupt them.
- Weapons that make noise. If you fear the predator, carry a plain stout staff and rely on good route choices instead.
- Anything you'd be tempted to take as a souvenir.

## 8. Conduct in the city

The Tuners have kept a fragile balance for centuries. You could unbalance it in an afternoon.

- **Touch little.** The walls are how the Tuners read the city's mood. The pipes are tuned. Do not lean on the clay towers, climb the organ ranks, or brush the harp-wires on the bridges. If you must brace yourself, use plain rock or the bare stair.
- **Stay off the plants and planters.** Step around beetles and lizards. Do not pick, clip, or cut a frond. Do not take cuttings. The plants seem to remember, and you would carry a wilting piece of a listening thing to a quiet place where it dies.
- **Do not "fix" anything.** A silted pipe and its neat brown ring of dead plants may look like a problem. The Tuners walk around these places without comment. Do the same, and do not ask about them.
- **Walk evenly.** In a place where the plants may be tracking sources and a blind hunter feels footsteps in stone, your gait is part of the soundscape. Move slowly, deliberately, and steadily, without stamping or scuffing.
- **Ask without asking.** They are not likely to hold long conversations with strangers. Sit where they can see you, with your palms visible, and let them decide how much contact to have.

## 9. The predator

The pale, blind, many-legged thing that hunts by footstep is probably starved or mad, which is not the same as harmless.

- Do not wander into silted or silent districts, where it might shelter.
- Stay in the lit areas and near the Tuners. Do not walk bare floors at night.
- Sleep off the ground, in the padded inner rooms with the Tuners if they permit, or on a raised platform with your party. Put a watcher awake in shifts.
- Do not try to hunt it, trap it, or lure it. If you sense a heavy, purposeful rhythm in the stone that isn't a Tuner, do not run. Stay calm, keep close to your party, and move toward light and the stair.

## 10. Timing your visit

The day has a shape. Use it.

- **Pre-dawn:** Cross the plateau while it is cool. Find the Throat by its pitch, and mark it.
- **Early morning:** Descend. The stair takes about an hour, and you want to be settled by mid-morning. The dawn vent-fog is the only moisture, and it can be a good moment to see the plants at their most alive.
- **Late morning:** The gust and scream. Be in the padded inner rooms with your protection doubled. Do not be on the stair or the terraces.
- **Noon:** The sound may drop out of hearing altogether for a few heartbeats. Expect it and be still. Stay calm, keep your voice quiet, and stay near the stair or an exit, not out in the middle of the plant beds. The Tuners will turn their faces to the dome, waiting for something that has so far not arrived. Do not try to give it a signal, and do not fill the silence with a shout.
- **Afternoon:** The light columns crawl and the plants turn. Good time for careful, quiet observation from the upper terraces.
- **Dusk:** The wind reverses. The pipes sing lower, and the drone becomes almost a lullaby. This is the best window for rest and for recovering your ears. It is also the worst time to climb, because the light-wells are going dark.
- **Overnight:** Sleep with the Tuners or high on a terrace. Leave the next morning, not at dusk.

**Plan a minimum of one night, and no more than three.** Longer stays cost hearing, and the strangeness of being listened to accumulates.

## 11. The climb out

The stair up is an hour of worn, dust-slick bowls, in heat, with less water than you started with and an aching head. Do it early in the day, before the plateau bakes. Rehydrate at the top, out of the wind, and get out of the sun. Do not stay on the pan longer than you have to.

## 12. After

- Expect ringing, fatigue, and a strange emptiness in ordinary quiet for a day or two.
- Get your hearing rechecked.
- Give yourself time to recover, and be honest about what you noticed. Some travelers report a persistent feeling of being *attended to*. That is not a symptom you need to treat, but pay attention to whether it fades.

---

## The one-sentence version

Arrive early with double ear protection, all your own water, a non-acoustic way home, and quiet, padded gear. Move slowly and take nothing. Be still at noon, and leave before the place decides what it wants from you.
