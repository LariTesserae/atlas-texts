# Preparing for the Mulch Works at Karrow Bend

## The short version

This is not a dangerous place in the dramatic sense. Nothing here hunts you, and the one large machine moves once every dozen seconds. The risks are the slow, ordinary ones of a warm, wet, rotting, rusting place: cold and damp working into you over hours, ground that lies about how deep it is, air that may be bad in low spots, and a lot of biology you'd rather not inhale or carry home. Prepare as you would for a cold-water marsh crossed with a compost heap and an abandoned foundry, and go quietly. The place has reached a balance, and the best visitors leave it undisturbed.

## Arriving: the towpath

You will enter through a green tunnel about two people wide, at around nine degrees Celsius, in air that never quite rains. Everything you carry will be slightly damp within twenty minutes, so prepare for that before you set out.

- **Outer layer:** A real waterproof shell with a hood, not water-resistant. The drip-tips will find every gap at the collar and cuffs. Sleeves darken early, and wet cuffs pull cold up the arm.
- **Insulation:** Wool or synthetic mid-layers, never cotton or down. Down will clump and fail. Nine degrees sounds mild, but the damp and the hours of standing still to watch things happen make it feel colder. The warmth of the mat is deceptive: it warms your hands and knees, not you.
- **Feet:** Waterproof boots with real ankle support and a stiff sole, plus gaiters. Bring at least two spare pairs of socks in a dry bag. Wet feet are the most likely way this trip goes wrong. Rubber boots are tempting, but you'll want the grip and support on the cobbles hidden under the moss.
- **Hands:** Thin waterproof gloves for climbing root and touching iron, with a warm liner. You will want to touch things. Rusted iron, rough bark, and sap you don't understand all justify gloves.

## The ground: it is deeper than it looks

The mat is knee-soft at the edges and chest-deep in the hollows, and it is uniformly green and springy, so you cannot tell which is which by looking.

- **Bring a probe.** A trekking pole or a plain stick, used before every step onto unfamiliar flat. Test for firmness, and feel for the thin lines where the old streets run. Those depressions have cobbles just below and are your safest routes. Follow the pale lines of lighter moss where you can.
- **Do not go into hollows.** A chest-deep pit of wet, warm rot is hard to climb out of alone, and the mat is heavy and clinging. If you sink past the knee, lean back, spread your weight, and crawl out. Don't thrash.
- **Travel with a partner,** or at minimum tell someone where you are. Bring a light rope. The tender visits twice a week, so a stranded visitor could be a long time unnoticed.
- **Kneel with care.** Warm ground invites lying down. Don't.

## The air: warm rot makes its own atmosphere

This is the part most likely to be underestimated. Two centuries of composting matter produces gas, and steam ribbons rise from the hot spots.

- **Gas in low places.** Carbon dioxide, methane, ammonia, and possibly hydrogen sulfide can pool in hollows, stone pits, and enclosed spaces, especially on still, misty mornings. A personal multi-gas monitor is worth carrying. If you smell rotten eggs, or feel sudden headache, dizziness, or an urge to sit down, climb out of the low spot at once and move upwind and uphill.
- **Spores and dust.** The ferns release visible yellow puffs of spores in late morning, and disturbing the mat sends up fine debris. Compost-type environments harbor *Aspergillus* and other organisms that are harmless to most people and dangerous to some. Wear a well-fitted P3/FFP3 or N95-or-better respirator while walking on the mat, or at least during the late-morning spore release and any time you're kneeling or digging. Anyone with asthma, a compromised immune system, or a fungal sensitivity should think hard about going at all.
- **Warm, misty water.** Warm aerosolized water is a good environment for *Legionella*. The Drying Hall, where the condensation rains hardest, is the place to keep your respirator on and your stay short.
- **Steam.** It is low-pressure and gentle, but the copper pipes buried in the mat and the engine's exhaust can burn. Don't dig where you can't see the pipes.

## Water, iron, and the sap

- **Don't drink anything.** The canal, the pump channel, the seeping mortar, and the drip-water are all unknown. Bring your own water and food and eat away from the mat. Wash hands with your own clean water before eating.
- **Tetanus:** Have it current. You're surrounded by rusting iron, and every glove-less grasp of a gantry is a chance for a puncture.
- **Leptospirosis and canal-borne infection:** Keep cuts covered and don't touch the canal water.
- **The ropewort sap** runs clear and rusts orange within a minute. I can't tell you whether it's toxic or irritating, and you shouldn't assume it's safe. Don't break stems casually. If you must, wear gloves and eye protection, and keep it away from your face. The plants concentrate iron heavily, so avoid tasting or handling them barehanded. The warm-bread-and-iron smell is a reason to be curious, not a reason to sample.
- **Skin and eyes:** Bring a small saline eyewash and a first-aid kit that includes wound irrigation. Cover any break in the skin.

## The structures

- **The Bell Tower** is a hollow lattice of living root. Climb it slowly, test every step, and go alone only if you're confident. The steps are root, and roots are wet. Keep three points of contact. Because the district is "always mid-demolition," things give way. Don't climb in strong wind or right after rain, when the wood is slickest and heaviest. The view from the top is the best way to understand the town's pattern, so it's worth doing carefully.
- **The Drying Hall** has a woven canopy that sheds rain and drips constantly inside. Watch overhead as well as underfoot. A collapse of rotted beam or brick is an ordinary event here.
- **The Chimney Row** is soot-dark at the top and moss-furred below, with water seeping down. Don't stand under or lean on the stacks, and keep well back. A dropped brick course lands quietly in the mat and is gone in a season, but it lands hard first.
- **The engine pit** is stone-walled and open. Stay out of the swing path of the beam and away from the pump rod and the shaft. The beam is slow, but it is a cast-iron beam moving under great force, and the *thunk* of the pump is a reminder of that. Don't lean over the pit edge, since the stone will be wet and mossed.

## Etiquette: this is a loop, not a ruin

The most important thing to grasp is that the whole district is a working system. The plants heat the pipes, the pipes drive the engine, the engine drains the ground, and the drained ground keeps the plants balanced. If the engine stops, the mat saturates, the roots rot, the heat fades, and nothing restarts. This is not a museum piece.

- **Don't touch the engine's mechanisms,** grease points, copper joints, or the pump channel. The tender maintains these on a schedule.
- **Don't dig into the mat** near the buried pipes, and don't compact or channel it. Even a small trench could shift water flow or cool a hot zone.
- **Don't block the stone channel,** and don't leave anything in it. Fallen leaves are the tender's job to clear, and a dropped pack strap is a real problem.
- **Don't take living material away.** This is partly ecological courtesy and partly caution: a plant family adapted to iron and heat, carried to another ecosystem, is an unknown risk. Clean your boots, gaiters, and pack thoroughly before you leave. Seeds and spores travel on wet fabric.
- **Carry no open flame** in the mat. Self-heating compost can smolder, and methane is a possibility in enclosed spaces. The lantern in the engine house is the tender's, and should stay theirs.
- **If you meet the tender,** be respectful and let them work. They are the only regular human presence, and they know the ground. The logbook (*turned; clear; steady*) is theirs. Read it if it's offered. Don't write in it unasked.
- **Be quiet.** Voices carry oddly here, and the wildlife is sparse and easily displaced. The heron warming its legs in the warm outflow has a reason to be there. Stay back from it.

## Timing your visit

- **Mornings** give you the mist cycle, snails pearling the warm iron, and steam columns. They are also when gas is most likely to sit in the hollows and the cold is worst.
- **Late morning** brings the spore puffs. Wear the respirator.
- **Afternoon** is when the ropewort's tendrils swing toward the light. Plan an hour to watch it: fix on one tendril and count.
- **After rain,** the moss brightens from olive to emerald in minutes and the engine slows. It's beautiful, but the ground and roots are at their slickest and heaviest. Take extra care on the tower.
- **Dusk** is calm and gray-gold, but the temperature drops and the mist gathers at the edges. Be back on the towpath before full dark. The mat doesn't have reliable edges in poor light.

Two or three times a week real rain arrives from the west, so check the sky before committing to a long stay.

## What to bring, consolidated

- Waterproof shell, wool/synthetic layers, spare socks, gaiters, waterproof boots
- Waterproof gloves with liners, eye protection
- P3/FFP3 respirator (spare filters) and a multi-gas monitor
- Trekking pole or probe stick, light rope, whistle
- Headlamp with spare batteries (the canopy is dim even at noon, and the engine house is unlit)
- Own water, food, hand-washing water, sanitizer
- First-aid kit, saline eyewash, tetanus up to date
- Dry bags for everything, and a waterproof notebook (paper turns soft here)
- A hand lens, if you're inclined to look closely at the plant family, the pale under-leaf, and the snails
- Something to scrub boots and gear on the way out
- A dependable way back to your point of departure. The towpath is your only marked route, so know how you'll return, and leave the way you came.

## Preparing yourself

Expect slowness. There are no voices, no other engines, and almost no birdsong. You will feel the engine's breath in your sternum every eleven or twelve seconds, and it takes a little while to stop bracing for the next one. Give yourself an hour of just standing and noticing. The place is dozens of small clocks running at different speeds, and you only see them if you stop moving.

It can also be a little unnerving. The plants share a smell and a look, the ground is warm like a sleeping animal, and everything is quietly turning old iron into leaf. Nothing about it is hostile. But it is a place that has reached its own condition, and you are a temporary, slightly clumsy addition to it. Come prepared, step lightly, take nothing, and let it keep going: *turned; clear; steady.*
