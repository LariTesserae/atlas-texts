# Preparing for the Long Lowland

## The Short Version

This is a gentle place that punishes carelessness in quiet ways. Nothing here will chase you, but you will be wet for the whole trip, exposed to sun and lightning on a plain with no shade, and walking over water that can hide a three-meter drop. Plan for four things: **wet feet, open sky, hidden holes, and a horizon with no landmarks.**

## Timing

The season matters more than anything else you decide.

- **Rainy season:** The shallows join into one sheet, and the fish and birds spread out. Walking is easy because the water is even and warm, but the channels and hollows are harder to read. A few centimeters of rise can flood a hundred hectares, so your route may vanish under you. This is the season for wading, and it is beautiful.
- **Dry season:** The land breaks into shrinking pools crowded with fish, birds, and turtles. Wildlife viewing is spectacular, and the smell of warm mud and green rot is strong. Pools may be far apart, and the drying ones are unpleasant to drink from or touch.
- **Transition:** This is the hardest to predict. Expect changed channels and silted routes if you have any old information.

If you have a choice, arrive early in the rains. Plan to be off the open flats by mid-afternoon, since rain falls most afternoons.

## Feet and Legs

You will be wet from the ankle down for most of every day, so this is where preparation matters most.

- **Footwear:** Closed-toe water shoes or light boots with real tread. The stone is worn flat, but cyanobacterial films on the domes are slick as soap, and crayfish and small biting things live in the shallows. Bare feet are a poor idea, and thin sandals are barely better.
- **Socks:** Bring several pairs of synthetic or wool socks and change them each evening. Constant soaking softens skin, and blisters and fungal trouble can end a trip. Dry your feet at night on a warm dome crown, airing them and powdering them if you can.
- **Gaiters or quick-dry trousers** protect against sedge blades, insects, and sun on the shins.
- **A walking staff** is the most useful single item. Use it to probe ahead in tea-colored water, because **solution hollows are invisible in silty water and drop away without warning.** In clear water over bare rock you can see them coming. Over tannin-dark or silt-clouded water, assume the bottom is a lie until your pole confirms it. A staff also helps you read the depth and the tilt.

## Sun, Heat, and Lightning

The plain is open and the water doubles the glare.

- Wear a wide-brimmed hat, long light sleeves, and sun protection for the skin under your chin and nose, where reflected light strikes.
- **Polarized sunglasses** are close to mandatory. They cut the glare and let you see through clear water to the bottom, which is the main way you'll spot hollows.
- Midday water is bath-warm, so it won't cool you much. Plan for heat exhaustion, carry electrolytes, and rest during the hottest hours on a dome's shady flank or in an island's tree shade.
- **Lightning is the serious danger.** On a flat plain you are the tallest thing for kilometers, and you are standing in water. Watch the afternoon build-up. If a storm approaches, get low and away from the domes and lone trees, spread out from companions, and keep your staff from being the highest point. Never shelter under an island tree cluster. The rain arrives as a wall of hiss, so you'll usually have a few minutes' warning.

## Navigation

The horizon is one unbroken line, and the light doubles itself in the pools. Sense of direction fails quickly.

- Use the **domes as landmarks.** Sketch them, name them, and note their spacing and profiles. They are the only fixed features.
- Bring a compass and a physical map or notebook. Don't count on electronics, especially since a multiverse traveler's devices may not work by local rules anyway.
- Leave no trail marks that harm anything, but check your back-bearing often. Everything looks alike from the middle of the flats.
- Remember the slope: about a meter of fall per kilometer. If you're lost, water moves toward the sea, and following the channels downhill is a reliable way to travel, if slow.
- **Fix your return point.** If your way into this world depends on a particular spot, mark it against three domes. Don't trust that a channel or tussock will look the same on your return.

## Water and Food

- **Do not drink the water untreated.** Warm, shallow, animal-rich water breeds parasites, and a traveler with no local immunity is very vulnerable. Assume worms, flukes, and unknown organisms, and don't rely on immunity or antibiotics that may not suit local biology.
- Bring your own drinking water for at least the first days. Then use a good filter plus chemical or heat treatment. Tannin-stained water is safe from some standpoints but tastes bitter and clogs filters.
- Avoid wading with open wounds. Avoid sitting in the water when you rest.
- **Bring all your food.** The land is nutrient-poor and the plants aren't food for humans. Eating unknown fish, crayfish, or the pretty flowers is an unneeded risk. Don't assume local species are edible or safe.
- Carry food in sealed containers, since crayfish, rats, and birds will investigate.

## Insects and Small Life

- Mosquitoes come in clouds at dusk. Bring a head net, permethrin-treated clothing, and repellent, and plan to be inside a mosquito-proof shelter by evening.
- Check your skin, socks, and shoes nightly for leeches or ticks (if this world has them). Expect midges by the sundews.
- Carnivorous plants are harmless to you. Please don't pick them, trample them, or feed them your dinner. They are how this poor ground stays alive.

## Sleeping

There is almost no dry ground. Your options:

- **A dome crown:** Warm, dry, and airy, with the best view and the best lightning exposure, so use it only in settled weather. Rock holds heat well into the night. Bring a sleeping pad, because bare stone is hard, and stakes won't hold, so use weights or a freestanding tent.
- **A sand ring beneath a dome:** Damp, peaty, and full of crayfish burrows and insects. Avoid it.
- **A raised hammock or platform in an island of trees:** Shady and out of the water, but it is the loudest place in the country and full of life. Don't harm the roots or the tree.

Whichever you pick, **do not camp in a channel or on a low spot.** A few centimeters of rain can put you underwater.

Bring good earplugs. The frogs begin an hour before dusk, build to a shaking chorus, and continue well after dark, and one species bellows like a ship's horn. You will sleep better if you decide in advance to enjoy it.

## Animals to Respect

- **The herds** of stocky, long-haired grazers wade the flats and look placid. Keep a good distance, never come between a mother and calf, and don't stand in their paths. Their hoof-wells make new pools and hidden holes, which you can step into.
- **Turtles, herons, and spoonbills** flush at your approach. Move slowly and let the sound return. When you disturb a chorus, wait for it to swell back before moving again.
- **Small air-breathing fish** may cross rocks around your feet. This is normal.
- Unknown predators are possible, especially since the description cannot list everything. Travel with a partner and keep local knowledge if you can find any.

## Leave-No-Trace, Amplified

This ecosystem runs on almost nothing. The rock is leached and the soil is a few centimeters of sand and peat, so even small additions have big effects.

- No soap, sunscreen, or repellent in the water if you can help it. Wash on rock far from pools, or use none.
- Carry out all waste, including human waste. Bury nothing in the peat rings.
- Don't walk on cyanobacterial films, moss mats, or lichen crowns of the domes if you can avoid it. They grow slowly, and your footprints will outlast you.
- Don't move seeds, mud, or water between pools. Clean your boots before you arrive.
- Don't introduce anything to a world you can't fully understand.

## Multiverse-Specific Notes

- **Bring an emergency plan for extraction.** Because the landscape is so uniform, be sure you know how to reach your departure point even in bad weather or if you're injured.
- **Assume your medical kit is only partly useful.** Carry broad-spectrum supplies, water-purification, wound care, anti-diarrheals, rehydration salts, and anti-fungals. Have a plan for infections you can't identify, and consider quarantining yourself on return.
- **Be careful about biological contamination in both directions.** You may carry unknown organisms out, and others in.
- Leave no technology behind and take nothing living or preserved from the place, however lovely.

## Mind-Set

The Long Lowland isn't dramatic, and it doesn't reward hurry. Its scale is time rather than height. The stone beneath you is older than most mountains, and the land is settling toward a flatness it will never reach. Bring patience and stillness.

Plan your days as a slow walk between domes, resting at midday, staying dry when the rain comes, and getting to shelter or a good dome by evening. Then sit on warm stone as the light goes amber and copper, with every pool holding a second sky. Wait for the first frog, and watch a heron fly over its own reflection toward a dome that has been weeping since before there were herons.
