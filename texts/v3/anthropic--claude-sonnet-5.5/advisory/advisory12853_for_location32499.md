# Preparing for the Pour Country

## The first thing to understand

Most places you visit are old, and their rules have settled. This one is young and partly unfinished, and that changes what preparation means. You are preparing for a hard, bright, cold, quiet, and very orderly place, with pockets where the order gives out. Most of your preparation should aim at two things: being a light presence in a fragile, carefully tended place, and not being permanently altered by anything unfinished.

## Body and altitude

The sky is "thin, hard blue," so assume real altitude, probably somewhere between 3,000 and 4,000 meters. Spend at least two or three days at a comparable altitude before you come, or arrange the jump so you arrive at the basin's lowest terraces and rest a full day before climbing to the catwalks. Expect headache, poor sleep, and breathlessness on stairs, of which there are thousands. Bring:

- Any altitude medication your physician recommends
- Electrolyte salts, since dry cold air dehydrates you unnoticed
- Sturdy, broken-in boots with aggressive tread. The gratings and stair-risers are precise and the slab is glazed, so slipperiness is the constant hazard, especially in the fine, unpredictable rain

## Light

You are lit twice, from above and from the glazed surfaces below, so your eyes and skin take double the exposure.

- Real glacier-grade sunglasses with side shields. Better still, get a reflective visor like the workers wear, and buy one locally if you can, since it shows you belong to the place's habits.
- High-SPF sunscreen, and don't forget under the chin, under the nose, and the insides of the ears. Wear lip balm with SPF.
- A brimmed hat under the visor if you're fair-skinned.
- Rest your eyes at dusk. The basin holds its light long, and the reflections at lamp-lighting are beautiful but dazzling.

## Cold and clothing

Days are cool, nights are cold, and the mist is damp. The workers wear pale wool, and you should too: layers of merino or similar, a windproof shell that doesn't rustle loudly, and insulated gloves. Choose quiet fabrics. Crinkly nylon sounds like a shout in this place. Gloves matter for another reason as well. The ridgelines are unweathered and their edges are still sharp, and the frost-grit is angular. Fingernail-deep seams sound charming until you cut a palm on one.

Wear light colors or neutral tones. Dark, heavy gear absorbs the sun and makes you conspicuous against the pale slab.

## Water

Water is the economy and the religion of the place, so treat every drop that way.

- Carry your own supply for at least the first days. Do not assume you can draw from the cisterns.
- Once you're settled, arrange your water through the workers. Offer to pay in labor, trade goods, or whatever they value. Scraping scale from condensers is a fair exchange.
- Bring a reusable bottle and a small filter, but drink only what you are given or have arranged. Do not dip into a cistern on your own, especially not in a soft place, where a cistern that's "full whenever you look and empty whenever you walk to it" may not contain what you think.
- Don't bathe casually. Ask what is acceptable.
- Never drain, touch, or wade into the still black pools. They were left undrained on purpose.

## Sound and the body's response to the engines

The engines run below hearing but not below feeling. You will feel them in your sternum and soles. Some travelers find this calming, and others find it produces low-grade dread, nausea, or difficulty sleeping. It is the same effect infrasound has in any world.

- Bring earplugs for sleep. They won't stop the pressure, but they help you sleep through the small sounds.
- Sleep away from the central works if you have a choice.
- If you feel anxious for no reason, check whether a piston is rising. Once you know the rhythm, it usually eases.
- Keep your own voice low. Normal speech seems loud, and people mostly don't speak. Adopt the quiet early, as it is politeness here.

## Contamination and etiquette

This ecosystem is only just habitable, and it is tended, not wild.

- **Quarantine your gear.** Clean boots, packs, and clothes of seeds, spores, insects, and soil before arrival. A single foreign weed seed or mite in a lichen colony could be catastrophic. No fresh food, no plants, no pets.
- **Lichen is sacred and recorded.** Workers chalk growth notes beside colonies. Don't touch, sit on, or lean against north-facing slab. Don't rub out chalk.
- Don't feed the animals. They are unafraid, so you'll be tempted, but they graze the lichen and the balance is delicate.
- Don't step on the moss beneath drips.
- Give the workers room. They walk the soft boundaries with rods, tapping. If you see someone doing this, stop, watch, and don't cross their line.
- Take nothing. Not a flake of frost-grit, not a piece of slab. This is a young country, and taking pieces of it may be taking pieces of its future.

## The soft places

This is the part that requires the most preparation.

**Basic policy: never enter one alone, and never enter one without a local who knows it.** The workers can feel softness the way you feel a missing step. You cannot, at least not at first. Hire, befriend, or be assigned a guide before you go anywhere beyond the main terraces. Ask directly: "Which ways are soft this season?" The answer changes.

Practical measures:

- **Do not rely on counting steps, GPS, pedometers, or compasses.** The count will differ each time and will never be wrong, which is to say it will be unreliable in an entirely trustworthy way. Navigate by landmarks, chalk marks, and your guide.
- **Bring a long rope or cord** and tie yourself to your guide or to a fixed anchor whenever you approach a soft boundary. Distance may stretch, so a physical tether is the one measure that doesn't depend on distance.
- **Use a tapping rod** like the workers do. Learn from them how to read the response.
- **Stairs:** if a staircase feels wrong, if the landing looks too much like the underside of something, stop and look back before continuing. It's best to turn around at the first sign. If you find yourself upside down, keep calm, keep a hand on the rail, and go back the way you came slowly, saying aloud where you are so your guide can follow.
- **Shadows:** if your shadow leans toward the sun, don't step further. Don't put your hand in the dark strip between two lit faces of a wall. It is cold as a well, and it may be as deep as one.
- **Water:** do not drink from anything in a soft place. Do not trust a cistern's level.
- **Time:** the description doesn't say whether time slips, but distance does, and I would treat time as suspect too. Carry a mechanical watch and a note of when you entered. Tell someone your expected return, and do not extend your stay inside a soft place.

## Copies

This deserves its own preparation, because it's the risk most particular to you.

The animals get copied. The workers accept it, and some think the country is still deciding what a goat is. Assume that you, too, could be copied or partly altered if you spend time in unset ground.

- Before you come, record your own distinguishing details: scars, moles, fingerprints, dental work, handedness, a private phrase or two known only to you and someone at home. A mirror is not enough, since reflections here are "at a slightly wrong angle."
- Agree on a verification ritual with your companions, something simple and not easily guessed. Use it whenever you leave a soft area.
- Bring nothing you cannot bear to see duplicated, such as keepsakes, medicine you need exactly one of, or documents. Duplicate keys and passports create a mess, and a duplicated anchor for your return route could strand you.
- If you meet someone who is identical to a companion, don't panic and don't accuse. Stay with your guide, and defer to local custom. The workers do not chase copies away, and you shouldn't try to either.
- If you notice a second shadow of your own, or an extra eye on something that wasn't there before, get out of the area immediately and report it to the workers. Do not wait to see if it fades.

## Arrival and return

The safest arrival point is a **long-cured, fully set area**, ideally somewhere the workers use as a recognized landing. Coming through into a soft place would be a poor start. If your method allows you to choose, aim for the outer ring of terraces, near the formwork-grained ridge edges, where the mineral is oldest and hardest.

Set your return anchor in the same place, and confirm regularly that it is still where you left it. Soft places advance and recede, and a stable terrace can turn soft after a long dry spell. Check your return route each day, and if the season seems to be shifting, leave early rather than late.

## Gear checklist

- Glacier glasses and a reflective visor
- Sunscreen and lip balm with SPF
- Merino layers, wool hat, windproof quiet shell
- Insulated, cut-resistant gloves
- Broken-in, high-traction boots
- Earplugs
- Water bottle, filter, and electrolytes
- Altitude medication if advised
- 30+ meters of light rope or cord, with a carabiner
- A mechanical watch, a paper notebook, and pencil (chalk if you can get it)
- A small first-aid kit with wound closure strips, since cuts from unweathered edges are likely
- A small lamp with a soft, warm light, as you may want your own in the dark, though be sparing with it
- Trade goods such as good salt, thread, needles, or clean wool. Ask before offering anything.
- A verification ritual and a personal-marks list (memorized, not just written down)

## Attitude

The strongest preparation is temperament. Go slowly. Speak quietly. Notice small things: the drip that has its own pitch, the way the goat's hoof lands on a grating. The people here are attentive by habit. They chalk lichen growth and listen to pressures they can't hear. If you meet that attentiveness, you will be a welcome guest, and you will see how the country grows: by an error being made and then, by staying, becoming correct.

Above all, remember that you are visiting something still being made, and you are also being written into it. Leave it a little better than you found it, and leave with all of yourself.
