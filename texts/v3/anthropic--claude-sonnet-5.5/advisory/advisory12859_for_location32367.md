# Preparing for Verrin Flat

## The Short Version

Verrin Flat won't attack you. It has no predators worth fearing, no violent weather, and few people. The danger is that it is comfortable-looking, dim, and quiet, and it will wear down a careless visitor with heat, salt, and lost bearings. Most of what goes wrong here goes wrong slowly. Prepare for a place that is warm, wet, corrosive, featureless, and indifferent.

## Heat: The Main Threat

The air sits near blood heat and is almost saturated. Sweat can't evaporate, so it just runs off you. Your body's main cooling method barely works. The overcast fools people, since the light is dim and shadowless and feels mild, but the heat load is real and constant, and it does not drop at night.

- **Expect to lose a lot of fluid.** Plan on 1 liter per hour of moving activity in the afternoon, and 6 to 8 liters per day even at modest effort. Bring more than you think you can carry, and cache water at your base.
- **Bring electrolytes, not just water.** Drinking plain water while sweating heavily in salt air is how people get hyponatremia. Carry oral rehydration salts and use them on a schedule.
- **Nothing local is drinkable.** The pans are brine, the channels are brackish and bacterial, and the condensation dripping in the works has run over rust and chemical residue. Do not treat any of it as a backup supply. Bring a sealed supply and a means of resealing it against humidity.
- **Learn the early signs:** headache, a sudden stop in sweating, unusual irritability, and goosebumps in the heat. Stop before you feel bad. Rest inside a pumping house, out of the light, with wet cloth on your neck and wrists.
- **Do your walking in the morning and in the hour after the afternoon storm.** The hours from noon to four are for sitting still. The mirage-shimmer of the stacks is your signal to stop.
- **Bring a cooling method that doesn't rely on evaporation:** cold packs are useless by the second day, so favor soaking a cloth in water and wearing it, and rest in shade with airflow.

## What to Wear

The environment is hard on fabric, skin, and feet.

- **Light, loose, long-sleeved clothing** in quick-drying synthetic or very thin cotton-blend material. Long sleeves and trousers protect against salt scald, flies, and abrasion. Nothing will actually dry in the air here, so bring at least three full changes and accept that you'll often put on damp clothes. Keep one set sealed and dry for sleeping.
- **Footwear is the most important choice you'll make.** The dried crust breaks into curled plates with sharp edges, the mud beneath is sucking and hot, and the works have rusted metal underfoot. You want closed, high-sided boots or shoes with a stiff sole that can get soaked and still drain. Gaiters help keep mud out. Sandals are a bad idea. Bare feet are worse. Bring two pairs so one can rest, even if neither dries.
- **Wash your feet and inspect them daily.** Salt and constant wetness cause maceration, fungal infection, and blisters that become slow-healing sores.
- **Gloves** for handling rails, ladders, and railings. Everything is coated in salt scale, rust, or sharp cone shells.
- **A brimmed hat and dark, wraparound eye protection.** The sky is dull, but the pans reflect a broad glare, and UV comes through the overcast. Sunburn here is deceptive because you don't feel it building.

## Skin, Wounds, and Infection

Warm brackish water full of bacteria is exactly the environment where small wounds turn dangerous.

- Cover every cut, scrape, and blister immediately. Clean it with fresh water, not pan water, and keep it dressed.
- Avoid wading in warm brackish water with any open wound. Certain marine bacteria can cause fast, serious infections in warm salty conditions.
- Salt scale will cut you in ways that don't seem serious. Treat all scratches as real.
- Carry a substantial first-aid kit with waterproof dressings, antiseptic, antibiotic ointment, blister care, antifungal powder, antihistamines, and oral antibiotics if you can get a clinician's guidance. Seal it in a dry bag.
- Bring barrier cream and use it for chafe. Damp skin wears through quickly.

## Air, Eyes, and Insects

- **Midges and brine flies.** At dusk the midge columns rise a hundred meters tall, and brine flies lift in waves at every step. They're not biting pests in the way mosquitoes are, but they get into eyes, nose, mouth, and ears. Carry a fine head net, sealed eye protection for dusk, and a scarf or buff to filter what you breathe. Keep your tent mesh fine enough to stop them.
- **Bring insect repellent anyway.** Even where the reports mention midges rather than biters, any warm wetland has other hazards, so plan for mosquitoes and biting flies until you know.
- **Guano dust and mold.** The gantries, towers, and roosts are coated in bird droppings, and the sweating buildings grow mold. Dry guano and moldy dust can carry fungal spores and respiratory pathogens. Wear a fitted respirator (P100 or equivalent) when you enter enclosed buildings or climb roosting structures. Don't sweep, scrape, or stir anything up.
- **Sulfur.** The black mud under the crust smells of hydrogen sulfide, and breaking through the crust releases it. In open air this is mostly unpleasant. In low pits, sumps, tank bases, and closed rooms, it can accumulate to dangerous levels. Do not enter enclosed low spaces without a gas monitor, and never alone.

## The Ground

- **Stay on the causeways and banks** whenever you can. The mud is warm and black beneath the crust, and it can be deeper than it looks. A crust that holds one step can break on the next. If you break through, spread your weight, lie back, and work your leg out slowly rather than pulling straight up.
- **Walk with a staff or pole** to probe the surface ahead.
- **Avoid the colored pans.** The pink, green, and orange mats are slick, the brine is corrosive, and stepping in them harms the ecology and you.
- **Watch where you step on the causeways.** The snails cover everything, and you'll crush some no matter what. Walk deliberately and don't tread on more than you must. Do not go off the rail beds to avoid them, since that puts you onto worse ground.

## Navigation and Tide

This is where an unprepared traveler is most at risk.

- **The horizon has no edge.** The sky and flat land merge, and there is no reliable sense of distance. Causeways run dead straight toward landmarks that disappear into the haze. A landmark that looks a few kilometers off may be ten.
- **The tide arrives as a thin silent sheet.** There is no wave, no roar, and no clear line. In the hour after the turn you cannot say where the coast is. A dry route can be ankle-deep, then knee-deep, before you notice, and channels that you crossed easily on the way out can fill behind you.
- **Get a tide table or learn the local pattern before you go anywhere.** If you cannot, then make your first two days observation only: stay near your arrival point, watch the water, and log high and low times. Two tides a day is regular enough to learn quickly.
- **Never leave for a distant works on a rising tide.** Start walks on the falling tide, and be back on solid ground or inside a building well before the turn.
- **Carry a good analog compass** and know your bearings. Electronic devices may fail in the humidity and salt, or may not work at all in a different reality. Bring spares of everything, and plan as if none of them work.
- **Mark your route.** Use bright flagging or stakes at intervals, but collect them on the way back. Note the shape of each pumping house and stack, because the landmarks are your fixed points.
- **Do not trust your sense of scale.** Time your walks and turn back at half your available daylight or tide window.

## Old Industry

The works are an unmaintained industrial site the size of a district.

- **Structural failure is the main risk.** Iron sashes, catwalks, stairs, and ladders are rotted by salt and sweat. Concrete is cracked and often undermined. Do not climb the cracking tower, tanks, or stacks. Do not trust any railing or grating without testing it, and never with your full weight until you've tested it.
- **Standing water in pumping houses reflects the windows in perfect squares.** That is beautiful, and it also hides floor depth and pits. Probe before every step.
- **Chemical residue.** Tanks and pipes may hold residues, and old chemical works are hazardous even when empty. Don't open valves, hatches, or drums. Don't touch sludge, and don't breathe fumes from a pipe. Assume asbestos in the lagging and roofing and don't disturb it.
- **Working machinery.** One pumping station in five or six is active. Diesels, wheels, sluice gates, and signals can move without warning. Stay clear of gates and channels near the main sluice, where the current can be strong when gates change. A signal changing at a dead crossing is harmless. A gate opening beside you is not.
- **Never enter buildings alone** or without telling someone your route and return time.

## Storms

- **The afternoon storm** builds around four and lasts about twenty minutes. The rain is warm, so hypothermia isn't an issue, but lightning is. On a flat plain you are the tallest thing. When the clouds drop, move away from stacks, towers, and open ground, and get inside a low building or crouch in a low spot, away from metal.
- **Rain will soak everything.** Waterproof bags for all gear, and store the important items (map, compass, medication, communication device) in sealed pouches.
- **The steam afterward** is thick and disorienting. Don't set off across the flat immediately.

## People

Somewhere in the region are a few dozen people who keep the gates and diesels running. They live in the tin-roofed compound near the main sluice and are almost never seen.

- **Don't count on them for rescue.** They may be helpful, wary, or absent. Plan to be self-sufficient.
- **If you meet them, be polite and unhurried.** They are not guards and you are trespassing on their work, in a sense. Don't photograph their compound, enter it uninvited, or interfere with the machinery.
- **Keep a low profile at night.** A lit tent in the dark is visible for kilometers. That's fine, but it's their home, so be considerate.
- **Bring something to offer** if you make contact, such as sealed food, medicine, or simple tools. Do not assume they want or need it.

## Animals

The wildlife is abundant and mostly harmless to you. You are a greater danger to it.

- **Do not flush the birds.** Shorebird flocks, flamingo drifts, and tern roosts feed on tight energy budgets. Walking through them wastes their reserves. Observe from a distance with binoculars and skirt the flocks. Approach slowly, and stop if they lift.
- **Crabs and mudskippers** are harmless, but step carefully. The crab bands are dense enough that you can't avoid them, so keep to the causeways.
- **Monitor lizards** are shy and will avoid you. Give them space, and never corner one.
- **Bats in pumping houses.** Avoid disturbing roosts. Their droppings and the dust in their roosts carry disease risk. Wear your respirator.
- **Do not feed anything, and do not take anything.**

## The Migration

Twice a year, on the highest spring tides, the crabs cross the causeways and climb the walls of the works, and for two nights the rail beds are impassable.

- **Find out the date** before you go, and either time your visit to it deliberately or avoid it entirely.
- **If you're there for it, camp on a raised bank or inside a building well in advance,** not on a causeway, and expect to be pinned in place. A night of crabs climbing everything is a spectacle, but you can't travel through it.
- **Bring food and water for at least three days** as a buffer.
- **Secure your tent.** Crabs will climb it. Ground sheets should be sealed at the edges, and boots should hang up off the ground.

## Camping and Sleep

- **The heat doesn't break at night.** Expect to sleep badly. A hammock or raised cot with airflow is better than a ground sheet. Use a bug-proof mesh shelter rather than a sealed tent.
- **Camp on a shell-and-silt bank or inside a dry, solid building,** above the high-tide line and away from the causeways.
- **Everything will feel damp.** Keep sleeping gear in a dry bag until you need it, and keep a set of clothes that never leaves it.
- **Don't camp in pumping houses with drips and mold,** unless you can find a dry, ventilated corner. Don't sleep near diesel exhaust.
- **Cook sparingly.** Bring food that needs no cooking or minimal heat, since heat is the last thing you want. Sealed, salty, calorie-dense foods do well. Everything not in a sealed container will attract flies and absorb moisture.

## Kit Summary

**Essential**
- Water in large quantity, plus oral rehydration salts
- Boots, gaiters, spare socks, gloves
- Long-sleeved, quick-drying clothing, hat, eye protection
- Respirator (P100), head net, buff
- Analog compass, tide table or logging notebook, route flagging, staff
- First aid kit, waterproof dressings, antifungal, antibiotic ointment
- Waterproof bags for everything
- Beacon or signal device, whistle, mirror, headlamp with spares
- Hydrogen sulfide/gas monitor if entering any enclosed space

**Strongly recommended**
- Binoculars
- Hammock or raised cot, mesh shelter
- A dry sleeping set
- Salt-resistant lubricant for zips and hinges
- Desiccant packs and rugged sealed cases for electronics
- Sunscreen and barrier cream
- Spare batteries, since heat and humidity drain and corrode them

## Multiverse-Specific Notes

- **Your arrival point matters.** If you have any control, arrive on a raised bank or inside a solid structure, not on a pan or causeway. A tide, storm, or crab migration can strand you.
- **Secure your return anchor.** If your exit depends on a fixed location, choose one above the tide line and away from crumbling structures, and mark it. Salt and damp will destroy anything that isn't sealed.
- **Assume your devices won't work as expected.** Bring analog tools for the essentials.
- **Set a strict schedule.** The place lulls you. Days blur under the pale light. Set alarms and check in with your own log so you leave when you planned.
- **Leave no trace.** Carry out all waste, all flagging, and all gear. The place is delicate even though it seems rugged.

## What It Will Feel Like

You'll arrive to warm damp air, a dull sky, and a hush that is not silence but a thousand tiny sounds. It will be strangely peaceful. Then you'll notice you're sweating without cooling, that the distances are lying to you, and that the horizon has no line. The trick is to slow down. Move in the morning, rest at midday, and let the place set the pace. If you respect the heat, the tide, and the ruins, you can spend days among the flamingos and rusted towers and come away with a sense of a landscape that has settled into itself and doesn't need you at all.
