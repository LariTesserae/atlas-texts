# Preparing for the Sounding Uplands

## The Core Idea

You are going somewhere you will never see. Every skill, piece of gear, and habit you bring should serve one aim: to be a competent listener and feeler in a place where sound is sight, warmth is the map, and silence is a warning. Most travelers fail in worlds like this by bringing their eyes and expecting them to work. Prepare to work without them.

## Training Before You Go

**Learn to be blind, on purpose.** Spend at least two or three weeks doing ordinary tasks in total darkness, not dimness. Use a proper blindfold or a lightless room, and include an unfamiliar outdoor place with someone watching over you. Aim for these skills:

- **Walking by ear and foot.** Sweep a cane or stick ahead of you, and learn to read texture, slope, and give underfoot.
- **Tongue-click echolocation.** It's learnable in a few weeks. Sharp clicks can tell you about walls, trunks, and drop-offs at close range. Practice softly. In the Uplands, your clicks will resemble the clickers, so stay near the low end of loudness.
- **Sound identification.** Learn to separate a point source from a diffuse one, near from far, and moving from fixed.
- **Staying calm when unseen things touch you.** The curtains are warm with breath, and eel-like fliers will cluster around you and follow. Get used to not flinching.

**Prepare for the altitude.** The plateau is high. Spend several days at elevation first, or use a proper acclimatization schedule, and expect fatigue to hit harder because of the heat and humidity.

**Prepare your ears.** Hearing is your primary sense, so have an audiologist check it beforehand. Treat any ear infection or wax problem before departure.

**Prepare your mind.** Total darkness is not restful. Expect sensory overload from constant sound, loss of day and night, and the disorientation of having nothing to look at. Travel with a partner or team, and agree beforehand on how you'll tell someone "I'm not okay."

## Dress and Body

The air is warm and wet, the water is body-temperature, and the risks are overheating and constant dampness, not cold.

- **Clothing:** Light, quick-drying synthetics or merino, in long sleeves and long trousers. They protect your skin from cold rope-vines, abrasive moss, and spores. Bring a shell for the warm downpours, but expect to be wet anyway.
- **Ridge layer:** The ridges are exposed and cold, so carry a warm insulating layer in a waterproof bag. Wet plus wind at altitude can cause hypothermia even in a warm-feeling world.
- **Footwear:** High, sturdy, waterproof boots with ankle support and aggressive tread, plus gaiters. The mud will try to keep your boots, so lace them tight. Bring several pairs of socks and change them often. Feet that stay wet and warm are prone to fungal infections and maceration. Dry them at every rest, and bring powder and antifungal cream.
- **Gloves:** Thin, grippy, and durable, for wet rope-vine, rusted iron, and rough rock.
- **Head and ears:** A brimmed hat sheds the drip. Choose ear protection carefully (see below).

## Breathing

- **Respirator:** A half-mask with P100 particulate filters and acid gas/organic vapor cartridges. The fungal shelves exhale spores, and the geothermal seams may carry hydrogen sulfide and other gases. The warm-bread smell is not an invitation. Treat every spore as unknown.
- **Multi-gas monitor:** Carry a personal detector for H₂S, CO, CO₂, and oxygen, with a vibrating alarm you can feel and an earpiece-free audible one you can also hear. Heavy gases pool in sinks, caves, and roofless station basements. If the monitor alarms or you feel sudden headache, giddiness, or sleepiness, climb out of the depression immediately.
- **Avoid the smell trap:** Hydrogen sulfide at high concentration deadens your sense of smell. Don't trust your nose to warn you.

## Water, Food, and Health

- **Drinking water:** Assume all local water is off-limits. It's warm, mineral-rich, and possibly full of dissolved metals, sulfur compounds, and unknown organisms. Carry your own supply, or bring a serious purification system (filter plus chemical or UV treatment) and a way to test for heavy metals. Boiling isn't enough.
- **Food:** Bring all of it, sealed in mold-proof, waterproof containers. Don't eat local plants, fungi, or animals. Nothing here is adapted to you, and you can't see what you're picking.
- **Medical kit:** Add to the standard kit broad-spectrum antibiotics, antifungals, antiparasitics, rehydration salts, anti-nausea medication, burn dressings (for steam), ear drops (for infections and water in the ear), and a decent trauma kit. Bring more than you expect to use, and know how to use it all by touch.
- **Heat management:** Humid heat prevents your sweat from evaporating. Drink often, take electrolytes, and rest in the shade of the rock overhangs rather than pushing on.
- **Vaccinations and prophylaxis:** Get the full set your home world recommends, and assume the local microbiology is unknown.

## Gear

**Rule one: waterproof everything.** Use dry bags, sealed cases, and silica gel. Sulfur and iron-laden mist corrode metal, so prefer stainless steel, titanium, and plastic. Pack a small maintenance kit.

**Listening and sensing tools:**
- **Bone-conduction headset** or **open-ear earbuds**, so your ears stay open to the environment.
- **Hearing protection that filters** rather than blocks: musician's plugs or electronic ear-pro that compresses loud sound but keeps the world audible. The rain is the loudest thing in the Uplands. Use it during downpours, and take it out when things settle.
- **Bat detector (heterodyne or time-expansion):** Lets you hear the clickers' ultrasonic calls and get a sense of how many are around you.
- **Contact microphone or small geophone**, plus an **infrasound-capable sensor or vibration meter:** These let you read the ground, so you can detect drifter herds approaching before you feel them in your chest.
- **Thermal camera or thermal monocular:** Heat is the map here, and thermal imaging works in total darkness. Fog and steam will limit it, but it's the single most useful "vision" tool you can bring.
- **Handheld thermometer** with a probe, for reading seeps and vent temperatures without touching them.

**Navigation:**
- **Compass and barometric altimeter,** with a note that magnetic readings may be unreliable near basalt or iron structures.
- **Pace counter/dead-reckoning log.** GPS won't work and radio may be unreliable under the cloud and in the steam.
- **Reflective tags and tactile markers:** Notched cord, knotted rope, and tactile flagging tape to mark your route by touch. Use a spool of guide line (thin and strong) for anything risky.
- **A tactile watch and a clock:** You have no sun. Keep a schedule, and log time so your sleeping and eating stay regular.
- **Sound landmarks:** The operating stations hum and clank on steady schedules, and they're the closest thing to fixed beacons. Record and describe each one as you pass. The eastern basin station, with its steady whine, is the tuning fork of a whole ecosystem. Use it as an orientation landmark, but never interfere with it.

**A light:** Bring a small, dim, preferably red one, and use it rarely and briefly. The local rule is "what glows is dead." Most creatures avoid glowing decay, which may make light a useful deterrent. But it may also draw whatever feeds on decay, and if you're the glowing thing, that could be you. Treat light as an emergency tool and test its effect cautiously, at low intensity, in a safe place.

**Other essentials:**
- **A long probing pole or staff:** Test every step. The ground can swallow your leg, and silk lines are invisible.
- **Mud boards or a flat pack of lightweight sheeting** to spread your weight if you sink.
- **A stove and fuel:** Stay away from flame near vents, where flammable gases may accumulate. Use a sealed, low-risk stove and check gas levels first.
- **Emergency signaling:** A whistle for close calls, and a way to signal your return point (see below).
- **A hammock or raised sleeping platform:** Camp off the wet ground.

## Your Sound Discipline

The Uplands runs on sound, so how you sound matters.

- **Silence is dangerous.** In the ecosystem's grammar, a silent place is where something hunts, and a note that drops out of the chorus draws predators. If you go quiet, you may register the same way. Make ordinary, low, steady noises as you move: a rhythmic staff tap, quiet footfalls, a soft hum. Don't try to sneak.
- **Don't break the chord.** If the bell-frog chorus around you is intact, you're fine. If a note drops out nearby, stop, hold still, and listen. Then move away from that spot quietly.
- **Silence your devices.** Turn off every beep, alarm, and notification. A new, steady tone might be picked up by a frog population that tunes itself to engines. Use vibration alerts. Muffle jingling gear.
- **No engines, generators, or amplified sound.** Do not add a new pitch to the landscape.
- **Never imitate infrasound or drifter calls,** and don't try to make sounds you don't understand.
- **Group communication:** Use tug signals on a tether line, tap codes on a stick, and short, soft verbal cues. Agree on a "stop and freeze" signal, since freezing may be your most important move.

## Arrival Protocol

You arrive by hearing, so let it work.

1. **Stand still for a full ten minutes** at your arrival point. Breathe through your respirator. Let the sound picture form.
2. **Do a single soft echo survey:** One small click or a light tap of your staff. Listen to the returns to gauge nearby space. Don't clap or shout.
3. **Read the ground** with your soles and a hand on stone. Note any rhythm or tremble.
4. **Check your gas monitor** and your thermal view. Look for hot or cold gradients.
5. **Establish your anchor.** Mark the arrival point clearly by touch, and by sound if you can. Whatever your method of return, it must be findable in total darkness. Set up a return beacon that makes a quiet, steady, natural-sounding tick, tuned to blend into the ecosystem.
6. **Take your first route from the anchor** along a guide line, and make your first outing short.

## Moving Through the Land

**Follow the warmth, but carefully.** Heat leads to water, food, and machines. It also leads to scalding seeps, gas pockets, and the animals that live around them. Move toward warm ground, not into it.

**Marsh:** The channels are a hand's depth of warm water over deeper mud. Test each step with the staff. If you sink, don't struggle. Lie back, spread your weight, and work out slowly. Don't cross a marsh when a drifter herd is nearby. Watch for water trembling in rings, a sign of infrasound, and listen for the tearing sound of grazing.

**Drifters:** The size of buildings, and mostly harmless unless you're in the way. Their infrasound can cause nausea, disorientation, or a sense of dread. If you feel a pressure in your chest and your ribs, don't assume it's fear. It's them. Move to higher, firmer ground, away from their path.

**Lattice-spiders:** Silk strung between flute-trees over hundreds of feet, sensitive to vibration, and completely invisible to you. Always probe ahead with your staff, and take time to feel the air for a faint pull on your sleeve. If you touch silk, freeze, and back away slowly along the path you came. Rain masks your vibrations, but it also masks the sounds you need to hear. Choose carefully whether to move in the downpour.

**The eel-like air-swimmers:** They'll cluster around your body warmth and follow you. They're mostly harmless, but they signal something useful: they sense the electric halo of living things and can crowd around you in the thick fog. Don't swat at them. If they suddenly scatter, treat that as a warning.

**Stations:**
- Many are ruined, roofless, and vine-wrapped. Floors may be rotten, boilers may be holed, and basements may be full of gas.
- Live stations are high-pressure steam systems. Steam is invisible in the dark, and a leak can scald you before you notice. Approach with a gloved hand extended, feeling for heat. Never stand in line with a vent.
- Roosting clickers mean guano and fungal spores. Wear your respirator inside.
- **Don't operate, repair, or dismantle anything,** including valves, pumps, and belts. The animals have built their lives around these machines, and the frogs' whole chorus may depend on one station's pitch.
- They're warm and dry-ish, so they're tempting camps. Consider sleeping near one but not in one, and never in a place where you can't quickly get out.

**Ridges and lightning:** Electrical storms build on the highest ridges, and lightning strikes silently and unseen. You'll smell ozone and hear a flat crack. Avoid the highest ground and exposed metal (including the old rails and cables) during storms. If your hair rises or your skin tingles, get low and away from tall objects.

## Camping and Rest

- **Where:** On a firm terrace above the marsh basins (which may flood in the rain) and below the ridges (which attract lightning). Avoid sinks, where gas collects. Avoid drifter paths, which you can identify by wide trampled channels and the feel of the ground.
- **Setup:** A raised platform or a tent with a good floor. Ground sleep will be damp and warm and may carry vibrations. Put a geophone or vibration alarm at the edge of camp.
- **Sound:** Do not make camp silent. A small, steady, low-key sound (a wind-up ticker or a quiet music box) helps you blend in. Keep it modest and pitched in the range of the ambient chorus.
- **Shifts:** Keep watch in shifts, so someone is always awake and listening.
- **Sleep:** Expect your body clock to drift. Use melatonin sparingly, keep to a fixed schedule, and rest even if you don't feel tired.

## Ethics

You are a guest in an ecosystem that is fragile in ways you can't see. Leave no persistent sound, no lights, no litter, and no food. Take nothing living. Don't move fungi, and don't feed anything. Don't touch the machines. Whatever key the eastern station provides, you don't know what else you might be tuning.

## Leaving

Plan your exit before you arrive. Know how your return works, where it is, and how you'll find it in the dark. Set a firm turnaround time, not "when we feel like it." On the way out, retrace your guide line, collect your markers, and give the landscape one last long listen. If you've done it right, the chorus will not have noticed you at all.

## The Mindset

The Uplands is not hostile, but it is indifferent and overwhelming. The best preparation is humility and patience. Stand still more than you think you should. Listen longer than feels comfortable. Let the place teach you its rhythms, and remember that you're the one who doesn't belong. It's a world that has learned to see with everything except light. The trick is to learn that too.
