# Preparing to Visit the Sink at Vellum Row

## What you are walking into

This place is a hot, humid pit with unstable walls and a working industrial town bolted inside it. The dangers are ordinary and physical: heat, falling rock, bad air, and long drops. Nothing here is hostile, but nothing is forgiving either. The town is built around small habits that keep it alive, and a visitor survives by learning those habits quickly and quietly.

Plan for a short, disciplined visit. Think of it as a mine or a cave system that happens to have a village in it.

## Arrival and exit

- **Try to arrive at or near the rim.** If your method lets you choose, do so. Descending gradually lets you judge the heat and air as you go. If you might arrive at the bottom, prepare for the worst case: thick, hot, dim air near the fissure.
- **Fix your return before you descend.** Whatever brings you home, whether an anchor, a beacon, or a timed recall, should work from the *rim*. Do not depend on anything that must be activated from the pit floor. Have a fallback that works in high heat and humidity.
- **Set a hard time limit.** A day or two is reasonable. Heat exhaustion is cumulative, and the rock falls the same on day three as on day one.

## Heat and body management

The heat comes up through the floor and lower walls, and it worsens with depth. Metal is never quite cool, and the air is humid enough that sweat barely evaporates.

- **Water:** Bring all the water you will need, plus a generous reserve. Do not count on the town for any. Its water is the product of a fragile cycle, and every cistern is spoken for. Taking it would be theft in the plainest sense. The rain is warm, chalky, and unfiltered, and the standing pools are mineral-laden and fenced for a reason. Treat all local water as undrinkable.
- **Electrolytes:** You will sweat heavily. Carry oral rehydration salts and use them on a schedule, not when you feel thirsty.
- **Cooling:** A wet neck cloth or evaporative bandana helps a little, and a phase-change cooling vest helps more. Expect them to be overwhelmed in the lower levels.
- **Know the warning signs:** headache, cramping, goosebumps in the heat, confusion, a sudden stop in sweating. If any appear, climb toward the rim, drink, and rest in shade. Do not push on.
- **Pace yourself:** Move slowly, rest often, and do the strenuous climbing early. Stay in the upper levels if you can. Only descend to the floor if you have a real reason, and then briefly.

## Clothing and gear

**Wear:**
- Loose, light, long-sleeved layers in cotton, linen, or wool. Avoid synthetics that could melt against hot metal.
- **Hard hat.** This is not optional. Hand-sized flakes let go from the rim every few minutes, and they fall four storeys.
- **Sturdy boots with thick, heat-resistant soles.** Walkways are perforated plate that glows orange from below. Avoid narrow heels and thin soles, which catch in the holes and pass heat straight through.
- **Heat-resistant gloves.** The locals wrap their grips in cloth, and you should not rely on that. Rails, ladders, and handles are feverish, and in the sun-bar they can burn.
- **Eye protection.** Fine grit trickles constantly.

**Carry:**
- **A P100 or equivalent respirator with acid-gas and particulate filters.** Dust from fresh limestone, gypsum, and salt is irritating, and the pipes are wrapped in cloth that looks like asbestos. Assume it is asbestos and do not handle it or disturb it.
- **A multi-gas detector** covering CO₂, H₂S, and oxygen. This is the most important item on the list. A pit above a volcanic chamber is exactly where heavy gases pool, and the floor and vents are the likeliest places. Bring spare batteries and a low-tech backup, such as colorimetric detector tubes. Do not bring an animal as a "canary." There are none here, and you have no business introducing one.
- **Reliable lighting.** The light is thin and tea-colored. Carry a headlamp and a spare, and keep them on a lanyard.
- **A climbing harness, two lanyards, and a short length of rope.** Every level has a second way out, and every bridge has a rope beside it. Clip in on exposed sections and use those ropes for what they are.
- **A mechanical watch.** The only natural clock is the bar of sunlight crossing the roofs at midday, which is not much to go on.
- **Basic first aid,** with burn dressings, cooling gel, and eyewash prioritized.

**Leave behind:**
- Open flame, lighters, and anything that sparks. The engines' working fluid may be an alcohol, and leaks would mean flammable vapor in hot, close air. Use LED light only.
- Anything you cannot afford to have condensed on, corroded, or cooked. Walls sweat, salts bloom, and metal is hot.

## Moving through the town

- **Learn the vertical geography first.** The real avenues are stairs and cable-lifts, not streets. Stairs zigzag, cross, and bridge to each other. Note each twin route as you pass it, so you always know your second way out.
- **Use the cable cars carefully.** They are small, counterweighted, and ratcheting. Board only if you are sure of the direction, and don't overload them. If you are unsure of a lift, take the stairs.
- **Listen constantly.** A trickle of grit is your warning. A sharper crack from the rim means a slab has shifted, so move away from overhangs and out from under the wall. Do not stop to look up.
- **Don't shelter or rest beneath overhangs,** however shady. The newly exposed yellow-white undersides mark places where rock has recently come off.
- **Watch your footing on the gratings.** Slots glow orange, and you can see the heat below you. Step deliberately.

## The town's rules, which become yours

These are the habits written into the construction. Follow them as if they were law.

1. **Keep nothing in the sun-bar.** When the light crosses the roofs at midday, exposed metal becomes too hot to touch. Cover your gear, or better, move it and yourself into shade before it arrives. Don't leave a metal canteen, a tool, or a bag on a sunlit roof.
2. **Never lean on the lower walls.** The rock there is warm and slightly soft, and some has begun to slump. Don't rest against it, hang gear from it, or put a hand into cracks. Keep your weight on the pins and plate.
3. **Always know your second way out.** Do not use a route that has no twin, and don't be the reason one is blocked.
4. **Never interfere with the condensers.** Don't touch the racks, block the drip-lips or mesh screens, or divert a gutter. If the condensers dry, the water stops and then the engines stop. Treat the whole water system as life support, because it is.

Add a few rules of your own: don't touch the engines or the shafts under their hoods, don't lean over the fissure, don't step near the vents, and stay several paces from the floor's crack. Nothing stands near its edge except the pipes.

## The fissure and the floor

Go down only if you must, and treat it as a controlled excursion.

- Check your gas detector at every landing, not just at the bottom.
- Wear the respirator. Air near the floor is thick, mineral, and tastes of warm iron.
- Limit time to minutes. The heat is far higher, and the lower walls are the soft ones.
- Go with a plan, a light, and a clear route back up. If the detector alarms, leave at once and don't investigate.
- Never approach the crack. It exhales steam and glows, and there is no reason to stand close.

## Meeting the people

The lanes are quiet. You will see work being done and things carried up the lifts, but few voices and few faces. Whether the residents are unfriendly, shy, absent, or something else is unknown, so don't assume.

- **Be unobtrusive and courteous.** Keep out of workshops and sheds unless you are clearly invited. Don't pick things up or rearrange them.
- **Don't try to follow the goods.** Baskets of copper, tile, and wire go up to the rim and vanish. Whatever happens to them is not your affair.
- **If a bell sounds once, low and dull, just stay put and listen.** You don't know its meaning, and nothing seems to follow it. Don't respond.
- **Offer no goods, ask for none, and leave nothing behind.** Take out everything you brought, including waste. There are no animals to clear it, and the town keeps a fine balance.
- If someone approaches, be calm and slow, and show open hands. Ask nothing that requires water.

## The plants

Treat the wall mats, the vent ferns, the gutter moss, and the lone pale tree as protected. They live on drip-lines and steam, and they cannot survive being trampled, scraped, or dried. Don't touch the fern fronds when they've curled. Don't taste, sample, or collect anything.

## Mind and mood

The place is dim, brownish, silent of small life, and it thrums in your feet. Some visitors find it calming. Others get uneasy fast: no birdsong, no insects, no rustle, just ticks and drips and the distant ratchet of a lift. Vertical disorientation is common. The rim is a small pale ellipse a long way up, and the light shifts slowly.

Tell yourself these things in advance:
- The silence is normal.
- The thrum is the engines, not something wrong.
- If you feel dizzy, the cause is more likely heat and dehydration than anything mysterious.
- If you feel anxious or slow, that's a reason to climb, not to press on.

## Final checklist

- Return method works from the rim, with a fallback
- Water and electrolytes for the whole stay, plus reserve
- Hard hat, eye protection, heat-resistant gloves and boots
- P100/acid-gas respirator
- Multi-gas detector, spare batteries, backup tubes
- Harness, lanyards, short rope
- Headlamp with a spare, mechanical watch
- Burn and heat-illness first aid
- No flame, no sparks, no animals
- Rules memorized: sun-bar, lower walls, second exits, condensers
- Time limit set and a plan to climb out early

The town has lasted by staying attentive to a place that is still falling apart. Bring the same attention and leave before it costs you anything.
