# Preparing for the Sweat Terrace

## The first thing to understand

The terrace looks gentle, and much of it is. But it is a warm, saturated, chemically active shelf on a young volcano, and it is held together by something that pays attention. Your preparations should cover two things: the ordinary hazards of heat, wet, height and fresh volcanic rock, and the etiquette of visiting a place that is quietly running a closed system.

## Body: heat without cooling

The air is near blood heat and saturated. Your sweat cannot evaporate, so your main cooling mechanism is mostly gone. This is the most likely thing to hurt you.

- **Expect heat illness, not just discomfort.** Rest often, move slowly, and learn the early signs: headache, flushed skin, irritability, a sudden loss of sweating pattern, nausea. Carry a cooling towel and a small electrolyte supply. If your core temperature climbs, the house is your refuge.
- **Bring your own water and plenty of it.** Assume you will drink more than you do on a hot desert day, but in smaller, more frequent amounts. Add electrolytes.
- **Do not count on the pans.** They are clear and bath-warm, which is exactly what you want in a culture dish. Warm standing water at altitude near volcanic vents can carry amoebae, *Legionella*, and dissolved minerals or gases you cannot see. Don't drink it, don't get it in your nose, don't dunk your head, and don't breathe the rising mist more than you have to. A simple filter mask for the dawn fog is sensible.
- **The rosette cups are off-limits.** Amber, jade, sour blue: nobody knows why they differ, so assume the differences are chemical and not friendly.

## Body: altitude

At 3,500 meters you should feel thin air and cold. The terrace tells you it feels neither, which is exactly why people get caught out. Prepare as though the oxygen is what the altitude says it is, even if the air feels thick.

- Acclimatize before arriving if your route allows. Otherwise, go slowly, stay hydrated, and avoid alcohol.
- Carry a pulse oximeter and a small emergency oxygen supply. If your fingertip reading drops or you develop a pounding headache, confusion, or breathlessness at rest, leave.
- Pack the medications your doctor would recommend for altitude, and know who is waiting for you and when.

## Body: wet, all the time

Everything you own will be damp within an hour.

- **Clothing:** light, loose, quick-drying, long-sleeved for the fronds and sundews, with no cotton. Bring a full dry set to leave in the house.
- **Feet:** the moss is a sponge, so your footprint fills with water. Wear drainable, grippy, closed-toe shoes with quick-dry socks, and check your feet daily for maceration and fungal irritation. Talcum or barrier balm helps.
- **Gear:** dry bags, silicone gaskets, and desiccant for anything electronic. Assume a phone will fog inside and out. A notebook should be waterproof paper and pencil.
- **Skin:** wash and dry your skin in the house when possible. Warm wet air promotes rashes and infections quickly.

## Body: the ground and the edge

- **The rock is new.** Basalt columns have edges you can shave with, and the obsidian seams are literal glass. Wear cut-resistant gloves, sturdy trousers, and eye protection when moving near the back wall. A small cut here will be constantly wet and slow to close.
- **The lip ends in cloud.** You may not see where the shelf stops, and the ground near it is spongy. Move on a tether or clipped line if you go near it, and probe with a pole. Never walk toward the edge in fog or in the afternoon cloud.
- **The ground is water-supported and volcanic.** Bring a handheld gas monitor (CO₂, H₂S, SO₂). Heavy gases can pool in hollows and pans, especially at night when the air is stagnant. If the monitor alarms, or you feel sudden drowsiness or headache, climb, don't sit.
- **Tremors and the mountain:** you are on shoulder rock that is still cooling. Know your evacuation route and don't camp at the base of the back wall, where broken column tops can fall.

## Planning around the daily turn

The terrace has a timetable. Use it.

- **Dawn:** thin fog rises from the pans. Visibility is poor and the rain-keys nearly vanish. Good for arriving quietly and orienting. Wear the mask.
- **Midday:** the heat peak. Do no hard work. Rest in the house or in fern shade, and expect the glass to crackle and the sundews to open fully.
- **Afternoon:** water rises from the pools in threads, the flat cloud forms, the rain-keys begin to hum in your teeth, and then it pours for about twenty minutes. Be in the house or under your shelter before the cloud lowers. It is disorienting in there, with a lid of cloud and vertical water, and you may lose your bearings. Anything left out will be soaked. Note that the hum is felt in the body, and some travelers get nausea or a pressure headache from it. Earplugs may not help, but a dry seat and a slow breath do.
- **Night:** the glass seams glow dull red at the edges, or seem to. Do not treat the glow as light to navigate by. Carry a red-filtered headlamp, since white light will ruin your night vision and startle the frogs. Watch your gas readings.

Plan to stay through at least one full cycle so you understand the rhythm, but don't plan to stay many. The terrace is easy to slip into.

## Etiquette: what you are visiting

The terrace is a **kept** thing. Its water is circulated, not spent, and the plants, frogs, and rain-keys all seem to take part in that. Treat yourself as a temporary guest in the system.

- **Take nothing out that holds water.** No bottles of pan water, no moss, no rosette cups, no cut fronds, no glass shards as souvenirs. The obsidian's edges and freshness make it tempting. Resist.
- **Bring nothing in that you cannot carry out.** No litter, no soap, no sunscreen washed into pans, no dropped food. Even a small chemical trace in a closed circulation may return to you by afternoon. Use pack-out waste bags for everything, including human waste. Your own water will circulate here whether you like it or not, but keep the contribution minimal.
- **Leave the rain-keys alone.** They're not obstacles or tools. Touching one gently to feel the pulse is probably tolerated, but do not lean on them, mark them, attach gear to them, or try to move or study the fallen one. Respect the moss ring around it: don't step in the margin the plants leave.
- **Sundews:** stay an arm's length beyond an arm's length. They turn toward movement and their dew is sticky and possibly digestive. Keep sleeves, cords, and hair clear. Don't feed them.
- **Frogs and the gray bird:** don't handle the frogs, since translucent skin usually means toxins or extreme sensitivity. If the bird walks through, give it the whole path and don't stare at it or follow its gaze.

## The house

The half-egg dwelling is the only dry, cool place and will feel like relief, but it is not yours.

- **Arrive as a guest.** Pause at the oval door, announce yourself, and step in slowly. Leave the door plate exactly as you found it (slid aside).
- **Don't be alarmed by the prints.** The floor remembers feet, and older, narrow, bare prints will rise faintly and sink again. They don't seem to be threatening. Don't try to step in them, follow them, or match their pace.
- **The bowls:** three shallow bowls, always half full, are almost certainly a hospitality gesture or an instrument, and either way they are the terrace's water. Test the first day's rules on caution: don't drink from them until you've watched them through a full cycle, and if you do accept, take a single small sip with thanks, not a fill. Don't pour them out or try to prove they refill.
- **Use it, don't move in.** Rest, dry your gear, and cool down. Don't rearrange anything. Sleep on the bench-shape or the floor near the door so you can leave easily.

## Multiverse logistics

- **Anchor and exit:** humidity can wreck a fragile return device, so use one that's sealed and tested in steam. Set a fixed return window before you arrive. Also carry a secondary method that doesn't depend on electronics.
- **Interference:** the rain-keys pulse slowly and something about them seems large and deep. Test your instruments, compasses, and timing devices away from them and compare readings. Don't be surprised by drift.
- **Timekeeping:** use a mechanical or sealed watch and a written log. The daily cycle is a good clock, but the terrace's quiet can make hours blur.
- **Tell someone:** leave your itinerary, the terrace description, and your intended exit time with a contact in your home reality.

## The mind

The terrace's silence is textured and seems to listen. That's not necessarily hostile, but it can make you feel observed, sleepy, or strangely calm. Nothing here is asking you to stay, but the idea that you are a shape the water is temporarily taking is easy to believe after a day in the wet heat.

- Keep routines: eat on schedule, log your readings, check your body temperature.
- Bring a companion if you can, and check each other for confusion or unusual contentment.
- If you catch yourself not wanting to leave, not sweating, or sleeping in the pans, that is your signal to go, at once and without ceremony.

## A minimal kit

- Sealed multiverse return device plus a non-electronic backup
- Pulse oximeter, small oxygen bottle, altitude medication
- Gas monitor (CO₂, H₂S, SO₂)
- Personal water and electrolytes, plus a filter and purification tablets
- Filter mask, cut-resistant gloves, eye protection
- Quick-dry long sleeves and trousers, drainable shoes, spare dry socks and clothes
- Dry bags, desiccant, waterproof notebook and pencil
- Rope, harness, and probing pole for the lip
- Cooling towel, first aid kit with antifungal and wound care
- Pack-out waste bags
- Red headlamp

## The short version

Go slowly, stay cool, carry your own water, and respect the edge. Be dry in the house before the afternoon cloud comes down. Take nothing that holds water and leave nothing that dissolves. Don't disturb the needles, and don't stay so long that you start to feel like part of the circulation.
