# Preparing to Visit the Boiler Vug

## The honest summary

This is not a place you visit so much as a place you survive briefly, the way you might survive inside a working kiln. The animals are not especially hostile. The environment is what will kill you: bread-oven heat, toxic gas, crushing noise, and a floor with no free space on it. Everything below assumes you go in fully sealed, on a short clock, and that you treat the chamber as a machine you must not jam.

## Getting in

There is no door. The surface crack is a forearm wide, so walking in is out.

- **Do not drill or blast a shaft.** The hammerers keep the crust cracked so gas can vent, and the chamber is a pressure-balanced system. A big new opening could change its ventilation, flash-cool part of it, or cause a collapse onto thousands of animals and you.
- **Portal in, but scout first.** Lower a slim probe through the crack: fiber-optic camera, thermometer, gas sampler, and a contact microphone, all ceramic- or metal-sheathed. It will cook within minutes, so plan for a one-way, disposable probe. Use it to find a cooler edge ledge with a little clear floor.
- **Anchor at the periphery.** The cool edges are where the young and old are pushed, so they are crowded but not violent. Do not anchor near the seam, where the large smelters hold territory and the air is hottest. Avoid arriving in the middle, where you may materialize inside a body or under a cluster of hammerers.
- **Keep the return anchor separate and protected.** Bring a heat-shielded, ideally self-cooling extraction beacon, and place it somewhere nothing will pile onto it. Tenders wedge loose metal into crevices, and an unattended device may be treated as building material.

## Surviving the heat

The air is about 200 °C, and the rock and animals are hotter. Ordinary hot-environment gear will not do.

- **Wear a fully enclosed, actively cooled suit.** Think firefighter proximity gear layered over a refrigerated liquid-cooling garment, not a mountaineer's kit. Phase-change ice vests alone will last minutes, not hours. Carry a heat-rejection pack that can dump heat into 200 °C air, which is hard because the environment is hotter than your radiator. Build in a large thermal reservoir, and plan around its capacity.
- **Set a hard time limit.** Assume the total safe time is the reservoir's capacity minus a generous margin for the trip out, not "until it feels warm." By the time you feel it, the margin is gone. Set alarms on core temperature, not comfort.
- **Insulate your feet and hands most heavily.** You will be touching stone and animals. Use thick, layered soles with an air gap and reflective layers, and gloves that can hold a briefly hot grip. Their blood is hot enough to sting bare skin, so a bite or a burst carapace will spray something that burns through cloth.
- **Avoid materials that soften or off-gas at 200+ °C.** That means most rubbers, PVC, standard plastics, and adhesives. Use silicone, PTFE, aramid, ceramic-fiber, and metal. Check every seal and every camera casing.
- **Radiant heat is a separate problem.** Near the seam the rock is glowing, and reflective outer layers and a face shield with a gold or metallic coating matter. Even so, don't approach it.

## Breathing

The atmosphere is sulfur dioxide, metallic vapor, and almost no moisture.

- **Use a fully closed breathing loop.** Sealed, self-contained, with a scrubber and oxygen supply. Do not rely on filters. The ambient air is not something to filter. It is something to keep out.
- **Supply your own cool air.** Air hot enough to scald the lungs comes through any intake, so breathe from a cooled reservoir.
- **Protect against fine metal vapor.** Wash the outer suit thoroughly on exit and treat it as contaminated with heavy-metal residues. The dry heat also makes for static and dust hazards, so ground your equipment where possible.
- **Dry conditions mean fast dehydration.** Your internal loop must handle sweat management, and you should hydrate heavily before entry.

## Light, sight, and sound

- **Expect near-darkness.** The only light is the dull cherry glow of the seam and sparks from the stokers' exhalations. You will see edges, movement, and silhouettes, almost no color.
- **Skip bright lights.** They may disturb the animals and they wash out your own night vision, which you need. Use a dim, low-intensity source if any, and let your eyes adapt for twenty minutes beforehand.
- **A thermal camera will saturate.** Everything is hot. It can still show relative gradients, such as which ledges are cooler and where vents are, so bring one with a wide dynamic range.
- **Hearing protection is essential.** The noise is a continuous roar with periodic clanging swells that you feel in your ribs. Use good isolating protection with an inner layer, since suit helmets transmit vibration. Prolonged exposure without it risks permanent damage.
- **Do not plan on talking.** Speech is impossible. Agree on hand signals, a tap code against the suit, or a wired link before you enter. Radios are likely to be swamped by noise, so use haptic or visual alerts.

## Understanding the animals

- **Smelters** are dog-sized, armored, and busy. They are not predators. But they are heavy, hot, and crowded, and they will shove. Don't stand between a smelter and a food crust or the seam.
- **Tenders** are scuttling scavengers that gather metal. Anything you drop or leave that looks like metal is theirs. Secure every tool with a lanyard, and never put equipment down.
- **Hammerers** cover the ceiling. Their rhythm can lock into a shuddering clang. Don't strike the ceiling or walls, since you may interrupt or mimic their signals. Expect debris and dropped bits from above, so wear a rigid, well-padded helmet.
- **Stokers** cluster at the seam. Sparks fly from their exhalations. Stay far back.
- **Communicate by contact, not sound.** They speak through vibration in the stone and each other's bodies. You can observe this by placing a contact sensor, or an armored palm, against the rock. Do not attempt to signal deliberately without understanding the pattern. A heavy footfall can be read as something.

## Moving in the chamber

- **Assume the floor is occupied.** Move slowly and deliberately, and don't step until you can see the surface. Crushing an animal is both harmful and likely to provoke a crowd response.
- **The ceiling is low.** You will need to stoop or crouch. Fresh rock has sharp, snapped edges, so wear cut-resistant outer layers and be careful with your suit's seals and hoses.
- **The rock is unweathered and may be unstable.** Ledges have cracked as the ceiling cooled, and the chamber is inflating. Avoid loading overhangs, don't lean on formations, and be ready for small falls.
- **Sulfur crusts and needle crystals are fragile.** They grow a thumb's width in a lifetime, so don't step on or break them. Watch for hot vents, where gas whistles out at high temperature.

## Rules of conduct

- **Take nothing, leave nothing.** In a place where every waste is someone's food or material, your discards are contamination. Carry out all waste and equipment. Your body's waste stays in your suit's closed system.
- **Don't take pellets or metal.** They're the animals' building material. A single souvenir is trivial to the ecosystem, but the habit is what you're trying to avoid.
- **Stay peripheral.** Heat is status, and being close to the seam signals dominance. Move toward it and you may be treated as a rival, or simply get in the way of much larger animals.
- **Minimize your footprint in time.** The vug has run for generations without an observer. The best visit is short, quiet, and forgettable.

## Kit checklist

- Fully enclosed active-cooled suit with large thermal reservoir
- Closed-loop breathing system with a spare
- Core-temperature monitoring with automatic alarms
- Ear protection (double layer) and a rigid, padded helmet
- Insulated boots and gloves, cut-resistant outer layer
- Contact vibration sensor, and a wired or haptic comms link
- Thermal camera, dim low-power light, camera housings rated above 250 °C
- Tool lanyards, and nothing loose
- Heat-shielded extraction beacon, plus a backup
- Gas monitor (SO₂, metal vapor, oxygen)
- Decontamination plan for the suit and gear afterward

## Contingencies

- **If the suit's cooling fails:** leave immediately. You will not have time to troubleshoot. Have a pre-agreed, no-discussion abort trigger.
- **If you're pinned or crowded:** don't fight. Stay still, protect your hoses and seals, and wait for the crowd to shift. Panicking in a press of hot bodies is how seals tear.
- **If the ceiling cracks or a vent bursts:** move toward the cooler edge, not the seam, and keep your back to the wall.
- **If your anchor is lost:** this is the worst case. Carry a redundant return method, because there is no way out on foot. The crack cannot admit you.

## What it will be like

You will arrive in dim brown darkness and feel the roar in your teeth before you make sense of it. The floor will heave with plated backs, pumping chests, and the ticking of metal pellets falling. Overhead, thousands of pale insects will strike the stone in rhythms that swell and scatter. Far off, the seam will smolder, and sparks will flare and fade as the stokers breathe. It is overwhelming, and you will want to stay longer than you should. That impulse, more than the heat or the gas, is the real danger. The vug is a self-contained world that has never needed a witness, so plan to be a brief and careful one, and be out before your margin is gone.
