# Preparing to Visit the Vault of Black Leaf

## The short version

This is not a dangerous place because anything in it wants to hurt you. There are no animals and nothing hunts. It is dangerous because it is hot, wet, enclosed, unstable, and quiet enough to make you careless. The main threats are heat, bad air, falling rock, and your own willingness to stay just a few more minutes. Prepare for a short, disciplined visit, not a stroll.

## Before you go: what to learn first

**Heat is the main killer.** The air is saturated and hot, so sweat cannot evaporate, and evaporation is how your body sheds heat. A dry desert at 45°C is easier than a wet cave at 38°C. Bring a **wet-bulb thermometer** and set your limits before you descend:
- Below about 28°C wet-bulb, work normally with breaks.
- 28-32°C, work in short shifts with active cooling.
- Above about 32°C, don't linger.
- Near 35°C, turn around. Your body cannot cool itself there.

**The air is unmeasured.** A vent that needs a flare is venting combustible gas, and a bowl-shaped chamber is where heavy gases pool. Expect CO₂, hydrogen sulfide, sulfur dioxide, carbon monoxide, and methane or hydrogen. "Warm coins and boiled stone" is what a trace of these smells like, and H₂S deadens your nose after a while, so smell is not a safety system. Bring:
- A **four-gas personal monitor** (O₂, CO, H₂S, LEL), worn at chest height, plus a second unit clipped low, because CO₂ hugs the floor.
- A **half-face or full-face respirator** with combined acid-gas and P100 cartridges. The pale spore dust is the reason for the particulate filter.
- A **5-10 minute escape hood or small emergency air bottle** for each person. You will probably never use it, but the cost of not having it is total.

**Watch the flare cycle from the top of the stair before descending.** The flame dims to a blue bead and swells again over about an hour. Don't guess what that does to the atmosphere. If the flame ever goes out completely, the vent is releasing unburned gas. Treat that as an immediate evacuation, and treat any sudden sulfur smell, headache, or dizziness the same way.

## What to wear

Dress for three things at once: heat, cuts, and contact with unknown plants.

- **Hard hat with chin strap.** The collapse is fresh and the roof is still shedding pieces. Small rocks fall from a long way up.
- **Thin, light-colored, long-sleeved, moisture-wicking clothing.** Sleeves protect you from blade-sharp basalt and warm leaves.
- **Cut-resistant gloves** (no bare-handed scrambling on fracture faces) and **knee and elbow protection**.
- **Sturdy boots with thick soles and ankle support**, with gaiters. The ground is glassy and jointed, and a cut boot means a cut foot.
- **Eye protection** that seals, not just glasses. Falling drips, spore dust, and grit all end up in eyes.
- **Cotton or antistatic outer layers.** Avoid synthetics that spark, since there may be flammable gas.
- **A cooling vest with phase-change packs.** They will run out in under an hour, so bring spares in an insulated bag left at the top of the stair.

Do not bring lighters, matches, or ordinary electronics that could spark near gas. Use **intrinsically safe** lamps and radios if you can get them, and switch off anything else.

## Water, food, and body management

- Drink **more than feels reasonable**, with electrolytes. Sweat here is invisible because it never dries, so you will not feel how much you are losing. Drink on a schedule, not by thirst.
- Eat nothing inside. Wet hands, spore dust, and a respirator do not mix.
- **Do not drink or taste anything in the vault.** The pool is mineral-laden and heated from below, and it is probably scalding near the middle. The iron-red slime is not something to touch.
- Know the signs of heat exhaustion: headache, nausea, cramps, cold clammy skin, confusion, or irritability. If you feel any of them, get out and cool down. Heat stroke can impair your judgment before you realize anything is wrong, so **use a buddy system and agree on check-in phrases.**
- Check your core temperature with an ear or forehead thermometer at every break.

## Communication and navigation

Voices "go flat and are swallowed at once." Shouting won't work. Bring:
- **Whistles** with an agreed code (one blast: attention; two: come to me; three: emergency, leave).
- **Hand signals** learned in advance and practiced in light.
- A **tether or lifeline** anchored to the iron stair for anyone going more than a short way in.
- **Chemical light sticks or reflective markers** to mark your path. Everything is a low, red-brown dusk, and every stand of plants looks like the last one.
- A **headlamp** with red and white modes. The plants track heat, not light, so lamps are unlikely to bother them, but red preserves your night vision, and you will need it to see the five orange lamps as landmarks.

Sound will tell you things if you listen: the tick of iron, the valve's sigh, the drip. A change in any of those rhythms means something has changed.

## What to do at the top of the stair

Stop. Take five minutes. Check the gas monitors *before* you step down. Sit and watch the flare through at least part of a cycle. Test the stair, since it is iron bolted into fresh-broken rock and both are unproven. Descend one at a time. Leave one person at the top, out of the fall zone, whose only job is to watch and raise the alarm.

## Moving through the vault

- **The rock cuts.** Test each step and never lean on a fracture edge. A shallow cut in wet heat gets infected fast, so carry a proper first-aid kit with irrigation and antiseptic.
- **Keep your hands off the plants.** The leaves are "almost feverish" and finely pitted, and you don't know what they secrete. They have no herbivores to fear and possibly odd chemistry. Look, photograph, and walk around them.
- **Expect the plants to notice you.** They lean toward heat, and you are a warm animal standing still. It is probably harmless and slow, but don't stand in one place for long, and don't sit with your back against a stand.
- **Avoid the spore dust.** The grey bladder clusters burst with a small pop. Stay away from the oldest fronds, keep your respirator on, and don't kick up the leaf litter.
- **Don't walk on the litter unnecessarily.** It is springy, deep, and untouched, and it hides sharp rock and cracks. It is also the vault's only record of who has *not* been here.
- **Give the pool a wide margin.** The mineral crust is thin as frost, brittle, and may be over hot water.

## The works: look, don't touch

The station is the most tempting and most hazardous thing in the vault.

- **The boiler** is rusted "the color of dried blood" and still under some pressure. Rust means thin metal, and thin metal means a failure you can't predict. Stay well back and never stand in line with its blowoff or a seam.
- **The engine and flywheel** turn on their own every few minutes, with no warning but a gasp of steam. Don't put a hand, a strap, or a sleeve near the flywheel or belt, and don't step over the belt.
- **The dynamo and copper cable** are live. The floor is wet, the verdigris is conductive, and roots are wrapping the posts. Wear rubber-soled boots, don't touch the cable or posts, and don't follow the cable through standing water.
- **The lamps** are old carbon-filament lamps in wire cages. They will be hot. They are also the only reliable light sources in the vault, so don't interfere with them.
- **The handcart and rail** are old and rusted. Don't ride, push, or lean on them.

Do not try to repair, restart, stop, or improve anything. The station has been running for an unknown length of time and the plants have adapted to it. Stopping the engine might kill the largest plants, and starting anything might kill you.

## The mind

The vault is quiet, warm, and slow, and the effect is hypnotic. Combined with low oxygen or mild gas exposure, it becomes a real risk: you start to feel calm, then a little sleepy, then reluctant to leave. Fix this with rules made outside:

- **A hard time limit**, 20-30 minutes for a first visit, enforced by a person, not a feeling.
- **Regular verbal check-ins** ("Name and count backward from ten").
- **A rule that anyone can call an exit** without explanation.

Also, don't stare up through the big break in the ceiling. "The underside of another dark" is exactly the kind of thing you should not investigate or climb toward. Treat it as an unexplored boundary, not a route.

## Multiverse-specific concerns

- **Anchor your way back.** Make sure your transit method works from the top of the stair, not from the vault floor, and confirm it before descending. Never depend on a route through the ceiling break.
- **You are a contaminant.** The vault is a closed ecology with no animals at all. You carry bacteria, fungi, seeds, and mites. Wear clean, sterilized outer clothing, cover your boots, and don't shed anything. Take out everything you brought.
- **You will carry something out.** Spore dust and mineral slime cling to gear. Decontaminate before you go home: wash down, bag and seal your outer layers, and rinse respirator filters.
- **Humans "if they were human."** Whoever built the station left records only in metal and rust. If you find writing, keep it exactly where it is, photograph it, and note where you were standing.
- **Leave the flame alone.** Someone lit it, the plants read the world by it, and everything living here is tuned to its long breathing.

## Leaving

Exit before you're tired. Cool down, rehydrate, check for cuts, and wait an hour at the top to see whether headaches or cough develop. Log your gas readings and the flare timing. The next visitor will need them, because the roof is still letting go of small pieces, the rust is still eating the boiler, and the vault is not finished changing.
