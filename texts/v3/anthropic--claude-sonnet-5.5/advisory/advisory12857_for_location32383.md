# Preparing for the Sunken Terraces of Ess

## The honest summary

This is not a dangerous place in the way a battlefield or a jungle is. Nothing here hunts you. But it is unforgiving in a quiet, arithmetic way. You will be about 240 feet down, in near-freezing water, under a continent-sized ceiling of ice, in a forest that catches at anything loose, with visibility that can drop to zero when you touch the ground. There is no shore to swim to and no direction up that leads anywhere. Nearly every serious risk here comes from the environment itself, so the preparation is mostly engineering and discipline.

Treat it as the hardest kind of dive: deep, cold, and overhead. Plan it as a cave dive that happens to be very wide.

## Before you commit: find out the unknowns

**Send something first.** If your method of crossing allows it, send a weighted instrument package before you go. Have it record:

- Temperature, salinity, pressure and depth
- Dissolved oxygen, pH and hydrogen sulfide
- Ambient sound, especially the ice
- Light levels, ideally through the day/dark cycle

The description says the water holds no fish, no jellies, nothing that crawls. In a cold sea that grows this much kelp, that is strange, and you should not assume you know why. It may be nothing more than the history of the place. It may be a chemical you would not want to breathe, absorb, or cut your skin in. Until the probe says otherwise, treat the water as possibly hostile and the absence of animals as a warning.

**Know your arrival point.** Confirm where you will appear: depth, position relative to the ice, and whether you arrive dry, wet, or in open water. Arriving at 240 feet without being ready is not a survivable mistake. Ideally, arrive in a pressure-rated vehicle or habitat you can seal before you do anything else.

**Choose your season.** Go in the short bright season, when the light above lifts and you can see your own hand at midday. In the dark season you will be working entirely by your own lamps, and every problem gets worse. The thinning ice is not an exit, so don't count on it, but the extra light helps you orient.

## The pressure problem

At this depth the ambient pressure is roughly eight atmospheres. That rules out ordinary scuba on air. Air at this depth gives you dangerous oxygen levels and heavy nitrogen narcosis, which will make you slow, foolish, and happy, in exactly the environment where you cannot afford any of those.

You have three sensible options:

1. **An atmospheric diving suit.** This is a rigid, jointed suit that keeps you at one atmosphere inside. It solves narcosis, oxygen toxicity, and decompression all at once. It is clumsy in kelp, and the manipulators are coarse, but for an ordinary traveler it is the safest choice. Train in it before you go.
2. **A small pressure-hulled submersible or crawler,** possibly with a manipulator. It has the same advantages, a bigger life-support margin, and less freedom. Be careful with propellers and thrusters. Kelp will wrap them.
3. **A technical dive on trimix** (helium, oxygen, nitrogen) with a closed-circuit rebreather. This gives you the most freedom and demands the most training. A dive here is a long, planned decompression obligation, and the ice makes it worse. You can't make free ascent stops in open water, so you would need a decompression habitat or bell anchored in the terraces. Attempt this only if you are already an experienced technical diver, and even then only with a support team.

**Plan your return before you leave.** Moving between worlds does not remove the nitrogen from your blood. If you are breathing gas at pressure and your exit is a door into a world at one atmosphere, you will get decompression sickness on the other side. Either your exit point must be something that lets you decompress properly (a chamber, a bell, a slow ascent), or you must be in an ADS or hull that never left one atmosphere. Bring a portable recompression chamber to the return point either way.

## The cold

The water is at freezing point, salt-depressed slightly below zero Celsius. It will kill an unprotected person in minutes and a poorly protected one in an hour.

- A dry suit with heated undergarments, or a hot-water suit if you have a vehicle to run it from.
- Dry gloves with heated liners. Your hands will fail first, and you need them for cutting, clipping, and equipment.
- Warm the breathing gas in a rebreather. Cold gas at depth strips heat from your lungs.
- Keep your dive times short by the calendar of your own body, not by your ambition. Cold makes you clumsy well before it makes you frightened.

## The ceiling

The ice is what turns this from a deep dive into a hazardous one. There is no surface to swim to. Any emergency ascent ends at a roof, and what is above the ice is unknown to you.

- Follow full overhead protocols. Carry more gas than you need to return, in independent supplies. The usual "thirds" rule is not enough here; plan for a full team emergency with one person's failure.
- Carry three independent lights and two independent cutting tools.
- Lay a continuous guideline, and mark every junction.
- Keep a buddy or a tethered partner within reach at all times. Do not solo.

## Light and silt

The silt is the most likely thing to hurt you. It is fine, pale, and everywhere, and it lifts easily. A careless fin stroke will put you in a cloud that takes hours or years to settle, depending on how much you kick up. In a silt-out under a ceiling, you cannot see the line, the wall, or your partner.

- Hold neutral buoyancy exactly. Learn to hover and to use a frog kick or arm-only propulsion so no water is blown at the ground.
- Never touch the terraces. You are a visitor on something that took generations to make.
- If you lose visibility, stop. Hold the line. Wait. Don't swim.
- Use wide, dim, diffuse lighting. A hard beam into suspended silt reflects straight back at you. Low-angle side lights give better contrast on the terrace walls.
- Carry a red-filtered lamp too. Some of the kelp and its surroundings will read better under it.

## The kelp

The forest is soft, but it is a trap. Stalks as thick as your wrist, blades that drape, bladders that pull upward. Nothing bites, but everything can catch.

- Streamline your kit. Nothing may dangle: no loose hoses, no open clips, no unsecured straps.
- Don't thrash if you're caught. Stop, breathe, and cut methodically. Your buddy should help.
- Never push straight up through the canopy. A vertical ascent through the stalks is the most likely way to tangle. Move along the contours, where the stands are lower and more regular.
- Check that any propellers on your vehicle are shrouded and can reverse.

## Navigation and time

There is no center, no landmark, and the terraces repeat every forty paces. Everything looks alike, and that is a danger.

- Use the contours as your guide. Following a terrace keeps you at constant depth, and the lines of kelp are a rough map of it.
- Bring an inertial navigator. Assume a compass may misbehave in a world not your own.
- Carry a reel and leave a line. Note the drift: the great gyre moves things gently in one direction, so you will feel a steady push. Use it as an orientation cue, but don't trust it to take you home.
- Keep a clock, a depth log, and a gas log. Long, quiet, repetitive scenes are the ones in which you lose track.

Be careful around brine. Sinking curtains of cold brine pour down terrace walls and may pool in the channels. Cold dense water can be lower in oxygen. Avoid lying or resting in low channels. If there is any icicle-like stream of brine reaching from the ice, don't touch it. In polar seas such streams can freeze whatever they meet.

## The mind

Silence is the hard part. There are no fish, no clicks, no calls. The ice booms like a door in another house, and the kelp squeaks and hums. The place feels like a tidy room after everyone has gone. That feeling can settle on you until you begin to feel unobserved and observed at once.

- Speak often on your comms. Have your partner do a check-in on a schedule.
- Watch for narcosis or cold-induced slowness. If you are a little too calm, be suspicious.
- Don't push deeper or longer for a good view. Nothing here is going anywhere. The place is patient and you can be too.

## Respect and contamination

- **Do not take anything.** The adze in the mud, the fingerprint in a tile, and the unfinished courses are the only voices of a vanished people. Photograph them. Leave them.
- **Clean your gear thoroughly before you go.** You are entering a closed ecosystem with no fauna. Sterilize suits, tools, and vehicles so you don't carry in spores, microbes, or organisms.
- **Take nothing living out** without first thinking through what it might do in your own world.

## Kit list

- ADS, or a rated submersible, or a trimix rebreather with full bail-out
- Heated undergarments, dry gloves, redundant thermal protection
- Three lights, two cutting tools, spare lines and reels
- Inertial navigator, depth gauge, gas analyzer, hydrogen sulfide monitor
- Acoustic comms and a hardwire tether
- Portable recompression chamber at the exit
- Sample kit for sediment and water (sealed and labeled)
- Camera with wide-angle lens and low-light capability
- Food, water, and warmth, all carried in with you; nothing here is edible

## What to do once you're there

Take it slowly. Don't try to cover distance. Go down to a single terrace, settle at the level of the kelp, and let your eyes adjust. Look at the walls, the roundness, the tiles with their fingerprints. Follow a contour for a while and watch how the stands change height. Listen to the ice. Then go back the way you came, along your own line, without disturbing anything more than you have to.

Leave it as you found it.
