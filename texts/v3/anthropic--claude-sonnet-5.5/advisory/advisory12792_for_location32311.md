# Preparing for the Tablelands of the Struck Bell

## The Short Version

You are going to a cold, deep, sealed, and continuously loud place. The plateau is 73 meters under the water, roofed with ice, and edged by cliffs into unmeasured depth. The pressure, cold, sound, and lack of any surface to escape to all threaten you at once. Most of the preparation is about deciding which of these you will defeat with equipment and which you will simply endure.

## 1. Choose Your Body: Suit, Sub, or Dive

At about 8 atmospheres, with water just above freezing, you have three realistic options.

**Atmospheric diving suit (my recommendation).** A rigid, jointed suit keeps you at one atmosphere. It removes narcosis, oxygen toxicity, and decompression, and it is the best insulation you can get. You can work in it for hours and come out able to walk. You lose fine touch and some mobility, but there is little to manipulate here except to look, sample, and photograph. Walking, or more accurately stumping, on that knife-edged glass is exactly what a hardened suit is for.

**A small crewed submersible or crawler.** This is better for range and for carrying a second person. It is worse for feeling the place. Fit external lights, skids for landing on pillow basalt, and manipulators with sample trays. Be careful about the hull as a sound amplifier (see below).

**Ambient-pressure diving.** I advise against it. If you must, use a closed-circuit rebreather with trimix, since air is narcotic at this depth. Add a heated drysuit with heated undergarments, a redundant gas system, a full-face mask, and a tether. Every hour you spend on the bottom costs far more time in decompression. That obligation means you cannot simply leave if something goes wrong. You would need a staged decompression habitat or a hyperbaric chamber at your anchor point. With the ice lid, you can never do a free ascent, so the "emergency swim to the surface" that divers rely on does not exist here.

## 2. There Is No Surface

The single most important planning fact is that the sea is capped. The ice is kilometers wide, and any crack in it that you could reach would be far above you and unlikely to be wide enough to climb out of. Plan as if you were in a cave system:

- Your anchor is your only exit. Carry a portal anchor or return beacon, and place it on the shelf well back from the cliff edge, so that any drift takes you across flat rock and not into the dark.
- Carry a second, independent way home, with its own power source and separate from the first.
- Plan gas, power, and heat for a return trip that is much longer than you think you will need. Bring a reserve equal to a full second excursion.
- Never trust the ice seams as exits. They are useful as a map, and only as a map.

## 3. The Sound

Sound is the defining problem of the place. The beat comes every seven seconds. Treat it the way you would treat radiation: measure it, budget your exposure to it, and shield against it.

- **Measure first.** Send a hydrophone and a pressure transducer through your portal before you go, or take them down first as a probe. You need the actual levels and the frequency content of the stroke, which is a deep bell note, and of the bearing shriek and pump roar between strokes. Underwater sound levels are on a different scale from air, and comparing them naively will mislead you.
- **Know what it does.** Loud low-frequency sound underwater passes through you. You do not hear it only through the ears; it couples through bone and soft tissue into the chest, sinuses, and gut. Gas-filled spaces are the vulnerable organs, and a sound that "fills the chest" can, at sufficient intensity, damage them. In a suit or sub, the hull's own resonance can amplify particular frequencies.
- **Isolate.** Mount your seat, helmet, and any rigid contact points on damping material. Use in-ear protection under the helmet, tuned for low frequencies. Keep the stroke's frequency off any hollow panel in your vehicle.
- **Set a ceiling.** Decide beforehand, from the measurements, what exposure ends the dive. Stay farther from the halls than seems necessary. The sound is stronger where the trapped paths cross, and you will not be able to feel that on arrival, so map it on the way in.
- **Communicate without sound.** Voice and acoustic modems will be swamped. Use a fiber-optic or wired tether to your base, light-signal codes, and written slates. Do not use active sonar or pingers. Even if the fauna have never known quiet, a new pattern is a disturbance, and you would be adding noise to noise.
- **Plan for tinnitus.** Some threshold shift on return is likely. Schedule an audiologist and a rest period afterward.

## 4. The Cold

The water is just short of freezing and stays that way. It will take heat from anything not built to resist it.

- In a suit or sub, carry active heating with redundancy, and plan for heater failure with passive insulation good enough to keep you alive for the time needed to return.
- In a drysuit, wear layers with independent heat sources, plus heated gloves. Hands go first, and cold hands will fumble a release or a valve.
- The warm outflow from the stacks is tempting. Use it for a controlled, brief warm-up if you have to, but do not shelter in the halls. They are full of machinery that does not care about you.

## 5. The Ground and the Water

- **The rock cuts.** Fresh basalt glass and hyaloclastite sand will slice a glove, a hose, or a suit joint. Use cut-resistant outer layers and protect every hose and cable. Do not kneel. Do not crawl. Do not brace on pillow lava. If you are in a sub, keep its skids from grinding into the sand.
- **The vents.** Water near vents can be hot enough to injure and may carry hydrogen sulfide and other reduced compounds. The colorful bacterial mats mark it. Don't touch them, and don't walk through them. This is the only living surface of the ground, and it feeds everything.
- **The cliffs.** The edge is a vertigo hazard and a physical one. Deeper water means much higher pressure. If you are on ambient gas, sinking over the edge could kill you within minutes, and even in a suit, an uncontrolled descent past its depth rating is a crushing hazard. Stay at least a hundred meters back, and keep positive control of your buoyancy at all times. Clip in to a line whenever you are within sight of a drop. Do not try the carved stair.
- **The clarity.** Visibility of a kilometer or more is good for spotting hazards, and it also misleads. Distances look shorter than they are, and a herd seen "close" may be ten kilometers away. Range-find with instruments, not eyes.

## 6. Navigation

The shelf is a continent with no landmarks except the works, the ice seams, and the herds.

- Lay a line, or a chain of light beacons, from the anchor outward. Track your position by inertial navigation and by visual reference to the ice seams, which are fixed and unchanging. Photograph the sky pattern above the anchor before leaving it.
- Do not rely on a magnetic compass; you don't know what the basalt or the iron halls do to it. Test it at the anchor.
- The works are a day's travel apart. Don't try to cross between them on a single charge. Plan the trip as an out-and-back from the anchor, and don't wander off the line.

## 7. The Fauna

**Drifters.** Three to four meters long, harmless to you in themselves, and they graze in herds of thousands. They beat their tails together, in time with the engines. Stay out of their herds; a person moving through them breaks the rhythm.

**Hunters.** They are the real risk. They are dark, slow, and huge, with trapdoor jaws, and they hunt by waiting for the herds' rhythm to falter. A visitor who moves out of step is a stumble in the pattern. My advice:

- Keep the herds between you and open water when you can, and stay out of the wide gaps where hunters trail.
- Move at a steady cadence, ideally a smooth, regular one, with any movements tied to the seven-second beat. Avoid sudden bursts. Don't thrash.
- Carry a non-lethal deterrent, such as bright strobes, and have a plan to retreat to a hall's outflow or to your vehicle. Weapons are unlikely to help and could escalate a hunt.
- In a sub or suit, you are probably too armored and too odd to be worth the effort. Even so, watch for a hunter's slow approach, and don't let it get above you.

**Crawlers and tube-worms.** Armored crawlers live in the boiler rooms, and tube-worms colonize the flywheel housings. They are not dangerous at a distance, and you should not test them by entering the halls.

## 8. The Works

The halls are the safest places to look at and the most dangerous places to be near.

- Don't enter the beam engine's swing or the cylinder's path. The engine has run unattended for an unknown time and will not stop for you. A moving beam the length of a ship's mast does not register a diver.
- Don't touch the rails, carts, crane, or stair. They may be structurally unsound, and the sharp edges of corroded iron are as bad as the glass.
- Stay clear of valve gear, pumps, and intakes. The pumped water can pull hoses and limbs.
- Photograph. Don't remove anything. There are no closed doors, bodies, or tools to explain the builders, and you will be tempted to look for them. Resist the urge.

## 9. The Body's Own Rhythm

This is easy to overlook. You will be immersed for hours in a seven-second pulse that the whole ecosystem obeys. Heart rate and breathing may begin to entrain, and you may feel it in your chest before you notice it. That is not necessarily harmful, and some people find it calming. But:

- Monitor heart rate and breathing with a telemetry system, and have someone at the base watching the data.
- Take breaks from the sound, if your vehicle allows. If not, keep dives short.
- Choose a partner who is steady. Panic in a sealed sea under ice is dangerous, and the sound makes it hard to talk anyone down.
- Expect some dread or a vague sense of being observed. It's the sound, not a presence.

## 10. Ethics and Footprint

The shelf is a stable pattern that has not changed in living memory, and nothing there has ever known quiet. You will be the first thing to arrive that doesn't belong to the rhythm.

- Leave nothing: no lost equipment, no cable ends, no marker beacons you can't retrieve. Use recoverable lines only.
- Take only what you must. Any sample should be from a place that will not be missed, and should be small. Don't take mat from near the vents.
- Don't try to change anything, and don't try to stop an engine. If you did, you would produce silence for creatures that have never known it, and that is not a kindness.

## 11. Leaving

When you go back, the sound falls off behind you, and what you notice is not how quiet it has become but that you can hear something small: your own pulse, still keeping time with the bell.

Plan for that. Give yourself a decompression period even if you were in a suit. Be somewhere quiet for a day or two afterward and don't rush into conversation or travel. Expect your ears to ring, your sense of rhythm to feel wrong for a while, and your heart to search for a beat that isn't there. It will pass. Let it.

## Checklist

- Atmospheric diving suit or armored sub, with redundant heat, life support, and a surplus of endurance
- Portal anchor plus an independent second exit
- Low-frequency hearing protection and vibration damping
- Hydrophone/probe survey completed before the crewed dive
- Wired or fiber-optic tether; slates and light signals
- Cut-resistant outer layers; protected hoses and joints
- Line or beacon chain to the anchor; sky photographs; inertial navigation
- Strobes and a retreat plan for hunters
- Recoverable-only equipment
- Medical monitoring at base; audiology and rest afterward
