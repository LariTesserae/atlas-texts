# Preparing for the Sump Terraces of Ahn-Vessel

## The Short Version

You are going somewhere cold, wet, dark, loud, high, and seismically restless, and where fire is forbidden. Almost every comfort a modern traveler relies on (dry clothes, a lamp, a stove, a conversation) is unavailable or rude. Prepare to be a competent, quiet, damp guest who navigates by hand and ear. Prepare for the body's problems first, then learn the local senses, then think about manners.

## 1. Before You Go: Bodies and Altitude

**The ridge road is at 4,000 meters.** Arriving cold from sea level can make you seriously ill within hours. If your travel method allows staging, spend several days at intermediate altitude first, ideally 2,500-3,000 m. If it does not, treat the first two days as a medical event: move slowly, drink steadily, and expect headache and poor sleep. Talk to a physician about acetazolamide or similar prophylaxis. Know the danger signs: confusion, stumbling, a wet cough, and a headache that will not quit. The answer to all of them is to go down at once. Descent is your only reliable treatment. Note that in Ahn-Vessel you are descending into the basin, which helps a little, but the basin is not far below the ridge.

**Cold without freezing is the real enemy.** Air just above freezing and saturated with water pulls heat from you faster than dry cold at much lower temperatures. People die of hypothermia in exactly these conditions, in weather that feels merely unpleasant. Locals keep moving for this reason. Plan to do the same, and eat often and heavily. You will burn far more calories than you expect.

**Unknown biology.** The mats, the fog, and the animals are built on chemistry that never met your immune system. Get current vaccinations, but understand that they will not help against something that has no counterpart at home. Wear a well-fitted filtering mask (P100-class or better) when moving through the lower terraces, where mat spores, mite shed, and sulfur-tinged aerosol are thick. Cover cuts before you go out. The scree is glassy and clean-edged, and a cut in a wet, microbe-rich place is an infection risk. Carry a serious first-aid kit with wound irrigation, antiseptics, and broad-spectrum antibiotics your doctor has prescribed for the trip.

**Contamination goes both ways.** You could carry something into a closed, delicately balanced system. Scrub and disinfect boots, packs, and tools before entering, and again on leaving. Bring nothing organic that you do not need, and carry out all waste.

## 2. Clothing and Gear: Assume Everything Gets Wet

The rule of the place is that nothing is dry, so build a kit that works wet.

- **Base and mid layers:** merino or synthetic. Never cotton. Cotton in this climate is a slow way to die. Bring several sets, and sleep in a set you keep sealed.
- **Outer layer:** the locals wear oiled, water-shedding wraps for a reason. A hard-shell jacket and trousers will work, but they will wet out from the inside through condensation if you overexert. Vent them often. A waxed or oiled outer layer, of the kind the residents wear, may suit you better. Ask your hosts.
- **Gloves are a special case.** Handrails carry information in temperature and texture: cool ridges mean left, warm ridges mean right. Thick mittens will make you blind. Bring thin, waterproof-but-sensitive liner gloves for navigating, and warmer overmitts you can remove quickly when you need to read a rail. Practice this before you go. Fresh basalt and rhyolite will also slice bare palms.
- **Footwear:** waterproof boots with aggressive rubber lugs and real ankle support, because streets are narrow, stepped, wet, and film-coated. Bring gaiters, spare insoles, and at least three pairs of socks to rotate. Wool socks still warm you when damp, and a foot that is always cold will end your trip.
- **Head and neck:** a close-fitting hood or balaclava. Mites will get into your collar and boots. This is considered lucky, and they are harmless as far as anyone reports, but a snug neck gaiter makes it less of a surprise.
- **Bags:** everything in roll-top dry bags, and then those in a pack liner. Assume the outer pack is a sponge. Anything that must stay dry should be triple-protected: sleeping bag, spare base layers, medications, and any paper.
- **Sleep system:** a synthetic-fill sleeping bag, a vapor-barrier liner, and an insulated pad. Down is a liability here. You will be sleeping in a shared-wall building of cold, humming, dampness-drinking material, so plan for chill from below and around you.

## 3. Light, or the Lack of It

There is no ambient light, and the city was never designed for eyes. Do not fight this.

**Bring a light, but bring a modest one.** A small red or very dim amber headlamp is reasonable as a backup for things like first aid and gear repair. Do not carry a powerful white beam. Nobody knows how the mites, mist-bats, or choristers respond to intense light. The one documented case, fire, causes stampedes, and a sudden blaze in a dark, tightly packed, multi-level street could be dangerous. Ask your host before using any light in the streets. Assume the answer is "sparingly."

**Train for the dark before you arrive.** Spend a few evenings moving through an unfamiliar space blindfolded or in true darkness, using only hands and ears. Practice reading textured and warm-and-cool surfaces. Your fingertips will become your primary eyes, and they need to learn to trust the input. Expect disorientation, vertigo, and moments of low-grade panic in the first days. These pass, but only if you keep moving deliberately and calmly.

## 4. Sound: The Other Half of Navigation

**The roar is loud enough to feel in your chest.** In the thick fog phase, it will be dangerous to unprotected ears over long exposure. Bring musician's earplugs or filtered plugs that lower volume evenly without muffling pitch. You need to hear the resonant stones and the hummed signals of others. Foam plugs will make you deaf to the very information you are relying on.

**Bring a striker.** Travelers ping resonant junction stones with small hammers of bone or glass. Ask a local to make or lend you one, or buy one at the first settlement. A steel hammer might be too harsh, might damage the stones, and would be the wrong pitch. Learn the first junction-notes with your host, and don't strike stones you don't understand.

**Learn the vocabulary.** Practice before arrival if you can:
- The greeting: a low two-note hum.
- The warning: a rising slide.
- Shoulder taps and touch-signs. Ask your host to teach you a basic set: yes, no, stop, follow me, danger, I'm lost, I need help, and I'm cold.

You will not be able to talk in most places. If you have any vocal training, hum-signs will come easily. If you don't, practice pitch matching.

Bring a **waterproof writing slate or grease pencil and synthetic paper** for quiet places, and keep them in a chest pocket. Most conversations will happen in the thin fog phase, or in sheltered rooms.

## 5. Timing: The Fog Cycle

The thirty-day cycle matters more than any season at home.

- **Thick phase:** the choir swells to a roar, the pumps labor, and the lanterns dim. This is the most spectacular and the most disorienting. It is the hardest on the ears, the hardest on navigation, and the coldest and wettest. It is also the most dramatic time to witness the city, but unless you are experienced, don't arrive at the peak.
- **Thin phase:** the animals go nearly silent, and you can hear every seep and drip. This is the best time for a first visit. Conversation is possible, signals are clear, and light is slightly better. It also feels eerie and can be more unsettling than the noise.

**My advice:** arrive at the start of the thin phase, spend your first few days learning the streets, and time your stay so you experience the swell. Leave before the next swell unless you feel ready. Ask your host to confirm the cycle date. Your instincts and calendar may disagree with the local reckoning.

## 6. Food, Water, and Fire

**Nothing green grows here.** You will eat what you carry, what your hosts can share, or what the system produces. Bring more calorie-dense, no-cook, waterproof-packaged food than you think you need: nut butters, hard cheese, jerky, oil, chocolate, oats you can soak, and electrolyte mixes. Food will get damp, so repackage everything into sealed containers. Bring a reserve you do not plan to eat. Don't eat local organisms, mat material, or mites unless invited to and told how.

**Water is everywhere and mostly unfit.** Seep water crossing chemosynthetic mats will carry sulfur, iron, dissolved metals, and unfamiliar microbes. Filter, then chemically treat or boil. You can't boil, so bring a good pump filter plus chemical or UV treatment, and test by smell and taste only if nothing else is available. Ask what your hosts drink and whether it is safe for your physiology. Their answer may not apply to you.

**No fire.** No matches, no lighters, no stoves, no candles. Bring **flameless** heat sources: chemical hand-warmers (test them in humid air, since some fail), insulated bottles filled with hot water at the last stop before the ridge, and a self-heating meal system that does not use open flame. Keep in mind that your hosts may not want unfamiliar chemical reactions running near the lattice. Ask before using them.

## 7. Electronics and Tools

**Assume all electronics will fail.** Saturated air, condensation, and low temperatures will kill batteries and corrode contacts. If you must bring a device, use waterproof, sealed hardware, and keep it against your body. Cameras, radios, and phones are likely to be unreliable, and a flash would be inappropriate. Compass and GPS may be affected by the local geology, and satellites, if any, are irrelevant. Bring paper maps sealed in waterproof pouches, but expect that no maps of the city exist, since the city keeps changing.

Bring **mechanical, tactile tools**: a good multi-tool, a small folding knife (never for cutting the lattice), waterproof cord, duct tape, and a small repair kit. Do not bring anchors, pitons, drills, or anything that pierces walls. The lattice is a living, drinking system, and you would be piercing a vessel.

## 8. Physical Hazards of the Terrain

- **The rock is new and sharp.** Scree and scarp are unweathered glass-edged fragments. Wear gloves, avoid sliding on slopes, and stay off freshly broken faces.
- **Slippery mats.** The pink, orange, and violet films are slick as ice. Step on dry lattice or textured stone, and use handrails.
- **The mountain shifts.** When the choristers all fall silent at once, the ground is about to move or already has. Stop, brace yourself against the nearest solid handhold, keep your feet under you, and stay clear of drop-edges and overhangs. Terraces can drop or lift a hand's breadth. Expect it. Do not run. Wait until the roar resumes.
- **Tension-lines.** They are tuned and load-bearing. Do not touch, hang on, or strike them, and do not shelter directly beneath them if you feel a change in pitch.
- **The drains.** Sump-eels live there, blind and slow but large. Stay out of open drains and culverts. Don't put hands into dark water without a local guide's word.
- **Mist-bats.** Not known to be dangerous, but the ticking overhead is unsettling. Keep your head covered.

## 9. Etiquette and Attitude

These people maintain the city the way a shepherd keeps an ancient animal. You are a guest in a living organism.

- **Hire or be assigned a guide.** Do not attempt the city alone, at least at first. Settle terms, gifts, and lodging in advance, if you can, by whatever means the locals accept.
- **Keep moving, keep quiet.** Do not clog stairwells, especially in a thick phase. Stand aside for those who hum a warning.
- **Don't damage anything.** Do not scrape the lattice, harm mites, or sample the mats without permission. Take nothing. Leave nothing.
- **Bring a gift that suits the environment.** Something durable, waterproof, and useful: good oilcloth, high-quality cord, a hand-forged tool, or dense food. Ask what is welcome. Avoid anything electronic, flammable, or perishable.
- **Join in when invited.** During the swell, when people press their palms to the walls and hum along, do it too. It is not a performance for you, but you're welcome to be part of it.

## 10. Mental Preparation

The place is beautiful, but the beauty is largely inaccessible to the eye. It arrives through the sternum, the fingertips, the cold on your face, and the taste of iron in the air. Expect three phases:

1. **Overwhelm.** Sensory overload, cold, disorientation.
2. **Adjustment.** You learn the pitches, the handrail codes, the rhythm of movement, and you begin to feel the city as one system.
3. **Attunement.** Sometime after the first thin-to-thick transition, you may find yourself humming with the choir without deciding to. That is the sign to eat, warm up, and sleep. You are probably about as far in as an outsider should go on a first visit.

Set a firm exit plan and a firm exit time before entering. Tell someone outside the city when you are going in and when you are coming out. Bring an emergency signal that will work without fire, light, or electronics. The best one is likely a mechanical whistle, and you should learn the local equivalent of the rising slide. If you are cold, confused, or sleeping badly, treat it as an emergency early. In Ahn-Vessel, the moment you feel that you're okay is often the moment you should already have started warming up.

## Checklist Summary

**Body:** acclimatization plan, altitude meds, first-aid kit, vaccinations, P100 mask.
**Layers:** merino/synthetic base, oiled or hard-shell outer, thin waterproof liner gloves plus overmitts, rugged waterproof boots, gaiters, spare socks.
**Dry systems:** roll-top bags, pack liner, synthetic sleeping bag with vapor-barrier liner.
**Senses:** musician's earplugs, dim red headlamp (use sparingly), bone or glass striker, touch-sign and hum training, blindfold practice.
**Food/water:** calorie-dense no-cook food in sealed containers, filter plus chemical treatment, flameless heat sources.
**Tools:** multi-tool, repair kit, cord, tape, paper maps in pouches. No drills, no anchors, no flame.
**Etiquette:** guide, gifts, a policy of leaving no trace and taking nothing, and readiness to join the chorus.

Go slowly, keep your hands warm enough to read the walls, and listen. The city will tell you where you are.
