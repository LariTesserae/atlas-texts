# Preparing for the Cordillera of Standing Wires

## The short version

This is not a hostile place. It has no predators, no storms of any size, and no obvious poisons. The danger comes from four quiet things: **altitude, sharp new rock, cold damp, and electricity you can't hear.** The fifth is psychological: a silence deep enough to unsettle you. Prepare for each and the plateau is one of the gentler and more beautiful places a traveler can go.

---

## 1. Arrive with a plan for your lungs

The plateau sits around 3,000 meters. That is not extreme, but a multiverse traveler doesn't ascend gradually. You step out of a doorway from sea level, or from wherever you were, and your body gets no warning.

- **Assume you are unacclimatized.** Plan your first 24-36 hours as a rest period. Walk slowly, sleep, and drink a lot.
- Expect a mild headache, shortness of breath on slopes, a racing pulse, and poor first-night sleep. These are normal. Nausea, a headache that won't fade, confusion, clumsiness, or a wet cough are not. Descend.
- Carry a fingertip pulse oximeter and know your baseline. Bring headache and nausea medication. Consider a preventive altitude medication, after consulting a physician if you have one in your home world.
- Eat more carbohydrates than usual. Avoid alcohol.
- The thin air makes exertion feel like a loan. Keep a pace at which you could hold a conversation. Given the acoustics (see below), you may want to do that literally.

## 2. Sun, glare, and light

The light is described as blue-white, hard, and nearly unfiltered. Treat it as high-mountain radiation even though the temperature is a mild ten degrees.

- **Sunscreen (high SPF), lip balm with SPF, and a brimmed hat.** Cool air will convince you that you aren't burning. You are.
- **Real sunglasses, category 3 or 4, with side shields.** The pale frosted vegetation and mist amplify glare. Snow-blindness-type injury is possible on bright, cloud-lit days. Bring a spare pair.
- The clarity of the air makes distances deceptive. The next bench looks like a twenty-minute walk and may be three hours. Judge by contour, not by sight.

## 3. Cold, wet, and the fog tide

Midday is around ten degrees. The nights are much colder, and everything condenses. By dawn every strand is beaded, and every morning a slow bank of cloud climbs from the lowlands and soaks the benches until noon.

- **Layer system, entirely non-cotton.** Wicking base, insulating fleece or synthetic-fill mid layer, and a real waterproof shell with a hood. Fine mist soaks more insidiously than rain.
- Bring insulated gloves, a warm hat, and dry spare socks sealed in a bag. Wet feet and hands are the main way people get cold here.
- Expect **everything to be damp by morning**, including your tent, your pack, and your paper. Use dry bags, keep a sleeping-system layer that stays dry, and don't store your only insulation in the tent vestibule.
- **Schedule around the tide.** Travel and route-find after the fog burns off, roughly noon to late afternoon. In the morning cloud visibility drops, the rock is slick, and it is easy to walk toward a scarp edge you can't see.
- Brief afternoon showers of fine cold droplets are normal. They aren't dangerous, but they rewet everything.

## 4. Footing and the fresh-broken rock

The land was raised recently, and nothing has had time to round off.

- **Sturdy, stiff-soled, ankle-supporting boots** with aggressive tread. The volcanic stone splits into plates and blades. Trail runners will be shredded and will fail you on scree.
- **Cut-resistant gloves** for scrambling. Fresh grey-green rock cuts skin like broken slate. Carry a good first-aid kit with wound closure strips, pressure dressings, and antiseptic. A cut at altitude, in damp cold, heals slowly.
- Scarps up to house height, with clean faces, are edges. **Don't approach them in fog, and never walk blindly toward one.** Test loose slabs before committing weight. Plates tilt.
- Gullies are steep-walled and V-shaped. They can trap cold air and are hard to climb out of. Pick your line before descending into one.
- The ground is a *fault zone*. The fence lines have parted by a hand's width, so the creep is slow. But an uplifted plateau can still shake. Don't camp directly beneath a scarp face or in a narrow cut. Know where the open benches are.
- Cushion plants are firm enough to sit on. Nevertheless, **please don't.** See section 9.

## 5. The electrical system: the real hazard

This is the part that a traveler from an ordinary world is least equipped to intuit. The condenser lattices are *live*. Nothing is humming, sparking, or announcing itself, and that's what makes them dangerous.

**What to treat as energized, always:**
- Wire screens, pylons, catenary cables, and copper bus-bars.
- Transformer houses and the rectifier hall.
- The insulators in the rectifier hall, which are reported to still hold charge, and the moss growing on them in lightning-shaped patterns.
- The damp ground near any of the above. Wet ground conducts.

**Rules:**
1. **Do not touch any conductor, screen, or bus-bar.** A prickle on your arm hairs is a warning from a static potential. Static potentials can be very high in voltage even when current is low, and you have no way of knowing which situation you're in. A startle jump near a cliff or a cable is its own injury.
2. **Keep at least 10 meters from lines and pylons, more if hair-raising or skin-prickling begins.** If your arm hairs lift, back away slowly and don't run. Rubber-soled boots help but do not make you safe.
3. **Do not walk along the copper bus-bars or lie on their ceramic stands.** Ground gradients (step voltage) are real when a system leaks charge into damp earth. If you feel tingling in your feet, keep your feet together and shuffle away.
4. **Leave pylons before dusk.** The blue glow at the pylon tips is corona discharge, a sign of strong local fields. It's beautiful and it is not a place to be. Watch it from a distance, from a bench away from wires.
5. **Metal poles are a liability.** Leave metal trekking poles, metal-framed tent poles, and long metal tools behind if you'll be near the works. Use fiberglass or wood, or carry nothing tall. Do not raise a tall antenna.
6. **Don't hang anything from wires.** Don't drape wet clothing on fences either, in case the fences are part of the system.
7. **Don't enter the rectifier hall**, the transformer houses, or the winding house without an expert and a firm reason. Insulators there may still be charged, and the cabinets are dark and mossed. If your purpose is scientific, go with proper high-voltage protocols and non-conductive tools, and assume nothing is dead.
8. **Avoid the charged mist columns.** The vertical pillars of mist before dawn in the wire-heavy basins are stable, unwinded, and charged. Don't walk into them and don't camp in them.

**If you have implanted medical devices** (pacemaker, insulin pump, neurostimulator), don't go. Fields here are strong and of unknown character. If you rely on any sensitive electronics, expect trouble.

## 6. Your gear and the fields

- **Electronics may behave oddly** in strong fields, especially near the lattices. Bring backups that don't depend on a single device: paper map in a waterproof case, a mechanical watch, a simple baseplate compass.
- **Compasses may deflect near conductors.** Take bearings well away from the works, and cross-check against terrain (ridgelines, scarps, and the field-aligned lean of the trees, which can itself be a rough guide once you know the pattern).
- **Your multiverse anchor, transit beacon, or return device** is the item that matters most. Test it before you go. Keep it well away from pylons, protect it against damp, and if it's electronic, consider whether the fields could disturb it. **Set your return point on an open bench well away from the works**, and know a second exit option in case the first is compromised. Do not depend on being able to recall it from inside a charged basin.
- Consider an anti-static bag for sensitive equipment. Static discharge when handling gear near the lattices is likely.
- Cameras are welcome. The field-aligned vegetation is best appreciated from a high point.

## 7. Silence

This is the odd one. The plateau swallows sound: thin air, deep moss, fog-damp. Voices sound "small and close, as if spoken into cloth." In the deepest groves you hear your pulse.

- **Sound will not work as a signal.** Shouting will not carry. A whistle is muffled. Plan for this: bright clothing, mirror or flag signals, light signals at dusk (avoid the pylons), and a **firm buddy system with visual contact**. Don't get out of sight of your companions.
- Establish hand signals and a check-in schedule before you set out.
- Don't rely on hearing to detect rockfall, another person, or a hazard. Look constantly.
- **The silence affects people.** Some find it profound. Some find hearing their own heartbeat unnerving, especially combined with altitude, and you can become anxious or dissociated. If it starts to grate, talk to your companions, sing softly, hum, or listen to something in your own ears. Don't go alone if you know you struggle with sensory deprivation.
- Bring a quiet routine: breathing exercises, writing in a journal, a small pleasure. Don't fight the silence, but don't let it swallow you either.

## 8. Water and food

- **Bring all of your food.** There is nothing edible you should assume is safe, and you shouldn't strip the plateau anyway.
- Bring more water than you think you need, and a way to make more. The streams and tarns are clear and cold, but water that looks like empty space can carry organisms, minerals, or something altered by the electric works. **Filter and chemically treat it, or boil it.** If possible, test a sample first. Don't drink from tarns or seeps directly beside the lattices. Do not collect from the troughs of the condensers, since you don't know what they've been carrying.
- At altitude your appetite drops and dehydration is subtle. Drink on a schedule, not by thirst.
- Pack warm drinks. In cold damp, a hot drink is both morale and medicine.

## 9. Respecting the place

The plateau is a slow, delicate system, and everything on it is much older and more patient than you.

- **Stay on rock, bare ground, and established routes where you can.** Moss, lichen, and cushions take years to recover from a single boot. Sitting on a cushion plant, however firm it feels, crushes life that took decades to build.
- Don't disturb the field-aligned growth. The lean of the rosette trees and the direction of the moss are a record of the system. Don't cut, trim, or rearrange them.
- **Don't try to fix, switch off, cut, or harvest the works.** You don't understand them. They've run longer than anyone remembers and may be a load-bearing part of the plateau's water supply and ecology. Take nothing from the sites: not insulators, not wire, not moss from the glass.
- **Clean your boots, gear, and clothes before you arrive** and again if you move between basins. Seeds, spores, and fungal fragments from your home world could take root in a system that lives on a delicate balance. Bring nothing organic that you're not prepared to keep contained.
- Pack out everything, including food scraps and human waste, which decomposes slowly in cold, dry, thin-aired conditions. Use a waste-carry system.
- The animals here (rodents in the cushions, moths on the transformers, the single high bird) keep out of the way. Keep out of theirs. Don't feed or chase them, and don't block their tunnels.
- Do not lower anything, or send anything, into the fault crack at the winding house. Nobody knows what the drum once carried up or down, and that is a good reason to leave it alone.

## 10. Where to camp, if you camp

- **Choose an open bench** away from scarps, away from gullies where cold air and mist pool, and well away from any pylons, cables, transformers, or bus-bars, ideally at least 50-100 meters. Higher ground avoids the coldest pooled air but is more exposed. Look for gently sloping, rocky, or gravel-covered ground rather than thick plant cover.
- Face your tent into the prevailing flow, and use a good vent. Condensation will be heavy inside.
- Non-conductive poles, and no metal guy-lines running toward a line.
- Keep a small bag packed by the door in case you need to move in a hurry.
- The nights are still, cold, and silent. Sleeping can be hard at first. Bring earplugs? No. You'll want to hear anything, and there's little to hear. Bring a good sleeping bag, an insulated pad, and a hot water bottle.

## 11. A sample kit list

**Body:** Wicking base layers, fleece, insulated jacket, waterproof shell and trousers, warm hat, brimmed sun hat, insulated gloves, cut-resistant gloves, spare socks, sturdy boots, gaiters.
**Eyes and skin:** Category 3-4 sunglasses (spare pair), sunscreen, SPF lip balm.
**Navigation:** Paper map in a waterproof case, baseplate compass (used away from wires), mechanical watch, altimeter, signaling mirror, bright flag.
**Shelter and sleep:** Four-season tent with non-metal or well-insulated poles, warm sleeping bag, insulated pad, dry bags.
**Water and food:** Filter and chemical treatment, stove, warm drinks, high-calorie carbs.
**Medical:** Pulse oximeter, altitude medication, painkillers, wound care, blister care, thermal blanket, personal medications, a plan for descent.
**Multiverse essentials:** Tested anchor or return beacon (protected, damp-proofed, stored away from lines), backup exit, contact schedule, a companion.
**Not brought:** Metal poles, long antennas, anything conductive you'd need to hold up in the air, and anything organic that might seed the place.

## 12. What it will feel like

You'll step through and your first impression will be of *light*, clean, blue, and hard, and of cold air that tastes faintly of wet stone. Your ears will feel stuffed. You'll take a few breaths and find they don't quite fill you. Then you'll notice the sheer *texture* of the world: silver-furred rosettes, moss in orange and purple, lichen beards, the pale broken faces of the scarps. And then you'll notice you can't hear your own footsteps.

The first hours are the difficult ones, with the altitude, the silence, and the dawning sense that the far-off pylons are *alive*. Take it slowly. Sit on the rock, not the moss, and breathe. Let the fog tide come up and pass through you and burn off. By the second day the place tends to settle around you. You'll find your pace, the silence turns from a pressure into a kind of attention, and you'll see the patterns in the vegetation, the great curving field lines drawn across the benches.

Walk gently, and keep your hands to yourself around the wires. Watch the place run on patience. And leave before the blue glow rises on the pylon tips, or watch it from far enough away to enjoy it.
