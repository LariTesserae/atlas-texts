# Preparing for the Sump at Harrow Lock

## The short version

You are visiting a machine that keeps a river out of a living room. Everything about the place follows from that. It is warm, wet, loud, green, and ruled by a routine that has run for generations. You will be fine if you come dry-shod in spirit, keep your ears protected, stay out of the way of the morning cutting, and treat the pumps as the most important thing in the world, because to sixty people they are.

## Before you go: arrival and logistics

**Anchor your exit carefully.** The settlement is ten minutes across, which makes it easy to get to and easy to be trapped in. If your return method depends on a fixed spot, choose one above the water line. The bowl sits a body-length below the river's high line, and the wall is the only reason the ground is dry. Do not anchor in a lane, a cellar, a canal-side cutting, or anywhere that could be under a meter of water if the works failed. Choose an upper walkway or a rooftop garden box, with permission. Also make sure your method does not rely on silence, dryness, or fine electronics. Assume all three are unavailable.

**Plan for a stay of days, not hours.** With so little to see, you might think an afternoon is enough. It isn't. The place reveals itself slowly: the routine of the crew, the hour the rain comes, the moment you stop hearing the pumps and start hearing what's underneath. Give yourself at least three days. You need one just to adjust to the noise.

**Language and payment.** Expect a small, close-knit community with its own shorthand, spoken in short, loud sentences. Bring a translation method that works at close range and doesn't rely on audio, such as written cards, a phrase slate, or a companion who can read lips. Cash from your home world will mean little. Bring things a wet, industrial settlement actually uses: good steel files, sealed tins of grease, oilcloth, dry spare cotton, salt, spices, seeds that tolerate heat, and pruning blades. Machete and saw edges are consumed daily by this place. A well-made replacement blade is the best gift you can bring.

## What to wear

You will be wet from below, from above, and from within. Plan for all three.

- **Footwear:** Waterproof boots with deep tread, ankle-high at least, with the tops tucked under your trousers. The ground is a mat of moss over brick over silt. Your boot sinks a finger's depth and water rises around the sole, so the tea-warm brown wet will get in unless you seal it out. The moss over wet brick is slick, especially on the rusted iron and mossed slopes near the canals. Bring a second pair to rotate, because boots won't dry here. Bring wool or synthetic socks, and several pairs.
- **Clothing:** Light, quick-drying long sleeves and trousers. Cotton is a trap; it will stay wet and grow a faint green tint in days, which you'll notice on the washing lines. Synthetic or blended fabrics are better. Choose loose fits for the heat, but nothing that dangles near moving machinery.
- **Rain gear:** A light, ventilated rain shell, or a wide-brimmed hat with a waxed finish. Rain arrives most afternoons, warm and heavy. A heavy waterproof will just make you sweat, so pick something breathable and accept that you'll be damp anyway.
- **Gloves:** Thin, grippy work gloves. The valve wheels are slick, the pipes sweat, and the bamboo scaffolding has splinters and sometimes sharp cut edges.
- **Head:** A brimmed hat helps with the drips from gutters and fern fronds. A cap or hard hat is better if you plan to go into the pump house or under the walkways.

## Hearing and the noise

This is the first thing you meet and the last thing you stop hearing.

- **Bring proper hearing protection**, not just foam plugs. Get good-fitting earmuffs or high-attenuation plugs rated for industrial noise. You'll want to wear them near the pump house, especially when the big pump starts and the bell on the pulley swings and strikes on its own.
- **But don't wear them all the time.** In the settlement lanes, the sound is a constant thudding and hiss. You'll need to be able to hear a shouted warning, a pulley bell, or a change in the pumps' rhythm. Locals notice when the sound changes, and you should learn to as well.
- **Learn to talk at the pump house.** People here speak in short, loud sentences and watch each other's mouths. Face the person, keep your sentences short, and don't be offended by bluntness. A hand signal set helps: point, thumbs, a flat palm for "stop."
- **Expect fatigue.** A steady low-frequency thud tires the body in ways you don't notice until you leave. Plan quiet rest, and don't schedule anything demanding on your first day.
- **Sleeping:** The thudding goes through the floors and into the beds. You'll feel it in your sternum. Most visitors sleep poorly the first night and well the second. Earplugs will help, but the vibration won't stop. Accept it as a heartbeat.

## Health and hygiene

Warm, standing water with organic matter, rust, and a hundred years of settlement is a rich environment. Take it seriously.

- **Vaccinations and prophylaxis:** Get up to date on tetanus, typhoid, hepatitis A, and any regionally appropriate insect-borne disease. Ask about leptospirosis, which is spread by contact with contaminated water and is a real risk anywhere rats and standing water mix. Bring antimalarials or the equivalent if a mosquito-borne illness could exist in a delta like this, and assume it does.
- **Water:** Do not drink from the canals, the drips, or the pipes. The tea-brown color is tannin and silt, not flavor. Bring a filter and purification tablets, and accept locally boiled water from the canteen, where steam is always pouring, if it's offered.
- **Skin and feet:** Fungal infections are the most likely thing to go wrong. Keep your feet as dry as possible. Change socks at midday. Bring antifungal powder and apply it daily. Carry antiseptic and waterproof dressings for cuts, because a scrape from rusted iron or bamboo needs prompt cleaning.
- **Insects:** The insects are small and constant. Bring repellent with a high concentration of active ingredient, and treat your clothes. Sleep under a net if your lodging doesn't provide one.
- **Wildlife:** The monitor lizard the length of a table will cross a lane with great deliberation. Let it. Don't crowd it or block its route. The heron on the outfall wall is likewise best left alone. Snails striping the walls after rain make the brick slick.
- **Allergies and spores:** Mold and pollen are ambient. If you have asthma or fungal sensitivities, bring inhalers and a fitted mask, and think twice about staying in the lower rooms.

## Protecting your gear

Your equipment will suffer.

- **Electronics:** Assume anything unsealed will fail. Use waterproof cases with desiccant packs, and swap the packs daily. Keep devices off cold iron, where condensation forms instantly. Paper notebooks with pencil work better than most devices. If you carry a camera, bring lens cloths and silica gel and expect fog.
- **Metal tools:** Rust runs in orange streaks. Oil everything and re-oil daily.
- **Bags:** Use dry bags inside your pack. Everything else will be damp by the end of the first afternoon.
- **Documents and money:** Laminate or seal them.
- **Power:** The settlement has its own switch room humming with transformers. Ask before you plug in. Never assume you can draw from its supply, since it runs the pumps.

## Movement and safety

- **Walk on the worn brick lines** in the lanes, the two lines with the pennywort spine between them, rather than across the moss. The crew's own paths are the safest, since a century of boots has kept them low and firm.
- **Do not climb the bamboo scaffolding** unless invited. Some of it is alive and sprouting, and no one has bothered to tell which poles are old and which are growing. A pole that looks structural may be a living stem, and a pole that looks alive may be the only thing holding a gantry up.
- **Stay clear of the fig roots on the warehouse corners.** They have taken up the walls, and in places the wall is mostly root with brick trapped inside. Don't pull, pry, or cut.
- **Watch the boiler stack.** The fig on top is beautiful and probably fine, but it's a good place not to stand beneath.
- **Guard rails and intake screens:** The intake grates, flywheels, and belt guards are cleared each morning by machete and saw. Keep clear of the cutting zones while the crew is working. The tools are sharp and the crew is moving fast.
- **The pump house:** Ask before entering. Hats on, hair tied back, sleeves down, no loose straps. The belt somewhere in there is clattering, a panel rings with each thud, and the vine curtains part with hooks. It's not a place to wander.
- **Canals:** They're covered in a duckweed skin so even it looks like a lawn. It is not a lawn. Do not step on it, and keep children and pets away. Falling in is dangerous because of the weed, the depth, the current near the intake, and the water quality.
- **The river wall and upper walkways:** From up there you can see the river as a slow brown ridge higher than the rooftops. This is the view that explains the place. Go up with a local, in dry conditions, and don't lean on the wall. Do not go near the embankment alone.

## Conduct and etiquette

These sixty people are running a life-support system, and you are a guest in their engine room.

- **The pumps come first.** If anyone says "stop" or "clear," obey immediately, without asking why. Ask afterward.
- **Don't disturb the vegetation** in ways that matter. The daily cutting is a border war, and the line between cleared and uncleared zones is meaningful. Do not plant anything, do not carry out cuttings, and do not introduce seeds, spores, or invasive species from your own world. A foreign fern or vine in this environment could be a catastrophe. Wash and inspect your boots, packs, and clothing before you arrive. Sanitize them again before you leave, so you don't carry any of this place's growth into another world either.
- **Offer to help, within your skill.** Skimming duckweed with the long rakes is a weekly job, and a willing pair of hands is welcome. Do it the way you're shown.
- **Don't romanticize.** The settlement is beautiful, but the beauty comes from a fight that never ends. The people who live here don't feel picturesque. They feel busy. Ask questions, listen, and don't treat their home as a ruin, a museum, or a metaphor.
- **Doors swell in the wet** and have to be shouldered open. Do it gently. Don't force them.
- **Be modest with photography.** Ask before pointing a lens at anyone. The pump house, the canteen, and the schoolroom are working places.
- **Meals:** The canteen's steam is always pouring, and you can expect a hearty, spiced meal built from chilies and greens from the rooftop gardens. Eat what you're offered, contribute what you can, and don't waste food.

## The rain, the light, and the daily rhythm

- **Morning:** The crew does the cutting. It's a good time to watch from a distance and see how they work. The heron is on the outfall wall.
- **Midday:** Heat and humidity peak. Rest if you can.
- **Afternoon:** Rain, most days. The roofs drum a wet, thick sound like fingers on a stretched hide. Everything speaks at once. Take shelter under a roof or in the canteen, and enjoy it.
- **Evening:** Yellow lamps, haloed by damp. The frogs sit silent in the duckweed. The pumps keep on.
- **Night:** The thudding goes through the floors. Sleep to it.

## The psychological part

Most visitors find the place calming after the first day and unsettling after the fourth. The noise stops being noise and becomes the shape of the world. Then you notice that everyone's life depends on it not stopping, and that everyone knows the arrangement can't last forever, and everyone knows it will last longer than they will. That awareness sits in the settlement like weather.

If the pumps ever stop while you're there, even briefly, you will feel it in your body before you understand it. The whole settlement will wake at once. Do not run. Do not shout. Stand where you are, listen, and go where you're directed. Have your exit plan ready, know the path to the high ground, and keep your pack packed. The stories say the people lay listening to the sound of the water thinking about coming in. You'll do the same. It's probably a false alarm. The pumps have always started again.

## Leaving

- Dry your gear as soon as you cross back. Spread everything out, wash your boots, and check your skin, especially your feet and any cuts.
- Check your clothing and bags for seeds, spores, snails, and stowaway insects.
- Keep the earplugs for a few days. You'll find silence strange, and you may catch yourself listening for the thud.
- Write down what you remember while the sound is still in your body. It fades faster than you'd expect.

The Sump at Harrow Lock doesn't ask much of visitors: patience, respect, and dry socks. Give it those, and you'll leave with something rare, a clear picture of what it looks like when people and a machine and a jungle all agree, for now, to keep a river where it belongs.
