# Preparing to Visit the Ossuary Plateau

## First, understand what kind of danger this is

The plateau is not violent. Nothing there will bite you, chase you, poison you, or collapse under you. The animals are slow, mineral, and attentive rather than hostile. The usual survival instincts (watch for predators, avoid the storm, find shelter) will mostly go unused, and that is a hazard in itself, because the real threats are quiet:

1. **The mountain**: 5,000 meters of altitude, cold, and dryness.
2. **The supply problem**: nothing there can sustain you.
3. **The settling**: the place's tendency to make you fit, to turn you into one more repeated, low-resolution, counted thing.

Prepare for the first two the way you would for any high-altitude expedition. Prepare for the third the way you would for a long stay in a very persuasive room.

## Physical preparation

### Altitude
- You arrive by several days of climbing, so use them. Ascend slowly and camp lower than your highest point each night. Above about 3,000 meters, add sleeping elevation gently, no more than 300 to 500 meters a day.
- Spend at least a week or two acclimatizing on a lower world or a lower shelf before the final approach. The plateau's stillness makes altitude sickness easy to misread: headache, nausea, and slow thinking can feel like the place working on you when it is only thin air. Learn the difference in advance.
- Bring supplemental oxygen and a regulator, even if you don't plan to use it. Stillness and cold make hypoxia sneaky. Carry a pulse oximeter and check it on a schedule.
- Bring a pressurized bag, or the equivalent, and medication for altitude illness. The plateau offers no easy descent, because the road down is days long.

### Cold and dryness
- The air is cool rather than brutal, but you will be there long enough for it to matter. Wear layers: a moisture-wicking base, an insulating mid-layer, and a wind shell. The steady west wind never changes, so a good windproof outer layer matters more than heavy insulation.
- Frost forms before dawn on north-facing stone. Your tent, boots, and sleeping bag will get a rime. Use a sleeping bag rated well below what you expect, plus a vapor barrier liner.
- Dryness will crack your lips and dry your throat. Bring heavy lip balm, saline nasal spray, eye drops, and skin cream. Drink on a schedule rather than by thirst, because thirst is unreliable at altitude.
- Sun exposure is a lesser but real concern at that elevation. The light is described as ordinary, but thin air filters less, so bring sunglasses with side shields and sunscreen.

### Water, food, and fuel
- **Carry all your water in, or the means to make it.** The plateau has no usable water. The frost sublimes without ever becoming wet, and what forms is not something to gather. Bring a stove and fuel to melt any ice you brought, plus a dependable supply. Budget generously: four to five liters a day at altitude, and more with exertion. Bring more than the trip needs, since you will not be able to replace any.
- **Bring all your food.** Nothing there is edible, and the fauna's chemistry is built on oils, resins, and silica rather than water. Do not attempt to eat, taste, or cook anything native. Don't lick the frost either, however much your tongue tastes of flint. Bring dense, familiar, high-calorie food, and plan for reduced appetite at altitude.
- Bring fuel for the stove with a margin. The cold will eat it faster than you expect.
- Pack out all waste, including human waste. Leaving organic material on ground where the biology is different and the population is this dense is, at best, rude and, at worst, something you cannot predict. Use a bag system.

### Navigation and equipment
- Bring a compass, paper maps of the approach, and a notebook for your route log. Electronic navigation should be backup only. Distance is unreliable on the plateau, so a landmark that looks an hour away may be five minutes off, or the other way round.
- Bring a rope or long cord and use it (see below).
- Bring a simple mechanical watch and a small, reliable timer. Time on the plateau appears to loop: the light at each hour feels identical to the same hour yesterday. A mechanical watch can help you tell whether you have been there one day or ten.
- Bring a first-aid kit that includes altitude medications, a splint, and a way to signal for help.

## The settling: how to remain yourself

This is the core problem. The plateau's logic is copying. It reduces things to repeats, then to outlines. Anything that stays long enough becomes one more entry in the pattern. Your defense is to stay distinct, and to keep being someone new.

### Rules of conduct
1. **Do not stay long.** Plan for a short visit, days rather than weeks. There is a warning in the text: you may stay a week and not age, and that is not reassuring. Timeless is the opposite of a safe outcome. The moment a gap opens near you, a space just your size, is the moment to be leaving.
2. **Never rest in a way that lets the ground take you.** Don't sit for long in one spot. Don't sleep in the middle of a herd. Set up camp on the edge, and keep your route out open behind you.
3. **Don't accept the arrangement.** If a heron leans to make room, move away. If a gap appears, walk out of it. Do not step into the space that has been opened for you.
4. **Do not walk toward the figure who looks like you.** You may see it from a dome, standing among the herons, slightly out of focus. Don't approach. Don't wave, call, or acknowledge it. Do not let yourself be counted.
5. **Don't count.** Counting is a trap here. If you tally the animals in a hollow you will get an answer that never changes, and you will feel your own count being fixed in place. Decline the exercise. If a companion insists on counting, treat that as a warning sign.
6. **Don't touch the made things.** The cairns, the hut, and the carved tallies have been absorbed, and the people who made them have either stayed or left so completely that no one can say which. Do not enter the ring of animals around the hut. Do not read the tally to its end. Do not wear or take beads, and do not accept any object from a hare-thing.
7. **Leave nothing behind.** A cairn you build, a stone you set, or a line you scratch will be taken into the pattern, and so may you.

### Techniques for keeping distinctness
- **Make yourself unrepeatable.** Repetition is how the place spreads, so keep introducing genuine difference. Vary your route, your pace, and your routine. Do different things in a different order each day. Talk aloud, sing, and tell stories that nobody has told before. Songs are useful: the plateau is nearly silent, and your footsteps already seem rude. Make them rude anyway. You are the only new thing there.
- **Keep a log with details only you would know.** Write down small, specific, personal facts: what you ate, the joke you thought of, the name of a street from home. Reread them each evening. If the log starts to feel like it was written by someone else, or begins to repeat itself, leave.
- **Bring a companion, or better, several, who are very different from you and from each other.** A group of near-identical people, such as a uniformed team or a family who share features, is at higher risk of being copied. Diversity of temperament and appearance helps. Check on each other constantly. Establish a code phrase, a personal question that only the real person would answer, and use it whenever you doubt someone.
- **Bring an anchor to elsewhere.** Carry an object from home that is unmistakably yours and unmistakably not of this place: a specific ring, a weathered book, a photograph. Keep it in your hand often. Have a plan for what you will do the moment it feels unfamiliar.
- **Keep a tether.** Tie a rope to a fixed point outside the herd, ideally at your last camp or at the plateau's edge, and pay it out as you walk. Do not rely on cairns or other markers, since they will be absorbed and the animals will close the gaps behind you. A rope may be nudged aside, but it will leave a path. Consider a bright, non-native, obviously artificial marker, and check it often.
- **Do not lose sight of your exit.** The gaps close behind you, and distance is loose. Take bearings at every stage, and log them.

### On the animals
- Walk carefully in the gaps and step over nothing. Do not climb on, lean on, or push against any creature. Stacking is strict, and disturbing the order may matter in ways you cannot see.
- Do not shine bright lights into their eyes or make sudden loud noises. The nearest herd will turn its heads toward a creak; imagine what it would do with a shout. They do not seem to react with fear or hunger, but you should not test this.
- Assume that any physical contact could leave residue, resin, or something worse. Wear gloves and eye protection near the herds, and keep your sleeves and pant cuffs sealed. Their tissues are a mineral-resin composite carrying sulfur compounds and dissolved silica, which are not friendly to skin or lungs at close range.
- Keep your voice down. The hush is not just atmosphere. Quiet is the baseline, and you will feel the room full of people who know something about you. Do not try to hold long eye contact with any of them.

## Mental preparation

- **Expect the uncanny, and don't fight it.** Counting will fail, shadows will disagree, and scale will slip. These are not signs you are going mad. They are how the place works. Practice ahead of time by reading descriptions and rehearsing the sensation of the stair that isn't there, so you can shrug and keep walking.
- **Watch for the calm.** The most dangerous feeling on the plateau is peace. If you begin to feel restful, unhurried, and comfortably accepted, treat it as a warning. You should be a little bit alarmed. If you are not, walk out.
- **Have a hard stopping rule before you leave.** Decide in advance, with your companions, what conditions will end the trip: a specific number of days, a specific symptom, a specific sighting. Write it down. The place will make lingering feel reasonable, so the decision has to be made elsewhere.

## Recommended kit checklist

- Oxygen system, oximeter, altitude medication
- Layered clothing, windproof shell, insulated sleeping system, vapor barrier liner
- Sun and eye protection, lip and skin care, saline spray
- Stove, fuel, water, and enough food for the entire trip plus margin
- Waste bags and pack-out supplies
- Compass, paper maps, notebook, pencil
- Long rope or cord, bright artificial markers
- Mechanical watch and timer
- Gloves, eye protection, sealed cuffs
- First-aid kit and emergency signaling gear
- Personal anchor objects
- Multiple, distinct companions

## Final thought

Most places in the multiverse test whether you can survive. This one tests whether you can remain particular. The air is thin and the water is absent, but those are ordinary problems with ordinary solutions. The real preparation is deciding, long before you climb, that you are one unrepeatable person who will walk in, make some noise, look around, and leave before the ground finds room for you.
