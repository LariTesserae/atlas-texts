# Preparing for Karrow Cut

## The short version

Karrow Cut is not a dangerous place in the way a war zone or a hostile jungle is. It is a place where several ordinary hazards are stacked closely together: scalding steam, sharp rock, cold wet air, low ground that might collect bad gas, and a spring on an eleven-day rhythm that changes the character of the whole valley. Come dressed for a cold drizzle, treat every pipe as though it could burn you, keep away from the cliff bases, and plan your visit around the pulse.

## Timing: decide where in the cycle you want to be

The Chime cycle is the most important variable. If your travel method lets you choose your arrival, choose deliberately.

**Arrive at or near ebb** if it's your first visit. The pipes are cool, the mills idle, the mist is thin, and the residents have time for strangers. The frogs are at their loudest, so you'll hear them from the moment you arrive. Movement is easy, and you can learn the layout while nothing is screaming or venting.

**Plan to witness the Chime, but not to be caught unprepared by it.** At full flow the valley fills with mist to the rim, the pipes shriek, the mills run fast, and the cliff faces stream with water. Visibility drops, sound becomes disorienting, and valves blow off at unpredictable moments. Watching it from a fixed, sheltered, known position, such as a window in a terrace or a spot the locals recommend, is very different from wandering the meadow in it. Ask someone where they watch it from.

**Do not arrive blind at the peak** unless you have an experienced local to meet you. Finding your way through a mile of fogged meadow with steam venting overhead and no idea where the drainage cuts are is the likeliest way for a visitor to get hurt.

Build in slack. The pulse runs about eleven days, so a stay shorter than that misses part of the story. A stay of twelve to fourteen days lets you see a full swell and ebb. Also bring enough supplies to stay a few days longer than planned. The place is odd enough that getting out on schedule may not be entirely in your hands.

## Clothing and personal gear

The air is cool and damp all the time, with a brief afternoon rain and a hot bright sun right after it. Cotton will stay wet. Dress in layers that stay warm when damp.

- **Base and mid layers:** wool or a good synthetic. Bring a spare set kept dry in a sealed bag.
- **Outer shell:** a properly waterproof, breathable jacket and trousers. The rain is light but constant, and condensation drips on you from every surface even when it isn't raining.
- **Hat and thin gloves:** the rim winds carry mist sideways.
- **Cut-resistant gloves.** The text says the cliff edges can cut a glove, so bring gloves rated for it. Leather over a cut-resistant liner is ideal. You'll want them any time you use your hands on rock, and freshly broken basalt is glass-sharp.
- **Boots:** waterproof, with aggressive grip and ankle support. The floor is mud and wet sedge, the ledges are moss-slick, and the basalt is wet-slick. Leather with a good sole will do. Consider gaiters for the sedge and mud.
- **Eye protection:** clear safety glasses for anywhere near cliffs, where chips can fly, and near the Boiler House, where a valve can blow off without warning.
- **Sun protection:** after the rain the sun returns at full brightness. A brimmed hat and sunglasses are worth the space.
- **Insect defense:** wet sedge meadows, alder carr, and standing water mean midges and mosquitoes, and possibly ticks. Bring repellent and a head net if you're sensitive.
- **Towel and fast-drying spare socks.** You'll be glad of them every day.

A sun hat and a wool cap in the same pack will both get used in a single afternoon.

## Hearing

The valley is loud in layers: pipe ticking, mill hammering, valves blowing off and echoing back doubled, and at dusk the frog chorus. During the Chime the pipes ring in a slow chord.

- Bring good earplugs and use them at the mills and near the Boiler House. Prolonged exposure to a stamping press, even a syncopated and picturesque one, damages hearing.
- Bring a second pair for sleeping. At ebb, frogs in the thousands will vibrate the meadow all night.
- Don't wear earplugs everywhere, though. The chorus, the starlings mimicking the chord, and the tick of the pipes are part of what you came for, and they warn you of changes in pressure. A change in the pitch or rhythm of the pipes tells you the flow is shifting. Learn to listen to it.

## Steam, hot water, and heat hazards

This is the hazard most likely to hurt you.

- **Assume every pipe is scalding.** The lagging is rope and tar-cloth, and the joints are wrapped and algae-green. Do not lean on, hang things on, or climb the trestles. Do not stand beneath a joint or a valve.
- **Valves blow off in bursts.** Learn where they are and don't stand in the line of one. The residents will know the usual ones.
- **Water at 60 °C rises at the north end.** That's hot enough to cause serious scalds within seconds. The meadow near the spring has hot seeps under a rusty red skin, and the mud is warm there. Do not step off marked paths near the Boiler House, because a crust can hide a hot seep.
- **Test before you sit.** Moss cushions are thick enough to sit in, and near warm rock they may hold hot water.
- **The tram is charged with steam and has no fire.** That makes it safer than a locomotive, but treat its pressure vessels as you would any others. Board and alight where locals do.

## Gas and air

The valley floor is low, enclosed by cliffs, and volcanic in character. Geothermal springs can carry hydrogen sulfide, carbon dioxide, or other gases, and in still air at night these can pool in the low pockets, drainage cuts, and cellars.

- The mud is described as faintly sulfurous. That's normal. A smell that suddenly gets stronger, or a smell that *vanishes* while you're near a vent, is not normal. H₂S deadens smell at higher concentrations.
- Bring a **personal multi-gas monitor** (H₂S, CO₂, O₂, at a minimum), if your travel rules allow it. Keep it dry.
- Don't sleep in a basement, cellar, or ground-floor room without asking locals whether it's safe. Prefer upper floors.
- Be wary of hollows, sumps, and drains at night, and during still, foggy conditions.
- If you feel sudden headache, dizziness, or a strange calm in a low place, climb, don't sit. Leave the low ground immediately.

Locals have lived here for generations and will know which places to avoid. Ask.

## Rock and fault hazards

The fault slipped within living memory, and the valley walls are unweathered.

- **Stay out from under the cliffs** whenever you can. Slabs still fall. The locals remember each fall for months. Frost, rain, and roots are only beginning to loosen the columns.
- Never climb the basalt. The columns lean, the fractures are fresh, the moss hides loose edges, and everything is wet.
- Don't camp or rest at the base of the walls.
- Keep away from the rim edge in wind or mist. The cliff lips drip and are slick.
- Be prepared for tremors. A young fault can move again. Know your route out of any building, and don't shelter under overhangs. Ask the residents what they do and follow it.
- Don't pick up conchoidal fragments as souvenirs without care. The edges are razor sharp.

## Water, food, and health

- **Bring your own drinking water and purification.** The meadow is full of voles, shrews, frogs, deer, and herons. That means leptospirosis and other water-borne risks. Never drink from drainage cuts or standing water, and never assume hot spring water is safe just because it's hot. It's mineral-rich and may carry dissolved metals or bacteria that thrive in warm water. Boil or filter anything you're given unless the locals show it's from a good source.
- **Do not soak in any spring water unless locals recommend a specific pool.** Warm mineral water can carry *Naegleria*-type amoebae or *Legionella*. Keep your head out of it, and don't let it go up your nose.
- **Cover cuts immediately.** Between rusty iron, sharp rock, mud, and animal droppings, a small cut is an infection risk. Be current on tetanus vaccination, and carry a proper wound kit with antiseptic and waterproof dressings.
- **Food:** the settlement makes rapeseed oil, felt, and tin goods, and keeps hens and allotments. Expect plain, hearty food. Bring some emergency rations and electrolytes, in case your stomach dislikes the local water or diet.
- **Medications:** bring enough for a stay longer than planned, plus a general kit with antihistamines, anti-diarrheals, and pain relief.
- **Skin and joint care:** persistent damp can cause immersion foot and rashes. Dry your feet at night and change socks.

## Electronics and equipment

- Everything is damp. Use waterproof cases and desiccant packs.
- Steam and mist will fog lenses and cameras. Bring lens cloths and let equipment acclimatize before you take it out.
- There's no reason to expect electricity, network coverage, or charging. Bring a manual backup for anything essential, such as a paper map, a compass, and a whistle.
- Carry a headlamp with spare batteries. The valley is deep, sunset comes early on the floor, and the cliffs shorten daylight.
- A small notebook in waterproof paper is worth it. This is a place to record sounds and rhythms.

## Getting around

- The **tram** coasts down the line on stored steam. It's slow and getting slower, and locals love it for that. Ride it. Ask about the schedule, which is shaped by the recharge at each mill and possibly by the pulse. At the peak it may run faster, at ebb slower.
- **Walking** is the main way to travel. The valley is about a mile long, with long stretches of open meadow between structures. Stay on raised paths and tram beds where you can, and carry a light.
- The hardest part is navigation in mist. During the Chime the mist fills the valley to the rim. Use the pipe line as a handrail in your mind (but not with your hands). The pipe runs the whole length of the valley, and if you know which way is the spring and which is the coast, you can always orient yourself by following the trestles from a safe distance.

## Social preparation

The settlement is small: about forty structures, terraces set apart from one another, allotments, hen runs, washing lines. It is not a tourist site. You will be noticed.

- **Announce yourself and be humble.** Find the Boiler House keeper, a mill foreman, or whoever handles visitors, and ask for guidance. They know the cycle, the hot seeps, the fall zones, and the safe spots.
- **Be useful.** Hands are welcome at laundry days, market days, and repairs. The pipes are patched, repainted, and patched again, and the work is constant.
- **Trade goods.** Bring things a steam-and-brick community can use without being disrupted by them: good needles, steel wire, sound tools, dried fruit, tea, spices, salt, high-quality thread, seeds. Avoid anything that will confuse local economies or technology, such as batteries, plastics, or advanced medicines you can't explain. A tin lid or a felt blanket bought locally is a more fitting gift to take home than anything you might give.
- **Take the pulse seriously.** The Chime sets market days, laundry days, and, older women say, births. Don't dismiss it as superstition. It organizes the community, and ignoring its rhythm will make you inconvenient.
- **Be gentle with the birds.** Starlings, jackdaws, and swifts nest in the dead stacks, and bats pour from the Boiler House eaves. The nests are part of the fabric of the place. Don't disturb them, don't climb the stacks, and don't feed the gulls.
- **Language and manners:** you don't know the local tongue or customs. Learn a few phrases, be patient, and follow the lead of others.
- **Photographing people** without asking is rude anywhere. Ask first.
- **The mimicry of the Chime by the starlings** is a local wonder. Don't try to explain the origin ("which came first, the birds or the pipes") or press the question. Nobody knows, and it's part of the charm.

## Multiverse-specific precautions

- **Anchor your return.** Know precisely how you're getting out. The pulse is unexplained, and a spring that pulses on an eleven-day rhythm for no known reason may be tied to something more than geology. If your travel method is sensitive to geothermal, tectonic, or resonant conditions, test it before committing. Carry a way of leaving that doesn't depend on the local environment being in a particular phase.
- **Check the resonance.** If your device or gateway uses harmonic or acoustic tuning, the Chime's chord may interfere. Test this at ebb, well away from the peak.
- **Leave a trail.** Tell someone where you are and for how long. Carry a beacon if you can.
- **Minimize footprint.** This place has had only a few generations to become what it is. Don't leave litter, don't introduce seeds, and take care with boots and gear that carry spores or insects from elsewhere. The moss, sedges, and the small ecosystem are the thing you came to see.
- **Say little about other worlds.** The residents here are absorbed in their pulse and their pipes. You don't need to unsettle that.

## A suggested packing list

**Wear and carry:** waterproof shell, layered wool, spare dry set, warm hat, gloves (cut-resistant), boots with good grip, gaiters, brimmed hat, sunglasses, safety glasses.

**Safety:** gas monitor, headlamp and spares, whistle, first-aid kit with wound care, water purification, earplugs (two pairs), repellent, small fire-safe lighter (unlikely to be needed, but don't rely on local supplies), emergency blanket.

**Sustenance:** water and rations for extra days, electrolytes, tea.

**Comfort:** dry bags, desiccant, towels, spare socks, notebook, pencil.

**Social:** trade goods, small gifts, a modest attitude.

## How to walk it

If you can, do what the text suggests: walk from the spring to the coast end at ebb, in the low light after rain. Go slowly. Let the frogs stop as you approach and start again behind you. Listen to the pipes tick above you. Stay off the cliffs and out from under the joints. At the far end, watch the starlings pour from the stack in a black thread. Carry your gloves, your earplugs, your patience, and your questions, and be willing not to have every answer.
