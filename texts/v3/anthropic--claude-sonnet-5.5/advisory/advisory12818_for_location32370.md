# Visiting the Lantern Pocket

## The Right Attitude

The Lantern Pocket is not dangerous the way a volcano or a war is dangerous. It is dangerous the way a sleeping person's house is: nothing is hostile, but everything is arranged around someone else's rhythms, and you can do harm or come to harm through carelessness. Nothing here will chase you. The risks are that you get lost in a room the size of a chapel, that you lose time, that you leave something of yourself behind, or that you take something out that was holding the place together.

Go slowly, quietly, and lightly, like a guest who has arrived early to a solemn event.

## Before You Go

**Practice soft attention.** The room's geometry rests on the attention of whoever is in it. Staring hard, hunting for the exit, or trying to fix the drip's source with your eye makes the room more unstable, or at least more insistent. Practice widening your gaze so that you take in a whole scene without pinning any part of it. Some people already do this while walking in the woods. If you don't, rehearse it for a few weeks in a park or on a long slow walk. You will need to be able to look at a thing and let it stay unresolved.

**Decide what you're willing to lose.** Probably an hour or two. Possibly a day. Possibly a season of your thumbprint, or your reflection's punctuality. Go only if these costs are acceptable, and go alone or with at most one other person. The throat is barely wide enough, and a crowd would crowd the room's attention.

**Tell someone where you are going, and give them a wide window.** The pocket's time pools, so your return time will not be reliable. Tell them: "If I'm not back in three days, wait another three before you worry, and don't come in after me." Nobody should follow you in. The passage may not be the same passage twice.

**Do not go if you have a serious fear of enclosed spaces.** The throat is about shoulder width and its walls give slightly, like hardening dough. The chamber beyond is roomy, but you have to get through the throat to reach it.

## Body and Gear

**Clothing:** Dress for cool, damp air, cold enough to show your breath. Wear layers you can adjust without stripping fully: a thin wicking base, a wool or fleece mid-layer, and a waterproof shell that doesn't rustle. Rustling carries in the held-breath silence and feels rude. Pick a dull grey, tan, or green, nothing bright or reflective. Wear a soft cap or beanie. The throat is tight and the ceiling is cracking overhead.

**Footwear:** Flat-soled, waterproof boots with a wide footprint that spreads your weight over the mud's crust. The plates at the basin's edge are fragile and still lifting. Avoid deep-lug soles, which would gouge the floor and leave tracks the snails would then have to write around. Bring gaiters or tuck your trousers in.

**Hands and knees:** Thin waterproof gloves protect the clay from your skin's oils and keep the clay from taking your fingerprints. Knee pads help in the throat and let you kneel at a chosen height without sinking. Do not press bare hands into the walls. The clay will keep a thumbprint for a season, and you don't want to leave yours anywhere it might be mistaken for part of the local practice.

**Pack:** Keep it small and soft, with no hard frame. Push it ahead of you through the throat on a short lanyard rather than wearing it. A large pack will jam, and jamming in a passage that is still deciding is a bad idea.

**Light:** You won't need a lamp, since the room supplies its own. Carry a small, dim, red-filtered light in a pocket in case the Turning ends and the room goes blue-dark, or in case you need to see your own hands. Do not shine it at the moths, and never sweep it across the walls.

**Eyes and ears:** Bring dark, close-fitting glasses or a soft blindfold for the Turning, when every wet surface blazes. Bring soft foam earplugs too, though they will not fully help. The long glassy note seems to come from inside you as much as from the room. The point of the plugs is to keep you from flinching or crying out, not to shut it out.

**A tether:** Bring a thin cord, about thirty meters, tied to something solid outside the crack, and let it pay out behind you. Do not trust it. Distances lie inside, and the cord may lead you back to the far wall as easily as to the throat. But it is a comfort, it helps you find the entrance at all, and it can be a gentle way to say "I mean to leave." Use natural fiber cord if you can, because if the room keeps it, it will rot rather than persist.

**A home object:** Carry one small, ordinary, unremarkable item from your world in an inner pocket, such as a house key, a pebble from your garden, or a coin. Touch it when you want to reorient. It will not fix the room's geometry, but it will fix you.

**A watch:** Wear a plain mechanical or analog watch, not a digital one. It will lie to you, but it will lie in interesting, useful ways. If it runs slow when you crouch and fast when you stand, you are learning the room's gradients.

**Food and water:** Carry sealed water and a small sealed snack in an inner pocket, for after. Do not eat or drink in the chamber. The ecosystem is a tight circuit turning without hurry, and crumbs, sugar, and foreign microbes will get into it. Do not drink from the pools. They are not level with each other or with anything else, and what they reflect may not be what's above them.

## The Throat

Go in feet first if you can bear it, or head first with your arms in front and your pack pushed ahead of you. Exhale to make yourself narrow, and go a hand's breadth at a time. The walls will press back with a faint, yielding give. That is normal. Do not push against it. Wait a breath, and it will shift.

If you feel the clay begin to tighten, stop. Breathe out, count to seven, and back up a little before moving forward again. The crack is still deciding, so give it time to decide in your favor.

## Inside: How to Move and Behave

**Move at the room's pace.** Walk slowly, with soft steps, placing your feet as if on ice. Speak, if you must, in a whisper. The silence has the pressure of many people being careful, and you are joining them.

**Keep your body at one height.** The pocket's time is stratified. It runs slow in the low places, where the mist pools, and quick near the ceiling. If you crouch for long, your feet and knees will lag behind your head, and if you climb or stretch upward, your hands will race ahead. Stay standing or in a shallow stoop. Do not lie down. Do not sleep on the floor. Do not climb the walls. If you must kneel, do it briefly and then rise, and check that your watch and your pulse agree afterward.

**Breathe deliberately.** Your breath doesn't rise but drifts sideways and pools along the floor. That may be harmless, since the hover-fish graze on it. But pooling breath in a closed low place is exactly how cave air goes bad, so if you feel lightheaded, a little dull at the edges, or yawny, stand straighter and step toward a higher spot. A small handheld CO₂ monitor in your pocket is a sensible precaution even if it reads nonsense. If it does read nonsense, that is information about the room, and you should note it and leave.

**Leave no trace, especially no thumbprint.** Touch nothing that you don't have to. Do not step in the pools. Do not pluck the crystal blades, which are growing audibly and are more delicate than they look. Do not lift a snail, and do not block a track. If a snail's path crosses your boot, wait or step back.

**Do not follow snail-tracks far.** If you follow a track it leads to the snail that made it, and if you keep following, it leads to the snail that will make it. That is a fine thing to observe for a few steps and a poor thing to walk down for long. You could lose your place in sequence.

**Do not answer the reflections.** Some pools show a ceiling that isn't there: an overcast sky, a night with a single low star, or once a face turned away from you, waiting to be noticed. Look, and let the image be. Do not speak to it, wave at it, or wait for the face to turn. If a reflection moves before you do, don't try to catch up with it or trick it. Step back and let your attention soften.

**Do not add your thumbprint to the pillars.** The ring of clay pillars, each with a deep, slightly wrong thumbprint, is a practice in progress, and you have not been asked. Don't press your thumb into the flat tops, don't rearrange the pillars, and don't add a stone to the stack. If a stone is fresh and still being etched, don't try to read the script aloud.

**Do not walk through the arch.** The low arch of interlocked crystal blades leads nowhere, but the air on the far side is warm and smells of rain on hot stone. That is a scent from a different place. It may be a doorway, or it may be the room's own longing. You can stand near it and smell it, but do not crawl through. If it is a passage, it is not one you've been invited to use, and its far end is not a place you can prepare for from here.

**Treat the heron-thing as company.** It is the shape the room makes when it wants to watch itself, and it is not hunting you. If you look straight at it, it is a stalagmite. If you look away, you'll hear a joint click. That is enough. Don't try to trick it into being seen, and don't get so close that you interrupt it. If you do approach and it turns out to be nothing, thank the room quietly and back away.

## The Turning

You will know it is coming when the moths lift, the mist begins to climb in a white column, and the hover-fish spiral up into the glare. You will have only a few breaths.

Close your eyes or put on your dark glasses. Stand still. Keep your feet planted. Do not run for the throat. The drip will land at last, every pool will ring at once, and the long glassy note will fill you until you can't tell whether it is in the room or in your chest. This is not a threat but a kind of release, and the room is exhaling.

Do not try to hold on to the moment or record it. Let it pass through you and out.

When it ends, the light settles, the moths drift down, and the clay sighs. Wait a full slow count of seven before opening your eyes.

## Leaving

Do not hunt for the throat. Most who visit find it only once they've stopped looking. When you feel ready to go, do something small and deliberate to say so. Touch your home object, take a slow breath, thank the room aloud in a whisper, and then let your gaze go soft and turn toward the direction you came from without staring at it. Walk without counting steps. If you find yourself at the far wall, don't argue with it. Rest a moment, then try again. It may take several attempts, and it will take fewer if you are patient than if you are eager.

If your tether is still attached, follow it gently, but don't pull or haul. If it seems to lead nowhere, drop it and trust the soft-eyed method.

When you emerge through the throat, do not look back to check that it's still there.

## After

Sit outside for a while in ordinary light. Eat and drink. Check your watch against a reliable clock, and expect a difference. Check your own hands and feet: do they feel like the same age, and does your reflection in a window move with you or slightly ahead? If it's ahead, wait an hour and check again. It usually settles.

Do not tell the story quickly, and do not try to return the same week. The room is still growing its new passage, and it does not need another visitor before it has finished.
