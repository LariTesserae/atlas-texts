# Preparing for the Loud Table

## The short version

You are visiting a place that is physically gentle and sensorially violent. The rock is stable, the rain is warm, and the slopes are shallow. What hurts people here is noise, thin air, wet cold, fog, and the hidden holes in the floor. Preparing well means protecting your hearing without losing your awareness, arriving acclimatized, staying dry and warm, and never being alone in the fog.

---

## 1. Understand what kind of danger this is

Most dangerous places announce themselves with cliffs, heat, or predators. The Table lulls you. The ground is smooth and broad, the horizon is friendly, and the rain is warm. The real hazards are:

- **Altitude.** Nearly 5,000 meters is high enough to make an unacclimatized person seriously ill.
- **Sensory overload.** You cannot hear speech, warnings, or your own companions.
- **Hidden voids.** Slots roofed by rock bridges, and wells masked by moss.
- **Weather that reverses in minutes.** Sun, then cloud, then fog, then wind from the opposite side.
- **Disorientation in fog.** Sound and sight both fail, and the horizon you were navigating by is gone.
- **Slow hypothermia.** Wet, wind, and altitude cool you while you feel mild.

If you treat it as a pleasant upland walk, it will hurt you. If you treat it as a high-altitude expedition into a working musical instrument, you'll be fine.

---

## 2. Before you go: your body

**Acclimatize first.** This is the most important step, and it happens before you arrive. If your entry point into this world puts you at the plateau's altitude, spend days at a similar elevation elsewhere beforehand. If your crossing point is lower, for example in the lowland forests below the rim, spend several days climbing in stages, with rest days, before reaching the top. Then:

- Bring altitude medication if your medicine permits it. Discuss it with a physician first.
- Know the signs: headache, nausea, poor sleep, and breathlessness at rest. Severe signs are confusion, stumbling, and a wet cough. If they appear, go down. Nothing on the Table is worth staying for.
- Drink steadily. The thin, dry air on louder days pulls water out of you quietly.
- Avoid alcohol and heavy sedatives.

**Get a hearing baseline.** If your home world has audiologists, see one. Plan for hearing protection you can trust, and accept that some of your noise exposure will be at damaging levels regardless.

**Prepare for infrasound.** The deepest tones arrive as pressure in your chest and teeth. Some people find low-frequency vibration merely uncomfortable. Others get nausea, anxiety, dizziness, and a nameless dread. Expect to feel unwell for the first day. Know that this is the plateau, not a personal failing, and that it fades or at least becomes tolerable.

**Vaccinations and biology.** You have no idea what lives in the tank-water, moss, or frogs. Standard inoculations help, and a broad first-aid kit with antihistamines, anti-diarrheals, and wound care is mandatory. Assume anything unfamiliar can sting, cause rashes, or make you sick.

## 3. Sound: the central problem

### Protect your hearing

The wall of sound is dangerous over hours and days. Do not rely on bravery.

- **Layer protection.** Bring high-rated foam or molded earplugs, and wear over-ear defenders over them when standing near slots, wells, or the rim. The locals use wax and plant-down plugs, which suggests those work on the local frequency mix. Trade for a set if you can, but carry your own too.
- **Know that plugs won't stop the bass.** Low tones travel through bone and flesh. Sound will still reach you through your sternum and skull. That is unpleasant but also useful, since it is how you feel gusts coming.
- **Pay attention to pitch, not volume.** A swell of pitch underfoot warns of a big gust. Learn this early. If your hearing protection is so complete that you cannot feel the chord bend, you've lost a warning system.
- **Rest your ears.** Spend time in fog, in lee hollows, or beside the tarns, where the noise feels distant. Build rest into each day. Treat quiet places as recovery zones.
- **Watch for symptoms.** Ringing, muffled hearing, ear fullness, and headache all mean you need a break and probably more protection.

### Learn to communicate without voice

You cannot shout across a room. Learn a **basic hand-signal vocabulary** before you go, and better, learn the local one from the people who live here. At minimum, agree with your party on signals for:

- stop / go / slow
- water / danger / hole
- turn back
- I'm cold / I'm sick / I'm fine
- fog coming
- gather to me
- emergency

Practice until it's automatic. Also bring:

- **Waterproof slates or notebooks** for written messages.
- **A whiteboard or chalk** for tent and hut conversations.
- **Bright cloth signals** such as flags and armbands, in strong colors that show against dark rock and green moss.
- **Headlamps with flash modes** for signaling in fog and dusk.
- **Tug codes** on a shared rope. Decide what one, two, and three pulls mean.

Their faces are expressive because faces are how they speak. Be visible. Take off your hood or mask when you need to communicate, look directly at people, and exaggerate your expressions.

## 4. Clothing and gear

The plateau is mild but the wet and wind steal heat. Dress for cool, damp, windy, and sunny in the same hour.

**Layers:**
- Moisture-wicking base layers, ideally synthetic or wool, never cotton.
- An insulating mid-layer that still works when damp.
- A **wind- and rain-proof shell**. The rain is warm and fine, so breathability matters more than heavy waterproofing. Also make sure it does not flap, since flapping fabric adds to the noise and wears you out. Cut, tape, or secure loose parts.
- A warm hat, a sun hat with a chin strap, gloves, and a buff.
- Sunglasses or glacier glasses. UV at this altitude is severe, and cloud does not stop it.
- Sunscreen and lip protection. You'll burn under overcast skies.

**Footwear:**
- Sturdy boots with **aggressive tread**. The rock is smooth, and wet cushion-moss over rock is slippery in the way that ice is.
- Waterproof or fast-drying. Your feet will be damp constantly.
- Gaiters help with the wet moss.
- Spare socks in waterproof bags.

**Other kit:**
- A sturdy pack with rain cover, and dry-bag liners for everything.
- **Trekking poles**. They let you probe moss and dubious surfaces ahead of you and give stability in gusts.
- A helmet, if you'll go near slots or rock bridges. Loose stone can drop.
- **A rope**, at least 30 to 50 meters, plus harnesses, carabiners, and a way to tie your party together in fog.
- Headlamps and spare batteries, keeping the cells warm.
- A reliable **mechanical compass** and paper maps or route notes. Electronics may fail, drain quickly in cold and damp, or simply not work in a foreign world.
- A **whistle** you can use, plus a second and third. Rule 4 is not decoration.
- A small emergency **signal mirror** and bright markers.
- First-aid kit including altitude, hypothermia, and fall injuries.
- A repair kit: cord, tape, needle, spare buckles.

## 5. Shelter

**Do not rely on a normal tent.** Gusts change direction without warning, and a tent that flaps in this wind is loud enough to make sleep impossible, or it will simply tear.

- If you bring a tent, make it a **low-profile, four-season, storm-rated dome** with many guy points. Stake it into gaps in the rock. Where the stone is bare, use rock anchors, heavy stones on the guy lines, and pitch it in the lee of a dome with the door away from the wind.
- Site selection matters more than gear. Avoid slots, wells, and rock bridges, since they are hazardous and their tones carry into your sleep. Avoid the open crown of a dome. A lee dish or a hollow is better, but check drainage, because rain films run through the low points.
- **Never camp near an old stone pier.** More on this below.

**The windbreak huts** are the best shelter available: low, round, corbelled stone with turf roofs, and doors turned from the wind. But they belong to someone. They're empty most of the year, not abandoned. Treat them as you would a stranger's house:

- Use them only when necessary, or with permission if you can obtain it.
- Leave them cleaner than you found them.
- Replace any firewood or supplies you use, and leave a small gift.
- Do not disturb the painted hand on the wall.

Sleeping is hard. The noise, the pressure, and the altitude all interfere. Bring earplugs you can sleep in, a warm sleeping bag rated well below what seems needed, and an insulated pad. Expect broken rest, and build spare days into your itinerary.

## 6. Food, water, and fire

**Water is abundant but not free of risk.**
- The tank-plants hold liters of clear cold water, and locals live off them. But they are also nurseries for larvae, algae, and small frogs. Filter and purify everything, using a filter and chemical treatment or boiling, because you don't know the local microbiology.
- Take water from the **tarns** only after checking for animal signs, and treat it too.
- **Don't harm the tanks.** They are living habitats and the locals rely on them. Take modestly and never strip a plant.
- Don't handle the jewel-colored frogs bare-handed. Bright color often means toxins. Assume they're poisonous until proven otherwise.

**Food:**
- Carry all the food you'll need, plus a safety margin. High-energy, easy-to-eat items are best. Altitude suppresses appetite, so bring things you'll actually eat: nuts, dried fruit, dense bars, and warm drinks.
- The stilt-palm pith, seed-pods, and wild tubers are local food, and they may be edible or may need preparation. **Don't experiment.** Never eat anything you can't identify with a local guide. The wild tubers are noted as small and bitter, and unfamiliar plants can carry serious toxins.

**Fire:**
- Wood is scarce, wet, and precious. Don't burn living plants. Carry a **stove and fuel**, protected from wind, and cook in the lee of a dome or inside a hut with good ventilation.
- Never run a stove in a sealed shelter. Carbon monoxide is quiet, and the noise will hide any warning.

## 7. Movement and navigation

### The walking rules

1. **Probe before you step** onto moss-covered ground near any joint, dish edge, or bridge. Use poles.
2. **Stay off rock bridges** unless you know they're stable. Drape-vines and moss can hide how thin they are.
3. **Keep away from the edges of slots and wells.** Some are narrow enough to step into by accident.
4. **Never enter a slot canyon in rising wind.** This is rule 3 on the list, and it's not negotiable. The tones climb, pressure builds, and then the wind is squeezed through. You could be knocked down, pinned, or simply overwhelmed. If you feel the pitch rising, leave upwind and up.
5. **Stay roped or within sight** of a partner in any low visibility.
6. **Move in small groups**, ideally of four or more, so that an injury doesn't leave anyone alone.
7. **Don't run.** The domes are smooth, the moss is slick, and falls can send you into a joint.

### Navigating by ear and eye

- The **whistling cairns** are the local way-finding. Each bone has its own note. A high double note means water 200 paces east, for example. Learn the local system from the people if possible, and photograph or sketch each cairn for your own records. Do not disturb them.
- **Record your route obsessively**: bearings, landmarks, cairns, and time. Rock-and-moss country looks similar in all directions.
- Stay on **known routes** between fog refuges. Wandering off may take you across unmapped joints.
- If the multiverse anchor you used to enter is a fixed point, mark it clearly, and know a bearing back to it from anywhere you go. Never lose the way home.

## 8. Weather

Expect everything. Weather turns in minutes.

- **Cloud builds fast.** When a gray thumbprint forms, you have limited time. Decide whether to shelter, move to a lee hollow, or continue.
- **Wind reverses.** Don't assume the direction you faced when you set off is the direction it will come from later. Camp and rest accordingly.
- **Lightning.** It mostly flickers within the clouds, but that's not a guarantee. At 5,000 meters you are close to the cloud. If the storm builds:
  - Get off high, open domes.
  - Avoid isolated tall objects like tin-leaf giants and standing stones.
  - Crouch on an insulating pad if caught in the open, and keep your party spread out.
  - Stay away from wet slots and wells.
- **Rain is warm and brief**, but the shifting water films across the rock can carry you off balance or wet gear you needed dry.
- **Downdrafts** can hit suddenly. Keep three points of contact if you're on any slope, however gentle.

## 9. The fog

The fog will be your rest and your greatest risk.

**What it offers:** when the low cloud is really down, the roar drops away. Your hearing recovers. You can talk again, at close range. You can rest, think, and sleep. The locals treat it as their sanctuary and travel in the fog seasons for this reason.

**What it takes:** almost everything else.

- **Visibility drops sharply.**
- **Sound vanishes.** Whistles carry only a few paces, and voices even less. Your own footsteps sound dull and near. Shouting doesn't help. This is why rule 4 says to carry something that whistles, but you should also understand its limit: in the densest fog, a whistle may only reach a few paces. Combine it with other signaling.
- **Your sense of direction fails.** With no horizon and no sound cues, you'll drift.

**Fog protocol:**
1. **Stop moving** when it thickens, unless you are on a known, safe route.
2. **Rope up** or hold onto a partner. Never separate.
3. If lost, **stay put**. Wandering blind on a plateau riddled with hidden slots is far more dangerous than waiting.
4. Signal with light, whistle, and, if you have it, a **tap or knock code** against rock, which carries better through solid material.
5. Use the quiet to **check yourselves**: hydrate, eat, warm up, and reassess.
6. Wait for the fog to lift or for the locals, who move confidently in it, to find you.

## 10. Plants and animals

- **Don't touch what you can't identify.** Leaf edges like sheet tin can cut. The hollow seed-pods are fragile and loud. The cushion-mat is a living carpet, so tread carefully, and camp on bare rock where you can.
- **Tin-leaf groves:** beautiful and noisy. Don't shelter under them in lightning, and don't damage them.
- **Drape-vines** can be weight-bearing or not. Don't use them as handholds without testing.
- **Frogs and beetles:** treat as potentially toxic. Wash your hands after any contact with plants or water sources.
- **The thick-coated, nearly blind grazers** move silently through fog and may not notice you until you're close. Startled large animals are dangerous even if herbivorous. Give them a wide berth, stay still, and don't try to touch them.
- **The heavy, scaled, deaf sleeper in the slots** is the best reason of all to stay out of slots. It can't hear you, so it can't be warned of you. Never step into a shadowed crevice without looking.
- **The high birds** are harmless and a good weather indicator. Many soaring birds coming down means a change is near.

## 11. The people

Very few, but if you meet them, this matters more than anything else in your gear.

- **Approach slowly and visibly.** Don't shout, and don't grab. Use open hands, calm gestures, and clear, friendly expressions.
- **Learn their signals.** Even a few basic gestures such as greeting, thanks, and peace go a long way. Be humble about mistakes.
- **Don't raise your voice** in any setting. The fog is for quiet and private song. Don't intrude on it.
- **Bring modest gifts** that are useful and non-disruptive: needles, cord, salt, good stone tools, a bit of metal. Native copper is prized, so don't offer flashy items that disrupt their economy. Do not offer anything that might introduce disease or change their way of life.
- **Don't take photos** or draw them without clear permission.
- **Respect the painted hands** and other marks. They're personal and possibly sacred.
- **Ask, don't assume,** about routes, water, and huts. They know the Table in ways no map does.
- **Health:** you carry unfamiliar pathogens. If you're ill, stay away from them. A cold to you could be catastrophic to a small isolated group.

## 12. The piers

Rule 5: *The old piers are not to be slept near.* Nobody says why.

You don't need to know why. You need to obey. Whatever the reason, whether it's the sound, something in the stone, an old ritual, or a real hazard, the locals avoid building near the piers, and they've survived here for generations. Treat this as a hard rule:

- Don't camp or rest within sight or earshot of a pier.
- Don't climb on them, block their tops, or fill their hollows.
- If a particular wind comes and one sounds its cutting note, don't be curious. Move away, cover your ears, and don't approach out of fascination.
- If you find yourself near one at dusk, keep walking.

---

## 13. The rim

The edge is spectacular and dangerous.

- The noise is loudest near the rim, where the wind pours off the plateau. It may be overwhelming. Wear full protection.
- **Gusts near the edge can knock you down or drag you.** Approach on hands and knees in any strong wind, or don't approach at all.
- **Rope up** and anchor to something solid.
- The ground "simply stops." There may be no warning or gentle slope. Don't rely on your eyes in cloud.
- Enjoy the view of the silent forested lowlands below, but do it carefully, with a partner, and with distance from the edge.

## 14. Multiverse-specific preparation

Because you're a traveler between worlds, additional points apply:

- **Your anchor point.** Know exactly how you return, and whether the method works at altitude, in fog, or in strong wind. If it needs a quiet moment, a specific device, or line of sight, plan around the noise and weather.
- **Time and communication.** You may not be able to contact anyone from home. Leave a detailed itinerary with someone you trust, along with a "if I'm not back by X" plan, if it's possible for that to work across worlds.
- **Device reliability.** Assume electronics are unreliable. Carry analog backups for navigation, light, and fire.
- **Local physics.** The world is ancient and stable, but you don't know its subtler oddities. Test small things such as your compass, water boiling temperature, and weather patterns before committing.
- **Translation.** If your world uses magical or technological translators, they won't help with a language of gestures and whistled notes. Don't count on them.
- **Ethics.** Leave nothing behind. Don't trade technology, and don't teach anything that could upset the people's lives. The Table has been at peace with itself for a very long time.

## 15. Sample kit checklist

**Hearing and communication**
- Foam/molded earplugs (several pairs) + over-ear defenders
- Whistles (3+), signal flags, colored armbands
- Slates, waterproof notebook, pencils
- Headlamps + spares
- Signal mirror

**Clothing**
- Base, mid, and shell layers, non-cotton
- Warm hat, sun hat, gloves, buff
- Sunglasses/glacier glasses
- Sturdy boots, gaiters, spare socks

**Shelter and sleep**
- Storm-rated low-profile tent (or plan to use huts responsibly)
- Cold-rated sleeping bag, insulated pad
- Sleep earplugs

**Safety**
- 30–50 m rope, harnesses, carabiners
- Trekking poles, helmet
- Compass, paper maps, route journal
- First-aid kit + altitude medication
- Stove, fuel, lighter/matches
- Water filter + purification tablets

**Food and water**
- Enough high-energy, easy food for the trip + reserve
- Water bottles/bladders, insulated

**Trade and courtesy**
- Small useful gifts, non-disruptive
- Cloth or cord for hut offerings

## 16. A note on what it will feel like

You will hate it at first. The sound is too large to be anything but an assault, and your body will complain about the height. Then something will change. You'll learn to feel the pitch bend before the gust arrives. You'll start reading faces properly for the first time in your life. You'll walk into the fog and find that silence has a texture, that your pulse and the drip of a leaf are enough. And at a black tarn with the whole roaring plateau muted behind glass, you'll understand why the people here don't sing loudly.

Go slowly, go together, and keep your ears rested and your feet on the rock you can trust. The ground never changes and the air never stops. If you plan for both, the Table will let you leave the way you came.
