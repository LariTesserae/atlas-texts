# Preparing to Visit the Cistern Under the Fold

## The right frame of mind

This place is not dangerous in the way a storm or a predator is. It is dangerous the way a flooded cave is: it is completely indifferent to whether you have air. The strangeness (stretching distances, aging knots of root, a tilting room) is gentle, and the gentleness is what makes it risky. Nothing will hurry you, but you will run out of breath on schedule. Prepare as for an overhead-environment dive in a cave that will not hold still, and then add humility.

Nothing is known about this place in the way a survey knows things. Treat every rule below as a reasoned guess and keep a margin for being wrong.

## Before you go: look, don't enter

Your first visit should be a reconnaissance, not a dive.

- Sit at the seam for a few hours. Watch the horsetail, the flow of the weeping water, and the light. Note whether the trickle changes when the hum passes through the rock. You may feel it in your palm against the stone before you hear anything.
- Lower a small waterproof camera and a temperature probe on a thin cord into the crack. See whether the cord comes back longer or shorter than you sent it, or whether the images match the cord. Record the discrepancy and don't argue with it.
- Take a water sample for analysis. You want to know what is in it before your mouth is anywhere near it.
- Learn the crack. It is roughly the width of your forearm and its edges are unweathered and sharp. You cannot get a human body through a gap that size by any ordinary means. Yet the site's nature implies you can be admitted. Do not try to force, chip, or widen the seam. That would damage a young rock and probably injure you. Approach it slowly, arm first, and see what it allows.

## Training you need

You will be in a full overhead environment: no surface, no direct ascent, and a thick living tangle around you. Do not enter without:

- Cave or overhead-environment diving certification, with recent practice.
- Practiced drills for lost line, zero visibility, entanglement, and gas failure, done blind.
- Comfort with sensory deprivation. Water near skin temperature in near-darkness dissolves the sense of where you end. A flotation tank session or two, repeated until you can stay calm, is worth more than another piece of gear.
- Rehearsal in a flooded, vegetated site (a weedy quarry or a lake bed), moving slowly through dense growth without snagging or breaking it.

If you lack these, do not go in. Stay a surface observer.

## Gear

**Breathing.** You will need a redundant system and far more gas than any plan calls for. The room's sense of distance is loose and time may be too, so gas is the only clock you can trust.

- A closed-circuit rebreather has long duration but is bulky and snags easily. Sidemount open circuit is slimmer and lets you pass tanks ahead of you.
- Whichever you choose, carry independent redundancy, not a spare of the same failure point.
- Use a full-face mask if possible. The water at the lintel is said to taste of iron, and you want nothing in your mouth or nose that isn't your own gas.
- Keep your profile clean: no dangling hoses, gauges, or loose straps. Everything in there grabs back.
- Bring a depth gauge and check whether the chamber is at surface pressure. If it is not, plan decompression accordingly.

**Exposure and abrasion.** A thin wetsuit or drysuit with a hood and thick, cut-resistant gloves will protect against the fresh basalt, which will open your palms. The suit also gives you a slight sense of boundary, a useful counterweight to the water's skin-warm dissolving effect. Keep any wound sealed and covered: an open cut is a poor idea in a closed, young ecosystem and in the path of whatever moves through it.

**Cutting tools.** Carry two shears or blades, one at hand and one elsewhere. They are for emergencies only. This is a fragile, crowded place, and cutting is a last resort.

**Light.** The plants glow faintly and your eyes will adapt. Bring a dim red light and use it rarely. Bring a proper backup light and keep it off unless it is an emergency. A hard white beam would flatten what the room shows you and might disturb the growth.

**Navigation and timekeeping.** Expect instruments to be unreliable.

- Carry a compass, a mechanical watch, and a digital one, and expect them to disagree with each other. Log the disagreement.
- Use a guideline for physical continuity, but do not trust it as a measure of distance. The room may lengthen or fold it. Tie it off at the seam and check the knots regularly, since a line here may loop through "older" root tangles.
- Plan by gas, not distance. Turn back at a third of your supply, or earlier. The hum, about once an hour, is the only external marker: treat the first hum inside as a checkpoint and the second as a firm signal to be heading out.

**Communication.** Sound comes from the wrong places, so do not rely on voice or on any acoustic pinger. Use line tugs and hand signals, with light cues as a backup.

## People

- Do not go alone. Two people, at most three. Every extra body is more pressure on a room already too crowded.
- Tether your buddy to you. Distance is unreliable, and a buddy two body lengths away may become a buddy on the other side of a field.
- Have a surface person who stays at the seam and knows your plan. Tell them to wait far longer than seems reasonable. You may return unsure how long you were gone, and the evening outside may not match your body's count of hours.
- Keep your multiverse anchor or return device outside, on the bank in the ordinary world. Do not rely on transit devices inside a space that stretches itself.

## Ecology and hygiene

The chamber is a young, tightly packed ecosystem. Introducing a pathogen or a seed could do real harm. Sterilize your gear (dry, UV, and a careful rinse suited to your materials) and inspect it for stowaways on the way out. Take no specimens, no cuttings, and no shrimp. Your palms will come back green, so assume something transferred to you. Plan a quarantine and a medical check afterward, and keep the stain for analysis rather than scrubbing it away.

## Behavior inside

1. **Move slowly.** Part the growth with your hands and let it close behind you. Do not break stems. You are one more thing leaning on the room.
2. **Hold on to your edges.** The temperature will tempt you to forget where you stop. Touch your gear. Say your name into your mask. Do not let it become pleasant enough to drift.
3. **Expect drift.** The ceiling moss will grow toward you and away, and the room may tilt. Nothing falls, so tilting is a matter of disorientation, not physics. Fix your gaze on a stable thing, your line or your buddy, and wait it out.
4. **Do not chase distances.** If a wall is not where it was, it is not where it was. Note it and go back the way you came.
5. **Follow the cold thread.** The current back to the seam is your true guide. Learn its feel on your face and hands early, when you are calm, so you can find it again when you are not.

## What not to touch

- **The ring of seven stones and the black stem.** Look, do not move a stone, do not touch the stem. Whatever this is, it was built by attention, not hands, and it may respond to yours.
- **The spoon in the crack.** Leave it exactly where it is.
- **The lintel.** This is the riskiest object in the room. The water on its far side is colder, iron-tasting, and lit amber, and the plants are different. It is probably a threshold to another place. Do not swim through it on impulse, and never untethered. If you cross at all, cross once, on a short line, with your buddy holding the near end, and come straight back. Note that it is the only stone with moss on it. Something has taken an interest in it.

## The larger presence

You will probably not see it. You may see fronds part in a body-wide line where you are not, or feel a shift against your ribs. The blind shrimp will all face the same way afterward.

- Stop moving, breathe quietly, and anchor yourself with one hand on a sturdy root, not on anything delicate.
- Do not shine a light toward it or follow it.
- Let it pass, and wait until the shrimp turn back before you move on.

Nothing suggests it is hostile. But it is large, the room is tight, and a disturbed water column is enough to knock you off your line.

## Leaving

When you go, the distance will close "apologetically." Trust that, but don't count on it. Follow the current and your line, keep your turn rules, and don't linger for one more look.

On the bank, in the horsetail, do a full gear and body check. Tell your surface person the time you think it is and compare it with theirs. Write down everything while it is fresh, especially what your instruments did and how long the trip felt.

## A last note

You are not conquering this place. It is deciding how large to be, and you are a small pressure inside it. Go in the way the room seems to do everything: slowly, gently, and with room to leave.
