# Preparing to Visit The Kettle

## First, what kind of place this is

The Kettle is not dangerous the way a predator or a battlefield is. Nothing here hunts. It is dangerous the way a very loud, very warm, very confusing room is dangerous: it takes away your hearing, your sense of up, your sense of elapsed time, and possibly your composure, and it does so gently enough that you may not notice until you are in trouble. Preparation is mostly about deciding what you will do while your judgment is impaired, and about making sure you can always get out.

## Before you go: mental preparation

**Rehearse the breathing.** This matters more than any equipment. The lake lets you breathe on the condition, or so the accounts say, that you don't interrogate how. People who stop and reason about it "cough up the lake." If you are an analytical person, be honest about that and train against it.

- Practice a simple absorbing task you can do in a state of low-grade panic: counting steps, tracing a rhythm on your thigh, reciting a memorized verse.
- Decide in advance that your answer to "why can I breathe?" is "not now." Have a mantra ready.
- Practice steady, unhurried breathing with your eyes closed until it needs no attention.
- If you have a history of panic attacks or claustrophobia, or are prone to spiraling intellectual curiosity, consider not going.

**Expect thought to fail.** The sound is described as an environment where thought has trouble finding purchase. Whatever you need to remember, write it down and put it where you cannot lose it: a waterproof slate on a lanyard, or a laminated card sewn into your sleeve. It should have three lines at most, such as **WHERE THE EXIT IS. HOW LONG I STAY. HOW TO SIGNAL.**

**Decide your goal narrowly.** "Look at the sand-figures for a short while and come back" is a plan. "Explore and see what's here" is how people get lost in a place where direction is a rumor.

## The approach

The Kettle sits on a shoulder of young volcanic upland. The rock is raw, and the caldera edge is sharp enough to open a palm.

- **Footwear:** Sturdy boots with ankle support and a stiff sole. The scree is loose and the basalt is unweathered.
- **Hands:** Cut-resistant gloves are essential. You will be grabbing columns to steady yourself against currents, and the columns are sharp.
- **Assess the mountain itself.** It broke recently. Vents are active, gas is present, and the ground is trembling faintly. Note the wind, watch for gas pooling in low spots on the scree, and don't camp in the bowl of the approach. Carry a gas monitor if your home world has one, though don't trust it inside.
- **Study the wall from outside first.** Spend time on the dry scree watching the dome. Notice the spray, the steam, and the line where gravel is wet and then is not. Choose your entry point deliberately. Light is brightest on the upslope side inside, so entering there gives you a natural reference for your exit.

## Equipment

**Tether.** Bring a long, light, strong line, and anchor it to something solid outside: a drilled anchor, a heavy boulder, or a vehicle. Attach it to a harness at your chest, not a wrist. Inside, currents will lift you, drop you, and set you down a hundred paces away, and direction is unreliable. The rope is your only dependable "out."

Before committing yourself, **test the wall with a weighted line or a dyed marker** to confirm that a rope can pass through. The accounts speak of people walking through the wall but say nothing on this point. If the line will not pass cleanly, reconsider the whole plan.

**Signals.** You cannot shout. Your voice is "a small dry thing that never leaves your mouth." Agree on a tug code before entering, and keep it very simple:
- one pull: I'm fine
- two pulls: haul me in slowly
- three sharp pulls: emergency, haul now

Hand signals also work at close range, but only if you can see each other. The light is dim, shadows disagree, and the water shimmers. Tie in with a buddy on a short line if you go together. For a first visit, one person goes in and one stays outside on the rope.

**Hearing.** Earplugs and good earmuffs will help, and you should use them, but do not expect them to solve the problem. The sound arrives through the sternum, the teeth, and the backs of the eyes. Bone conduction will carry it. Aim to reduce the load, not eliminate it. Skip electronics that need to be heard, such as radios and beepers, because they will be useless.

**Eyes.** Swim goggles or a soft-seal mask. The water is slightly sweet with dissolved minerals and probably irritating over time. A mask that seals your nose is a small risk, because it invites you to think about breathing through the nose versus the mouth, so choose goggles that leave your nose free.

**Clothing.** The water is bath-warm, so a wetsuit will overheat you. Wear light, abrasion-resistant long sleeves and long trousers, closed at the wrists and ankles, to protect against the basalt. Avoid anything loose that a current can snag on the columns.

**Weight.** Bring a small weight belt or heavy boots if you want to stay near the floor. But if you have a clear reason to expect that gravity behaves, discard it. Reports mention the "floor" being above you at times. You are better off holding on to a tether and a column than trusting your weight.

**Marking.** Bring several bright, water-resistant markers and a way to mark the rock: chalk stick, paint crayon, or reflective tape. Use them to mark your route from the wall, and don't use them on anything old (see below).

**What to leave behind:**
- Electronics you can't afford to lose.
- Anything you can't afford to lose to the water.
- Valuable objects you'd want to return in the same state. Dropped objects here behave strangely and may "arrive" years old.
- Food and drink you plan to consume inside. You don't need them, and swallowing on purpose draws your attention to your throat.

Before going in, **eat lightly and hydrate well.** Warm water and heavy sound are both draining, and you don't want to feel faint.

## Entering

The wall takes you "like a hand closing." Expect a squeeze and a moment of disorientation.

1. **Do not stop at the wall.** Step through at a steady walking pace with your eyes open and your breathing already slow.
2. **Breathe out slightly as you go through, then let the next breath come.** The first breath is the hard one. Start your mantra before it.
3. **Once inside, stop and get your bearings.** The sound will hit you. Give yourself a full minute of standing still, gripping the tether. Feel for the pulse in your sternum. Find the brightest, greenest light. That is upslope and, by the plan, your exit direction.
4. **Check your gear by touch.** Slate, tether, markers.

## Inside: how to behave

**Move slowly and deliberately.** Currents will carry you. Hold on to basalt columns when you need to stop, but avoid the hollow ones, which "resonate like organ pipes" and will vibrate painfully in your hands.

**Stay off the vent throats.** They glow orange, scream, and are hot. Give them wide berth. The loudest vents are also where time frays, where "a single bubble may be seen rising in three places," and where you are most likely to lose track of how long you have been in the water.

**Don't trust your sense of time.** It frays. Set a turn-back rule that doesn't depend on a clock: for example, when your partner outside has hauled the rope a set number of times, or when a marked feature of the light changes. Better still, make your visit short by design: a fixed number of slow breaths counted on your fingers, then out. If you notice yourself lingering, leave.

**Don't trust your sense of direction.** Bubbles rise sideways and the surface may hang like a wall. Trust the rope. If you become confused, stop moving, hold a column, and follow the tether hand over hand.

**Watch the sand-figures.** They are the great sight of the place, and the safest thing to study. Rings, star-lobed rosettes, and lattices rise and dissolve as the vents change key. Look, but don't kneel and stir them, and don't step on the steady ones, which look like floor plans. They seem to hold longest where two notes sit in a fixed interval. Pay attention to how the sound pressure shifts: in the thickest roar you may see the vent-notes as faint ribs of pressure, and you can follow them to their source. Do so only with a tether that has plenty of slack and a plan to return.

**Respect the living things.** Filament tufts, humming reeds, orange mats, thumb-sized grazers, and the pale eel-thing are fragile, and they live in the only place they can. Don't collect them. If something very large passes at the edge of sight, you will feel the pressure change before you see anything. It does not hunt. Stay still, let it pass, and keep breathing.

## The marks, and what not to do

The lake holds a few things that seem to belong to someone:

- A **cairn of seven flat stones** on a ledge near the north wall, furred with algae and impossibly old.
- A **curved low wall** of fitted basalt, ten paces long, then nothing.
- A **ring of pale stones** with a warm hollow at its center, the size of a sleeping body.

Leave all of it exactly as it is. Don't add a stone, don't take one, and don't lie in the hollow, however tempting the warmth and however tired you are. In a place where thought has trouble finding purchase, a warm bed-shaped hollow is exactly where a traveler stops and never gets up. The sand near the ring settles into the shape of a spread hand pressed to the floor, as if feeling the mountain's pulse. Whoever made that mark isn't here, or isn't visible. The best courtesy is to look, remember, and not disturb.

## Warning signs to leave immediately

- You realize you've been analyzing how you're breathing. Reset with your mantra. If the tightness doesn't ease within a few breaths, pull out.
- You start to cough or feel water "as water." Do not fight it. Signal, and go.
- You can't tell which way the rope runs.
- You've stopped counting, or you can't remember what you came for.
- You feel unusually sleepy, warm, or content.
- You find yourself walking toward the ring, the hollow, or a vent.
- The sound seems to be speaking to you.

Any one of these is enough. The plan says: signal, follow the rope, exit.

## Leaving

Follow the rope toward the brightest green light. The wall is as sudden on the way out as it was coming in. Step through steadily and don't hold your breath. Out on the scree the dry air will feel thin and silent, and it may be hard to breathe normally for a minute or two. That is normal. Sit down, drink, and let your partner check you.

**After-effects:**
- Ringing in the ears, probably for hours and possibly for days. The scree beneath you will seem to tremble "in the key of the lake." This is likely a mix of real vibration and your own nerves.
- Fatigue, and a lingering sense of pressure in the sternum.
- Disordered memory of how long you were inside. Compare notes with your partner and the rope-hauler, and believe them over yourself.
- Ask a medical professional to check your hearing after any visit, and don't drive or operate machinery until the ringing settles.

## A few final thoughts

Most people who go to The Kettle will be fine if they treat it with patience and humility. It isn't hostile, but it's indifferent to your ideas about what should be possible, and it will exploit the moment you insist on them. Bring a rope, a plan, a partner, and a mantra. Stay short, stay curious, don't touch what was left here, and come out the way you went in, still able to say why you came.
