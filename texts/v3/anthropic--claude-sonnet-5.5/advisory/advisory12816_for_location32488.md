# Preparing to Visit the Kiln at Ashgrave Col

## The Right Frame of Mind

Nothing in the Kiln is aggressive. Nearly everything there is small, quiet, and busy. The danger comes from the environment (heat, gas, dryness, darkness) and from what you are: a large, warm, vibrating disturbance in a sealed, finely balanced ecosystem, and eventually, if you go wrong, a large dead thing in a place where dead things are collected and taken to the fire. Prepare to be a small and gentle visitor, and to keep yourself alive.

## 1. Don't Plan to Stand Inside It

The chamber has no bare floor. Every step would crush thousands of lamplice, collapse beetle pits, and send alarm through the vibration network. The floor is also hot and covered in loose clinker. My recommendation is to observe from a **suspended platform or harness seat** hung from the shaft anchor, so that your weight never touches the crowd. A few meters of clearance above the rim would let you see the light ripples and the Threadwalkers without becoming part of the surface. If you must touch down, use a **rigid board or spreader mat**, and set it down once, slowly, on the cooler rim.

## 2. Getting In: The Shaft

The shaft is doorway-sized and bends, so a straight drop is impossible.

- **Rope system:** Use static kernmantle rope with a **redirect or deviation anchor** at each bend. Pad the rock contacts, because the basalt is glazed and blistered and may abrade the rope. Rig for a haul system (at least 3:1) so a person can be raised from the chamber by people on the surface.
- **Two anchors, two ropes, every time.** You cannot easily be carried up a bent shaft, so equipment failure must be survivable.
- **A wired or tugline signal.** Radio through a bent lava tube is unreliable, and voice will not carry around the corners.
- **Never be alone.** Someone stays at the top and watches the shaft, the weather, and the gas monitors.
- **Enter at the ebb.** Do not be in the shaft or the chamber when the fissures pulse.

## 3. The Air Will Try to Kill You Quietly

The gas is mostly carbon dioxide, with sulfur compounds and something sweetish and mineral. CO₂ is odorless in dangerous concentrations, and it pools in bowls, which is what this chamber is. It doesn't feel like suffocation. It feels like a headache and dull confusion, and then you stop making good decisions.

- **Do not rely on the ambient air.** Wear a **self-contained breathing apparatus or closed-circuit rebreather** with generous reserve, plus a separate **escape bottle** you can reach without help.
- **Wear personal multi-gas monitors** for O₂, CO₂, and H₂S, with audible and vibration alarms. Test them at the surface first.
- **Lower a sensor line** on a cord before you go down, to record baseline readings at the rim and the first terrace.
- **Watch the O₂ number as much as the CO₂ number.** If either moves the wrong way, leave, and don't stop to finish what you were doing.
- **Assume the pulse is worse than it looks.** The long exhalation of gas that rolls up the terraces is the main hazard. Learn its period by watching from a safe position, and set a turnaround time well ahead of the next expected pulse.

## 4. The Heat

The rim is like an oven left ajar, and the center is much hotter. You will be dry-baking in the dark.

- **Wear a sealed, heat-rated suit** with reflective outer layers, insulated gloves, and boots with thick heat-resistant soles.
- **Use active or phase-change cooling.** Ice packs are only a stopgap in that heat. The best option is a closed-loop liquid-cooled undergarment with a heat-rated pump and battery.
- **Hydrate hard before you go**, with electrolytes. Limit sessions to short, timed stays with a mandatory cool-down at the surface.
- **Know heat illness:** confusion, stopped sweating, cramps, nausea, a strange calm. Any one of these means immediate ascent.
- **Protect your electronics.** Most batteries fail or become hazardous at high temperatures. Use heat-rated cases, and avoid lithium packs near the vents.

## 5. The Water Is Not Yours

The only water in the chamber is held in the salt crusts, which take moisture from the air in the cooler hours and give it back in the heat. **Everything in there depends on those films of damp.**

- Don't breathe out freely into the chamber, don't sweat into it, and don't spill anything.
- Use a **sealed suit and closed-loop breathing gear** so your moisture stays in.
- Don't touch, scrape, or sample the salt crusts.
- Carry all drinking water in sealed containers and drink only at the surface.

## 6. Light and Vision

The place is dark, lit only by the faint glow of the fissures and the fauna's green-white flicker.

- Use a **dim red headlamp** at the lowest useful setting, or night-vision or low-light optics. Bright white light is not proven harmful, but it would spoil your dark adaptation and the ripples of light, which are the most beautiful thing there.
- Bring a **thermal imager**. The Wall-Mimics are described as slightly warmer than the surrounding rock, so a thermal camera may be the only way to tell a "stone" from a hunter. Even so, don't trust it alone.
- Wear **sealed goggles or a full-face mask**. The air is dry, dusty, and salty, and it will scour your eyes.

## 7. Vibration: You Are Very Loud

The chamber's real language is vibration through the rock. You don't need to be silent, but you need to be **soft and predictable**.

- Move slowly and place each foot deliberately, the way a Threadwalker does.
- Avoid stamping, dragging gear, clanking carabiners, or tapping the rock to test it.
- Pad your hardware and rig your ropes so they don't drum against the walls.
- Expect that your presence will still produce ripples of green light. That is the place noticing you. Don't chase it, and don't test it by making noise on purpose.

## 8. What Not to Touch

- **The walls.** Wall-Mimics look like blistered basalt and hang there for years. Keep your hands and gear off vertical faces, and never lean on rock you haven't identified.
- **The Threadwalkers.** They stand still for an hour and then strike. Keep a few body-lengths away and cover all skin, especially your neck, wrists, and ankles. Wire-thin legs can find gaps.
- **Lamplice sheets and beetle pits.** Don't reach into them. A Bellows-Beetle's hot huff at close range is a burn risk.
- **The Sextons' lines.** Don't block them, don't step over them, and don't leave anything for them to carry off. If you fall unconscious, they will treat you as material, so keep your tether and your buddy.

## 9. The Honeycomb and the Ring

Two features deserve special care.

**The honeycomb on the eastern wall** is empty, clean, and avoided by everything in a chamber where every other surface is packed. I would treat that as information. Don't touch it, sample it, or shine your light into it. Something built it and left, and the crowd won't settle there.

**The ring of stacked stones** on the third terrace is the oldest thing in the chamber and the only bare, cool ground. It will be tempting as a refuge from the heat, and I'd advise against using it that way. Something laid those stones deliberately, the crowd has left the inside untouched for a very long time, and that is not something to trample or test on a whim. If you are in real trouble, the ascent rope is a better plan. If you choose to approach it, do so as a study from the outside, from your suspended platform, with your instruments and no sampling.

## 10. Keep the Chamber Closed

Everything eaten here arrives through the shaft, and it has been dead for a while. This ecosystem is sealed except for that thin stream of drift.

- **Sterilize everything** that goes in: suit, boots, rope ends, instruments. Dry heat works well and is thematically appropriate.
- **Bring nothing organic**: no food, no seeds in your cuffs, no pollen on your clothes.
- **Take nothing out.** Don't collect specimens, don't pocket a bronze-backed beetle, and don't chip the glassy chimney mineral.
- **Don't drop anything.** If something falls, note it, report it, and don't try to retrieve it.

## 11. The Surface and the Mountain

Two thousand meters is not extreme altitude, but the combination of thin air, exertion, dehydration, and elevated CO₂ makes you much more vulnerable.

- **Acclimatize** for a day or two before the descent.
- **Expect a temperature shock.** You'll come out of an oven into a cold, dry, star-hard night, soaked in sweat. Bring insulated layers, a wind shell, and a warm, sheltered staging area at the top. Hypothermia after heat exposure is a real risk.
- **Survey the massif for other vents.** A dead volcano can still leak gas into hollows on the surface, and camping in a low spot is a serious mistake.

## 12. Multiverse Logistics

- **Arrive on the surface, in daylight, well away from the shaft.** Do not use the chamber as your arrival or departure point.
- **Ensure your return method doesn't depend on being inside the Kiln**, or on gear that heat or moisture could compromise. Keep your anchor or beacon at the surface camp, where your support team can also reach it.
- **Have an emergency recall** that doesn't depend on speech, since nothing carries around the shaft's bend and nothing in the chamber will answer you.

## 13. Preparing Your Mind

You'll be looking at a moving floor of millions of small bodies in a dim, hot, nearly silent place. If you are uneasy about insects, crowds, or tight spaces, expect it to hit hard at first. Rehearse the ascent until it is automatic. Decide beforehand what will make you leave, whether that is a gas reading, a time limit, or a bad feeling. Agree it with your surface partner, and honor it.

The Kiln is "not hostile and not welcoming. It is simply full, and finished, and continuing." Go as someone who understands that, and see it without becoming part of it.
