# Preparing for the Throat of Ossary Col

The Throat is not dangerous in the way a battlefield or a jungle is. It is dangerous the way a quiet room with a slow gas leak is dangerous. Nothing here attacks you. The risks are altitude, cold, unbreathable or flammable air, a sound that gets into your body, and a stillness that makes you want to stay. Prepare for a place that is gentle and indifferent, and that will not notice if you never leave.

## 1. Getting there

You cannot see the entrance from anywhere, so plan to find it by search or by local knowledge.

- **Arrive low and climb.** Don't materialize on the ridge if you can choose. The col is high, thin-aired and wind-scoured, and there is no shelter on a blade of polished rock. Arrive several hundred meters below and spend a day or two acclimatizing on the way up. Altitude sickness would ruin this trip, and it makes you stupid in ways the pocket will exploit.
- **Search in daylight.** The seam is a vertical slot barely a body wide, and the rock around it is grey-violet, glittering with frost and lichen. Walk the ridge in a rope team and look for the low chord before you see the crack. Sound may be your best guide, since the slot is a throat and the wind is forced through it.
- **Bring standard high-alpine kit:** insulated layers, windproof shell, goggles, gloves you can work in, crampons if the rime is slick, a rope, and a first-aid kit that covers hypothermia and altitude illness.
- **Bring all your own water.** The frost is an oily rime, not ice. Do not melt it, drink it, or use it for cooking. There is no water anywhere on this ridge. Carry more than seems reasonable, because the air is desert-dry and you will breathe out a lot of it.

## 2. The air

The pocket smells of hot pennies and pine tar. That means volatile hydrocarbons and possibly other compounds, in a sheltered fold where heavy vapors from the tar seep could pool near the floor even with the roof open to the sky.

- **Carry a multi-gas detector** with oxygen, combustible gas (LEL), hydrogen sulfide, carbon monoxide, and a photoionization detector for volatile organics. Test at the slot, then again just inside, at floor level and at head height. If it alarms, don't go in.
- **Respiratory protection depends on what the detector says.** A half-face respirator with organic-vapor cartridges and P100 filters is the minimum, because the ferns loose spores into the air and you should not inhale them. But filter masks add breathing resistance at altitude and give no oxygen. If readings are meaningful, use a small supplied-air rig, such as a compact SCBA or an escape bottle, instead of filters. If in doubt, use the bottle.
- **Never rely on your sense of smell.** It fatigues quickly, and some of what you smell may be dulling it.
- **Stay off the floor** as much as you can. Heavy vapors settle there, along with the tar and the spore ridges.

## 3. Fire and ignition

There is a cold seep of flammable tar, plants that sweat oil from their leaves, and dry air over iron. Treat the whole pocket as a place where a spark could matter.

- **No flames, no lighters, no stoves, no flares** inside the pocket, and none on the approach if vapor is drifting out of the slot. Cook and warm yourself well away.
- **Use intrinsically safe lamps** if you can get them. A standard headlamp is probably tolerable in the cold, but check for arcing switches and cheap battery contacts. Skip camera flashes.
- **Wear natural fibers or antistatic layers** over your insulation. Fleece and synthetic shells build static in dry cold air. Touch a bare rock before you enter the slot to discharge.
- **Bring non-sparking tools** of brass or bronze if you expect to do anything mechanical. Don't strike iron on iron.

## 4. The slot

You turn sideways and press in. That has practical consequences.

- **Take only what fits.** A pack will jam. Carry a slim kit on a chest harness or a small bag you can push ahead of you, and leave the bulk at a cache outside.
- **Use a tether.** Rig a line from an anchor on the ridge, through the slot, into the pocket. It is your way out if you lose your bearings, if your light dies, or if the chord starts to take over your head.
- **Have a topside partner.** One person should stay on the ridge with the rope, the spare air, and the clock. Radios probably won't carry through the rock or over the sound, so agree on a rope-signal system, such as three tugs for "coming out" and four for "help."
- **Go in one at a time** unless the pocket proves roomy enough for a team. Two people wedged in the slot is a bad place to discover someone is claustrophobic.

## 5. The sound

The sound is loud, constant, and low. You feel it in your sternum, and you cannot speak over it.

- **Wear layered hearing protection.** Use foam or musician's plugs under good earmuffs. Neither will stop the low tone, which travels through bone, but they will spare your ears the shimmering whistle from the ferns.
- **Skip the trip if you have a pacemaker,** a serious heart condition, epilepsy, an inner-ear disorder, or a history of panic. A steady infrasonic pulse at your chest is not something to test on a weak system.
- **Agree on signals before you go in.** Touch a shoulder for attention. Use hand signs for "okay," "stop," "leave now," and "I feel wrong." Bring a small slate and chalk or a waterproof notepad for anything more.
- **Limit your exposure.** Set a short stay, such as thirty minutes for the first visit, and step out into the wind afterward to let your body reset.

## 6. The pull to stay

This is the hazard most likely to get you, and it needs the most preparation. The chord, the flywheel's slow beat, and the amber lights on the walls form a rhythm about eleven seconds long. Within minutes your breathing will slow to match it without your choosing. The place is a stable equilibrium, and it will try to make you one too.

- **Set an alarm, and a second one.** Use two independent timers, one on your wrist and one with the topside partner. Your sense of time will not be reliable.
- **Decide your turnaround time before you enter** and treat it as fixed. Don't reason with yourself inside the pocket about "just a little longer." That reasoning is a symptom.
- **Break the rhythm on purpose.** Walk, count breaths out of phase with the pulse, hum a note that clashes with the chord, or tap a different beat on your leg. If you notice you have stopped doing this, that is your cue to leave.
- **Watch each other.** Check your partner's breathing every few minutes. If it has slowed to the pulse and they no longer respond to a touch, pull them out. Agree beforehand that either of you can call the retreat without argument.
- **Note your mood on entry.** Serenity is not a warning sign on its own, but feeling that nothing needs to be done is.

## 7. Your travel method

- **Keep your return anchor outside.** If your method for leaving this world relies on electronics, a resonant device, or anything sensitive to strong low-frequency vibration, don't carry it into the standing wave. Test it on the ridge first. Assume the pocket could detune it.
- **Bring a second, independent way home** if you can.
- **Check the local physics.** The wind here is said to blow at one steady speed, and time seems to stand still. Verify with a clock and a second reference outside that hours in the pocket are hours on the ridge. If they diverge, leave.

## 8. Contamination, both ways

- **Don't bring life in.** No seeds, animals, insects, or soil on your boots. The ferns have no animals to contend with, and the ecosystem may be very fragile. Clean your gear and change out of anything that has touched other ground.
- **Don't carry it out.** Spores will cling to clothing and hair. Brush down and bag your outer layer, and don't open the bag in a place that cares about its plants.
- **Wear disposable coveralls, nitrile gloves, and washable boots.** The tar is sticky, and the sap may irritate skin. Rinse with solvent-safe wipes, not water alone.

## 9. Etiquette and restraint

You are a disturbance in a system that has held still for a very long time. The plants stand in bands set by the wavelength of the chord, and your body and pack are objects in a standing wave.

- **Move slowly and lightly.** Stay on the bare places between the bands if you can find any, and don't trample the spore ridges.
- **Cut nothing.** The temptation is to count the teeth on a frond to measure the note. Instead, photograph a frond in place with a scale, or take measurements without damaging it.
- **Don't repair, adjust, or start anything.** Leave the flywheel, the pump, the gauge, and the pipes alone. Don't open the sealed door on the ledge. You don't know what depends on the load holding steady.
- **Don't take the plate or anything else.** The moss around the hand-lettered plate has been trimmed, and by nothing you can identify. Take a rubbing or a photograph, and don't clear the moss yourself.
- **Assume you might be observed.** Someone or something keeps that plaque clear. Be calm and open in your movements, announce yourself aloud even if no one can hear, and don't do anything you would be embarrassed to be seen doing.

## 10. Minimum checklist

- Acclimatization time and an approach from below
- Water, food, and cold-weather layers for the entire party
- Multi-gas detector, supplied-air option, and P100/OV respirators
- Antistatic clothing, non-sparking tools, and no open flames
- Rope, harness, tether, and a topside partner
- Layered hearing protection, hand signals, and a slate
- Two timers, a fixed turnaround time, and a plan to break the rhythm
- Return anchor kept outside and tested
- Disposable coveralls, gloves, and bags for contaminated gear
- A rule to touch nothing that isn't yours

If everything goes right, you will spend half an hour in a room where nothing changes, listen to the mountain and the machine hold one long chord, and then walk out into the wind with your breathing your own again. The best sign of a good visit is that you still want to leave when the alarm goes off.
