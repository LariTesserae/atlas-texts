# Preparing for the Sump of Ossary Reach

## The Right Mindset

You are not visiting a city. You are entering a living, sweating, singing organism and asking to be tolerated by it. Three facts should shape every decision:

1. **The sound is structural.** It holds the upper city up. It is not a nuisance to be blocked out, and silence means danger.
2. **Everything is wet, warm, and biologically active.** Your body will be colonized, your gear will corrode, and your food and water are potential vectors.
3. **Nothing stays where you left it.** Stairs migrate, doors move, and addresses are meaningless. Your way back cannot depend on landmarks.

Plan for a short first visit, two or three days at most. The place will teach you what a longer stay would need.

## Medical Preparation

**Get a baseline.** Before you go, have your hearing tested and your cardiovascular fitness assessed. The heat is about body temperature and the humidity is near total, so your sweat will barely evaporate. Your body loses its main cooling mechanism, and heat illness is the most likely thing to hurt you. Anyone with heart conditions, severe asthma, or a history of heat stroke should skip this trip. Anything implanted and electronic, such as a pacemaker, should be treated as a contraindication until proven otherwise. The lattice is driven by fields no one fully understands, and lightning is harvested overhead.

**Vestibular and infrasound tolerance.** The floor trembles constantly and the pumps thud at frequencies you feel in your sternum. Expect nausea, unease, and disturbed sleep for the first day. Bring anti-nausea medication, and do a trial run in a loud, vibrating environment if you can.

**Immunity and gut.** The ecosystem is dense with microbes, molds, and spores you have no history with. Get every relevant immunization, and carry a broad-spectrum antimicrobial course, oral rehydration salts, and antifungal treatments for skin, feet, and ears. Ask a multiverse medical service about local pathogens. If none has data, assume the worst and carry accordingly.

**Acclimatize beforehand.** Spend a week or two in a hot, humid environment, such as a sauna or a tropical field site, drinking and salting deliberately. It won't make you comfortable, but you'll arrive with your body already used to the load.

## Clothing and Physical Kit

**Fabrics.** Wear quick-dry synthetics or fine merino, loose and light in color. Cotton stays wet and will macerate your skin. Bring at least three full changes and rotate them through a sealed dry bag. Dry clothes will be your most precious resource.

**Feet.** Wear closed-toe drainage boots or shoes with aggressive, non-marking tread. Wet ceramic is slick, stairs move, and mats are greasy. Carry four or more pairs of moisture-wicking socks, foot powder, and an antifungal. Immersion foot is a real risk. Barefoot is never acceptable.

**Hands and eyes.** Bring light, grippy gloves for handling weeping walls, rails, and grates. Bring wraparound eye protection, since rag-flocks come in clouds and spores hang in the air.

**Respiratory protection.** Use a P100 or equivalent respirator with spare cartridges. Humidity will saturate filters quickly, so expect to change them often and keep them sealed when not in use. Fungal cauliflowers slump and spore daily, and the flats are the worst place for it.

**Electronics.** Assume most consumer electronics will fail in the humidity. Use sealed, conformal-coated, or ruggedized equipment in waterproof cases with desiccant, and keep everything on lanyards. Expect no GPS or network. Do not bring anything with strong exposed metal contacts near the discharge-vanes.

**Water and food.** Bring your own for the first two days. The local water is part of a recycling system and its safety is unknown. Carry a filter plus chemical or UV treatment, and treat even condensate. Eat cooked, hot food that you watch being prepared. Be wary of the mollusk-farms and raw brine ferments, at least until your gut has had time to adjust.

## Sound: The Most Important Preparation

**Do not seal your ears.** You need to hear the tone, the animals, the pumps, and warnings. Use filtered, high-fidelity earplugs that cut overall level by 15 to 25 dB while preserving frequency balance, and carry a backup pair. Cheap foam plugs will distort the sound and may hide the very cues that keep you alive. Wear them selectively: use them at the roughest markets, take them out in stable, older sections.

**Learn the tone.** Bring a **pitch reference**, a tuning fork or a small tone generator, and tune your ear to the base note before you go into the deep city. Sag in the tone, dropouts, or drift are your early warning of slump-fall. If it wavers, move toward older, darker, lower masonry, away from the glassy upper courses.

**Never enter a quiet block.** If sound thins out or dies in a section, leave at once. Don't investigate. A dying block can go from silent to liquid.

**Bring nothing that could interfere.** No sonic tools, ultrasonic devices, powerful speakers, air horns, or high-amplitude resonant instruments. You could easily do harm you can't predict. Noise-cancelling headphones are especially unwise, both for safety and because they would deafen you to what matters.

**Contribute.** Locals add to the sound as a civic duty. Buy a **chime or resonant tube tuned to the local lattice** from a trader in the first hour, and use it as others do. It is your ticket to belonging, and possibly to safety.

## Navigation and Return

**Do not rely on maps, addresses, or chalk marks.** Water washes off chalk, stairs migrate, and doors open in old walls. Residents use sound-signatures, and you should adopt one: a short, distinctive pattern struck on your chime.

**Hire a local guide immediately.** Someone who knows the current sound-geography is worth more than any equipment you carry. Agree on terms in advance, and pay for the whole visit rather than by the hour.

**Secure your exit.** This matters more than anything else in this guide. Your portal or return anchor must not sit somewhere the maintenance swarms could dissolve or regrow. Anchor at the outer edge of the oldest, lowest, most weathered courses, where the structure is stable. Confirm that you have a **secondary exit**, ideally one you can reach by an independent route, and set a hard departure time.

**Tether when needed.** If you must cross the flats, use a guide, mud boards or wide-footprint shoes, and a lifeline. The mud is blood-warm, the channels braid with the tide, and the mats are slick, greasy, and hum under your hand. Avoid the flats at dawn, when fog forms and visibility collapses.

## Hazards

- **Slump-fall:** Avoid the newest, glassy, shimmering upper lattices. Stay in the older, rounder, darker masonry.
- **Sump eels:** They rise through grates at dusk, thick as a thigh. Don't stand on or near grates at dusk, and don't sleep near flooded galleries.
- **Glass crabs:** They are numerous and probably harmless, but locals read their flicker as information. Don't crush or collect them. Keep your hands off drip surfaces when you can.
- **Mat-grazers:** Slow but large, and they move in herds. Give them room on walls and stairs.
- **Rag-flocks:** Cover your eyes and mouth when they swirl overhead.
- **Lightning and discharge-vanes:** Keep clear of vanes, avoid exposed metal in storms, and remember that weather can rain sideways, upward, or stop mid-air.
- **Crowds and heights:** There is no empty space. Stay aware of edges, hanging cages, cables, and stall structures on every bridge.

## Social Conduct

- **Treat the animals as part of the machinery.** Some are pets, some food, some infrastructure. Don't kill or handle any casually.
- **Don't harm the lattice.** Don't scrape, chip, or drill it. The walls are engineered and possibly semi-alive.
- **Respect shrines and stalls.** Every ledge has an owner. Ask before you sit.
- **Bring trade goods.** Anything dry and durable, such as salt, sealed cloth, desiccant packs, or sealed spices, is likely valuable. Metal tools that don't rust will also be prized. Avoid anything that could look like a weapon or a sound device.
- **Language.** Expect that much communication is tonal. A translator may help, but a guide is better.

## Sleep and Psychology

There is no privacy and no open sky. You may feel claustrophobic, overstimulated, and exhausted by the roar. Plan to sleep in a rented niche in a stable, noisy area, never a quiet one. Bring a sleep aid you've already tested, and use fitted earplugs at night. Build in rest periods of at least ten minutes every hour.

Afterward you may experience tinnitus, disorientation in silence, or a sense of ordinary quiet as threatening. Give yourself time to recover before going anywhere else.

## Minimum Kit Checklist

- Filtered earplugs (two pairs) and a pitch reference
- P100 respirator with spare cartridges
- Three changes of quick-dry clothing and multiple pairs of socks
- Drainage boots with strong tread
- Wraparound eye protection and grip gloves
- Sealed dry bags and desiccant
- Water filter, purification tablets, and electrolytes
- Two days of food, and antifungals and antimicrobials
- Anti-nausea medication and a heat-illness plan
- Lifeline, mud boards, and a headlamp
- Chime or resonant tube (buy locally)
- Trade goods
- Verified primary and secondary exit, with a set departure time

## The First Hour

1. Arrive at the old, stable base of the structure, not the upper courses.
2. Stop and listen, and tune your ear to the tone.
3. Acclimatize in shade or near drip lines for ten minutes before going deeper.
4. Find a guide and a chime, and learn your signature.
5. Eat and drink something safe before your first climb.
6. Set your departure time and stick to it.

You are a guest in something that sings itself into being. If you keep to the old walls, keep your ears open, and never let the sound drop away, you can visit and leave with nothing worse than damp socks and a ringing in your head.
