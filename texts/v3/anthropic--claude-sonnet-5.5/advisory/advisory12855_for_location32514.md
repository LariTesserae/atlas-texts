# Preparing to Visit the Terraced Basin of Hallen Tarn

## First impressions

This is a gentle place, and the danger in it is that gentleness. There are no predators, no biting insects, no storms, no seasons, and nothing seems to want you gone. That makes travelers careless. The real hazards here are quiet ones: wet feet, hidden gas, warm water full of microbes, thin-ish air, and the temptation to disturb a balance that has held for a very long time. Prepare for a damp, warm, silent, high-altitude walk through a museum that may not be finished being used.

## Before you go

**Acclimatize.** Three thousand meters is high enough for headaches, poor sleep, and breathlessness, especially if you arrive straight from sea level in another world. Spend a couple of days at intermediate height before crossing over, or plan a slow first day with almost no exertion. Bring altitude medication if you know it works for you. Warm, humid air feels easy to breathe, and that can mask how hard your body is working. Drink more than seems necessary, and don't overdo the first evening.

**Set your anchor with care.** The basin has no weather and no sun to speak of, so it offers little help in finding your way back. Place your return point somewhere unmistakable and dry, ideally on the rim or on one of the higher ribs, not on the wet shelves near the lake. Record its position by more than one method: compass bearings to the rim's features, a written sketch, and a beacon or transponder if your kind of travel allows one. Don't count on GPS or any other world-specific infrastructure. The low cloud ceiling, uniform grey light, and repeating terraces make the basin a very good place to lose your bearings.

**Test for time drift.** Nothing in the basin changes, and a place that stable sometimes has odd relationships to elapsed time. Bring two independent clocks, a mechanical one and a battery one, and a logbook for entries at fixed intervals. Compare them on arrival and again before leaving. If you find drift, leave earlier than planned.

**Tell someone your route.** Give a trusted person on the home side your entry point, your intended path, and a firm overdue time.

## Gas and air

The basin is a bowl over a heat source, so treat it as a volcanic-hazard site even though it looks placid. Heat rises from the ground. In such places carbon dioxide and sometimes hydrogen sulfide seep up, and because they are heavier than air they pool in low ground, especially in still air, at night, and above all near the central lake. You cannot see or reliably smell these gases.

- Carry a personal multi-gas monitor with alarms for CO₂, H₂S, and oxygen depletion. This is the one piece of equipment I would not go without.
- Do not camp on the lowest shelves or beside the lake. Camp on a higher rib or near the rim.
- Don't sleep in cellars, pits, or sunken structures. The city's cool cellars full of jars are exactly the kind of low, still space where dense gas collects.
- If the monitor alarms, or you feel sudden headache, dizziness, or unusual drowsiness, climb at once. Don't stop to figure it out.
- The lake is the place to be most careful. If you go to it, go in daylight, in company, with the monitor, and don't linger.

## Water: seeing it, touching it, drinking it

The pools look clean, but a warm, still, nutrient-rich pool at bathwater temperature is nearly ideal for microbes, even without animals in it.

- **Do not immerse your head, and do not get the water up your nose.** Warm freshwater can carry dangerous amoebae. Wading is generally fine, but keep your face out of it.
- **Drink only treated water.** Filter it, then boil it or use chemical treatment. Geothermal seepage can add dissolved minerals or toxic elements like arsenic and fluoride that filters don't remove. Consider carrying all your drinking water for a short visit, or test the water and stay short-term if you can't verify it. The cliffs' weeping seams are not necessarily cleaner than the pools.
- **Avoid breathing the vapor close to the surface** where it's thickest and warmest. Warm mist can carry bacteria like *Legionella*. A mask helps.
- Wash and cook away from the pools and never put soap in the water. The place holds one delicate equilibrium, and detergents, food, and skin oils are the kind of things that could upset it.

## Clothing and footwear

Everything is damp all the time, and you will be damp too.

- **Footwear is the most important choice.** Waterproof but breathable boots with an aggressive sole, plus gaiters, for the mud and moss. Wet basalt is slick and the moss hides gaps. Bring a second pair, such as sturdy water shoes, for the shallow sheets and the causeway crossings, and let boots dry when you can.
- **Socks:** many pairs, wool or synthetic, no cotton. Change them at every stop. Carry antifungal powder and foot balm. Trench foot and fungal infection are the likeliest injuries.
- **Layers:** quick-dry synthetics or wool. The floor is warm, and the rim and cloud level are cooler, so you'll swing between sweaty and chilled. A light waterproof shell is more useful for the constant drip than for rain. A brimmed hat keeps drops off your neck.
- **Gloves:** thin, grippy, and waterproof. The basalt edges are sharp where fresh, and the moss hides cuts.
- Sun protection matters less than usual, but at this altitude an hour of open sun at midday can still burn. Sunglasses help against the flat glare off the pools.

## Shelter and sleep

- A good tent with ample ventilation, since condensation will gather inside anyway. Use a groundsheet, and pitch on turf or gravel on a rib, not in marsh.
- A thick insulated sleeping pad. The ground is spongy and holds moisture, and the thermal gradient from below can be uneven.
- A synthetic or treated-down bag, stored in a dry bag. Assume nothing stays dry unless sealed.
- Dry bags or rigid cases for electronics, paper, medicine, and food. Add silica gel. Optics will fog. Bring lens cloths and anti-fog treatment.
- Cooking: a canister or liquid-fuel stove. Don't burn any local wood. It's part of the site, it won't burn well, and the timber posts in the plaza should be treated as artifacts.

## Food and waste

There are no animals to hunt, no fish to catch, and no berries or roots you can identify as safe. Bring all your food. The plants are probably safe to ignore. Spore-bearing and wind-pollinated flora aren't known for being edible, and the fungi are unidentified.

The forest floor is broken down entirely by fungi, which means nothing else will clean up after you. Your food scraps, packaging, and waste would become part of that mat and change it. **Pack out everything**, including human waste, using a proper carry-out system. Don't bury it and don't leave it in water. You are the only animal in the basin, and it hasn't yet had to cope with one.

## Health kit

Beyond the usual: rehydration salts, antihistamines, anti-inflammatories, blister care, antifungal cream, wound irrigation and closure supplies (moss hides sharp stone, and wet wounds infect easily), a thermometer, and altitude medication. N95 or better masks are recommended. The air is loaded with fungal spores, and long exposure at close range could bother the lungs, especially if you have asthma or allergies. Wear a mask when disturbing the fungal mats or entering closed, damp buildings.

You won't need insect repellent or animal deterrent. It's strange to pack for the place and find that the usual worries don't apply.

## Moving through the basin

- **Use the causeways.** They are built to carry you dry-shod across the shelves, and they are the intended way through. Test each stepping-stone before trusting it. Basalt slabs can tip, and wet stone can be very slippery.
- **Go slowly on the talus.** The fallen blocks below the cliffs fit their gaps exactly, which means they haven't settled. They can shift underfoot. Descend from the rim carefully, on three points of contact, and never in a group directly above or below one another.
- **Don't climb the cliffs or shelter beneath them.** The faces are fresh and jointed. Nothing has fallen recently, but nothing suggests it can't.
- **Moss banks are traps.** They look solid but can be hollow or waterlogged, and pressing a boot into one makes water well up. Use trekking poles to probe.
- **Navigation:** the terraces repeat and the cloud hides landmarks. Take bearings to the rim at every stop, and don't trust the flat light to show you distance. Keep sight of your route back. If you mark your path, use temporary markers that you remove on the way out. Don't scratch, paint, or cut anything.
- A day's walk across means a day's walk back. Plan your route so you're never more than half a day from your anchor and can turn around without cost.

## The city: how to behave

The city has a feeling of being paused rather than abandoned, so treat it as though its people might return, or as though they might be somewhere watching. That's not a superstition, just good practice in a place you don't understand.

- **Touch as little as possible.** Every stone in the walls sits in a fit that took skill, and every weir holds a pool at exactly its mark. Don't move stones, adjust channels, unblock sluices, or step on the weirs. The water level is the city's structure, and altering it may change more than you expect.
- **Don't take anything.** No jars, tools, cloth, or carved fragments. Tools are set neatly by the doorways, and you should leave them that way. If you must handle something to examine it, put it back exactly.
- **Don't sleep in the buildings.** The benches are swept and the beds are made. Camp outside.
- **Photograph and sketch rather than sample.** Fibers, resins, and clay could be studied without collecting, and the vast, uniform stock of jars means someone's records or supplies are in there. Please leave them.
- **Enter narrow doorways sideways, slowly.** The doors are built narrow, and packs snag. Take your pack off. Don't wedge yourself into places you can't easily back out of.
- **Don't light fires inside.** The kilns and hearths are clean and ready. Don't use them.
- **Do not interfere with the plaza's central basin,** the spring-fed one that is always exactly full. It's the clearest expression of the balance that holds this place together. Don't drink from it, wash in it, or drop anything into it.

## Mind and body

The quiet gets to people. The voice goes flat over the water and doesn't come back, no birds or insects make any sound, and the sameness of light and temperature erases the usual sense of time passing. Some travelers feel calm, and some feel a creeping unease, as if they were being politely waited for. Both are normal.

- Travel with at least one companion, and agree on a signal or check-in routine. A whistle is better than shouting.
- Keep a routine, meaning regular meals, regular sleep, and regular logbook entries. It anchors you when the environment offers no natural rhythm.
- If you feel a strong pull to stay, to go deeper, or to sit by the plaza a little longer, take that as a cue to leave. Nothing here is likely to be trying to hold you, but a place with no change is easy to lose yourself in.
- The plaza carries a whisper across its tiers and gives no answer. Don't test this at length. Speak quietly and be aware of what you're doing there.

## Leaving

Plan to leave as you found it. Your visit will be the only change the basin has known in a very long time, so the aim is to make it a small one. Before departing, walk your camp perimeter, count your gear, check your waste, and confirm that nothing of yours is in the water, moss, or buildings. Compare clocks. Then return to your anchor by the way you came, and don't be surprised if you feel, when you cross back, that the loud world you return to is hard to listen to.

Take good notes, leave it exactly as it was, and let the water keep its level.
