# Preparing for the Warm Shelf of Ahn-Sethuret

## The Central Fact

The Shelf is two environments stacked on top of each other, and your body has to cross from one to the other in about ten paces. You spend three days in cold, thin air, then step over a lip of black rock into a hot, wet, roaring greenhouse that is still higher than most clouds. Almost every hazard comes from this mismatch: altitude with tropical humidity, frostbite gear with jungle rot, and lungs starved for oxygen in air thick with spores and steam.

Plan for two trips, the ascent and the basin, and pack for both.

## The Climb

**Fitness and acclimatization.** Three days up through scree and old ice is a serious mountaineering approach. Spend at least a week beforehand at moderate altitude, and don't gain more than a few hundred meters of sleeping elevation per day near the top. The Shelf floor is high enough that the sky goes violet at noon. Know the signs of altitude sickness, including headache, confusion, a wet cough, and loss of coordination. The warmth of the Shelf will tempt you to stay when you should descend. Warm air does not fix a lung or brain that is swelling. If someone in your party gets worse, go back down over the pass, however good the basin feels.

**Cold-side kit.** Bring standard high-alpine equipment: insulated layers, windproof shell, gloves, goggles, crampons, an axe, and a tent rated for the snow line. Get used to the fact that you will carry all of it into a place where you won't need it. Plan to cache it at the lip of black stone, sealed against damp and animals, and collect it on the way out. Don't leave it lightly. The cairn line suggests other people have used this route, but you can't count on anyone to keep your gear safe.

**The transition.** Stop at the lip. Before stepping through, strip layers slowly. If you go in fully bundled, you'll be soaked in sweat within minutes. When you come back out, that damp clothing can kill you. Plan for the crossing in both directions.

## Clothing in the Basin

The Shelf is warm, wet, and steamy, but not scorching. The main enemy is constant moisture, not heat.

- **Fabrics:** Synthetics and merino that dry quickly and keep working when damp. Avoid cotton entirely.
- **Rain and mist:** A light, breathable shell is better than a heavy waterproof, which turns into a sauna. The mist drips, and there's no true rain to shed, so you want something that manages condensation.
- **Footwear:** This matters most. You need boots with thick, heat-resistant soles, because some ground is hot enough to make you lift your foot. They should also be waterproof to the calf, since the marsh will swallow you to the thigh. Bring two pairs if you can and rotate them. Pack plenty of wool or synthetic socks and change them at every stop. Prolonged wet feet lead to trench foot and fungal infection.
- **Gloves:** Thin, tough gloves for handling rock, plants, and anything else. The pale veins and the weeping rock water are unknown. Treat them as skin irritants until proven otherwise.
- **Head and neck:** A wide-brimmed hat, and a neck cover for sun at that altitude. UV is strong in thin air and reflects off the white cloud floor below the rim.

## Water, Food, and What Not to Drink

Everything is wet, and almost none of it is safe to assume drinkable.

- **Weeping rock water** runs warm and tastes of coins, which means dissolved metals or minerals of unknown type. Don't drink it raw. Even after boiling, minerals stay. Avoid it as a regular source.
- **Pools** can be scalding, with some hot enough to poach an egg. Never step into an unfamiliar one. Test with a probe or a stick, upstream first. The animals know which pools are safe, and watching where they drink is useful, though not foolproof. Their bodies may tolerate what yours can't.
- **Carry a robust filter and a chemical treatment**, and prefer collecting from the meltwater streams coming down from the rim over anything that has passed through the warm rock. Bring a fuel stove, since damp wood is poor fuel.
- **Food:** Pack everything you'll eat, in sealed, waterproof containers. Don't forage. The plants lean toward the veins for reasons botanists can't explain, and that alone says you don't know what is in them. Assume amphibians, epiphytes, and fungi are unsafe to eat. Frogs range from fingernail-sized to dog-sized, and many amphibians carry skin toxins. Handle none of them bare-handed, and wash your hands before eating.
- **Storage:** Hang food off the ground. Everything else, especially anything from the marsh, wants to eat it.

## Insects, Parasites, and Health

In a warm, saturated basin, you should expect biting insects, leeches, skin infections, and slow-healing cuts.

- Insect repellent with high-strength active ingredients, plus a fine-mesh net for sleeping. Treat clothes with permethrin before you go.
- A serious first-aid kit with antiseptic, antifungal cream, antibiotics for skin infection, blister care, and rehydration salts. Clean and dry every scratch daily.
- **Respiratory protection:** The mist may carry spores. It's mentioned that the mist-swimmers may feed on them. Bring dust masks or respirators rated for fine particles, and use them when the fog thickens or when you sleep. Watch for cough or wheeze that persists.
- **Vaccinations and prophylaxis** appropriate to tropical environments, ideally checked against your home-world's medical guidance. Because the ecology is unfamiliar, assume disease risk is unknown.
- **Mist-swimmers** glow green when disturbed and leave light on your skin. They look harmless, but don't rub them into eyes or mouth, and wash them off if you can.

## The Noise

The Shelf is loud, a roar like a crowd in a stone room, with frog choruses, bird calls, insects, and running water everywhere. Conversation fails beyond a few paces.

- Agree on **hand signals** before you go, and practice them. Use them for danger, stop, come here, and help. Tie in with a partner system and never move out of sight of each other.
- Carry **whistles** and possibly a small radio. Radios may work poorly given what the pylons do to instruments, so don't depend on them.
- Bring **earplugs**. Sleeping in the chorus is difficult. Keep a pair in for the night, but leave one ear free or set an alarm if you need to hear a warning.

The noise also hides other sounds. You won't hear a mud-mat giving way or an animal approaching. Slow down and look.

## The Marsh and Terrain

- Floating mats of sedge and moss over black water look like ground and aren't. Probe with a pole before stepping, and stay on the terraces and hills where you can. Carry a length of rope and know how to extract someone from mud.
- The reeds are taller than a person and rattle constantly, so you lose sightlines and sound both. Don't enter reed beds without a plan.
- The rock is warm everywhere and hot in the low places. Never sit or lie on bare rock without checking. Veins are hottest, and steam lines mark them.
- Bring a **trekking pole** or two, for probing and balance on slick stone.

## Navigation and Time

This is where the Shelf stops being ordinary.

- **Compasses lean** near the pylons. Clocks lose time and then regain it over a day or two. Instruments drift. Assume electronics can fail or mislead you.
- Navigate by **landmarks, paper maps, and the cairn line.** Keep a written log with sketches. Mark your route. Take bearings from the rim peaks, which stand well above and are reliable. Note the sun.
- Carry **several independent timekeepers**: mechanical watches, a digital watch, and a sun-based reckoning. Don't trust any one, and don't plan tight schedules around them.
- Keep track of days by your own marks. Because the time slippage seems to correct itself, don't panic if clocks disagree, but don't rely on them for medication, food rationing, or the weather window for your descent.

## The Pylons

These off-white ceramic spires are the most important thing to treat carefully.

- **Do not touch, climb, or damage them.** Nobody knows what they do, and there's a reasonable belief that they keep the entire basin warm. Interfering with them might harm you, the ecosystem, or the people who depend on it. That includes the herder.
- **Don't camp beside them.** Instruments misbehave near them, the hum can be felt in your body, and prolonged exposure has unknown effects. Sleep at least a good distance away, on high ground, not in a hollow where mist gathers.
- **Keep your distance in the lakes.** Pylons that rise from water without ripples and have mist that doesn't condense around them are strange even by Shelf standards.
- Observe, sketch, and photograph from afar. Don't take samples. If you're a researcher, this is the place to ask permission and defer to local custom.

## The Pale Veins and the Plants

The translucent, waxy mineral has never been matched by lowland geologists. The plants seek it for reasons unknown.

- Don't collect it, chew it, scrape it, or carry it in your pockets. It may be harmless. You have no way to know.
- Avoid sleeping directly over a vein. It's the warmest place, but also where the unknown lives.
- If you notice strange sensations, such as tingling, headaches, odd dreams, or a sense of being listened to, move away from the veins and pylons and tell your companions.

## The Pause

Every few minutes the entire basin goes quiet, and then resumes together. This is a fact of the place, not a warning sign.

- **Don't be alarmed.** The first time will feel eerie. Tell everyone in your party beforehand.
- **Don't shout or fire anything into the silence.** Whatever the animals are listening for, you don't know what it is. Stay still and let it pass.
- Notice how the pause affects you. Travelers who report feeling "listened to" are describing something real to them. Keep track of your mental state, and if you find you don't want to leave, take that as a signal to leave sooner.

## The Herder and Local Etiquette

Someone replaces the topmost cairn stones and, in the dry season, a herder may be living in the fern-thatched hut.

- If you meet them, greet them with respect and few words, and be ready to communicate by hand. Don't assume you share a language.
- Offer food or a small useful gift. You are a guest in someone's seasonal home.
- **Ask about the pools.** Local knowledge about which are safe, and where the marsh is treacherous, is worth more than any equipment.
- Don't move stones, cairns, the squared ring in the reed bed, or the stair to nowhere. Leave the ruins as you found them, and do not disturb any cairn you didn't build.

## Camping

- Set up on high, solid ground, away from pylons, marsh, and low hollows where mist and cold air pool.
- Use a raised platform or a ground sheet with a drainage trench. Everything will be damp.
- Use a hammock or tent with a fly and mosquito net. Store boots inside, upside down, and shake them out in the morning, because frogs and salamanders love shoes.
- Keep a dry set of sleeping clothes sealed in a waterproof bag, and never wear them during the day.
- Cook away from your sleeping area, and keep all food sealed.

## Multiverse-Specific Concerns

- **Your anchor and your exit.** Make sure your method of getting home is robust and doesn't depend on electronics that might fail near pylons. If it does, keep it far away from them and carry a backup.
- **Time drift.** If the Shelf's time slippage affects your return, it's a risk for synchronizing with your home timeline. Plan a generous buffer, and don't stay longer than you intended.
- **Leave no trace, especially biologically.** Sterilize boots, tents, and gear before you go, to avoid carrying in foreign spores, seeds, or pathogens. A closed high basin like this could be an ecological island, and you could be a plague. Do the same when leaving, so you don't carry Shelf organisms back to the lowlands or your home world.
- **Don't bring anything you can't afford to lose.** Instruments, cameras, and electronics may fail. Assume some will.

## Mindset

The Shelf is beautiful and slow, and it invites you to linger. The days repeat, the light shifts slightly, and the hum settles into your body. Most of the danger here comes from complacency: walking on soft ground because it looks solid, drinking water because it looks clear, staying an extra day because it's peaceful.

Go with a partner, a plan, and a turnaround date. Be quiet, be patient, and be wary of anything you can't explain. Expect to be changed a little by having listened when the whole basin held its breath. And when it's time, put your cold-weather layers back on slowly, step over the black lip, and go down.
