# Preparing for The Worn Table

## The Right Frame of Mind

Most dangerous places announce themselves, and this one doesn't. There are no predators, no storms of any size, no hostile inhabitants, and no obvious toxins. The danger is that a soft, green, mild-seeming place will talk you into being careless. Its hazards are cold, wet, thin air, and the loss of your sense of time and distance. Behind those sits an unanswered question: whose house you are standing in.

Go as a guest in a home whose owners have stepped out, and whose hearths are still warm.

## Before You Go

**Arrange your return first.** Distance and time are soft there, so any exit that depends on reaching a far-off place by a set hour is a bad exit. Prefer a return method that works from wherever you stand, or one you can trigger yourself. If you must use a fixed point, set your anchor at a granite dome. It will be the most stable thing in the landscape. Plan a margin of several days beyond your intended stay, and carry supplies to match.

**Go with at least one other person.** Two is the minimum and three is better. The place is hypnotic, with its slow color pulses, breathing sedge, and muffled footsteps. A solo traveler can lose an afternoon watching a hillside bloom, and no one will notice. Companions check on one another.

**Rehearse leaving.** Agree in advance on a phrase or signal that means "we go now, no discussion." The place will offer many reasons to stay one more hour.

## Body and Health

**Altitude.** The air is thin, though nothing looks high. Spend a few days acclimatizing at elevation beforehand, and don't rely on the flat landscape to tell you you're fine. Know the signs of altitude sickness: headache, nausea, poor sleep, and unusual clumsiness. Carry the medication and descend if symptoms worsen. The "descent" may be your exit, so be ready to use it early.

**Cold and wet.** Your breath will show at noon, and everything is damp. Hypothermia, not any predator, is the most likely thing to hurt you. Wear layers that keep insulating when wet, meaning synthetics or other non-cotton fabrics. Bring a full waterproof shell, waterproof pants, warm hats, spare gloves, and spare socks in dry bags. You will sink to the ankle or shin in moss, so bring tall waterproof boots and gaiters. Bring more dry clothing than seems reasonable, and change into it at the first sign of shivering, not after.

A note on materials: nothing in this world has ever been made of wool, leather, bone, or horn. The place likely has no opinion about your jacket, but it costs little to favor synthetics and plant fibers. Anything you carry will be conspicuous enough.

**Food.** Bring all of it, plus a reserve. There are no animals, no game, no fish you can count on, and no edible-looking thing that should be trusted. Pack calorie-dense food that doesn't need cooking, since fuel and dry conditions are uncertain.

**The porridge.** You will see bowls of pale porridge still steaming on tables. Don't eat it. It isn't necessarily poisoned. It is simply not yours, and eating a meal set out for someone else, in a house with a living hearth, is the sort of act that tends to have consequences in places like this. Whatever their nature, the residents' hospitality was not offered to you, and you have no way of knowing what accepting it would mean.

**Water.** The rivers are tea-colored with peat. Filter and boil everything, and carry enough purification for the whole trip. Do not drink from the tarns. Their reflections are not always of the sky above, and they are cold enough to numb you fast. Keep a safe distance from their edges, and do not lean over them and stare. Treat "late" rain and "early" rain the same as any other water: purify it.

## Gear

- **Shelter:** A strong, low-profile, four-season tent with a full fly, and a groundsheet. Pitch it on bare granite when possible, not on moss. The moss is wet, and you'll crush an unbroken carpet that took eras to grow.
- **Stove and fuel:** Carry your own. Do not burn the peat you find in hearths or stacked in the cities. It belongs to the residents.
- **Navigation:** Bring a compass, a paper map you draw yourself as you go, and a mechanical watch. Don't trust electronics to behave, and don't trust any single instrument. Cross-check them all. Expect disagreement.
- **Tether and cord:** Bring long, brightly colored cord. For crossings of open ground, an attached line between companions is cheap insurance against getting separated when the fog fills the swells.
- **Marking:** Cairns will not work as trail markers, because they restack themselves into arches, hands, and spirals. Use something else: biodegradable flagging that you collect on the way out, or notes in your log. Consider whether anything you leave behind is a message you would be willing to have answered.
- **Lighting:** Headlamps and spares with extra batteries, kept warm inside your clothing.
- **Recording:** A waterproof notebook and pencil. Log times, positions, and observations often. When distance and time are soft, your own written record is a fixed point.
- **Rain protection for everything:** Dry bags, pack covers, and a waterproof case for anything vital.
- **First aid:** Include blister care, hypothermia supplies, altitude medications, and a foil blanket.
- **Waste:** Bring bags and pack out everything, including human waste.

## Biosecurity

This ecosystem has grown for ages without any animal contact. It has never been grazed, chewed, or pollinated. You and your companions will be the first animals it has met, unless the residents are something we don't have a word for.

Before arriving, scrub your boots and gear, and check cuffs, pockets, and Velcro for seeds, spores, and insects. Wash your clothes in a fresh cycle. Do not bring pets, live bait, or fresh produce. Carry nothing living out either. The moss and felt-columns are not souvenirs.

## Behavior on the Ground

**Trust the ground, not the horizon.** Distance is a matter of mood there. A city an hour away may take ten minutes or a day. Watch your footing, take the path the land allows, and don't push against it. Pace yourself by rest and food, not by destination. You will get there when you get there, so avoid the trap of setting a schedule and then forcing it.

**Rain is not weather; it's a location.** Rain falls in sharp-edged columns. You can step out of them, so don't try to wait them out. Watch for columns falling upward, which are harmless but disorienting, and for rain arriving under a clear sky, which is some other morning's rain finally landing. Stay dry, and treat any wetting as a warming problem.

**Shadows lag.** Yours will trail you like a slow thought. That's fine, but do not use shadows to tell direction or time. At dusk, expect the felt-columns to keep their long shadows for an hour after the light is gone. Don't plan to travel in this window, since it's a poor time to judge anything.

**Sounds may arrive before their causes.** A splash, then a stone dropping seconds later. Sometimes a sound arrives with no cause at all. Do not investigate. Do not throw stones into the tarns to test it.

**Do not go below.** You will find grated wells and cracks, and hear a hammerstone tapping in the strata beneath the city, along with lamps burning in the lower levels. This is the hardest thing to leave alone, but the deep layers are inhabited in a way the surface only implies. Don't climb down, don't shout down, and don't answer the tapping with your own.

**Do not disturb the work.** Tools move on benches, and handprints appear in wet clay while you watch. If you see this, step back, stay quiet, and let it finish. Don't move an adze, don't add your handprint, and don't fill in what appears to be a gap. Work is going on here, and it is not for you to interrupt or complete.

**Do not take anything.** Leave the copper, the carved spoons, the stones, and the clay where they are. If you feel you must leave something, leave nothing that could be mistaken for an offering. Your thanks are best expressed by leaving no trace.

**Be courteous.** Knock at doors that stand open. Speak aloud when entering a hall, and state your business plainly. Don't sit on the beds, use the hearths, or lie down anywhere that looks made. You are a visitor in an occupied place.

**Camp outside the cities.** Move through them by day, and sleep on granite domes above the fog line when you can. Be somewhere else at dusk, when the lamps come on one by one in the order they were first lit. Watch it from a distance if you like. It is worth seeing, but not from inside.

**The seams.** Where the horizon shows a faint vertical line with different seasons on either side, keep away. Don't walk toward it, and don't use it as a landmark. If one drifts toward you, retreat and hold your ground until it passes or you have gained distance.

## Mental Preparation

The Worn Table will feel peaceful, and it is, but its peace comes from the slow wearing away of everything sharp, including the sharpness of your own attention. You will feel lulled, you will want to stay, and you may start to feel that the place understands you. Watch for these signs in yourself and your companions:

- Losing track of how long you've been sitting or walking
- Talking less, or agreeing too easily
- Reluctance to check the watch, the map, or the log
- A feeling that the city is expecting you

If two of you notice the same sign, use the departure phrase and start moving.

## What to Bring Home

Photographs, notes, and the memory of a green and quiet world. Nothing else. Leave the place as you found it: worn, patient, and warm at the hearth, with its work going on.
