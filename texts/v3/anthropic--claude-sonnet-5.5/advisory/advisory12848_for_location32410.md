# Preparing for the Stair-City of the Weeping Shelf

## The Short Version

You are going somewhere very high, very wet, very cold at the edges of the day, and almost entirely without help. The city is not hostile, but it is indifferent in the way large, old, water-shaped things are. Nearly everything that can go wrong here goes wrong slowly: altitude sickness, chilling, a bad step on a wet tread, a wrong turn in fog. So preparation means slowness, dryness, warmth, and humility.

## 1. Altitude Comes First

Clouds at knee level and a sky darker than it should be mean thin air, probably well above where most people have ever slept. Treat this as the main medical fact of the trip.

- **Acclimatize before you arrive.** If your route allows it, spend several days in a high world first, sleeping progressively higher. Arriving from sea level and starting a long walk is the classic way to get seriously ill.
- **Your first day should be nearly motionless.** Arrive, sit, drink, eat, sleep. Headache, nausea, and poor sleep are expected. Confusion, stumbling, a wet cough, breathlessness at rest, or blue lips mean you go *down* immediately. Here, that means back through your portal, not down a staircase.
- **Bring a pulse oximeter** and know your baseline. Talk to a physician beforehand about acetazolamide for prevention, and about emergency dexamethasone. Bring both if approved.
- **Drink far more than feels natural.** The air is dry in the sun and wet in the cloud, and you will not notice dehydration.
- **Skip alcohol and sleeping pills** for the first several days.
- **Pack sun protection.** Ultraviolet exposure is severe at this height, even under thin cloud and even on cold mornings. Bring glacier-grade sunglasses, a brimmed hat, and high-SPF sunscreen and lip balm.

## 2. Dress for Wet Cold, Not Just Cold

The daily cycle sets the plan: hard frost at dawn, pearl-gray damp at noon, steady soft rain in the afternoon, then cold fog in the low streets. You will be wet more or less continuously, which is more dangerous than being merely cold.

- **No cotton anywhere.** Wear merino or synthetic base layers, a warm fleece or wool mid-layer, and a real waterproof shell with a hood that fits over a hat. Add waterproof overtrousers.
- **Insulation that survives dampness** is best: synthetic fill, or down only if fully shelled and kept in dry bags.
- **Feet are the priority.** Bring waterproof boots with aggressive, soft-compound soles, gaiters, and at least four pairs of wool socks. Change socks at midday and evening and dry the spares against your body. Long exposure to cold water causes immersion foot, so watch for numb, white, wrinkled skin.
- **Bring warm hat, liner gloves, and waterproof outer gloves.** You will be touching wet stone constantly for balance.
- **Pack everything in dry bags**, then pack the dry bags inside a pack liner. Assume nothing not sealed will stay dry.
- **Wool in muted, undyed tones** will help you fit in with the people here. Bright neon shells make you a loud object in a quiet land. Earth tones are more polite.

## 3. Your Feet and the Stone

The stone is slate-like, always wet, and glazed with a thin sheen. Every stair tread is a shallow bowl of standing water. At dawn, the puddles carry ice skins and the steps may be sheeted in frost.

- Use **trekking poles** with rubber tips. Metal tips will skitter on the slate and are best avoided, since they scar old surfaces.
- Bring **light traction spikes** for dawn frost, but take them off on stone that is not iced. Do not scrape the treads.
- **Test before you trust.** Place a foot, weight it slowly, then commit. Mineral curtains hanging from lintels and stair edges are slippery and, when soft, fragile.
- **Do not lean on, climb, or pull at walls.** They stand because they lean into the slope and press against one another. A single dislodged plate can be a centuries-old repair job for someone else.
- **Do not leave a thumbprint in the growing mineral.** It is tempting and the soft ones will take it. Look, don't touch.
- **Never descend flooded stairs.** They continue below the surface for a dozen steps and further into darkness. The water is very cold, the depth unknown, and the cold shock alone can incapacitate you. Also remember that the underlying drains are large enough to walk through, but they carry water fast in the afternoon.

## 4. Water: Everywhere and Not to Be Trusted

You are surrounded by water and should treat almost none of it as drinkable without treatment.

- **Filter and disinfect everything.** Peat, gravel, frogs, and grazers mean parasites and bacteria. Use a filter plus chemical treatment or boiling. At this altitude water boils at a lower temperature, so boil longer.
- **Expect tea-colored water**, stained by peat. That is normal, but tannins and silt clog filters, so bring spare cartridges and a pre-filter cloth.
- **Some cisterns may be drinking sources.** Do not assume which. Watch what the locals draw from and ask by gesture before you take any.
- **Never wash yourself, your dishes, or your clothes in a cistern, channel, or spring.** Soap in the wrong basin is a serious offense against a system people depend on.
- **Pack out all waste, including human waste.** There is no place to put it that isn't connected to the water. Bring proper waste-bags and carry them out with you. This is the most important environmental rule for you.
- **Stay out of the drain tunnels** unless you are guided. They fill fast in the afternoon and echo in disorienting ways.

## 5. Food and Fuel

Assume no resupply. The population is thin, and their peat, wool, and grazers are not yours to spend.

- **Bring all your calories for the whole stay** plus a few days extra. Cold and altitude increase caloric need, and appetite drops at height, so pack foods you find easy to eat: nuts, hard cheese, oats, dried fruit, chocolate, broth, instant soups.
- **Use a stove that works at altitude and in damp**, such as a canister stove with a windscreen or a liquid-fuel stove, and bring plenty of fuel. Don't burn local peat. It is their winter, and their fires are their lifeline.
- **Cook in the morning, and at night.** Afternoon is for shelter.
- **Hot drinks matter enormously here**, both for morale and for core temperature. Pack a good insulated flask.

## 6. Shelter and Sleep

- A **four-season tent** with a full fly and a strong groundsheet is your default. Ground is either wet stone or springy, saturated peat. Camp on high, level stone, never in a channel, courtyard basin, or beside a cistern (cold air and water settle there). Never camp where a gutter could reroute under you.
- **Do not sleep in flooded or half-flooded houses**, however picturesque. The air is damp and cold, the stairs are hazards, and the space might be a resting place or a shrine to someone.
- **Rate your sleeping bag well below the frost temperature**, and bring a sleeping pad with a high R-value. Heat leaves you into the ground first.
- **If you are invited into a home**, accept graciously if you can, but be aware that peat smoke in a small closed room carries a real risk of carbon monoxide. Keep an opening and keep your head low, near the floor's cooler air. Do not fall asleep beside an unventilated fire.

## 7. Timing Your Days

The city runs on a clock you should adopt:

- **Dawn:** Cold and clear, with the best light. Move carefully over frost. Photograph quietly.
- **Mid-morning:** Travel. The cloud cap will form over the densest part of the city.
- **When the cap darkens:** Be indoors or in your shelter. This is the one rule to never bend. Rain follows, the channels rise, and streets become water-courses.
- **Afternoon:** Stay put, rest, eat, dry things, and listen to the murmur.
- **End of the rain:** Step outside. Join the people if you meet any. Stand in the street with your face lifted and say nothing. This is the best hour, and your presence is best when it is quiet.
- **Evening:** Fog and cold settle in the low streets. Do not go wandering. Get to where you will sleep before the light thins.

Storms with hail and roaring wind come once or twice a season. If the sky to the range turns a wall of gray, don't wait to see. Get under stone with a roof and stay away from the channels.

## 8. Navigation and the Anchor

The city has no center, and it extends for weeks of walking. You cannot see across it, fog blurs its details, and everything looks alike: dark stone, wet terraces, stair after stair.

- **Fix a return point** to your portal anchor and do not stray far from it. Weeks of walking are not something you want to be caught doing without a plan.
- **Carry a compass and paper map** of what you explore, and keep notes. Electronics may fail in the cold, in the damp, or because of the world's nature.
- **The main rule of the city is your rule too:** every stair descends toward water, and by water you find a path. If you are lost, go down.
- **Leave no marks.** No chalk, no cairns, no ribbons, no scratched arrows. Mark your map, not their walls.
- **Always travel with at least one other person**, and leave a written plan at your anchor. There is no rescue service.

## 9. Meeting the People

They are few, quiet, and unhurried. They have no metal and may never have seen it. They keep the city working by hand and by ancestral rule, and they will notice you long before you notice them.

- **Speak softly and rarely.** Sound carries oddly here, and loudness is rude.
- **Keep your movements slow.** Approach openly, in view, stopping at a distance. Wait to be acknowledged.
- **Learn to read hospitality.** If you are offered a place by a fire, a warm drink, or a spot in a doorway, accept with a nod and a hand to the chest or a similar simple gesture, and offer what you can in return.
- **Think hard about gifts.** Do not bring metal tools, knives, or shiny novelties as presents. A gift can reshape a small society. Food you are willing to share, or help with a channel or a stone (if they permit it), is safer. Watch and ask first.
- **Ask before photographing anyone,** and if in doubt, don't. Do not fly drones, play music, or use noisy machines.
- **Respect the rules:** don't build across a channel, don't wall in a spring, don't dam anything, don't move stones, and don't repair the old stones. If you see a fallen stone, do not reset it yourself. Point it out or leave it.

## 10. Leaving Nothing, Taking Nothing

- **Clean your boots, packs, and tent before you arrive.** Lichens here grow in slow, concentric maps that may be centuries old. Foreign spores, seeds, or insects could do harm. Never step on cushion plants or lichen crusts if you can help it.
- **Do not take stones, mineral tongues, lichen, frogs, or feathers.**
- **Do not feed the grazers.** Give them room on stairs, since they descend with great deliberation and you'd be in the way.
- **Take out everything you brought,** including food scraps, wrappers, tissue, and used filters.

## 11. A Basic Kit List

- Layered wool or synthetic clothing, waterproof shell and overtrousers
- Waterproof boots, gaiters, many wool socks, hat, gloves
- Four-season tent, insulated pad, cold-rated sleeping bag
- Stove, fuel, insulated flask, high-calorie food
- Water filter, spare cartridges, purification tablets
- Poles with rubber tips, light traction spikes
- Headlamp with spare batteries kept warm inside your clothing
- First-aid kit with blister care, hypothermia supplies, altitude medications, oximeter
- Waste bags, small trowel not to be used (you pack it out)
- Compass, paper map, notebook, pencil
- Sun protection: glacier glasses, sunscreen, lip balm
- Dry bags and a pack liner
- A quiet mind

## 12. The Right Frame of Mind

The place asks you to be slow, to be dry, to be quiet, and to follow water. It will reward you with things you can't buy anywhere else: a lit terrace far off like a lamp, a cascade heard through a wall, a grazer tearing moss three terraces away, the sound of your own pulse. Come prepared to be small in it. If you manage that, the last hour of the afternoon, when the rain stops and the light returns over the roofs and everyone stands silently in the wet street, will be one of the best you ever spend anywhere.
