# Preparing to Visit the Drum Beneath the Snowfield

This is a technical dive at the top of the world, in a resonating chamber that rewrites its own floor. Most of the ways it can kill you are ordinary. Only a few are unique to the place. Prepare for the ordinary ones properly and you will have enough attention left for the strange ones.

## 1. What you are really preparing for

Four problems stack on top of each other:

1. **Altitude.** At about 5,000 m the air holds roughly half the pressure of sea level.
2. **Glacier travel.** You must reach an unmarked acre and get down through ice that has been thinned by heat from below.
3. **A hard dive.** About 60 m deep, in water of unknown chemistry, with hot vents, gas seeps, and no free surface to escape to.
4. **A violent acoustic environment.** It is loud enough to be felt as force, and it changes without warning.

Any one of these would justify a dedicated expedition. Do not attempt this as a casual visit.

## 2. Before you leave: the multiverse side

- **Secure your return.** Bring a redundant anchor, and keep it on the surface and dry. Do not carry your only way home into the water. Assume the chamber's noise, mineral crust, and cold could damage a delicate device. If your method is something you carry, put a second copy in the surface camp and a third with someone who will not be diving.
- **Assume your arrival point is imprecise.** You may land on the wrong shoulder of the mountain. Plan for a day or more of walking on ice to find the right acre, with a map you drew from the description rather than one you trust.
- **Assume time is unreliable.** The tally-keeper counted in elevens, and the cairns are freshly stacked though no human has come down in living memory. Something is keeping time here, but not necessarily the same time you keep. Carry food, fuel, and oxygen for at least twice your intended stay.
- **Learn what is not known.** No one has recorded the chamber's water chemistry, the exact gas composition, the true pressure, or the state of the ice. Everything below is a plan for uncertainty.

## 3. Body and team

**Acclimatize for real.** Spend two to three weeks stepping up to camp altitude before you attempt anything technical. Plan for a rest day for every 600 m or so above 3,000 m. Watch for headache, confusion, and breathlessness at rest. Anyone who develops them goes down, without argument. Bring dexamethasone and a portable hyperbaric bag for emergencies, and know how to use both.

**Go as a team of at least six:**
- Two divers. One diving alone at this depth is unthinkable.
- One standby diver, fully kitted, at the bore hole.
- One surface tender who never leaves the line.
- One medic with altitude and dive experience.
- One glacier guide who knows crevasses and ice bridges.

Choose people who stay calm under sustained noise. Some don't. Test this by having candidates sit in a loud, vibrating environment for an hour, such as beside a running compressor with hearing protection on, and see how they cope.

**Medical screening:** No ear or sinus problems. No history of panic, cardiac arrhythmia, or seizure. No pregnancy. Sound is felt in the sternum and teeth, and the concussions affect air spaces in the body, so anything wrong with lungs or ears will be exposed.

## 4. Getting to the ice

- **Standard glacier kit:** rope, harnesses, ice screws, crampons, axes, probes, and snow anchors. Travel roped and probe as you go.
- **Thin ice is the real danger.** The chamber roof is a heat-thinned lens. The snowfield above it may look identical to safe ice but be a shell. Approach with ground-penetrating radar if your world's technology has it, and otherwise probe constantly. Avoid concentrated loads. Never walk in a clump.
- **Look for the bore hole.** It was made with fire and antler picks, so it will be narrow, old, and possibly iced over. Treat any tether line as unreliable. Don't trust hide and sinew that has been under water for centuries.
- **Weather:** Bring a mountain tent, fuel, and a stove that works at altitude. Have a plan to retreat to shelter if a storm arrives while divers are underwater.
- **Sun and wind:** Snow goggles and full skin cover. UV at that altitude is savage, and the white light bouncing off the ice will blind you in hours if you're careless.

## 5. Entering the chamber

Treat the bore hole as a potentially dangerous entry.

- **Test the air and water at the top.** Lower a sampling line first. You need to know whether there is a gas cap, whether bubbles carry CO₂, H₂S, or methane, and the temperature of the water. If there is a gas pocket, do not breathe it. Fixed sensors and a sampling pump should be used before any person goes down.
- **Never ascend into a gas pocket.** Silver blisters under the ice are gas, not air. Treat every one as toxic or oxygen-poor.
- **Rig a secured descent line** for the divers and tie it off at the surface with redundancy. The tethers already present are not for your use. Cut nothing and clip into nothing that isn't yours.

## 6. Diving gear

**Altitude diving at 60 m is a serious technical problem.** The reduced surface pressure means standard tables and computers are wrong. Get help from someone who computes altitude dive tables, and use a computer that accepts altitude settings. Assume much longer decompression than a sea-level dive to the same depth.

**Gas:**
- **Trimix or a closed-circuit rebreather** to manage narcosis and oxygen toxicity at depth. Air at 60 m is not acceptable.
- **Multiple redundant supplies:** a bailout for each diver, plus staged decompression gas on the line.
- **Expect regulator problems.** Mineral-rich water can crust valves, and hot water can cause free-flows. Bring spare regulators, rinse gear compulsively, and inspect it between every dive.

**Thermal protection:**
- **Drysuit with heated undergarments** for the cold parts, and a hood and gloves rated for extreme cold.
- **Hot vents exist.** They blast mineral water that may be scalding. Wear gloves and suit sections that resist heat, and never put a hand or face near a vent.
- **Temperature will vary sharply** from cold ice water to hot vent water over a few meters, so expect to feel it in the suit.

**Sound and pressure:**
- **You cannot block the noise.** Underwater sound goes straight into the bones and body. A thick hood helps with the ears, and a full-face mask helps with the sinuses and jaw, but you will feel it regardless.
- **Air-filled body cavities are vulnerable to sharp pressure pulses.** Keep your ears and sinuses clear and equalize constantly. Any cold or congestion means you don't dive.
- **Rockfall concussions** are the sharpest hazard. When a slab lets go, the pressure wave can injure lungs, gut, and ears. Stay away from walls and overhanging rock, and rest in the quiet nodes when possible.

**Lighting:**
- Daylight comes through the ice, so bring dive lights mostly as a backup. Turn them low, because lantern shrimp flare in response to sound and may respond to light.
- Carry a slate and a light code system. Voice and speakers are useless.

## 7. Diving in a sound-shaped room

**Communication.** Hand signals and slates. Use line-pull codes with the surface. The 11-second pulse is a natural metronome, so you can time signals and breathing to it.

**Spatial awareness.** The floor is a map of loud and still, and the map changes.
- Don't trust any old survey. What you crossed yesterday may be gone.
- Note the pillars, ridges, and fins as landmarks. They can also break, and the pillars are hollow, sharp at the tip, and thin as wrists. They will tear a suit or dislodge under a fin. Keep well clear.
- Use a compass and a reel to lay a guideline. The chamber's water is clear, but the noise and bubbles will disorient you.

**The nodes.** The quiet places are small and precious. When you find one, mark it and use it to rest, check gas, and recover. But do not assume a node will stay quiet. Have an exit route that doesn't depend on a single spot.

**Buoyancy.** Bubble plumes lower the density of the water, so you can lose lift suddenly near a seep. Stay off the floor near vents, and watch your depth gauge continuously.

**The 11-second pulse.** It will affect your breathing, heartbeat, and perhaps your balance. Some divers may find it hypnotic or nauseating. Agree in advance that anyone who feels dizzy, euphoric, or too calm signals and ascends. Calm can be a warning sign here.

**Time limits.** Keep bottom time short. The noise is tiring, cold saps you, and decompression at altitude is long. Plan turnarounds conservatively.

## 8. What to leave alone

- **The Sitter.** It rests with its face against the stone, apparently feeling the pillars grow. Don't approach, touch, light, or follow it. If it moves stones, let it. Give it a wide berth.
- **The cairns.** Someone, or something, is maintaining them. Don't rebuild, add to, or move them. Don't step on the nodes where they stand. They may be the only map that matters.
- **The diving bell.** The air inside is stale and cold, and could be dangerously low in oxygen or high in CO₂. Do not breathe from it. It is also an archaeological site: the flute, cord, scraper, and tally stone belong to someone. Photograph and document them in place. Don't remove anything. If you must enter, ventilate it first and keep your regulator in your mouth.
- **The pillars and ridges.** They are the room's writing. Breaking them changes the sound, which changes the growth. Don't collect samples unless you have a compelling reason and have taken only tiny fragments from an inactive area.
- **Sound generation.** Bring no active sonar, pingers, or noise-making devices. You could rewrite the floor and disrupt the animals. Passive hydrophones and simple recording are fine.
- **Contamination.** This is a sealed system with delicate life. Sterilize your gear beforehand and don't introduce anything.

## 9. Emergencies

- **Rockfall or major pressure event:** Move away from walls, avoid the floor, keep breathing normally, and never hold your breath on ascent. Signal your buddy.
- **Loss of the line:** Go to the nearest node if you can find one, and stay put until the pulse and noise have settled. Then follow your guideline back.
- **Equipment failure:** Switch to bailout immediately and ascend along the line, following your decompression plan as closely as you can.
- **Gas exposure:** If you smell rotten eggs or feel sudden headache or dizziness, treat it as toxic. Go up and get oxygen.
- **Hypothermia and scalding:** Both are likely. Keep warm blankets and burn dressings at the surface.
- **Standby diver:** Someone must be able to go in. The hyperbaric bag and oxygen must be ready at the surface.

## 10. State of mind

You are entering a room that has nowhere to put its own noise. Every disturbance is preserved and shaped into stone. The tally-keeper lived in the quiet places, counting in elevens. You are a much shorter visitor, but you will hear, feel, and be shaped by the same drum.

Expect to be overwhelmed at first. The noise takes over the senses, and the body wants to flee. Slow down, settle into the pulse, and let your training run your hands. Then, if conditions permit, find a node and listen. It is quiet there, not silent, but you can hear a distant thrum and the shifting light above.

If a shaft of light drops down onto a single pillar and it hums, watch the ghost-eels. Don't chase what they are turning toward. Just stay still, and note it.

Then go up slowly, honor your decompression stops, and leave the room as you found it, or as close as you can.
