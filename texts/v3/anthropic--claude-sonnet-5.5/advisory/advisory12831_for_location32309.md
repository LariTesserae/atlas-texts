# Preparing for The Dry Floor

## What kind of visit this is

This is not a dangerous place in the usual sense. Nothing hunts here, nothing moves, and nothing grows fast enough to matter. The dangers are the quiet ones: a deep pit with still air, a long climb on crumbly stone, fine dust, near-darkness, and a silence that makes it easy to lose track of time. It is also an archaeological site, possibly the last trace of a people who are gone, and it records everything you do. Prepare like a cave surveyor and a conservator at once.

## Before you go: the surface

The pit opens onto a plain under a sky of permanent dusk. Nobody has described the surface, so plan for uncertainty there.

- Bring layers. The basin is body-warm, but the plain above may be cold, and you will be sweating and then exposed at the rim.
- Carry a compass or star reference you trust. Under a fixed dusk sky, sun-based navigation is useless and the few stars may not match yours.
- Mark your entry point. A limestone plain with a sinkhole edge that has "forgotten it was broken" may be hard to spot again. Use a bright flag, a cairn, or an electronic beacon at the rim.
- Fix your multiverse return method (anchor, beacon, or pickup schedule) at the surface, not in the pit. Don't rely on it working from the bottom, where the walls may block signals or your device may not behave as it does in open air.

## The descent and climb

The walls are rounded, shouldered, and fluted, not sheer, so it is probably a scramble or a moderate rope descent. But the rock is chalky, greasy, and powdery, with a brittle crust over hollow ground. That is poor for grip and worse for anchors.

- Use a rope and a harness, and don't trust the rock for protection. Rig from something solid on the surface, such as a deeply set anchor, a vehicle, or a long-bearing sling around a substantial mass, and back it up.
- Use a fixed line that stays in place. The person who climbs down must be able to climb out, and the footprints show that someone once did.
- Wear gloves and knee protection. The rock abrades skin, and the powder makes hands slick.
- Test each hold gently. The crust breaks with a muffled crunch and may hide voids.
- Descend on a route you can identify, and note it. Ideally use one path both ways, so you disturb one line of wall rather than several.
- Never go alone. If someone is hurt at the bottom, a second person on the surface is the only reliable rescue.

## Air

The air is warm, motionless, and tastes of chalk. The heat comes from deep in the ground and the walls seep air constantly. A closed pit with a deep heat source and no wind is exactly where heavy gases can pool.

- Bring a personal multi-gas monitor (CO₂, O₂, and ideally H₂S and CO) and check it before you descend and periodically at the bottom. Alarm thresholds should be set conservatively.
- Take it seriously if you feel unusual headache, drowsiness, yawning, or calm contentment. In this place those are not charming.
- Have a plan to climb out at once if the monitor alarms. The warmth and the urge to sit against the wall make it easy to stay too long.
- Wear a respirator (P100 or better). The silt is fine as flour, and each step raises powder that will hang in still air. Carbonate dust is an irritant, and old silt might carry anything.
- Bring eye protection. Dust in the eyes in near-darkness is a real nuisance.

## Heat and water

Body-warm air with no wind means your sweat won't evaporate efficiently, and the dryness will still pull moisture from you.

- Wear light, breathable, long-sleeved clothing. It protects against abrasion and dust.
- Carry more water than seems necessary, several liters each, and drink on a schedule. There is no water here at all.
- Eat and drink at the surface, or at least take your food out of sight of the silt. A crumb or spilled drop stays forever, and any organic scrap is contamination. If you must drink below, use a closed bottle and a small designated mat.
- Bring a small amount of electrolytes and a thermometer for the group.

## Light

There is faint dusk-light pooled on the floor. Your eyes will adapt, but it takes time, and a bright torch resets it.

- Bring headlamps with red or very dim settings, and use them sparingly. Give your eyes twenty to forty minutes.
- Bring a stronger light for the climb and for emergencies, but decide in advance when it may be used.
- Don't rely on cameras. Expect long exposures, a tripod, and a small amount of light-painting only if you are certain it won't disturb anything. The best record may be sketching, measurement, and description.
- Bring spare batteries kept warm. The heat helps, but you don't want to test it.
- Carry a light that can be seen from the rim, both for your partners and for yourself.

## Movement on the floor

The floor undulates in swells with soft silt in the hollows, and the silt takes a footprint perfectly and keeps it. The old bare-foot prints run from the shrubs toward the eastern wall.

- Do not step in or across the old prints. Approach the shelf on a separate line, or better, stay on the swells and rock ridges where silt is thin.
- Choose one path in and one path out, walk it once, and note it. Every footprint you make is permanent, and the place will "forget nothing."
- Consider soft, clean, smooth-soled overshoes or bare-sock-thin footwear if the surface allows it, and clean them before you go in so you carry nothing foreign.
- Watch for hollow crust. Test ahead with a pole or trekking stick, carefully.
- Keep the group small, two to four people. A crowd leaves a lot of tracks in a quiet place.

## What not to touch

The shelf, the bowls, the grinding stone, the flakes, and the scraper are the only evidence of the people who came here.

- Look, measure, and photograph. Do not move, pick up, or "just try out" anything. A dropped pebble or replaced cobble is a permanent edit to the record.
- The obsidian-like flakes are still sharp enough to cut, so keep hands away for your own sake too.
- Do not take souvenirs, including a pinch of plant or a chip of rock. One item removed is a piece of a story missing forever.
- The shrubs are spaced by long habit, hold at the edge of survival, and give off a resinous smell when crushed. Don't crush them, and don't taste or burn them. They may irritate skin or lungs. If you want to know the smell, one leaf-tip between gloved fingers, once, is enough. Preferably ask whether you need to.
- The upright flat stones might be arranged or might not. Treat them as if they were.

## The sound, the silence, and your mind

There is a thin hiss from the walls, no echo, no wind, and your own pulse. In that kind of environment, people lose track of time, of distance, and sometimes of the wish to leave.

- Bring a mechanical or otherwise independent watch, and agree a fixed turnaround time before you go down. Make it early. When the warmth is rising through your back and nothing is changing, an hour is a very short time.
- Set alarms and have the surface person call the time by line-tug or radio (test radios first, since rock that swallows sound may swallow signals).
- Use rope tugs or whistle signals as a backup. Voices die quickly here.
- Stay within sight of your partner in the dimness. Being able to see the person you came with matters more than it sounds.
- Keep visits short, a couple of hours at first. The place is not hostile, but it is designed, in effect, for stillness, and stillness is what makes people stay.
- Some travelers report a sense of being "finished with" things. That is the place's mood, not a message. If you feel it strongly, go up.

## Other kit

- A small first-aid kit with eye wash and burn/abrasion supplies.
- A rescue and comms plan filed with someone outside the pit.
- A notebook and pencil (no ink to spill), a measuring tape, and a compass for recording positions relative to the shelf and walls.
- A packing bag for every scrap you bring, including tissues, wrappers, and skin flakes as far as you can manage. Take out everything you brought.
- A hand brush is tempting but should stay in your pack. Cleaning the silt is also altering it.

## Etiquette and ethics

The people who used this place "did not change it, and then they left." That is the model to follow. Treat the basin as a room in which someone is asleep, or one in which the sleeping is complete. Take your time, be quiet, take notes, and leave less trace than a breath. If you find yourself wanting to leave a mark, such as a stone set upright or a pebble placed on the shelf, write it in your notebook instead.

## A last note

The Dry Floor asks very little of you: caution on the way down, care with your feet, and discipline about the clock. If you keep to that, you can sit against the warm wall under the ellipse of blue dark, smell the clean resin of the shrubs, and hear the faint hiss of the walls. Then climb out along the same line you came, and leave the silt showing only the path you needed.
