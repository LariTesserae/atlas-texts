# Preparing to Visit the Pot-Wall Well

## The Short Version

You are visiting a small, old, careful community that lives inside a working structure, in dry country, surrounded by a very large number of birds. The main dangers are dust, heights, heat at noon, and disease from guano and bats. The main social risk is damaging something that looks like rubble but is a family's record of itself. Prepare your lungs, your feet, your water, and your manners.

## Arrival

You will most likely arrive on the hardpan, and the well gives no visual warning. The rim is a knee-high brick ring, and the first thing you'll notice is the sound. Eighteen paces of drop is easily fatal.

- **Arrive in daylight.** If your transit method lets you choose, pick early morning, when the walls are beaded with condensation, the air is cool, and the pit is active but not baking. Never arrive at night. The ring is easy to trip over in the dark, and you would not want to meet the wild cat on open ground.
- **Stop when you hear it.** Stay a few steps back from the ring and let your ears adjust. Approach the lip slowly, on hands and knees if you feel dizzy. Sound and vertigo together disorient some people.
- **Mark your return point.** Note the ring's position, the thorn shrubs, and the angle of the sun. If your return depends on the arrival spot, don't leave a beacon that could be mistaken for a construction or a ritual object. Note the spot mentally, or use something small and removable.
- **Wait to be seen.** The people are hard to startle but they are not naive. Sit near the rim where you're visible, make no sudden moves, and let someone come up the stairs. Being noticed by the hawk-rattle children is probably how you'll be introduced.

## Health and Body

This is the most important section.

**Respiratory.** The well is full of dried droppings, feathers, and guano compost. That means spores and dust: *Histoplasma* (bat and bird guano), *Cryptococcus* (pigeon droppings), *Chlamydia psittaci* (psittacosis), and ammonia in the deep pots.

- Bring **well-fitting P100 or FFP3 respirators**, plus spare filters. Wear one in the deep tiers, near the bat pots, and any time you disturb old guano, plaster dust, or compost. The residents seem fine, but they have generations of adaptation and constant exposure. You have neither.
- Wear one on windy days, when dust rises off the rim.
- Bring **sealed goggles or wraparound glasses** for grit and feather dust.
- Skip the bottom bat-pot tiers entirely. If you have asthma, a compromised immune system, or are pregnant, seriously reconsider the trip.

**Vaccines and prophylaxis.**
- Tetanus is essential, since you'll be around brick, bone tools, copper hooks, and hand-cut edges.
- Get rabies pre-exposure vaccination if you can. Bats and a wild cat both live here, and post-exposure care will not be available.
- Standard travel vaccines (hepatitis A, typhoid) are sensible. Diarrheal illness from local water and food is likely.
- Bring antibiotics and an antidiarrheal, and take a basic wound kit with irrigation syringe, antiseptic, and closure strips. Bring insect repellent, since the basins breed mosquitoes and the bats only partly help.

**Bees.** The east face is a working apiary. If you've never been stung, or you know you're allergic, carry two epinephrine auto-injectors and tell your hosts. Avoid that face in the afternoon heat.

**Snakes.** They are small, and they hunt geckos in the plaster. Don't put your hand into a pot or crevice you can't see into. Wear gloves around cracks and nesting holes.

**Heat and cold.** Noon in the pit is hot and blazing, and people retreat into shaded pot-rooms. Nights are cold enough to create condensation.
- Bring a wide-brimmed hat, a light sun-covering layer, and sunscreen for the midday hours.
- Bring warm layers for night and dawn.
- Drink before you are thirsty. The pit's dryness and heat will dehydrate you quietly.

## Water and Food

Water is the well's central resource and it is tiny. **Treat every drop you take from the cistern as a gift.**

- **Bring your own water for the first days.** Carry several liters if you can. Do not drink from the cistern until someone offers it, and don't draw from it yourself.
- Water from the cistern is condensation collected from guano-stained walls and clay gutters. Treat it with a filter and chemical purification, or boil it. A ceramic or hollow-fiber filter plus chlorine dioxide tablets is a sensible combination.
- Do not wash in the basins or channels. Don't pour anything into the drains.
- If you are given food, accept it. It will be pigeon meat, eggs, figs, caper, gourd, and bitter herbs. Eat what you are given, and expect the eggs and meat to be well cooked. If you have allergies to eggs, honey, or nuts, tell someone early and clearly.
- Bring your own calorie-dense backup such as bars, nuts, and electrolyte powder. Don't eat these in front of others without sharing, and don't dump packaging.
- Carry out all your waste. Ask where to relieve yourself. Never assume, since the compost is a crop and the drains are the water supply.

## Clothing and Gear

- **Footwear:** Sturdy soles with good grip. The stair treads are dished and smooth, and the herringbone floor gleams after rain. Locals go barefoot, but you don't have their calluses. Avoid noisy hard-heeled boots on the terraces.
- **Clothing:** Long sleeves and trousers in natural fibers, neutral colors. No bright synthetic flash or dangling cords. The locals wear undyed wool and grass weave with feathers in their hair, so plain, muted clothing will help you avoid standing out.
- **Gloves:** Thin work gloves for scrambling on brick and around nests.
- **Headlamp with a red or dim setting:** Use it sparingly. Sudden light startles roosting birds and can cause a panic that flings droppings and feathers everywhere.
- **Small pack:** Keep everything inside it. Loose items can fall into the pit or into someone's pot.
- **Rope or harness:** Don't bring these to climb. Do not rappel. The wall is not a cliff, it is a structure held together by mortar and continual upkeep, and your weight could break bricks.
- **Electronics:** Bring what you truly need, and keep it out of sight. Camera flashes, phones, and speakers are disruptive here. Dust and moisture also threaten electronics, so pack them in sealed bags with desiccant.

## Sound

- **Ear protection:** The ambient level is high but not damaging. Bring soft earplugs for sleep and for the dusk swift-drop, which can be startlingly loud up close.
- **Do not add sound.** No music, no whistling, no whooping, no clapping to test the acoustics. The wind-chord is a designed instrument, and a stray pitch may clash with it or set off a bird panic.
- **Listen to the chord.** If it pleases you, say so. A stranger who appreciates the singing pots will be warmly welcomed. A stranger who taps them, blows across them, or tries to "play" them is committing a minor sacrilege.

## Behavior and Etiquette

The most important principle is that **nothing here is scenery. Everything is a repair, a nest, a tool, or a record.**

- **Do not remove or take anything.** No pots, brick fragments, feathers, or shards as souvenirs. A broken chip may be a family's signature. A missing brick is a hole in someone's history.
- **Touch only what is offered.** Don't lean on ledges, prop your pack against pots, or grab railings and edges for balance. Brick edges are soft with age, and repairs are fresh in places. Ask about stairs before you use them.
- **Do not touch nests, eggs, or birds.** Pigeons are the economy. Owls and bats are working members of the ecosystem. If you disturb a nest, you may wreck a household's food supply.
- **Move slowly.** Sudden movement sets off birds in waves. If a hawk shadow passes, watch what the children do and follow their lead.
- **Never throw anything into the pit,** including food scraps, cigarette butts, or coins. The bottom fill lifts the floor, and foreign objects contaminate the compost, water, and drains.
- **No fire, smoke, or open flame** without explicit permission. Feather and dry dust are flammable, and the wall is a fragile cluster of thousands of nests.
- **Watch for and yield to work.** People will be on ledges with wet clay, awls, bricks, and mallets. Step aside and give room.
- **Do not photograph faces, nests, or mending work without asking.** The wall is a record kept by memory, and the people may regard image-taking as strange or as taking something from them. If you do photograph, avoid flash.

## Communication

- They keep no written records. Do not write in front of them as though it is normal, and do not leave marks, chalk, or written notes anywhere. Keep any notes private.
- Their language is unknown to you. Bring patience and use gesture, pointing, and demonstration. Learn a few words as fast as you can, especially greetings, thanks, and "please" and "stop."
- The wall is their literature. If someone shows you a section and tells you what family mended it, listen closely. Trying to follow and asking simple questions is the best way to gain respect. "Who made this?" is likely a good question. "How old is it?" may be less meaningful than "whose hands?"
- Expect them to be lean, capable, and unimpressed. Don't perform, flatter, or preach. Work is respected.

## Gifts and Exchange

You will want to offer something. Choose carefully.

- **Good:** Tools they can maintain or copy from local materials, such as a well-made bone needle or a fine reed-cutting knife of soft copper. Salt is pointless because the wall produces its own. Good clay-working tools might interest them, but observe first and ask what they need.
- **Consumables:** Small amounts of dried fruit or seeds for the fig and caper gardens, if they'd welcome them. Be careful about introducing new species. Seeds and plant material can seed disease or invasives.
- **Avoid:** Anything that changes the economy dramatically. No steel tools, plastic, or chemicals. No medicines other than first aid for an injured person, and only if asked. No alcohol.
- **Best gift:** Labor. Offer to carry baskets of compost or hand bricks, if welcome. That offer costs you sweat and dust, which is what everyone else is spending here.

## Rain

Rain here is rare and violent. When it comes, everyone will know before you do.

- **Move to where the locals move.** Water sheets down every terrace and finds every gutter at once, and channels roar. Slippery herringbone and fast drains make falls likely.
- **Stay off the stairs and away from open drains and gutters.** Shelter in a pot-room if invited.
- **Don't try to collect the water yourself.** It belongs to the well.
- **After the storm,** everything smells of hot clay and wet dust, and the birds get loud again. Treat the mud and runoff as contaminated. Wash hands before eating.

## Sleeping

If you're offered a place to sleep, it will likely be a pot-room behind the walls, which stay cool at noon and warm at night. Expect:

- A bed of woven grass or wool
- Continuous sound: murmurs, drips, and the low chord
- Rodents, geckos, and insects. Keep food sealed and shake out your boots.
- Guano dust. Wear your respirator to sleep if the room is near a roost.
- Bat proximity. Never sleep in the deep pots near the bottom. Keep your face covered.

## Bringing It All Together

**Essentials checklist:**
- P100/FFP3 respirators with spare filters
- Sealed goggles
- Sun hat, warm layer, long clothing
- Grippy shoes and thin gloves
- Personal water (several liters), filter, and purification tablets
- Calorie-dense backup food
- First-aid kit with wound care, antibiotics, and antidiarrheal
- Epinephrine auto-injectors (if allergic)
- Insect repellent and sunscreen
- Soft earplugs
- Dim/red headlamp
- Sealed bags for electronics, a small pack, and a trash bag to carry out everything
- Vaccinations (especially tetanus and rabies) done beforehand
- A modest, respectful gift and a willingness to work

## What It Will Be Like

The first hour will be overwhelming. It's a tide of cooing, clicking, dripping, and hammering, and the smell of clay, dust, and feathers. Then it settles into a rhythm you'll begin to hear as a kind of speech. You'll notice the different pitches of the drips, the patterns of patches in the wall, the warmth of the floor after sunset, and the way the chord swells when the wind is right.

The best visitors do what the residents do. They notice what's fraying and don't leave more fraying behind. They see that the wall is slowly closing over itself, and they respect it as a slow, deliberate act rather than a ruin. Take home the sound, the warmth of the brick, and the memory of the dusk when the swifts spiral down and each one finds its own pot by feel.

Leave nothing behind. Take nothing that isn't given. Keep your feet steady and your ears open.
