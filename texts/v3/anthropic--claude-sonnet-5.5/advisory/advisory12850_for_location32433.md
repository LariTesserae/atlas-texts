# Preparing for Harrow Height

## The Short Version

This is not a dangerous place in the way a volcano or a war zone is dangerous. It is dangerous the way a cold bath is dangerous: it is patient, and it takes the margin you didn't know you were spending. The main killers will be **cold, wet, and losing your way**. The weirdness is a secondary problem, and it mostly works by making the primary ones harder to notice.

Plan for a cold, saturated, loud, disorienting walk across ground that doesn't hold still, and treat every rule that has "come loose" as a reason to carry redundancy.

---

## 1. Before You Go: Mindset and Logistics

**Set a hard exit, and make it independent of place.** The plateau repeats hills and stretches distance, so any plan of "return to where I arrived" is fragile. Use a recall method that works from anywhere you can stand, if your travel method allows it. If it doesn't, pin your arrival point with something that persists: a tall pole and flagged line, or a beacon on a stream you can follow back. Assume you may arrive, walk an hour, and find the landing site is on a horizon you've already passed.

**Set a short duration.** Days, not weeks. The description ends on the knocking synchronizing with your pulse, and it says outright that the distinction between the two "erodes." That is a gradual process, and gradual processes are invisible from inside. Decide your departure time before arrival, tell a partner to enforce it, and don't renegotiate on site because "it's actually kind of peaceful."

**Go with at least one other person.** Ideally three. You need someone to check you against, since your own sense of distance, time, and rhythm is under quiet attack. Agree on daily "reality checks": what time each of you thinks it is, how far you walked, how long since the last meal. Divergence is data.

**Expect to be slow.** Wet peat is brutal to walk on. Halve your normal pace, then halve it again for planning. A ten-kilometer day here is a serious day.

---

## 2. Cold and Wet: The Primary Threat

The altitude is enough that breath shows. The moisture is constant and gets in everywhere, so cotton is death. You are dressing for a place that is always around 0–8 °C, drizzling, and windy, with no shelter from the wind and no dry ground to sit on.

- **Layering:** merino or synthetic base, insulating mid-layer that still works damp (fleece or synthetic fill, not down), and a truly good waterproof shell with taped seams. Bring a second, spare set of base layers sealed in a dry bag that you do not open until camp.
- **Feet:** this is where trips fail. Bring waterproof boots with high, snug gaiters, wool socks in bulk (at least one fresh pair per day, plus spares), and dry camp socks you never wear walking. Consider neoprene liner socks. Do **not** wear hip waders: if you go into a pool, they fill and drag.
- **Hands and head:** waterproof gloves plus spare liners, a warm hat, a hood you can cinch, and a neck gaiter you can pull over your mouth.
- **Dry bags for everything.** Assume nothing stays dry unless it is sealed. Double-bag electronics, sleeping gear, and medical supplies.
- **Hypothermia and immersion foot:** learn the early signs (fumbling, mumbling, stumbling, and numb, waxy, wrinkled feet). Stop and rewarm at the first sign. Bring a bivy bag, a chemical warmer supply, and an emergency shelter you can pitch in under two minutes.

---

## 3. The Ground

The ground is a sponge, a bog, and a mat over unknown depth. Some of it will hold you, and some will not.

- **Weight distribution:** bring wide bog-shoes or lightweight snowshoe-style platforms, and trekking poles with wide baskets. Use one pole as a probe: push it down before committing weight to any ground that looks or feels different.
- **Bright vegetation, black pools, and unnaturally smooth mat** are all suspect. Sphagnum "throats" that swallow streams can also swallow ankles, legs, and people.
- **Carry a throw line and a short rope** and rope up on any traverse that crosses wet mat. If someone goes through, lie flat and spread out. Don't thrash, and don't pull straight up. Ease out horizontally.
- **Do not stand on the Bellows-Beds.** They inflate with gas from below and collapse with a sigh. Beyond the fall, collapse releases pockets of whatever rot produces. Give them a wide berth, especially if they look distended.
- **The great furrow:** something large moves beneath the surface at walking pace. If you see a fresh furrow parting the moss, don't cross its line, don't follow it, and don't camp near it. Simply move away, perpendicular to its heading, and keep going.
- **Cold-stags:** where they pass, the ground seems to close and heal. That might mean their trails are firm, or it might mean the ground is fond of them. Don't assume their route is safe for you, since they're built for it.

## 4. Air, Gas, and Spores

The place breathes. Many of its structures are working with rot, pressure, and decomposition.

- **Gas detector:** bring a personal multi-gas monitor (at least CH₄, H₂S, CO, CO₂, and O₂), worn at breathing height. It should alarm audibly and vibrate, since you'll be deaf to a lot. Rotting peat under pressure is a gas problem.
- **Respirator:** a half-face P100 or better, with a spare set of filters. The Presses fire spores and sap into the air, and the mist carries fine grit and who-knows-what. Eye protection too: sealed goggles, not glasses.
- **Head protection:** a light helmet. The Presses launch seed-casks upward "in bursts," and what goes up comes down. The sound may arrive before, after, or without the launch, so you cannot use hearing to warn you.
- **Don't shelter downwind of the Stacks.** Their steam is warm and tempting, but you don't know what's in it, and warmth is exactly what makes you linger.

---

## 5. Water and Food

- **Bring all your food.** No foraging. Sealed, high-calorie, no-cook options for the wettest days, plus a stove for warm meals.
- **Water:** the local water is tea-stained and acidic, thick with tannins and organic matter, and you don't know what else. Filter it, then purify it chemically or by boiling. Better still, carry your own supply for most of the trip and treat local water only as backup. Never drink from the pulsing pools.
- **Fire:** don't. Peat burns, sometimes underground and for a long time, and you can't put out what you can't see. Use a stove only, on a raised platform or a stone slab.
- **Waste:** carry it out. And do not feed the Belts. Whatever you drop into a sink-pool is being "digested, strained, and pumped somewhere down into the peat," and you don't want to learn where.

---

## 6. Navigation, or the Lack of It

This is the part that will make people feel foolish. Do not.

- **Do not trust distance.** A Stack may be twenty minutes or forever away. Budget by **time and effort**, not by kilometers. Use a turnaround rule: if you haven't arrived by time X, you turn back, regardless of how close it looks.
- **Streams are your spine.** They "obey themselves." Follow them where they exist, and note precisely where they begin and where they vanish. Recognize that they start and end oddly, so don't plan a route that requires a stream to continue.
- **Instruments:** bring a compass, altimeter, and GPS, and expect all to disagree at times. Cross-check them against each other and against sun position when the sun gives you anything. Record bearings *and* what you were doing when you recorded them.
- **Markers:** footprints close, so they are useless. Use durable, non-organic markers (bright flagged stakes, reflective tape on rods, or biodegradable-free tags), and understand that the plateau may swallow them. Photograph and sketch landmarks, especially outcrops.
- **Outcrops that repeat:** if you pass the same black outcrop on three horizons in different stages of erosion, don't assume you're walking in a circle. Note it, sketch it, and treat it as a warning that your model of space and time is unreliable here. Do not try to rationalize it while walking.
- **Stacks lean toward attention:** don't stare at them. Take a bearing, look away, and note that it may shift. If you need one as a waypoint, sight it briefly, then navigate by something stable. **Camp out of sight of every Stack if you can, and never in view of more than one.** Verify by taking a bearing on a Stack before sleep and again on waking. If it moved, you move.
- **Dry-falls:** the rain rises and the ground is drier than a desert. They are tempting places to dry out, and they are traps. Your clothes will dry in seconds, but the edge is a threshold that returns rain "heavier, as though owed." Also, you will dehydrate faster than you expect. If you want to use one, do it briefly, drink first, and don't let the relief lull you into staying.

## 7. Sound and Sleep

The place is loud, and it's a deceptive kind of loud, because it looks quiet.

- **Hearing protection:** use filtered earplugs (musician-style) that lower the volume without deleting it. You need to hear what you can hear, but you also need your ears to last a week of foundry noise.
- **Don't rely on sound for danger.** It arrives out of order, echoes from places with no surface, and returns transformed. A goose-call can come back as a grind. Use sight, instruments, and your partner.
- **Sleep off the ground.** An insulated, raised platform or hammock-style setup if there's anything to hang from (there won't be), otherwise a thick insulating pad on a waterproof, raised platform. The deep knocking travels through the ground, so sleeping on it is the fastest route to synchronization. Bring a sleep mask, since the "light" never behaves like any hour.
- **Keep a clock.** A mechanical or well-shielded watch, with a fixed schedule you keep regardless of light. Eat, sleep, and check in on schedule. The place has no dusk; you do.

## 8. The Knocking and Your Own Mind

The description ends with a warning that is easy to read as ambience. It is not.

- **Bring a rhythm of your own.** A metronome earpiece, a steady walking cadence you count aloud, a song with a distinct tempo you rehearse. The point is a **competing anchor**, so your body has something else to entrain to.
- **Monitor your pulse.** A chest-strap heart monitor, checked at each stop. If your resting heart rate begins tracking a rhythm you can hear, don't argue with it. Change what you're doing: move, eat, talk, sing, and get a partner to break the pattern with you.
- **Talk.** Regular conversation and reality checks are cheap and effective. Journaling in a fixed format (date, time, location, mood, notable events) creates a record you can compare against later. Read it back if you feel unmoored.
- **Set a rule:** anyone who says "I don't think we need to leave yet" after day two is flagged. The rest of the group decides.

## 9. Fauna

- **Grub-smiths:** they swarm from collapsing bellows-beds, ticking like cooling metal. Keep boots and gaiters sealed, and do not camp near a Bellows-Bed. If a swarm emerges, move upwind and away and don't stamp.
- **Fog-geese:** likely harmless at a distance, useful as indicators (they graze where the ground is stable enough for them). Don't approach flocks or disturb them.
- **Skitter-frogs, vent-swifts:** mostly a matter of noise and nuisance. Vent-swifts drop out of the steam shrieking, so keep your head covered near the Stacks.
- **The large thing:** treat as unknown and potentially lethal. No bait, no investigation, no heroics.
- **General:** carry nothing that smells strongly of food outside sealed containers, and don't leave gear on the ground unattended.

---

## 10. Etiquette

The plateau has no builders and no audience, but it is not empty.

- Don't cut, tap, climb, or harvest the Stacks, Beds, or Presses.
- Leave no metal, plastic, or chemical in the pools or Belts.
- Take photographs, but don't assume they will show what you saw.
- Treat the weirdness as a condition, not a puzzle. You are not going to solve why the rain rises. You are trying to leave with your feet, your judgment, and your pulse still your own.

## Packing Checklist (Condensed)

**Clothing:** merino/synthetic layers ×2 sets, insulating mid-layer, taped-seam shell and pants, waterproof gloves + liners, warm hat, neck gaiter, 8+ pairs of wool socks, neoprene liners.
**Footwear:** waterproof boots, high gaiters, bog-shoes/wide platforms.
**Shelter:** four-season tent with a raised floor platform or insulated groundsheet, bivy, insulated pad, sleeping bag rated well below expected temperatures, sleep mask.
**Safety:** helmet, goggles, P100 respirator + spare filters, multi-gas monitor, throw line, rope, harness, probe pole, first aid, hypothermia kit, whistle, signal mirror, beacon.
**Navigation:** compass ×2, altimeter, GPS (skeptically), paper notebook, waterproof pencils, flagged stakes/reflective markers, headlamps + spares.
**Food/water:** all food, stove + fuel (extra), filter + chemical purification, water reserves.
**Comfort/mental:** filtered earplugs, metronome/rhythm device, heart-rate monitor, reliable watch, journal, something small and human that reminds you who you are (a photo, a song, a letter).

---

## The Last Word

Most people will imagine the danger as something dramatic, such as a Stack that reaches for them or a beast in the moss. The truth is quieter. The place will wet you, chill you, slow you, confuse you, and offer you a rhythm to fall into. It works on tired people. So the best preparation is the least glamorous: dry socks, a hard exit time, a partner who will say "we're leaving," and a pulse you keep checking is your own.
