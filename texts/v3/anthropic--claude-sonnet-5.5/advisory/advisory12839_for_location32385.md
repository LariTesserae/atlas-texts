# Preparing for Harrow Deep

## The one-sentence briefing

Harrow Deep will not kill you with anything exotic. The main dangers are heat, bad air, and an ordinary zoonotic-disease load concentrated far beyond what your body has met. All of them are manageable if you take them seriously before you go down, because the cage lift gives you no chance to change your mind.

## 1. Heat is the real threat

Forty-one degrees at near-saturation is the dangerous part. Sweat can't evaporate, so your body loses its main way of cooling. Residents survive on acclimatization, short shifts, salt, and hammocks near the vents. You have none of those on day one.

**Before you go**
- If you can, heat-acclimatize for one to two weeks: hot baths, a sauna, or exercise in a warm humid room, ideally 60-90 minutes a day. It's tedious and it works.
- Be in reasonable health. Cardiac conditions, blood-pressure medication (especially diuretics and beta-blockers), and anticholinergics all make heat worse. Talk to a physician who understands you're going somewhere with a wet-bulb temperature near the survivable limit.
- Plan to do almost nothing physical for the first two days.

**What to bring**
- Oral rehydration salts, plenty. Drinking plain water in large quantities down there is a mistake. Do as the residents do and drink *salted* water, and don't overshoot into hyponatremia.
- A cooling vest or a few cooling towels. They will warm quickly, but the town's cisterns can recharge them.
- A core thermometer or at least a reliable forehead strip. Learn the signs of heat exhaustion (headache, nausea, cramps, cold clammy skin despite heat) and heat stroke (confusion, stopped sweating, collapse).
- Thin, loose, light-colored, moisture-wicking clothing. You'll want long sleeves and trousers for the insects, the rock, and the cockroaches, so choose the lightest weave you can find. Bring several changes, because nothing dries.

**Once there**
- Sleep in the afternoon like everyone else. Find the vents, where the air moves.
- Follow the shift rhythm rather than your own ambition.
- If you stop sweating or start feeling oddly calm and confused, that is an emergency. Tell someone immediately and get to moving air and wet cloth.

## 2. The air

Ammonia, bat and insect musk, mineral dust, and the possibility of elevated CO₂ in low pockets make the air the second hazard. Residents get headaches for a week and red eyes permanently.

**Bring**
- A fitted half-face respirator with P100 particulate filters plus combined ammonia/organic vapor cartridges. A plain dust mask is not adequate. Practice wearing it in heat, because it's miserable, and that is why people fail to wear it.
- Sealed goggles, not glasses, for any time near the Guano Sheds. Contact lenses are a poor idea; ammonia and dust get trapped behind them.
- Saline eye wash and a soothing nasal saline spray.
- A personal gas monitor (CO₂, O₂, NH₃, H₂S) if your kit allows it. Ask the town where the low air pockets are anyway. They will know.

**Rules of thumb**
- Wet cloth over the face is what the workers use in the sheds. It's a decent supplement, not a substitute for a real respirator.
- Do not enter the Guano Sheds or dig in guano at all unless you are fully protected and have been invited.
- Expect a headache. Dark, ordinary painkillers and rest are fine, but a severe headache with vomiting or confusion may be CO₂ or heat, and you should tell someone.

## 3. Infection and bites

The chamber holds two million bats, uncounted rodents, and a guano ecosystem. Think of it as a small pandemic-preparedness exercise.

**Vaccination and medical prep (see a travel physician well beforehand)**
- **Rabies pre-exposure prophylaxis.** Bats are the big reservoir. If you are bitten or scratched, even slightly, you go to the town's medic, and you'll need post-exposure treatment regardless.
- Tetanus, hepatitis A and B, typhoid. Everything is wet, and cuts fester.
- Bring a personal course of broad-spectrum antibiotics only if a physician advises it, plus antiseptic, waterproof dressings, and antifungal cream. Cuts and skin infections go bad quickly in damp heat.
- An antihistamine and an epinephrine auto-injector if you have any allergy history. Cockroach and mite allergens are heavy here.

**The specific hazards**
- **Histoplasmosis** and other fungal spores from guano. This is the strongest reason to avoid disturbing guano and to wear the respirator around it. Symptoms (fever, cough, fatigue) can appear one to three weeks after exposure, so if you feel flu-like after you've left, tell your doctor exactly where you've been.
- **Rat-borne disease** such as leptospirosis. Don't drink or bathe in standing water, and cover any open wounds.
- **Centipedes.** Shake out your boots every morning without exception, and check your bedding and clothes. Bites are painful and occasionally serious.
- **Cockroaches** spread bacteria. Keep food sealed, and don't eat in the open.
- **The sump.** Do not swim, drink from it, or wash in it. It is warm, black, and full of things that live in warm black water.

## 4. Footing and structure

- **Sturdy, waterproof, high-ankled boots with aggressive tread.** The floor near the walls is slick with mineral mud, the gangways are wet grated iron, and boots are also your centipede defense. Bring a spare pair so one can dry.
- **A hard hat.** The ceiling sheds slabs "with a sound like a dropped anvil." The meeting-house wall lists the dates. Nobody has died yet, and you don't want to be the first name. Also duck under the pipes, which are hot and drip. Lagged pipes can still burn.
- **Gloves** for gripping rail and rock. Sharp new limestone edges are unweathered and cut readily.
- Avoid hanging around directly under seeps and fresh scallops in the ceiling, and follow local advice on which terraces are stable.

## 5. Equipment

- **Electronics will suffer.** Humidity, condensation, and ammonia corrode contacts. Seal devices in dry bags with desiccant, and keep them sealed when not in use. Expect lenses to fog when you first go below; allow time for them to equilibrate.
- **Light.** The town is dusk between amber pools, and your path may lead through the dark. Bring a good headlamp with a low, dim, warm or red mode. The bats don't need a floodlight, and the residents will not thank you for shining one at the ceiling.
- **A whistle and a printed map** of the terraces, with the cage lift and your extraction point marked. Batteries die.
- **Hearing protection.** The pump hall is loud enough that you shout there. Bring earplugs or muffs for extended time near the engines, but keep one ear free where possible. You need to hear the shift bell and any change in the pump rhythm.
- **Waterproof notebook and pencil** rather than pen and paper.
- **No metal jewelry or watches you value.** Everything rusts, and you'll be sweating into it.
- **Spare socks, many pairs.** They're the best trade good and the best personal comfort.

## 6. The descent

You're lowered in a cage over eleven minutes, out of daylight in ninety seconds, and into rising heat.

- **Wear layers for the surface**, and be ready to strip them fast. The change will be abrupt.
- **Eat lightly and hydrate before descending.** Don't arrive dehydrated or hungover.
- Your ears will pop. If you're prone to claustrophobia, the shaft is not the place to discover it. Ask the operator about the cage's emergency procedures.
- **Note the return schedule before you descend.** The cage is your only regular exit, and that single point of failure matters. Know when it runs, how to signal for it, and what the town's plan is if the lift fails. Discuss this with your hosts on day one.

## 7. If you're crossing from another world

- **Anchor your return point somewhere dry and stable**, ideally at the surface or in a well-ventilated corner near the cage lift, not in the low terraces, which would flood first if the pumps ever fail.
- **Prepare a fallback.** If your crossing device is sensitive to humidity or heat, keep it sealed and cool. A jammed return in a chamber where you're already heat-stressed is dangerous.
- **Time your arrival.** Being present for an exodus at dusk or dawn is memorable and worth planning for. But don't arrive *during* a shift crisis (such as a rising sump), when the town's attention is elsewhere and the third engine is warming.

## 8. Manners

This is a small, tightly interdependent town with old habits. A few notes:

- **Learn the sump gauge.** Everyone knows it to the centimeter, and children read it before letters. Ask what it says; treat any change as serious.
- **Respect the pumps.** The thud–hiss is the town's pulse. Don't touch the engines, don't lean on the rails in the pump hall, and don't joke about it stopping. If it does stop, you will notice, and so will everyone else.
- **The bats are the economy.** Don't disturb the roosts, don't shine light on them, and don't make sudden loud noise near the ceiling. During the exodus, stop what you're doing, as everyone does.
- **The cats are the pest control.** Don't kick them, chase them, or feed the rats.
- **Cockroaches are weather.** Reacting theatrically will mark you as a newcomer, and it's tiring besides. Just keep your food sealed.
- **A fern is a gift.** If you want a truly welcome present, bring something green and living, or something rare down here: fresh fruit, clean cloth, salt, tea, lamp mantles, spare bulbs, repair tools, rust-proof paint. Don't bring anything you'd mind seeing corroded.
- **Quiet is precious.** The far southern terrace is where people go to be private, and to cry. If someone is there, leave them be.
- **Pay for what you use.** Water, food, and salt are all carried down or condensed at some effort.

## 9. Mind and body

- **Sleep** will be hard for a few nights. The thud–hiss is soothing once you stop resisting it. Earplugs are counterproductive here for many people, since the rhythm is what lets you rest.
- **Light deprivation** is real. There's almost no daylight below, and the dusk wears on the mood. Keep a schedule, take the surface clock seriously, and use the exodus as a daily anchor.
- **Sensory overload.** The place is *too much*: sound, smell, movement on every surface. Give yourself short, quiet rests, ideally near a vent.
- **Headaches and a red-eyed look** are normal in the first week. Worsening ones are not.

## 10. After you leave

- **Quarantine your gear.** Wash clothes hot, wipe boots and equipment, and check bags for hitchhikers (cockroach egg cases in particular). Don't carry them into another world.
- **Watch your health for three weeks**, particularly for cough, fever, night sweats, or unexplained fatigue. Tell any doctor about the guano exposure and the bats.
- **Debrief with the medic** before leaving if you had any bite, scratch, or unusual symptoms.

## The short kit list

- Half-face respirator with P100 + ammonia/organic vapor cartridges
- Sealed goggles
- Hard hat, gloves, and sturdy waterproof boots (two pairs)
- Lightweight long-sleeved wicking clothes, many changes; lots of socks
- Oral rehydration salts, salt tablets, a cooling vest
- First aid, antiseptic, antifungal, antihistamine, personal medications
- Rabies pre-exposure vaccination (and the rest of the travel vaccines)
- Dry bags, desiccant, a low-glare headlamp
- Hearing protection, whistle, printed map
- Gifts: socks, salt, tea, bulbs, paint, and, if you can manage it, something green

Above all, arrive humble and unhurried. Harrow Deep has taken sixty years to reach its uneasy balance with the water, the animals, and the rock, and it rewards those who move slowly, drink salted water, and stop to watch the bats go up.
