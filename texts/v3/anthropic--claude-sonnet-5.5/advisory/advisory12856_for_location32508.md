# Preparing for the Chord Plateau

## The short version

The plateau will not hurt you quickly or dramatically. It is stable, and its animals are mostly small or wary. What it does is wear you down through several steady pressures that compound: thin air, relentless sound, hard light, a little cold, and very little water. The right approach is to be slow, quiet in manner, self-sufficient, and gentle with the place.

## 1. Before you go: choose your body and your window

**Get a medical check that asks about the right things.** Beyond the usual altitude questions (heart, lungs, blood pressure, past altitude illness), ask about:
- **Ears:** perforation history, chronic infections, tinnitus, hyperacusis. Constant loud low-frequency sound is a real hazard to all of these.
- **Photosensitive seizures or migraines:** the ridges flash like signal mirrors, and the tarn lace and glare are strong triggers.
- **Vestibular or anxiety conditions:** a permanent roar that you feel in your sternum can be disorienting, and some people cope with it badly.

If any of these apply, it is reasonable to go only as far as the plateau's lip, or not at all.

**Plan for the local rhythm.** The lowland hunters come seasonally. Ask in the valley towns when they do, and time your visit to overlap with them or deliberately avoid them, depending on your purpose. Either way you should know where they are. Plan to cross the lip after the dawn fog has burned off, and to be in camp before the dusk fog rises.

**Bring an exit that does not depend on sound.** Many multiverse anchors and recall devices use voice commands, audible chimes, or spoken confirmations. Here you will not hear them and cannot speak over the wind. Make sure your return mechanism can be triggered by touch or light, and test it at the loudest place you can find beforehand. Check that your device's own speaker and microphone aren't the single point of failure.

## 2. The climb: acclimatize like it's the whole trip

The noon sky the color of a bruise tells you the air is very thin, probably well above 4,500 meters by Earth-equivalent reckoning. Assume it is and behave accordingly.

- **Spend several days in and below the cloud forest**, climbing gradually and sleeping lower than your high point each day. The cloud forest is beautiful and worth the time anyway.
- **Do not fly or teleport straight to the tableland.** If your travel method lands you high, stop and acclimatize before doing anything else.
- **Carry a pulse oximeter, and know your turnaround rules:** headache that doesn't respond to rest and fluids, vomiting, loss of coordination, breathlessness at rest, or a wet cough means descend. Descent is the treatment. On this plateau you cannot shout for help, so decide your thresholds in advance and agree them with your partner.
- **Consider preventive medication** (such as acetazolamide) and an emergency kit (dexamethasone, and possibly portable supplemental oxygen) after discussing it with a physician.
- **Expect poor sleep and low appetite for the first nights,** and expect that the sound will make both worse. Eat anyway, and drink more than feels natural.

## 3. The sound: the central challenge

**Hearing protection is essential equipment.** The plateau is an instrument, and you will be inside it for days.
- Carry **high-rated earplugs plus earmuffs** for wearing together in slot canyons and near perforated fins. Bring spares, as they get lost and dirty.
- Bring a **sound-level meter** if you can. You will otherwise not be able to judge exposure, because the roar is a plateau of pressure rather than a series of peaks that tell you to cover your ears.
- Plan for **regular breaks in wind-shadow**, and treat cumulative exposure like industrial noise. Rotating one hour of exposure with an hour of shelter is a reasonable rule.
- Remember that **the lowest frequencies pass through plugs and muffs.** You will feel the drone in your chest regardless. Some people find this compelling and others find it nauseating. Find out which you are on the first day, not the fifth.

**You will not speak, so build a communication system before you arrive.**
- Learn from the lowland valleys some of the **hand-language** the hunters use. Even a few dozen signs will change how you are received and how safe you are.
- Agree on a small vocabulary with your companions: *stop, water, unwell, turn back, hazard, come here, I'm fine*. Add signals for *I am fine* and *I am not fine* that can't be confused.
- Carry **pale signal flags** and **a signal mirror.** The light here is fierce and the air is clear, so mirrors work over great distances. Whistles, shouts, and most radios do not work. Bring text-based communication devices and a small waterproof whiteboard.
- **Rope up or tether in poor visibility.** You will not hear a companion fall, call, or wander off.

**Pitch your camp with sound in mind.**
- Camp in the **wind-shadow of a fin,** where the air is dead and the roar becomes a pressure in the bones. This is where you will recover, sleep, cook, and think.
- Don't camp inside a slot, where the wind funnels and the pipe effect is at its worst. Avoid the base of tall or fractured fins.
- **Use a low, taut tent** with minimal loose fabric. Flapping nylon in a constant gale is an additional and very irritating noise source. Anchor with rocks, since pea-gravel holds stakes poorly.
- Take the goats' hint: they bed down only where a certain low note is strongest, so don't assume the quietest spot is the best one.

## 4. Light, cold, and weather

**Protect against the sun as though it were a glacier.**
- Category 4 glacier glasses with side shields, and a spare pair.
- High-SPF sunscreen, reapplied often, including under the chin and nose and on the ears. Light comes from every surface.
- Lip protection, a brimmed hat with a neck flap, long sleeves, and gloves. The sun is fierce while the air is cool, so you won't feel yourself burning.

**Dress for a thin skin of cold over strong sun.**
- Layers you can shed quickly, since sun and shade differ by many degrees and you will be moving between them.
- A **waterproof shell.** The dawn and dusk fog wets everything, and the short, hard squalls come without warning.
- A **four-season sleeping bag and insulated pad,** as nights will be near or below freezing.
- Dry sleeping clothes kept separate from anything that gets fog-damp.

**Manage the visual environment.**
- Shadows are black and exact, and the ground is read by its edges. That makes **depth perception unreliable,** so use trekking poles and slow down in shadowed areas, on scree, and around slot edges.
- Give yourself rest from glare. Time spent in the wind-shadow with your eyes closed is restorative.

## 5. Water, food, and fuel

- **Water is scarce and comes from fog seeps and tarns.** Carry enough to reach and leave your camps, and plan camps near a tarn but not on its moss.
- **Treat everything you drink.** Use filtration plus chemical treatment or boiling. Boiling takes longer at altitude and the water boils at a lower temperature, so allow for it.
- Take water sparingly. The tarns have stood at the same level for a thousand years, and that stability is the ecosystem.
- **Carry all your fuel.** There is nothing to burn, and you should not burn the tussock or the cushion plants, some of which are older than any tree in the lowlands. Bring a stove that performs at altitude.
- **Bring calorie-dense food you can eat with no appetite:** nuts, fat, sweets, and salty things. Cooked meals may be too much effort, so pack a few that need no cooking.
- **Pack out everything, including human waste.** Decomposition is slow here and the tarns are irreplaceable.

## 6. Navigation

Sixty kilometers of parallel fins and long slots that all lean the same way is a maze that looks the same in every direction.

- Carry **GPS with spare power, a paper map, and a compass.** Test the compass first, since volcanic tuff can behave oddly.
- The constant wind is a useful bearing, and so is the lean of the fins.
- Use the **forty or so cairns** as waypoints, and note each one you pass. They are the only real landmarks.
- Plan routes along ridge crests rather than through long slots, which can trap you in the loudest and least visible terrain.
- **Fog is a hazard, not just a nuisance.** At dawn and dusk the cloud sea pushes over the lip and you can lose your bearings quickly. Don't travel in it. Stop, anchor, and wait.

## 7. Meeting the people

- The hunters and egg-gatherers have older claims on this place than you. **Approach in the open, from the front, with hands visible.** Hold up a pale flag.
- Their tools are obsidian blades, spear-throwers, and slings. They hunt stilt-goats, and from a distance a person in sand-colored clothing crouched in the tussock may look like game. **Wear a bright or non-tan color** during hunting season.
- Ask in the valley before you go. A local guide is the best safety and courtesy measure you can take.
- Don't trade in ways that change what they need or want. Don't offer metals, plastics, or tools that could stay behind. If you give a gift, make it consumable and small.
- Do not disturb egg-gathering sites, and don't take eggs yourself.

## 8. Behaving in the ecosystem

This is a system in a very old balance, and a small foreign disturbance could have large effects.

- **Decontaminate before you enter.** Clean boots, clothing, packs, and trekking-pole tips of seeds, soil, and spores. Bring no live plants or pets, and don't carry fresh produce that could seed a new species.
- **Stay off the moss felt around tarns.** The rodent colonies burrow beneath it, and it recovers slowly if at all.
- **Keep away from the perforated fins during nesting.** A colony of tens of thousands is fragile. When the birds shift pitch, a raptor has crossed the ridge, and you should notice that you're not the cause.
- **Don't walk toward the stilt-goat herds.** They face the wind in unison and have grazed the same circuits for longer than anyone can say. Watch from a distance and let them be.
- **No drones,** and no amplified sound. They would be a violation of the plateau's one law, which is equilibrium.
- **Leave the ochre handprints untouched.** Don't lean on them, photograph with flash, or trace them.
- **Add one flat stone to a cairn if you wish.** This is the one tradition that seems open to visitors. Place it carefully, and do not disturb the lichen.

## 9. Your mind in a place that never changes

This is the least obvious preparation, and the one people underrate.

- **A constant chord, unending light, and standing ripples that never shift** can be hypnotic. Visitors can lose track of time, feel unusually calm, or feel as if they are slowing down to match the place. The plateau's history suggests that everything that stays long enough does.
- **Keep a schedule.** Wear a watch, write in a journal at set times, and eat and rest on the clock rather than by feel. Agree a maximum stay before you go, and treat it as fixed.
- **Watch each other.** If your companion begins staring at a tarn for long stretches, stops responding to signals, or seems reluctant to leave, that's a reason to move down.
- **Don't push the "composed" feeling too far.** The tuning is real, since the slots are cut at regular spacings, but it's a product of geology. Enjoy it without looking for messages.
- **Plan for sensory recovery.** Expect to feel strange on descent, with ringing ears and a world that seems too quiet. Give it a day before you make decisions or travel through anything dangerous.

## 10. Equipment for documenting it

- **Microphones:** ordinary ones will overload and clip. Use contact microphones, accelerometers, or recorders with wide dynamic range, with proper wind protection. Record at several depths, from open ridge to wind-shadow.
- **Cameras:** use polarizing and neutral density filters, because the contrast is extreme. Shoot the standing ripples in low sun for the lace pattern.
- **Sketchbook and notebook:** a low-tech way to record what the place is like. The tarns are worth drawing.
- Bring **a way of measuring** the fin spacings and slot lengths, if you want to test whether it is a musical scale.

## 11. Essential kit checklist

- Category 4 glacier glasses (two pairs), sunscreen, lip balm, brimmed hat
- Double hearing protection (plugs and muffs), spares, sound-level meter
- Signal flags, signal mirror, whiteboard, tether rope
- Layers, waterproof shell, insulated sleeping system, low-profile tent, rock anchors
- Stove, all fuel, water filter, chemical backup, calorie-dense food
- GPS with spare power, paper map, compass
- Pulse oximeter, altitude medications, first-aid kit, oxygen if possible
- Light- or touch-triggered recall device, tested in the loudest available noise
- Waste-carrying system for everything
- Watch, journal, schedule

## 12. What it will be like

You will climb out of the green cloud forest through cushion plants and scree, and then there will be a lip, and beyond it a blue-violet sky and a wall of sound. For the first hour you will want to run back. After that, if you have prepared, you will find the wind-shadows, learn the hand signs, and begin to notice the birds fitting their calls into the gaps in the chord, the goats turning as one, and the lace on the tarns.

At dusk the fins will glow along their whole length and the fog will rise. The birds will go silent colony by colony, and you will be left with the pale stone, the pale light, and the enormous sound. Be in camp, be warm, and stay long enough to hear it. Then go down before the plateau's stillness starts to feel like yours.
