# Preparing to Visit the Nursery Below the Frost

## The right frame of mind

Most dangerous places announce themselves with teeth. This one is dangerous mainly because it is gentle, wet, cold, and busy. There are four things to keep in mind:

- **You are entering a construction site that is also an organism.** The animals are its labor, the stone is unfinished, and the plants are the architect.
- **The threats are slow.** Hypothermia, hearing damage, bad air, and getting absorbed into the floor all arrive politely.
- **Your footprint matters.** A thumbprint in a column is a small edit to something under construction.
- **Time is unreliable.** An hour or a season can pass, so plan to leave before the place makes that decision for you.

## Getting in and out

The entrance is a slot about a door's width, ten body-lengths deep, with frost at the rim and a sharp frost line where the warm hollow begins.

- **Bring a fixed rope or ladder system**, not a free climb. The rim is frosted, and the wall below it will be wet. Anchor the rope well back from the slot on real ground, not on frost-heaved rubble.
- **Rig a haul line and a tag line.** If someone is injured, disoriented, or hearing-impaired, you need to be able to get them up without shouting.
- **Keep your return anchor at the rim, outside the frost line.** That means your multiverse beacon, portal key, or recall device. Do not carry it down into the hollow. The plants' signaling is unexplained, and the grains hold charge that doesn't decay. I'd assume any delicate electronics or dimensional instruments might behave strangely there, so leave the critical ones where they are known to work.
- **Set a hard turnaround time and a surface partner.** The hollow will not hurry you, which is exactly the problem. Someone at the rim should have authority to call you out.

## Staying warm while wet

The air sits just above freezing, and your clothes will be soaked within minutes. Wet at that temperature is more dangerous than dry cold.

- **Use a wool or synthetic base layer and an insulating mid-layer that keeps its warmth when damp** (wool or synthetic fill, never cotton or down).
- **Wear a waterproof shell with sealed seams and a hood.** The ceiling drips, and periodically the whole chamber shivers and rains.
- **Wear waterproof boots that come high, plus gaiters.** The moss is forearm-deep and saturated, and your feet will sink.
- **Wear thin liner gloves under waterproof outer gloves.** You'll want to touch things and shouldn't do it bare-handed. The hands are also where you'll notice the cold first.
- **Carry a full dry set of base layers, socks, and a hat in a sealed bag, and leave it at the rim.** Changing into it after you climb out may be the most important thing you do.
- **Bring a warm drink in a vacuum flask and high-calorie food.** Cold and slow, steady work burns more than you expect.

The vine-braids radiate slow pulses of warmth, and the pools are warmer than the air. Both are tempting, and both are wrong for you. Warming yourself against the vine means leaning on a living structure, and bathing in a pool means leaving the insulation you're wearing. Resist both. Warm up at the rim.

## Sound

The sound is the most hazardous element and the most easily underestimated. At the peak of the cycle it is deafening, and part of it is a low hum you feel in your ribs and teeth.

- **Wear dual protection**, meaning good foam plugs under earmuffs. Choose an attenuation rating that lowers volume without eliminating your ability to feel the rhythm, since you'll want to time your movements to it.
- **Expect the low-frequency component to bypass your ears.** It can cause nausea, chest pressure, dizziness, and disorientation. Take breaks at the rim, and don't stay through the peak if you feel unwell.
- **Agree on hand signals before you enter.** Shouting is useless and disruptive. Decide on signals for "stop," "I'm cold," "I feel sick," "come here," and "leave now."
- **Do not bring any noisemaker.** That includes radios, whistles, phones, and machines. The whole ecosystem tunes itself to the hum, and a discordant sharp sound could throw off the animals that are, in effect, doing the building.
- **Don't try to fill the silence, and don't be afraid of it.** In the quiet hours the stone stalls and gels. That's part of the normal cycle, so your presence shouldn't be either a help or a harm. Just be a quiet guest.

## Air

The hollow is a sealed pocket of dense plant life, and it's below ground level, where cold air pools.

- **Carry a personal multi-gas monitor** (CO₂, O₂, and ideally H₂S and CH₄) and check it before you go down and at intervals inside. Respiring plants and decomposing matter can push CO₂ up in still, low spots.
- **Bring a mask with particulate and spore filtration**, and wear it. The moss, the algae-mat, and the ferns will all throw off spores and fine matter.
- **Keep your head above the moss.** Don't kneel or lie with your face near the floor. That's where air quality is most likely to be poor and where bubbles burst.
- **Be alert for the algae-mat bubbles.** They burst with a flute note. That's fine, but the gas inside is unknown, so don't put your face over a pool.

## Footing and movement

Every step sinks and is slowly returned. It's soft, and it is a living surface.

- **Move slowly and place your feet deliberately.** Wide, shallow steps are better than deep ones.
- **Use trekking poles with wide baskets, or a light staff,** to test the depth ahead. Do it gently, though, and don't jab. There are amphibians and worms in that mat.
- **Watch for cave-peepers and glass-beetles on the surface.** They are soft or brittle and easy to crush, and they're the workforce.
- **Do not lean on the ribs of rock.** They are soft as cold butter and take prints for an hour. Keep your hands, packs, and tools off exposed stone. A print isn't catastrophic, but it does reshape something someone else is building.
- **Never lie down, sit for long, or sleep on the moss.** Whatever dies here is drawn down within an hour. Whether living things are treated differently, I would not test. If you fall or collapse, your partners must get you upright and out fast.

## Light

The ambient light is weak, and the hollow produces its own in slow blue-teal-ember waves every quarter hour.

- **Bring a headlamp with a red or dim setting**, and use it low. Strong white light on a slow living system that runs on light-timing is rude and possibly disruptive. The description says you can watch crystals grow under a lamp, so a lamp is tolerated, but keep it modest.
- **Bring a spare light and spare batteries kept warm** (in an inside pocket). Cold drains them quickly.
- **Time your movement to the wave.** In the dark stretches, stay put rather than stumbling.

## Kit list (compact)

- Rope system, harness, ascenders, haul line, tag line
- Insulating layers, waterproof shell and boots, gaiters, liner and outer gloves, hat
- Full dry change and a heat source at the rim
- Dual hearing protection
- Gas monitor, spore-rated respirator
- Red-capable headlamp, spares, warm battery pouch
- Trekking poles with baskets
- Waterproof notebook, pencil, and a watch (not a phone)
- Thermos, high-calorie food, water
- First aid kit with hypothermia supplies (foil blankets, chemical warmers, thermometer)
- Sealed containers for **all** waste, including human waste, which you carry out
- Clean, dedicated boots and tools, scrubbed with clean water and dried before entering, so you bring in no foreign seeds or microbes

## Conduct

- **Don't split a vine.** The description says "you should not," and it's right. The internal tubes carry an unexplained, charge-holding stream, and cutting is probably a wound to something that can't say so.
- **Don't collect.** No crystal, no moss, no beetles. Photograph only, and carefully.
- **Don't try to decode or interfere with the pattern-signaling.** If you have instruments that read or transmit fields, keep them off.
- **Leave nothing.** Something bare gets covered in spores within days, so even a dropped glove becomes part of the wall.
- **Treat the place as a host, not as a specimen.** A distributed intelligence with no brain and no center still has priorities, and one of them is finishing what it started.

## Health and psychology

- **Expect the rhythm to get into you.** The slow light waves, the hum, and the pulse are entraining. You may feel sleepy, calm, or oddly absorbed. Those are exactly the states in which people stop noticing their fingers going numb. Check your partner's lips, speech, and coordination on a schedule.
- **Cap visits at short shifts, an hour or two, with rests at the rim.** Long stays are for teams with proper support, not for a single traveler on a whim.
- **Keep a clock and a written log.** If you lose track of time, you have a record.
- **Have a plan for the feeling of not wanting to leave.** Decide the exit signal in advance, and honor it even if you feel fine.

## What I'd be honest about not knowing

I can't tell you whether the fauna are harmless to humans (the peepers' skin, the sheet-moths' scales), whether the pool water is safe to touch, or whether the pattern-signaling has any effect on nervous systems. Assume contact irritants and take precautions: gloves, no touching your face, and a thorough wash and rinse at the rim.

## The exit

Climb slowly, since you'll be cold and clumsy. At the rim, take off wet layers immediately and put on dry ones, and get something hot inside you. Then, if you like, stand a moment in the frost and silence with your back to the slot. The hollow hums against you like a large, warm, half-built animal. It doesn't need you, and you've done it no harm. That's the best outcome, and a good sign that you prepared well.
