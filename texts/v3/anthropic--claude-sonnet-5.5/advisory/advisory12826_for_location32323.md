# Preparing for the Grey Floor

## The core problem: you cannot go as a body

At this depth the water presses on you with several hundred atmospheres. Your lungs, sinuses, and the gas in your blood cannot cope with that, and the cold would finish what the pressure started. Multiverse travelers sometimes assume a good enough suit will do. It won't. No flexible suit worth trusting exists for this place, and the "ordinary human" plan is: **don't be in the water. Be inside something that keeps a small piece of the surface world around you.**

Your options, best to worst:

1. **A crewed, 1-atmosphere pressure sphere or hull** (titanium or thick ceramic/glass composite), rated well beyond the depth you expect. Rate it for the deepest plausible version of this plain, not the average. Abyssal floors vary by a kilometer or more.
2. **A hard-shell articulated suit** with 1-atm joints. Only if you are already trained in one. Mobility is poor and a joint failure is fatal.
3. **A remote presence.** Send a tethered or acoustically linked vehicle and stay somewhere safe. This is the honest recommendation for most travelers. The place will still reach you through cameras and hydrophones.

Whatever you choose, buy it from someone who has used it in deep water. A hull that has never been to depth has not been tested.

## Arrival: you come in from above

The description says you arrive sinking through the water column. That means:

- **You start at the top of a long descent.** Expect an hour or more of falling, with the light fading through green, blue, and charcoal to nothing. Plan the descent: ballast weights, a controlled sink rate, and lights off or dim for the last stretch so you don't blind yourself with backscatter.
- **You will not choose where you land.** Carry sonar, an altimeter, and a way to drop or shed weight to stop early. Do not hit the bottom at speed. The ooze is soft, and a heavy vehicle can sink in and stay.
- **Your way home might be up.** Assume the exit works from mid-water or at the surface. If your multiverse anchor only works at a certain altitude, you need to be able to reach that height on your own power. Bring more buoyancy than you think you need, plus an emergency ascent system that works without electricity, such as dropped weights or a chemical release.

## Life support

- At least **double the crew-days you plan on**, in oxygen and CO₂ scrubbing. The cold and the wait are the likely reasons you'll need it.
- Heating. The outside is just above freezing and the hull will pull heat out of you. Bring insulated layers, hot drinks, and a hull-condensation plan. Everything metal will sweat and drip.
- Backup power that does not share a failure mode with the main one.
- A realistic view of stillness. Once you've landed and shut down, you may sit in the dark for hours. Bring things to do.

## Light and seeing

- **Marine snow scatters a headlamp like fog.** Mount lights off-axis, away from the camera, so the flakes glow less. Use low-intensity or red light where possible.
- Let your eyes adapt. The bioluminescence, the blue smears and green pinpricks, is the best light in the place. If you can, use a dim cabin and look out at it.
- Sensors matter more than eyes: sonar, a current meter, and a turbidity sensor.
- Assume you will see one arm's length in a storm. Plan for zero visibility without panic.

## The storms are the real danger

Benthic storms and turbidity flows are the hazards that can actually kill you.

- **Listen for the hiss.** A rising, dry, far-off rush of sediment on sediment is the earliest warning. A hydrophone should be on at all times. If the sparks start flying more than usual, the water is moving.
- **Don't wait to be sure.** The near floor going brown and opaque means you are already late.
- **The safe direction is up.** Storms and turbidity flows hug the bottom. A few hundred meters higher, the water is usually far calmer. Don't sit on the floor and ride it out. Rise, hold station, and wait, or drift away from any slope.
- **Stay away from slopes and the mouths of channels.** A slump upslope means a dense river of mud runs downhill in your direction. Land far from anything that looks like a break in the flatness.
- **Silt will blind and foul things:** thrusters, intakes, viewports, and manipulators. Bring spares, and design for a vehicle that may be buried to its windows.
- **Do not tether yourself to the floor.** A nodule that has sat a thousand years can roll in a storm, and clay peels in sheets. Nothing there is anchored.
- Expect storms to last weeks. You will not wait one out from the bottom.

## Moving about

- **You will not walk.** Every step raises a cloud that hangs for minutes, ruins your view, and disturbs the animals. Hover a meter or two above the ooze, or use a crawler with wide tracks. Move slowly.
- Never settle on the ooze if you can avoid it. On the nodule pavements, a hard, hovering stance is better than a landing.
- Navigation is by inertial systems plus acoustic beacons. There is no GPS and no useful landmark. The plain is flat for kilometers, so drop a transponder where you start and treat it as your one fixed point.
- Communications are acoustic, slow, and choppy. Set fixed check-in times with your surface party, and agree in advance what happens if you miss one.

## Handling the strange bits

- **The nodules feel warm.** That "cannot be true, and is." Do not touch one with a bare hand or a manipulator without first measuring it: thermometer, radiation counter, chemistry sniffer. Warmth from a rock at 1–2 °C could be radioactive decay, exotic chemistry, or something from the plain's odd nature. Treat it as unexplained until proven otherwise.
- **Take nothing without a reason.** The nodules grew a few millimeters in a million years, and there are many, but there is still no way to replace what you take.
- **The flint flake is a grave marker, more or less.** It is the only trace of the people who once stood on this ground. Photograph it and map where it is. Leave it where it is. If you feel you must take something, take a photograph.
- **The octopuses are the nearest thing to neighbors.** They arrange stones, wedge flakes into walls, and pull doors shut. Don't move their stones, take their shells, or wash their homes with your thruster wake. If you shine a light, keep it dim and brief. If one retreats behind its shell, back off. You are a very large, very noisy storm to them.
- **Don't feed anything.** A dropped food scrap will attract amphipods within the hour, and that is a real change to a food web that runs on scarcity.

## Sound and mind

- The place is very quiet, and that quiet is part of the risk. People in it hear things: their own blood, the hull creaking, a whale far off that may not have been there. Decide beforehand what sounds mean trouble. Have a checklist for panic.
- A whale call is not a threat. It is a long tone from very far away, and you may never be sure you heard it.
- Isolation is real. Bring another person if you can. Keep to a schedule. Eat on time.
- The scale is hard on people. The floor is flat, the dark is total, and nothing helps you judge distance. Trust your instruments over your instincts.

## What to bring, in short

- A rated pressure hull or a remote vehicle, tested at depth.
- Redundant power, oxygen, and scrubbers, with double the planned time.
- Sonar, altimeter, current meter, turbidity sensor, hydrophone.
- Acoustic beacons and transponders.
- Emergency ascent that needs no power.
- Dim and red lights, external cameras, sample tools you plan not to use.
- Thermometer, radiation counter, and a way to test the nodules.
- Warm layers, hot food, and something to occupy you.
- An anchor to your home world that you have tested from under water.

## What to expect

Don't expect a landscape. Expect a slow accounting: snow falling, silt settling, small pale animals going about their business, and now and then the far hiss of a storm rising. You will be a guest in something that has no interest in you and is not hostile to you. If you go carefully and go quietly, leave nothing, and rise when the sparks start to fly, the plain will look afterward exactly as it did before you came. That is the best way to visit.
