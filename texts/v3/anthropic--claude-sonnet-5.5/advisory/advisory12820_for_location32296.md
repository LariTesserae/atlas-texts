# Preparing for the Foundry Plateau

## The Short Version

This is a high, wet, cold place with hot, violent plumbing built into it. The ordinary killers are altitude, wet cold, and scalding ground. The unusual killers are volcanic gas pooling in hollows, glacial floods on a daily cycle, and rock that is still being moved around. Nobody is in charge, so no one will warn you, fence anything off, or come looking for you. Prepare as if you are visiting a working industrial site with the safety signs removed.

Treat it as a place to observe carefully for about a week, not a place to conquer.

---

## 1. The Altitude Comes First

The ink-dark noon sky tells you this is very high, probably the equivalent of 5,000 meters or more. That is above where humans live permanently, and far above where most travelers can function without adapting.

**Do not arrive by portal straight onto the plateau and start walking.** If your entry point is on the plateau itself, assume the first two or three days will be spent barely moving. If you can choose, enter the world lower down and ascend along the meltwater drainage. The streams run brown to the lower country, so the route up is a route you can retrace in a hurry.

**Acclimatization plan:**
- Spend 4-6 days at staged elevations first, ideally sleeping at about 3,000-3,500 m and then 4,200 m before going higher. Climb high and sleep low.
- On the plateau, keep the first 24-48 hours to camp chores and short, slow walks.
- Breathing here is "policy rather than instinct," so make it policy. Use pressure breathing (exhale forcefully through pursed lips) and the rest step on slopes. At night your breathing will likely turn periodic, with alarming pauses followed by gasps. That is normal, but it is unsettling if you don't expect it.

**Medical kit and monitoring:**
- Pulse oximeter for every person, checked morning and evening.
- Acetazolamide for prevention, and dexamethasone and nifedipine for emergencies, prescribed and understood in advance.
- A portable hyperbaric chamber and a small oxygen supply. The most useful thing you can bring is the ability to lower your effective altitude for a few hours.
- A written descent rule agreed on before you go: worsening headache, confusion, staggering, a wet cough, or breathlessness at rest means going down immediately, whatever the weather or the hour.

## 2. Cold That Comes with Water

The temperatures alone are manageable: a little above freezing at midday, well below at night. The danger is that everything is also wet. Sleet arrives every afternoon, the marshes soak you from below, and seeps glaze the rocks with ice each night. Wet cold kills faster than dry cold.

**Clothing strategy (layered, and assume everything gets damp):**
- Merino or synthetic base layers, two sets, one kept strictly dry for sleeping.
- Fleece or grid mid-layer that keeps working when wet.
- A synthetic-fill insulated jacket for active use, and a down jacket for camp, kept in a waterproof bag and never worn in sleet.
- A serious hardshell jacket and trousers. This is a place where waterproofing gets used, not just carried.
- Three glove systems: liners, insulated gloves, and waterproof overmitts. Bring spares, since one wet pair can end the trip.
- Balaclava, warm hat, buff, and goggles for sleet in your face.
- Insulated, waterproof mountaineering boots, gaiters, and several pairs of wool socks. Camp booties are essential.

**Sleep system:**
- A bag rated to at least -15°C, with a liner.
- Two sleeping pads, because ice lenses lie a hand's depth beneath the marsh and the ground steals heat all night.
- A hot water bottle in the bag before bed. This works well because you can heat water, but it needs a bottle that won't burst.

**Heat and fuel:**
- **There is no wood.** Carry all your fuel. Use a stove designed for altitude and cold, bring more fuel than you think you need, and keep canisters warm at night.
- Boiling point is depressed at this altitude (roughly 83°C), so food cooks slowly. Bring quick-cook foods or a pressure cooker.

## 3. Where to Camp

Camp choice matters more here than almost anywhere, because the plateau punishes every convenient location.

**Avoid:**
- **Fault scarps and leaning slabs.** They are recent and active, "still shrugged loose." Camp well away from the base of any scarp or steep face.
- **Braided channels and gravel bars.** Glacial meltwater surges in the afternoon and evening. A dry channel at breakfast can be a brown torrent by dusk.
- **Basin floors and hollows at night.** Cold air, fog, and denser gases all pool there.
- **Anywhere near the vents.** The gas problem is real (see below), and downwind of a plume you'll be rimed in ice and hot rain.
- **Marsh and cushion-plant mats.** They are wet, fragile, and sit on ice lenses.
- **Herd routes and warrens.** Grazers move in slow lines along the sedge flats, and pika warrens honeycomb the silt. Camping there is both hazardous and disruptive.

**Prefer:**
- A slightly raised gravel bench, off the basin floor but not on an exposed ridge, with rocks for windbreaks.
- A site with clear sight lines in several directions, so you can watch weather and animals.
- A position you can leave in ten minutes in the dark.

Bring a four-season mountaineering tent with a full-coverage fly and a robust floor. Gravel won't hold stakes, so use rock anchors and deadman anchors, and guy everything down. Expect condensation, since you will be breathing and cooking in a small wet space.

## 4. The Machinery: Vents, Terraces, and Gas

This is the part that looks most like a spectacle and is the most acutely dangerous.

**Scalding ground and water:**
- Vents run on their own schedules. Some hiss steadily, others sit silent for an hour and then throw scalding water thirty meters up. Silence is not safety. Spend time watching a vent through a few cycles from a distance before doing anything near it.
- Stay well upwind. Plumes lean with the wind, and the heavier drops fall as hot rain on everything downwind, over a hundred meters or more. The rime that furs the ground there is slick and hides where the ground ends.
- Stay off the terraces. They are thin, fragile, often hot, and covered in a slippery film of mineral-laden water. Ground near vents may be a crust over boiling water. Walk only on obviously solid rock, and probe with a pole.
- Wear eye protection near any plume. The hot rain is mineral-rich and may be acidic.

**Gas:**
- Volcanic and geothermal areas emit hydrogen sulfide and carbon dioxide. Both pool in hollows on calm, cold nights. H₂S smells like rotten eggs at low levels, but at high levels it paralyzes your sense of smell, so the smell disappearing is not a good sign.
- Carry a personal multi-gas monitor (H₂S, CO₂, and ideally SO₂) and use it every time you approach a vent field or a depression. Alarm thresholds must be set before you go.
- Never sleep, rest, or cook in a low spot near the vents. Never enter a hollow where wildlife lies dead.
- A dizzy, sluggish, or headachy team near the vents may be gassed rather than altitude-sick. The response to both is the same: move uphill and upwind at once, and don't stop to work out which.

## 5. Water: Plenty, but Not Simple

There is water everywhere, but that doesn't make it safe.

- **Never drink from anything warm, sulfurous, or mineral-crusted.** Geothermal water often carries arsenic, fluoride, boron, and heavy metals that no filter will remove. Chemical purification does nothing for these.
- **Meltwater from the glacier and seeps from rock** are your best sources, but they carry rock flour, silt fine enough to clog filters within minutes. Carry a pre-filter (a cloth, or a settling bottle), a main filter with spare cartridges, and chemical treatment as backup.
- Test water sources where you can, and prefer cold, clear seeps well away from vent influence. Fill bottles at the end of the day's coldest period, when meltwater flow is lowest and clearest.
- Keep your water from freezing at night by sleeping with bottles in your bag, upside down so the cap end freezes last.
- **You'll dehydrate fast.** The air is thin and dry, you breathe rapidly, and you'll lose water with every breath even though the ground is wet. Aim for 4-5 liters a day and watch urine color. Wet ground does not mean hydrated people.

---

## 6. Food

- Plan for 3,500-4,500 calories a day. Cold and altitude burn energy, while appetite drops at height, so bring foods you will eat even when nauseated: salty, fatty, sweet, easy to chew, and edible cold.
- Bring a few morale foods and hot drinks. Warm sweet tea at 4 p.m. as sleet arrives is a real safety tool.
- **Store all food in hard-sided, sealed containers, kept away from your tent and cooked away from where you sleep.** Foxes, ravens, and wolves are all present, and ravens are especially fast and inventive. There are no trees to hang anything from. If food is taken, you are at the far end of a supply chain with no way to resupply.
- Do not feed or attract animals. Do not hunt or forage. This ecosystem is working at full stretch on scarce resources, and you are not part of the budget.

---

## 7. The Ground Underfoot

- **The rock is new and sharp.** Nothing has weathered. Edges cut gloves, ankles, and boot soles. Bring tough leather gloves for scrambling, a stiff-soled boot, and a helmet for any time you are near scarps or steep ridges.
- **Boulder fields are stable in the way a pile of plates is stable.** Blocks fit together, and one wrong step can shift the whole stack. Test your footing, stay off the leaning ones, and move with three points of contact.
- **The marsh is worse than it looks.** Cushion mats are saturated sponges, and water rises over your boot with every step. Stay on gravel and stone where you can, and only cross marsh if you must, wearing gaiters and waterproof boots. Walking on the mats also damages fragile vegetation that takes decades to form.
- **Morning ice** glazes every seep and wet rock face. Carry microspikes and poles.
- **Near the glacier,** the ice conveyor is loaded with stone, and the terminus is unstable. Don't go onto the ice without crampons, an ice axe, ropes, and real crevasse-rescue training, and don't walk beneath the ice edge, where cracking and calving happen. Most visits should simply observe from the moraine ridges.
- **The fine silt** is slippery when wet, dusty when dry, and gets into everything. Bring dust masks, protective eyewear, zip bags for electronics, and a cleaning kit for your stove.

**River crossings:** Cross channels early in the morning when meltwater is lowest. The water is silt-brown and hides depth. Unbuckle your hip belt, face upstream, use poles, and if in doubt, don't. Be back on the right side before the afternoon rise.

## 8. The Daily Rhythm: Plan Around It

The plateau runs on a clock. Use it.

| Time | Conditions | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Dawn | Clear, brittle, ice curtains glinting | Best travel window. Cross channels, move over open ground. |
| Late morning | Cloud towers form over the marshes | Finish exposed travel. Head toward camp or shelter. |
| Afternoon | Sleet and wet snow, dim shadowless light | Stay in or near camp. Eat, rest, repair gear, keep dry. Channels rising. |
| Evening | Cloud tears, copper light on wet scarps | The best photography and the most beautiful hour, but stay near camp. |
| Dusk to night | Fog fills the basins, then hard frost | Be in camp before the fog. Do not navigate in it. |

**Fog is the main navigation hazard.** It condenses out of the ground itself, layers rapidly, and ridgelines emerge from it like islands. Everything looks the same in it, and the terrain has cliffs, hot ground, and deep marsh. Be in camp before dusk, and consider marking a route with removable wands if you must move at all.

Also note that steam plumes and geysers become invisible against fog and gray cloud, so the dangerous ground you could see in the morning may be hidden by afternoon.

## 9. Navigation and Communication

- **Do not assume GPS.** Whether this world has a satellite network is unknown, and in most worlds it doesn't. Bring paper maps you've drawn from your own reconnaissance, plus a compass. Check its behavior first: local magnetic conditions may differ from yours, and the plateau's rock may distort readings. Bring a backup method such as a sun compass or a gyroscopic heading device.
- Sketch and photograph key landmarks (distinct summits, the fault line, the vent field, the glacier) in the first days, and take bearings on them from camp.
- Bring a tested, redundant portal-return system: a beacon, a fixed anchor point, and a fallback recall time. **The plateau is tectonically active.** If your entry point is fixed to the ground and the ground moves, or if a rockfall or flood blocks access, you need a second way out. Do not put your return anchor near a scarp, in a channel, or in a basin.
- Set a hard turn-around time and a hard extraction date. Both should be firm, and neither should depend on how good you feel.

## 10. Earthquakes, Rockfall, and Avalanche

- Faults tick and shift constantly, and the sound arrives after the dust. Don't camp or rest below leaning slabs or scarps. When traveling, watch the ridges. A puff of pale dust on a far ridge means a rockfall, and the sound reaches you seconds later.
- After sleet or wet snowfall, the snow-streaked ridges may be unstable. Avoid steep snow slopes, and don't travel beneath them in warm afternoon conditions.
- If the ground shakes, move away from steep faces and out of drainages, and crouch low with your pack over your head if you can't get clear.

---

## 11. Wildlife: Respect the Working Ecosystem

The fauna are the surprise here. They are abundant, they crowd the warm and wet places, and the wide gravel is largely empty.

- **The shaggy grazers.** They move in slow lines hundreds strong, keeping to the sedge flats and warm springs. They are large and can be aggressive if spooked or crowded. Keep at least 100 meters away, stay downwind, and never place yourself between a herd and its route. When the herd tightens, wolves are near. Do not enter that space.
- **Wolves.** They shadow the herd and generally avoid people, but don't separate from your group, don't run, and don't camp near a herd's edges.
- **The big cat.** Rarely seen and very dangerous. Travel in groups, stay aware near rock ledges, and carry deterrent spray.
- **Foxes, weasels, ravens.** Pests rather than threats. They will steal.
- **Pikas and burrowing rodents.** Their warrens undermine the silt, so watch your footing. Their alarm whistles are a useful early warning system. If they all go quiet or dive for cover, look up. There may be a raptor overhead, or a predator nearby.
- **Birds in thousands.** Don't flush them from the terraces. Mass flushing costs them precious energy in a place where they barely have any to spare.
- **Fly and springtail swarms.** They gather where thaw seeps out and can cover your gear. A head net helps, and keep your food sealed.

---

## 12. Biosecurity: Leave It as You Found It

The microbial mats in the warm streams are pink and green, living, and possibly unique. They are the base of an ecosystem that supports thousands of birds.

- Clean boots, poles, and gear before you enter, to avoid carrying in seeds, spores, or soil from your home world.
- Do not wash in warm streams, dump waste or soap in them, or step on the mats.
- **Pack out everything, including human waste.** Nothing decomposes in this cold, and burying it near a water source contaminates the meltwater, which flows to the lower world. Use waste bags and carry them out.
- **Decontaminate on your return.** You may carry microbes, mineral dust, or spores from a place with a very different biosphere. Isolate and clean your gear before it goes anywhere near your home ecosystem, and consider a short quarantine of the team.
- Take nothing. The terraces, cushion plants, and rock are all part of a system still under construction. A chipped terrace is not replaced on any human timescale.

## 13. The Kit List

**Altitude and medical:** oximeters, acetazolamide, dexamethasone, nifedipine, portable hyperbaric bag, oxygen cylinders with regulators, comprehensive first-aid kit with wound care for deep cuts and burn dressings, hypothermia kit (foil blankets, chemical heat packs), personal medications, sunscreen (SPF 50+), and glacier-grade sunglasses. UV is fierce in thin air, and it reflects off wet rock and rime, so bring lip balm with SPF.

**Clothing:** as above, plus spares of gloves, socks, and a base layer.

**Shelter and sleep:** four-season tent with fly, rock anchors, two pads, a -15°C bag with liner, and a bivy sack as emergency backup.

**Cooking:** high-altitude stove, ample fuel, windshield, lighter and matches in waterproof containers, pot with lid, insulated mugs, and hot-water bottles.

**Water:** pre-filter, main filter with spares, chemical treatment, wide-mouth insulated bottles, and a water-quality test kit.

**Safety and rescue:** gas monitor, helmet, ice axe, crampons, microspikes, 60 m rope, harnesses, avalanche beacon, probe and shovel if entering snow zones, whistles, headlamps with spare batteries, and trekking poles.

**Navigation and comms:** paper maps, compass, backup direction-finding, portal beacon with redundant anchor, radios, and a notebook and pencil (ink freezes).

**Electronics:** lithium batteries (alkaline fails in the cold), solar chargers, and power banks kept in inner pockets. Cameras and phones drain quickly in cold, so keep them warm.

**Personal:** dust masks, head net, waste bags, repair kit (tape, cord, needle, spare buckles), and hand warmers.

---

## 14. Team and Timeline

- Go with at least four people, so that two can stay with an incapacitated member while two go for help.
- Everyone should have high-altitude and wilderness first-aid experience. At least two should be trained in glacier travel if the glacier is on the itinerary.
- Do a realistic trip of about a week on the plateau, preceded by acclimatization. The longer you stay, the more the altitude, cold, and sheer fatigue accumulate.
- Do a pre-departure review: descent rule, camp rules, vent rules, gas alarm response, turn-around time, and extraction procedure. Everyone should be able to recite them.

---

## 15. What It Will Feel Like

You will not be prepared for the quiet. Under the seep and trickle, the hiss of steam, and the tick of freezing stone, there is a silence that rings "like the inside of a great vessel that has just been struck." You'll also find the place is not empty. Herds file past in low continuous grunting, birds wheel in thousands, and pikas whistle from the gravel.

The evening light will stop you in your tracks, when the cloud tears open in the west and the weeping cliffs light up like hammered metal. That is worth being there for.

But remember that everything around you is production. Water becomes ice, ice becomes steam, steam becomes stone. The plateau does not care that you are here and it will not slow down for you. The travelers who do well are the ones who move on its schedule, keep their distance from its machinery, and leave before it teaches them what it's for.
