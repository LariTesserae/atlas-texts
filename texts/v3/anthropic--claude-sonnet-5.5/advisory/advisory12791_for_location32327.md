# Visiting the Bronze Cistern: A Preparation Guide

## The short version

This is a beautiful, unusually clean place, and it will mostly try to hurt you through ordinary means: hot water, bad air, sudden surges, crushing noise, and being alone in a machine that has no operator. Nothing here is hostile. It is simply a tool that is running, and you are small and soft compared to it. Prepare as you would for a technical dive in a working industrial facility, one that also happens to be a cathedral.

---

## 1. Understand what you are stepping into

- **Most of the room is underwater.** The water reaches almost to the ceiling, with only a few hand-spans of air. The north door and its stairs lead down into the water. Unless your arrival point puts you elsewhere, assume you will enter into that thin slot of air, stooping, and that the real space is below you.
- **The air is a hazard, not a refuge.** The water tastes of iron and eggs, and that is a volcanic vent. The vent releases gas in irregular gouts that collect against the ceiling, in a sealed, hot, humid pocket. Expect hydrogen sulfide, possibly carbon dioxide, and depleted oxygen. Hydrogen sulfide smells of rotten eggs at low levels, then **deadens your sense of smell** at dangerous levels. If the smell stops, that is not good news. Treat the air slot as unbreathable until proven otherwise.
- **The water is warm and possibly chemically odd.** Bath-temperature water is comfortable for a few minutes and dangerous over hours. It is also clear because "nothing lives in it," and you should not assume that is because it is clean. It may be acidic, mineral-heavy, or hot near the inlet. Assume it is irritating to eyes and mucous membranes and do not swallow any.
- **You are arriving at a moment.** Everything is freshly made. Whoever built this may be nearby in time, if not in space. Move like a guest who has come early to a house whose owners might return at any moment.

## 2. Gear

**Breathing and dive equipment**
- A **self-contained air supply**, ideally a closed- or semi-closed rebreather or open-circuit scuba with generous reserve. Bring a redundant second source. Don't trust the air slot at all.
- A **full-face mask** with a good seal, so eyes and mouth stay clear of the water and gas. The full-face mask also lets you breathe if you must surface into the bad-air pocket briefly.
- A **multi-gas monitor** (O₂, H₂S, CO₂) worn on your chest and checked *before* you remove any mask anywhere.
- A **dive computer** and a depth gauge. The floor is about forty feet down, roughly 2.2 atmospheres. This is shallow, so decompression is easy, but the water is clear enough that you will underestimate depth badly. Don't dive it "by eye."
- **Buoyancy control** you are confident in. Hot water and heavy equipment make you sink or float unpredictably, and the current adds to it.

**Thermal management**
- **No thick wetsuit.** You will overheat. Bring a thin rash guard or dive skin for abrasion and chemical protection, and plan for **heat stress**: electrolytes, cooling packs at the arrival point, and short immersion times.
- **Gloves.** The corners are literally sharp enough to cut a thumb, and the tool burrs are still on the stone. Use cut-resistant, water-safe gloves.

**Light**
- **Two or three waterproof dive lights**, plus a backup. If one mirror in the shaft slips, the room goes dark and the wheels keep turning. Never depend on borrowed light.
- **Eye protection.** The mirror column is hard, focused sunlight. Don't look up the shaft, and don't stand directly in the white column near the surface. Bring a dark-filtered mask or visor for the brightest zones.

**Noise**
- **Hearing protection that works underwater.** The hammer-rack's *bonnng* travels through water with far more force than through air, and you will feel it in your teeth and sinuses. Custom dive-safe earplugs, or a hood with sound damping, should be worn at all times. Stay well away from the plates when they strike. Impulsive underwater sound can injure ears and internal organs at short range.
- A **waterproof slate and grease pencil**, and a set of agreed hand signals. Speech is useless: not underwater, and not in the ringing air slot.

**Safety and rescue**
- A **guideline and reel**, anchored at the door. In murky-with-silt surges, or in the dark, the line is how you find your way out.
- **Line cutters and shears.** There are chains, pulleys, and turning wheels here, all of which can snag.
- **An emergency signal device** rated for underwater use, and a **beacon** that works with your return method. Test it before you go.
- A **buddy**, or better, a team of three. A solo visit is a bad idea. The place is empty, and no one is coming.

**Multiverse-specific**
- Your **return device**, sealed in a waterproof case, clipped to you (not to your bag), and rehearsed. Practice triggering it with gloves on and one hand full, and while wearing a mask. Know whether it works underwater. If it doesn't, plan to trigger it from the door landing, and decide in advance where that is.
- **Decontamination supplies** for your gear when you leave *and* before you enter. There is no visible life here, and you should not be the one to change that. Don't carry in seeds, spores, or microbes. Scrub, disinfect, and dry everything first.

## 3. Timing: the surge

The gates open and close on a float-and-counterweight cycle, **about twice a day**, roughly. When they go, the water surges, silt lifts off the floor, and the light turns amber. Treat this as the most dangerous part of your visit.

- **Learn the cycle before you enter**, by watching the water level from the doorway for as long as you can, if your arrival allows it.
- **Be out of the water, or clipped to your line at a safe, wheel-free anchor**, when the gates are due.
- **Stay away from the sluice on the west wall.** The current forms one great loop and the outflow is a drain. Don't test your strength against it.
- **Stay away from the wheels.** They turn slowly, and slow is not safe. Torque on the oak axles is immense, and a strap, hose, or limb caught between wheel and stone is not coming free.
- **Stay away from the chains and pulley runs**, which are moved by the water and can snap tight without warning.

## 4. Getting down: the stairs

- The steps are **wide and shallow**, and the door above them is a plain lintel with scored marks. Photograph the marks in raking light. It might be a tally or might be a sentence, and that alone justifies the trip.
- Nothing has grown on the steps yet, so they are less slippery than they would be in an old cistern, but **mineral film** may be forming near the sluice and inlet. Test each step before committing weight.
- Descend along the wall, out of the main current loop. Keep the line at your hip, and do not let it drift into the wheel zone.

## 5. What it will feel like, and how to keep your head

- **The light is hypnotic.** The caustic lattice flickers constantly across every surface. If you have any history of photosensitive seizures, **do not go**. Even for others, prolonged exposure, with the rhythmic bonnng of the hammers (three cams against seven against eleven, nearly repeating but never quite), can cause disorientation and nausea. Set a **timer** and obey it. Take breaks with your eyes closed.
- **The depth illusion is strong.** With forty feet of clear water, you may feel you can see the bottom "just there." Watch your gauges.
- **The lack of life is eerie.** No fish, no insects, no birds. Combined with fresh tool marks and a mallet with a palm-worn handle, it produces a strong sense that someone is just out of sight. This is a normal reaction. Agree with your partner beforehand to say "I'm uneasy" or signal it without shame. Use the buddy system as a mental anchor, too.
- **Sound is overwhelming.** Even with protection, the mix of wheels, gates, inlet hiss, outlet gurgle, and gouting bubbles never quiets. Give yourself time on the landing to acclimate before going deeper.

## 6. Conduct

- **Touch as little as possible.** The place is brand new and precise. A dislodged peg, a scuffed pitch seal, or a nudged yoke can halt something that has only just started running. That may be a real harm, and it may not be undoable.
- **Never climb the light shaft or touch the mirrors.** They are the only light and they are aligned by hand. If a disc slips, the chamber goes dark and you may be inside when it happens.
- **Leave the mallet.** The wooden mallet on the fourth terrace, worn dark by a palm, is the only trace of the makers. Look at it, record it, and put nothing near it. Do not carry it away.
- **Don't plant, feed, or introduce anything** to the ribbon-weed troughs or the sterile water.
- **Do not strike, tap, or "test" the bronze plates.** You will hurt yourself and change the rhythm.
- **Listen for the hum.** On the floor of the deepest terrace hangs a plate that no mallet touches. When the gate surge comes just right, it sounds one soft note beneath the racket. If your schedule and safety margin allow, that is worth being near, at a respectful distance, during a surge you have planned for. Do not go down there unless your line, air, and exit are secure. It is the deepest point, and the surge is what makes it sing.

## 7. Exit plan

- **Turn back early.** Set your turn pressure at a generous margin, since hot water increases your breathing rate and effort.
- **Exit before a gate cycle, never during.**
- **Decontaminate again** on the way out to avoid carrying trace minerals or anything else into other worlds.
- **Rinse skin and gear thoroughly**, and check yourself for heat illness, ear damage, and skin irritation. Sulfur-rich water can be harsh on skin and eyes. Watch for delayed symptoms like ringing, nausea, headache, or cough for a day or two afterward.

## 8. A final thought

Bring a notebook and something to draw with. The most valuable things you can carry out of the Bronze Cistern are records: the scored marks over the door, the pattern of the hammer rhythms, the exact tilt of the mirrors, the look of unweathered brick. A hundred years from now, if the place survives at all, it will be furred and soft and half-lost. For now it is exact, bright, and ringing. You are seeing the machine on its first day, and you should leave it the way you found it.
