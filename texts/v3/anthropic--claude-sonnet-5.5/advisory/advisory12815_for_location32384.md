# Visiting the Governor Fen

## The short version

Nothing here is trying to hurt you, and that is what makes it dangerous. There are no predators, no hostile inhabitants, and no rescue. There is a warm, wet, sealed green bowl with a floating floor, a pulse you feel in your chest, and abandoned machinery hidden under moss. The main risks are ordinary ones: falling through, getting lost within two hundred paces of where you started, breathing what you shouldn't, and overheating. The main discipline is to move slowly, stay tied to a partner, and keep track of where the exit is.

## Before you go

**Go in a group of three or more, and keep one person at the arrival point.** The person at the rim holds the way out, watches the time, and is the one who can act if the others don't come back. Nobody else will notice.

**Health preparation.**
- Make sure your tetanus vaccination is current. Rusted iron, broken brick, and constant wet skin make small cuts likely.
- Warm standing water with a permanent mist is close to an ideal environment for *Legionella*, amoebae, leptospirosis, and unknown local organisms. Consider hepatitis A and typhoid protection, and talk to a travel physician about the rest.
- Take a P100 or FFP3-class respirator for each person. Wear it in the mist hour and anywhere you are close to the pipes and sweating manifolds. The mist condenses on everything and you will otherwise inhale it for hours.
- If you have a heart condition, or a pacemaker or other implant, think carefully. The thud is felt in the sternum before you hear it, and the low frequencies are strong close to the engine.
- Plan for heat stress. Body-warm water and 100% humidity mean sweat doesn't evaporate. Bring electrolytes and plan more rest than you think you need.

**Practice one skill at home: getting out from under a floating mat.** Take a peatland or bog-safety course, or an equivalent. The technique is to keep one hand on the underside of the mat, find the light of your hole, and not swim off blindly. It matters because the mat here is a meter or two thick over warm, tea-dark water with roots, pipes, and tanks below it.

## What to bring

**Clothing.** Wear light, quick-dry synthetic long sleeves and trousers, never cotton. Add a brimmed hat or hood, thin grippy gloves, and clear eye protection, which keeps out rust flakes, drips, and sap. Knee pads help, because you will crawl through green tunnels and over root bridges.

**Footwear.** Use sticky-soled canyoneering or river shoes with neoprene socks. Your feet will be wet the whole time, so plan around it. Carry a dry pair of socks in a dry bag for the end of the day, and check your feet for maceration. **Do not wear waders.** If you go through the mat, they fill and drag.

**Ground gear.**
- A probing pole or trekking pole with a basket. A monopod works too.
- A folding pad or two light boards to spread your weight where you must cross open mat.
- Harness and rope, so each person is tethered to a partner while crossing open mat.
- A hooked knife or line cutter worn at the chest where you can reach it with either hand, for roots and vine tangles underwater.

**Air and safety.**
- A personal multigas monitor (oxygen, methane, H₂S, CO₂). Peat, rotting vegetation, and a low bowl can hold bad air, especially in the pre-dawn hours.
- No open flame, no smoking, no sparks. Peat bogs hold methane.
- Do not enter tanks, boilers, or manholes. Rotting plant matter can leave the air inside empty of oxygen, and the way out may be filled with roots.

**Navigation.** Don't trust a compass. The cast-iron mains are large enough to disturb it, and it may just spin. Use these instead:
- The engine's beat. It is your best directional cue, though it is muffled and diffused by the leaves.
- The **stack**, or rather the ring of vapor it puffs after each thud. That tells you where the center is when you can't see it.
- Brightly colored biodegradable flagging at your turns, all of it recovered when you leave.
- A pace count and a written log.

**Communication.**
- The leaf mass swallows high sounds and returns only deep ones. Shouting is muffled, and a whistle will be lost among the whistle-stems in the mist hour.
- Use low, loud signals. Agree on a rapping code on a cast-iron main, which carries sound far. Add strobes, bright markers, and radios (they may work poorly).

**Electronics and cameras.** Everything fogs and drips. Bring silica gel, dry bags, and anti-fog cloths. Recorders will clip badly on the thud, so set a low-cut filter and lower your input gain.

**Water and hygiene.** Carry your own drinking water and don't rely on filtering what's here. Bring waste bags and pack out everything you produce, including human waste. This is a closed system, and you shouldn't add to the bath.

## Arrival

You come in by going down, and you won't see the bowl until you're in it. Do this before you move:
1. **Mark the arrival point** clearly and log it. The rim is a wall of leaf and every direction looks the same.
2. **Stand still and listen** through several cycles. The thud, the hiss, the ticking, then the forty-count of silence. Learn its timing. In the wet season the interval stretches toward two minutes and the hiss becomes a long sigh, and in dry weeks it quickens. That is the only weather report you get.
3. **Consider arriving before dawn and waiting at the rim** for the mist hour. Whistle-stems in the mist sing in one ragged tone, and the visibility is at its worst. Then move in the haze of mid-morning, when it thins.
4. **Clean your boots and gear** before and after. Seeds, spores, and bits of soil from another world could contaminate a system that has reached a delicate, interlocked peace. The fen might also send something home with you.

## Moving through it

**Follow what has grown, not the open floor.** The vine bridges, fallen trunks, and root walkways are load-bearing parts of the structure and are more reliable than the quaking mat. Test each step. When the ground ripples and water wells up around your ankle, you are on a thin section. Retreat to a trunk or crawl on your boards.

**Do not cut vines or trunks unless you must.** What looks like a tangle is a woven structure. A cut vine may drop a bridge or bring down a canopy section, and the vines are also wrapped into the machinery.

**Pipes are the only straight lines.** Walking along a moss-furred main is tempting, but moss on iron is slippery and old pipes may be rusted thin or hollow beneath. Use three points of contact, and put your weight down gradually.

**Move in short stages.** Two hundred paces of open ground might take hours here, because you'll be ducking, crawling, and rerouting. Rest at fixed points. Use the engine's beat as a break timer rather than letting it set your pace.

## Near the engine

- **Keep clear of the discharge and the valve gear.** The hiss is pressurized, warm sap or steam through a wooden flap, and it can scald. The flywheel turns once every eighty seconds or so, which looks gentle, but you cannot stop it and it can crush a hand.
- **Do not touch or interfere with the governor**, the eccentric rods, or the manifold. The whole fen leans to the governor's swing, and nobody knows what removing that rhythm would do to the place, or to you if you're standing in it.
- **Don't take heartiron wood, rust-leaf, or metal-veined limbs.** They are part of the exchange between iron and wood that has been going on for centuries. They may also carry unknown materials, and rust-leaf stains clothing orange.
- **Don't reach into pipes.** The beetles that live inside are harmless as far as anyone knows, but sap under pressure and rusted edges are not.

## Yourself

The fen has a pulse, and people tend to fall into it. You may find your steps and breathing drifting into the eighty-second cycle, and your sense of time may blur. The heron on the flywheel housing lifts its feet in time, perhaps only by chance, but you may find you do too. Counteract this:
- Keep a wristwatch, ideally a mechanical one, and check it against the engine's beat.
- Set alarms for turnaround time and treat them as fixed.
- Take a written log entry every half hour, and read the previous entry back.
- Check each other. If a partner is moving in perfect time with the thud, stop and speak to them.

The green tunnels, the mist, and the lack of any view can bring on claustrophobia or panic. Agree in advance on a word that means "stop, we rest."

## Leaving

Set your turnaround time early and keep it, since the fen has no natural end to a day. Retrace your route by your flagging, remove every marker, clean your gear at the rim, and check each other for leeches, cuts, and soaked skin. Log anything strange in your own condition for the first several days afterward: fever, cough, skin changes. If anything appears, tell a doctor where you've been.

Above all, remember that the place is at peace with what was left in it. You are a guest and should be a quiet one. Leave it to keep the beat.
