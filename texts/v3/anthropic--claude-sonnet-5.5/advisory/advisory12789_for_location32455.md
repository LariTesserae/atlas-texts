# Preparing for Hesper Col

## The most important thing to understand first

Hesper Col is not hostile. Nothing there will hunt you, and nothing will resent you. That makes it more dangerous in a quiet way. The threats are the ordinary ones (cold, thin air, thirst, dust, exhaustion, getting lost), and the city's habit of being unfinished will erode your margin for every one of them. A traveler who dies at Hesper Col will most likely die of something mundane, a day or two from help, in a place that had no opinion about it.

Prepare for a high-altitude desert expedition, then add a layer of humility about measurement.

---

## 1. Breathing

The sky is bruise-colored at noon and the air is thin, so treat this as an altitude problem of the severe kind.

- **Bring supplemental oxygen.** Assume you will want it at night and during exertion, even if you are acclimatized. Carry more than the itinerary implies, because the itinerary will not hold.
- **Bring a pulse oximeter** and check it on a schedule. Hypoxia's early signs are euphoria, clumsiness, and a feeling that everything is fine, and they are easy to mistake for the city's strangeness.
- **Acclimatize first**, in some other high place, for a week or more if you can. Plan a slow arrival. Don't sprint up the terraces.
- **Beware pooled carbon dioxide.** The Rime is a carbon dioxide frost, which means the cold is deep enough to freeze the air's own gas. Where that gas sublimes, it is heavy and can collect in hollows, basins, sunken plazas, and the lower rooms of buildings. Never sleep in a depression or a below-grade room. Sleep on a lip, a rise, or a windward terrace, where the Draw will keep the air moving. Carry a portable CO₂ monitor and treat any alarm, or any sudden headache or panting in a low place, as a reason to climb immediately.

## 2. Dust

The white powder is fine, dry, mineral, and mobile. It gets into everything.

- Wear a **fitted respirator** with fine-particulate filters whenever the Draw is running, which is most afternoons and evenings. Carry spare filters.
- Wear **sealed goggles**, not glasses. Salt dust in the eyes is miserable and can disable you.
- Assume the powder is mildly caustic and possibly chemically nasty. Cover your skin, and wash it off if you have water to spare, which you won't. Use wipes and brushes.
- Seal electronics in gasketed cases. Expect moving parts, including zippers, buckles, and camera lenses, to grind. Bring spares and a small brush and lubricant kit.
- Pack your tent so it can be zipped fully closed. The Draw will fill any open seam by morning.

## 3. Water, food, and time

**There is no water**, and the word has worn smooth for a reason. Bring all of it.

- Plan a baseline of several liters per person per day. Thin, dry air pulls moisture out of you with every breath, and you won't feel thirsty enough.
- Carry **at least double** what your route requires. Distance there is elastic, and a route of "two days" is an opinion.
- Bring food that needs no water to prepare, is dense, and is easy to eat with cold hands. Don't eat anything local. You wouldn't anyway, but say it to yourself once.
- Bring a **sealed water-recovery plan** if your kit allows. A solar still won't work in that light. A condenser might catch the Rime, though the yield is CO₂ vapor, not water, so don't count on it.
- Carry electrolyte supplements, because dry-air dehydration and altitude sickness overlap.

## 4. Cold

The steady cold has "no bite," but don't let that fool you. It is a deep, patient cold, and the coldest hour is cold enough to freeze carbon dioxide.

- Bring an **expedition-grade layered system**: base layers, insulating mid-layers, and a windproof outer shell rated well below what the daytime feel suggests.
- Bring a sleeping system rated for extreme cold, plus a bivy and an insulated pad. Ground contact bleeds heat fast on salt-crusted rock.
- Protect extremities: mittens with liners, insulated boots, face covering, and hat.
- Expect the **Rime hour** to be the danger point. Be zipped in, insulated, and not sleeping in a hollow.
- Heat your batteries. Keep them inside your clothing and swap them regularly.

## 5. Light and vision

The sun is large, dim, and heatless, and the light has little definition. Shadows are soft, contrast is low, and depth perception suffers. Terrace edges, some of them cliffs, will not announce themselves.

- Use **trekking poles** and probe ahead where a lip might be.
- Carry a strong headlamp. Also carry a *red* or dim light for night, because the crust's blue-white glimmer is beautiful and you'll want to see it.
- During **the grey shift**, when light comes from every direction, don't trust your eyes to judge edges or slopes. Stop and wait it out if you're near a drop.
- Sunglasses or tinted goggles are still useful against glare off pale, glossy rock.

## 6. Navigation, which is the real problem

This is where an ordinary preparation fails.

- **Do not trust counts.** Towers, doors, and streets will vary by one or two on a return trip. Don't build a route from "third tower on the left."
- **Do not trust estimates of distance or time.** A tower may be ten minutes or a day away, depending on your approach and whether you keep looking at it.
- **Learn to walk with your eyes half-lowered**, watching the road, not the destination. The locals' technique works. Arrival happens sideways. Practice it before you need it, ideally on the outer terraces where nothing depends on it.
- **Mark your trail with things that don't depend on the city's agreement.** Carry bright, weighted markers or stakes and tie-off cord. Note that the Draw will partly bury or redraw things overnight, so mark at more than one height and place duplicates.
- **Maintain a tethered base.** Establish a camp on a highly visible high terrace and stay in radial range of it. Don't wander in a straight line for days without a way to reverse.
- **Compass and GPS may misbehave**, so bring both alongside analog tools, including a paper map you draw yourself as you go, with *relative* notes such as "wind on left cheek" or "stripe of tarnish-green under the lip." Record uncertainty honestly on the map.
- **Set a turn-back rule** and obey it. Decide in advance: "if half my water is gone, I return, regardless of how close the tower feels."
- Keep a **reliable return mechanism** for leaving the multiverse layer, be it a portal anchor, a beacon, or a keyed transit device. Confirm it works *before* you leave the arrival point. Don't assume the arrival point will look the same. Photograph and sketch it from many angles, and wedge a durable marker in it.

## 7. The ground that won't stay level

"Up" is negotiated in some districts. Over the course of a street the ground may tilt until you're walking along a wall.

- Wear a **climbing harness** and carry a rope, carabiners, and a few pieces of anchor gear.
- Wear a **helmet** at all times in the interior districts.
- **Don't carry anything you can't afford to drop**, and clip essentials to yourself.
- When the tilt starts, **stop and sit**. Sit *before* the slope becomes disorienting. Let your inner ear catch up. Watch the drifting powder, which follows whatever gravity has agreed on that day, and treat it as your compass for "down."
- Avoid descending stairs in the deepest quarter unless you have a tether and a reason. The stairs go down to a landing that is higher than where you began, and your body will not enjoy resolving that.
- Never let go of your last anchor until you have another.

## 8. Doorways and rooms

Doors remember weather. The same door opens onto a bare room in calm and a hall of columns in high wind. Both are real.

- **Check the wind before you enter anything.** Wet a finger, watch the powder, and note the weather in your log.
- **Do not shelter in a room that may be different when the wind changes.** The Turning flips the wind in a moment, and you don't want to be inside a room when it becomes another room.
- Keep one hand on the frame while entering. Don't go deeper than a rope's length without a line back.
- If you find a hall of columns where you expected a bare room, don't panic and don't assume you are lost. Just retrace, noting the wind.

## 9. Sound and the early echo

In plazas, you will hear your own footstep *before* you take it, then again as you do.

- This is normal and harmless. **Don't try to match it, resist it, or outwalk it.** People who chase the early step get tangled in their own rhythm and fall.
- Walk with a steady, deliberate gait. If the echo bothers you, cross plazas along the edge.
- Thin air carries small sounds oddly well. A Tender's scratch may be clearly audible from very far away, so don't assume nearness.
- The **Turning** is the loudest event on the plateau, a low chord from ten thousand doorways. It is not dangerous and lasts a few breaths. Expect it, don't flinch, and don't shout. The skitterlings go still at once, and if you notice that, you have a few seconds' warning. If you're on a slope or a ledge, sit and brace, because the powder halts and reverses and can pull at your footing.

## 10. Living things

- **Tenders** are large, slow, six-legged, and stone-colored. They ignore visitors. Keep your distance anyway. Do not touch one, block its path, or lean against a motionless one, since it may be older and heavier than it looks. Never camp in a spot where one might decide to walk through. They scrape the crust, so if you see clean pale stripes, a Tender has been there, and the stripes tell you a direction.
- **Skitterlings** live in cracks. Shake out boots and gloves. They're harmless, but a crushed one in a glove is an annoyance and a possible contaminant.
- Don't collect specimens. You are a guest of an ecosystem so thin that your presence is a loud fact.
- **Do not scrape, sample, or destroy the crust-films.** They are the city's slow labor, and the city is what you are standing in.

## 11. The deep quarter

Travelers report a large presence there that is inferred from how the powder curves around a shape that isn't visible.

My advice: **don't go, or go last, or go briefly with a tether and a partner who stays outside.** It isn't hostile. But it is the part of the place where up is least settled and where the line between the city and something inside it may not exist. If you go, treat it as a threshold. Don't try to prove it's there. Don't throw powder to reveal a shape. If you feel large attention, do not stare, and do not try to speak to it. Pause, breathe, and back out the way you came, eyes half-lowered.

## 12. Companions and mind

- **Go with at least one other person**, ideally two or three. Solo travel here is possible, but the city's unfinishedness is a load on the mind, and a second person is the best reference for what is consistent.
- Agree on **check-in phrases**: a way to confirm "we're both experiencing this the same way." If one of you says the tower count is four and the other says five, that's not necessarily a problem. The city may hold both. But you should know it's happening.
- Bring a **paper journal** and write the date, the wind, the count, and your mood at regular intervals. Small, dull routines resist the city's dissolving pull.
- Bring an **anchor object** with strong personal meaning and unambiguous shape, such as a stone from home, a knotted cord, or a photograph. Touch it when you feel unmoored.
- Be aware that the place gives an impression that you are "one small thing it has begun to imagine." Don't argue with the feeling, and don't lean into it. Treat it as weather. Say your name aloud from time to time. Say where you are from. Say when you're leaving.
- Have a **hard departure date** and a partner who will hold you to it.

## 13. Etiquette and ethics

- Leave nothing behind. Even a dropped glove is a foreign object in a place that digests and remakes itself, and you don't know what that means.
- Take nothing that can't be replaced by wind: no wall fragments, no statue pieces, no crust.
- Don't name the unnamed structures, don't scratch your initials, and don't build cairns. The city's own layout is the only language it's fluent in.
- Move as though the city were a sleeping thing that might, in a small way, be listening.

## A suggested kit, condensed

**Survival:** Oxygen with spares. Oximeter. CO₂ monitor. Respirator, spare filters, sealed goggles. Water (double the plan), electrolytes, dense dry food. Extreme-cold sleeping system, bivy, insulated pad. Layered clothing, shell, mittens, insulated boots.

**Movement and safety:** Harness, helmet, 60 m of rope, anchors, carabiners. Trekking poles. Headlamp, red lamp, spare batteries kept warm. First aid, including altitude medication prescribed by a qualified physician. Emergency beacon (test it).

**Navigation:** Paper map and pencil. Compass and backup devices. Bright markers, stakes, cord. Camera or sketchbook. Return-transit anchor, tested and duplicated.

**Mind:** Journal. Anchor object. A companion. A departure date.

---

## What it will feel like

Expect the first hours to be quiet and a little embarrassing. You'll be over-prepared for a place that seems to want nothing from you. Then the small things will start: a count that is off by one, a footstep you hear early, a tower that is suddenly beside you. Don't let these accumulate into dread. They're the local grammar.

If you do it right, you'll spend a few days walking through the grey shift, watching the blue-white glimmer move in the stone, and feeling the enormous patience of a city that has been almost real for a very long time. Then you'll go home, carrying dust in your seams. Take your time on the way out. The exit is a place too, and it is just as unfinished as the rest.
