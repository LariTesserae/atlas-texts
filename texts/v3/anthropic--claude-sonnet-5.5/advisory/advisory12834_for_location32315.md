# Preparing to Visit the Drowned Terraces of Ossel Tarn

## The short version

You will be a warm, air-breathing, dry-land animal entering a cold, deep, crowded, loud underwater garden at high altitude. Almost everything about the visit is a logistics problem in cold water, breathing gas, and patience. The rest is manners. The place is old and slow and does not need you. Prepare to be a careful, quiet, useful guest.

---

## 1. Decide how you will breathe

There is no dry air in the basin as described, so this is the central question. Your options, from most to least sensible:

**A. Closed-circuit rebreather (best choice for most travelers).**
- It is nearly silent. Open-circuit regulators roar and spit bubbles in a place where sound is the language, the calendar, and the religion. A rebreather lets you hear the reeds, the drummers, and the bell-tree.
- It releases almost no bubbles. Bubbles trapped under lattice sheets, in moss-carpets, and against the arches are a small insult to things that have been trained for generations.
- It extends your gas supply, which matters when you are hours from a fill station and a hike above the treeline.
- It needs real training. Do not learn it here. Arrive with dozens of hours on the specific unit, and carry a bailout system.

**B. Open-circuit scuba with backup.** This is workable in the shallows, but loud and bubbly. Treat it as a fallback, not a plan.

**C. Surface-supplied or habitat-based stays.** If you plan to stay for days, you cannot spend that time in the water on a tank. Consider a small shore camp with a warm, dry tent where you recover, plus a compact diving-bell or lock-out habitat if you plan to go deep. Negotiate with the residents before anchoring anything in their water.

**D. Multiverse-grade water-breathing adaptations** (gill grafts, oxygenating fluids, a "lake-kin" transformation). If you have access to something like this, understand that it is the best approach for a long stay and the worst for an ordinary traveler's peace of mind. Getting the change reversed is also not guaranteed. If you take one, insist on a reversal plan that does not depend on the town.

**Depth planning.** The deepest part is about sixty fathoms (roughly 110 m). That is deep technical diving territory, around 11 atmospheres of water pressure. Because the surface is at 4000 m, the air pressure above the water is only about 60% of sea-level. Decompression tables must be altitude-corrected, or your computer must be set for it. Standard sea-level tables will hurt you.

For a first visit, stay in the shallows and upper terraces (under 30 m). The light is best there. The people live and work at many depths, so you will see a great deal without going to the bottom. Go to the deepest hollow and the bell-tree only with a guide, only if you are trained for it, and only with a proper gas plan (trimix, staged decompression, redundant computers).

## 2. Cold, exposure, and your body

The water is cellar-cold, a few degrees above freezing. This will limit your visit more than depth will.

- **Drysuit, not wetsuit.** Wear a proper drysuit with thick insulating underlayers, and consider heated undergarments with a redundant power source. Expect to lose finger dexterity fast, so bring a warm dry-glove system with liners.
- **Hood, hands, face.** These are where cold hits first. A full-face mask keeps your face warm and lets you hear a little better.
- **Cold shock and swimming failure.** Your first minutes matter. Enter slowly, control your breathing, and do not exert yourself.
- **Immersion diuresis.** You will urinate a lot and dehydrate. Drink warm fluids before and after, and plan for the drysuit's relief arrangements.
- **Stay time.** Limit your immersions. Cold, decompression stress, and work all add up. Plan for short dives with real rest between them.
- **Warm recovery.** At the shore camp, have a heated tent, hot drinks, dry clothes, and a way to treat mild hypothermia. Know the signs: shivering that stops, slurred speech, clumsiness.

---

## 3. Altitude

Four thousand meters is high enough to make you sick before you get wet.

- **Acclimatize for several days** at 2500-3500 m before approaching the basin.
- **Watch for AMS, HAPE, HACE.** Carry acetazolamide and dexamethasone if your doctor approves, and know that descent is the real cure.
- **Do not dive with a headache or breathlessness.** Altitude sickness and decompression illness can look alike, and each makes the other worse.
- **Wait before flying or climbing higher after diving.** You are already at altitude, so plan the way out with the diving as part of your gradient.
- **Carry everything up.** Gas cylinders, scrubber material, batteries, and heaters are heavy. Use pack animals, porters, or (with permission from your home universe's rules) a mechanical lift. Don't rely on being able to buy anything here.

---

## 4. Getting in and out

- The lake freezes over each winter and sits under a white lid for months. Diving under ice is an overhead environment, so you cannot swim straight up. You need training, a guideline, redundant gas, and a surface team. **The safest option is to visit in the ice-free season.**
- In spring, when the ice cracks, the sounds travel down the water column with a groan and boom. This is loud, disorienting, and potentially dangerous to hearing and nerves, and the moving ice is a physical hazard. **Avoid the breakup.**
- Late summer and early autumn give you the clearest water and the most stable conditions. The kelp is tall, the light is best, and the drummer-fish are active.
- Enter at a place the townspeople use, where stairs of slate or steps in the granite lead down from the shallows. Ask to be shown.
- Mark your entry point and keep a surface tender if you can. Have an emergency plan that doesn't depend on the townspeople rescuing you. They may help, but they may not know what you need.

---

## 5. Entanglement and navigation

This is a forest, and forests are easy to get lost in.

- **Ropes, lattices, and moss** can tangle fins, hoses, and loose straps. Streamline your gear. Clip off every dangling item.
- **Carry redundant cutting tools**: a blunt-tipped shears and a small knife, for emergencies only. Cutting a plant here is not a casual act. Every shoot has been fed or not fed on purpose. If you must cut, do it only to save yourself, and tell someone afterward.
- **Use the lanes.** They're pruned, marked with white river stones and carved floats, and they're the only sensible way to travel. Don't cut across the meadows or through the lattice to save time.
- **Use a guideline reel** in the less-marked areas, and be careful not to snag or leave line on plants.
- **Navigate by the marks** the residents use: the stones, the floats, the strings of lantern snails at dusk. Learn to read them from a guide.
- **Track your own position** with a compass, depth gauge, and a slate. GPS won't work underwater. Note the pitch of the bell-tree as a distance cue. It seems to come from the ground, but it's loudest at the center.
- **Beware silt.** The apron of dark silt at the basin's edge is forty feet deep in places. Kick it up and you'll lose visibility and cloud the moss. Use a frog-kick, hold a horizontal trim, and stay off the floor.

---

## 6. Sound: the overlooked challenge

This is the loudest place you will ever be quiet in.

- **Water carries sound fast and near-undamped.** Reed strokes roll for a mile. Drummer-fish thrum in your ribs at dusk. The gas bladders crackle at dawn and groan in the evening. The bell-tree is a tone you feel in the ground.
- **Expect sensory overload** in the first hours. Plan short first dives. Pay attention to how your body responds. The low tones can cause unease, nausea, or vertigo in some people.
- **Protect your hearing.** Don't stay close to the drummer-fish shoals or the bell-tree pods. Get out if it hurts. Avoid the ice breakup entirely.
- **Learn the reed calls.** There are calls for meals, meetings, lost children, deaths, and the simple *I am here*. Before you go in, ask someone to teach you at least: the call for a death, the call for a lost child, and the call that means *I am here*. Respond to the first two with stillness and respect. Answer the third if you're addressed.
- **Do not strike the reeds yourself** unless you're invited to. Every stroke is a public statement.
- **Keep your own noise down.** Avoid clanging metal, dropped tools, or noisy equipment. Use a rebreather. Keep your movements slow.

---

## 7. Talking and understanding

- You likely share no language. The residents communicate underwater with sounds, hand signs, and reeds. Bring a **waterproof slate and a pencil**, and learn a simple set of hand signals. It helps to bring a few images: simple drawings of what you want (food, warmth, rest, help).
- A multiverse translator may help. Underwater, a full-face mask with a bone-conduction speaker can work, but it will also intrude on the soundscape. Use it sparingly.
- **Be patient.** The residents live in decades. Conversations, agreements, and permissions may take days or weeks. Someone may simply not respond to you for a while, which usually means they're thinking.
- **Introduce yourself with something useful.** The best introduction is to help with a task, such as hauling, holding a line, or carrying a stone. Ask to be shown what to do.

---

## 8. Etiquette and taboos (as best we can infer them)

You're entering a culture organized around **tending**. Its central values seem to be attention, restraint, and continuity.

- **Don't cut, break, or pull any plant** unless you're shown how, and by whom. Shoots that turn the wrong way are simply not fed, and a stranger's hand in the wrong place is a real disturbance.
- **Don't touch the houses.** They're living structures, four generations thick in some cases. Don't lean on them, hang gear from them, or move the weights on their doors.
- **Don't disturb the silt tracks.** The grazing crustaceans' patterns are read by the gardeners to decide where to prune. Don't drag your fins through them.
- **The big trout are community members.** Never chase, touch, feed, or photograph them aggressively. Move aside when they drift over the meadow. Ask their names.
- **The lantern snails mark the lanes.** Don't pick them off the stems. Keep your dive light low and dim, and turn it off in the streets at dusk if you can.
- **The bell-tree is central and sacred.** Approach it only if invited. Don't touch it, tie anything to it, or make sound near it. Its pitch marks the seasons, and the people say the lake's memory of the town depends on it. Whether or not that's literally true, you don't want to be the one who tests it.
- **The court beneath the vault is a public space.** Sit or hover at the edge and watch first. People trade, argue, and tend the great stems there.
- **Watch how people ask for things**, and ask the same way.
- **Do not litter, shed, or leak.** Carry everything out, including the things you'd normally flush, spill, or wash off. That includes urine from your suit if you can manage it, and definitely your batteries, wraps, and lubricants.

---

## 9. Biosecurity: don't be the plague

A closed alpine lake with a unique ecosystem is fragile. You are a walking bag of foreign microbes, seeds, and spores.

- **Decontaminate all gear** before entering: soak wetsuits, boots, masks, and lines in disinfectant, then rinse and dry completely. Check for seeds, fragments of algae, and insect eggs from other waters.
- **Use gear that's new or dedicated** to this trip if you can, and never use gear that has been in any other lake, river, or sea.
- **Keep your body clean and healthy.** A cold or a skin infection is a risk both ways. Check that your vaccinations are current. There may be pathogens in the lake against which you have no defenses. Carry broad-spectrum antibiotics, antifungals, and wound care supplies, and clean scrapes immediately.
- **Leave no organism behind.** Don't bring in plants, seeds, food scraps, or pets.
- **Inspect yourself** for hitchhikers before you leave, so you don't carry the lake into your own world.

---

## 10. Food, water, and shelter

- Assume you won't be fed. The residents' diet is unknown, probably plant fiber, shellfish, and whatever the grazing crustaceans and fish provide. Don't count on being able to eat it. Bring your own rations, and pack out the wrappers.
- If you're offered something, accept a small taste if it seems safe, and only after you've thought about it. Refusing is a delicate matter, and accepting could make you sick. Pair any tasting with your own supplies.
- **Bring safe drinking water and a filter.** The lake is cold and clear, but not necessarily safe for a human gut.
- **Build your camp on the shore**, above the waterline, on rock, away from the lake's edge. Bring a four-season mountain tent, sleeping system rated to well below freezing, a stove, and enough fuel for altitude cooking (it takes longer to boil). Camp at a modest distance from the steps so you don't intrude.
- **Do not sleep underwater** unless you've arranged a lodging and have a source of breathing gas. The residents' houses are not built for you.

---

## 11. Gifts, trade, and metal

- The residents have little metal, mostly soft copper and meteoric iron, and they use flaked stone, shell, and bone for most tools. Metal is precious.
- **Be careful with gifts.** A steel knife could be a wonder, a disruption, or an insult. Introducing high-tech objects can change the balance of a very slow society in ways you can't foresee.
- **Good gifts** are small, made by hand, and consumable or repairable by them: well-made cordage, a hand-carved float, a properly ground shell tool, or seeds only if you are absolutely sure they're safe and permitted (they almost certainly are not).
- **Best of all is labor.** Offer to help mend, carry, or tie. A cord tied well will outlast you, and that is the highest compliment.
- **Do not leave anything behind**, including your equipment, unless it's an explicitly agreed gift. Don't let an object of yours end up in the silt.

---

## 12. Time, patience, and expectations

- The place changes at the speed of a hand slowly turning. A house takes forty years to close its dome, and a lane takes twenty to fix its course. You will see none of this happen.
- **You will see the daily rhythm**: the crackle of gas bladders at dawn, the drummers at dusk, the reed-notes, and the bell-tree's low answer. Plan to be there at least at one dawn and one dusk, ideally from a safe, shallow vantage.
- **The main thing you'll come away with is a feeling**: of being inside something old, settled, and kind, and of how much is done by attention rather than force.
- Don't expect to understand it in a week. If you can stay longer, go slower.
- **Bring a notebook** (waterproof paper, pencil) and write at the shore camp in the evening. You'll remember the light and the sound, and you'll want to keep them.

---

## 13. Multiverse-specific concerns

- **Anchor your return.** Know exactly how you get back: a fixed gate, a timed pull, or a beacon. Test it before you go in the water, and check it every day. Do not lose your anchor in a cold lake.
- **Time drift.** Places this old and stable can run at a different pace. Bring a mechanical watch and a sealed log so you can measure how long you actually spent.
- **Bring something that works without technology.** If your electronics fail in the cold, altitude, or water, you need a way out. That means a mechanical depth gauge, a compass, a paper tables backup, and a whistle for the surface.
- **Leave word** with someone in your home world about where you're going and when you should return.
- **Treat the town as it treats itself**: as a place that's alive, not a scene. If someone asks you to leave, leave.

---

## 14. Suggested kit list

**Breathing and diving**
- Closed-circuit rebreather with bailout, spare scrubber, oxygen sensors, and altitude-corrected decompression software
- Backup computer, mechanical depth gauge, compass, slate
- Drysuit with heavy insulation, heated undergarment, hood, dry gloves with liners
- Full-face mask, backup mask
- Guideline reel, lift bag, surface marker
- Blunt-tip shears, small knife, spare hose clips
- Low-power dive light with a red or dim setting
- Hearing-protective hood

**Surface and camp**
- Four-season tent, sleeping system, stove, fuel, water filter
- Medical kit including hypothermia, altitude, DCS, and wound supplies
- Oxygen for emergency first aid
- Warm dry clothes, hot drinks, and a way to heat them
- Satellite or multiverse beacon for emergencies

**Cultural and practical**
- Waterproof slate and pencils, notebook
- Small, hand-made, low-impact gifts
- Disinfectant for gear
- Rations and water for the full stay, plus extra

---

## 15. If something goes wrong

- **Ascend slowly** and follow your decompression plan, or use your bailout. Don't bolt for the surface.
- **Stay calm** if you're entangled. Stop, breathe, think, and cut only what you must.
- **Call for help** with your own signal, and watch for the residents' response. They may know how to help. They may also decide you're best left to solve it yourself, which isn't unkindness but a habit of patience.
- **If you hear the call for a death**, stop what you are doing and hold still until it ends.
- **If the bell-tree ever falls silent**, leave quietly. Whatever is happening isn't yours to witness.

---

## Last thought

The best way to visit Ossel Tarn is to come as a gardener's apprentice would: slowly, quietly, with clean hands, willing to be shown which shoot to leave and which to cut. Bring more gear than you think you need, spend less time in the water than you want to, and let the green go on turning at its own speed while you watch.
