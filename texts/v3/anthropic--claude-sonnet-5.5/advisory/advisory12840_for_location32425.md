# Preparing for the Upheaval Shelf

## The Short Version

This place will not attack you. Nothing here hunts, and nothing is hostile in intent. But it is a bad place for a body to be, and it changes faster than your plans will. The main risks are the air you can't breathe, the ground that isn't stable, the dust that abrades and shocks, and the assumption that you can retrace your steps. Go in small, go in sealed, go in briefly, and keep your way out independent of the terrain.

## 1. Breathing and Pressure

The sky is black at midday-equivalent, the air is thin and dry, and there are volcanic gases everywhere. Treat it as **not breathable, and possibly near the pressure limit where unprotected body fluids become a problem**. I can't tell you the exact pressure, so measure it before you commit.

- **Send a probe first.** Before anyone steps through, pass a small sensor package through to log pressure, temperature, gas composition (SO₂, H₂S, CO₂, CO), and particulate load.
- **Wear a full pressure-capable suit** with its own oxygen supply, not a mask and a warm coat. If the probe shows a more forgiving atmosphere, you can downgrade, but never plan for the best case.
- **Carry double the oxygen you think you need.** Exertion at altitude burns air, and anxiety burns more.
- **Watch for gas pooling.** Heavy gases such as CO₂ and SO₂ collect in hollows, fissures, and the ring formations. Even in a sealed suit, gas monitors matter for your suit's external chemistry and for knowing where a plume is heading.
- **Suit seals and filters must handle fine glass dust.** Abrasive particles ruin joints and zippers, so use a hard-shell or well-shielded design with sealed connectors.

Because the air is dry, you'll dehydrate faster than you feel, even sealed. Bring more drinking water than the schedule suggests.

## 2. Heat from Below, Cold from Above

The ground is warm like an animal's flank, and above you is a cold, near-empty sky. Your body will be heated through the feet and radiating away through the head and shoulders.

- **Insulated, heat-rated soles**, ideally layered with a reflective barrier. Ordinary hiking boots may feel fine at first and fail near a vent, where rock can be far hotter than the ambient warmth suggests.
- **Thermal suit control** that can both cool and heat. Expect to manage a real gradient.
- **A thermal pad or kneeling mat** for any pause. Do not sit or lie on bare rock.
- **A handheld contact thermometer or IR gun.** The general warmth is a comfortable baseline, but the gradient toward any vent is steep. Any glowing rock is dangerous, and rock that merely looks dark near a vent may not be.
- **Shadows are backward.** The undersides of things glow while the tops are dark, so don't trust visual intuition about what's hot.

## 3. The Ground

The land is rising and cracking on a timescale you can watch. This is the main hazard.

**Equipment:**
- A long probing pole or staff, for testing each step on crusted or ash-covered ground.
- A rope or tether system so team members are linked or can be recovered from a fissure. A fissure "you could lose a boot in" can become one you could lose a person in over an hour.
- Sturdy gloves rated for sharp, glassy edges. Every surface is fresh-fractured, and glass edges cut through ordinary fabric.
- Knee and shin protection. You will fall, and falling on this rock is different from falling on weathered stone.

**Behaviors:**
- **Do not trust a smooth surface.** Fresh ash drifts from collapsed dust columns cover fissures. A pale gray drift is a warning, not a path.
- **A dark, freshly crusted lava skin can be a thin shell over molten rock.** Stay off anything with a wrinkled, glossy, or freshly stiffened surface.
- **Blisters are warnings.** A swelling dome, even one that looks harmless, may split its crown and release hot gas that climbs for kilometers. Stay away from the crown and stay upwind of the vents.
- **Keep a buffer from active lobes.** Twenty meters is a viewing distance, not a safe one. Radiant heat, sudden bursts, and gas can all reach further than you expect.
- **Plan to travel on the flattest patches**, and treat any slope of tilting basalt slabs as moving even if it looks stationary.
- **Keep your route short and always have an escape direction.** Because the terrain changes, a corridor that was open when you started may be closed when you turn back.

## 4. Sound as an Early Warning

The air carries little sound, so your feet and shins do the listening.

- Bring a **seismic or vibration sensor** on the team, and have someone monitor it.
- Learn the baseline: the seated grinding, the ticking of cooling rock. Those are normal. A change in rhythm, especially a rise in the ticking rate, deserves attention.
- **A sharp crack is a fissure opening.** Stop, check the ground, and don't run blindly. Loose scree falling into a new gap can continue for a while, and the gap's edges are unstable.
- The **hum of glass threads** is harmless as a sound, but strands snag on gear. Don't touch them with bare skin, and don't chase the sound. It comes from many places at once and leads nowhere.

## 5. Dust Storms and Static

The storms are made of fine glass dust and ash, lit by internal lightning that doesn't reach the ground.

- **Keep distance from towering, turning columns.** They lean and merge unpredictably.
- **Glass dust is a hazard to seals, visors, joints, and electronics.** Cover exposed connectors, use sacrificial visor films you can peel, and carry a cleaning kit that doesn't scratch (brush, blower, not wiping).
- **Static is real.** Ground your gear and avoid dangling conductive lines. Shield electronics in conductive bags when not in use, and keep spare batteries protected.
- **If caught in a dust fall,** turn away, crouch low with your pack toward the wind, and wait. Don't move until visibility clears, because moving blind through drift-covered ground is more dangerous than the dust.
- **Collapsed columns leave soft gray drifts.** After any collapse, assume the ground beneath is hidden.

## 6. Light and Vision

The vents provide sparse, pulsing light in an otherwise black landscape.

- **A red-filtered headlamp** for general use preserves your night vision, and you'll want it to see starlit glass.
- **A powerful white flood** for emergencies, but use it deliberately since it ruins your adaptation.
- **Dark visor filters** for when a lobe of lava suddenly brightens. Going from near-darkness to a glowing tongue of rock can be blinding.
- **Reflective markers and chemical lights** to mark team positions and hazards, since the black glass swallows ordinary light.

## 7. Navigation and the Problem of Return

This is the part people underestimate. **A route walked once cannot be walked again.**

- Maps are snapshots. Use them for a rough overview, not for turn-by-turn navigation.
- **Vents are beacons, but unreliable ones.** They dim, pulse, and die, and new ones open.
- Carry **inertial navigation and a star-field tracker.** Confirm before departing that the stars are usable for orientation, since they may be unfamiliar in this universe.
- **Drop relay beacons** at regular intervals so the team can find the way back and be found.
- **Anchor your arrival point on the most stable slab you can find**, and don't assume it stays stable. A tilting, cracking shelf can bury, tip, or split the place where you arrived. Have a **recall mechanism that doesn't depend on being at the arrival point**, ideally a timed or remotely triggered extraction that you can invoke from anywhere on the shelf.
- **Set hard time limits.** A visit of a few hours is reasonable. A full day is pushing it. Overnight is a bad idea, since there's no shelter that isn't also going to move.

## 8. Communications

Thin air won't carry your voice, and the terrain will block line of sight.

- Use **radio suit-to-suit** with a relay chain for distance.
- Agree on **hand signals and light signals** as backups.
- Check in on a fixed schedule, and define what silence means. Missing two check-ins should trigger recall.
- Expect that static and dust storms may interrupt communication, so agree in advance on what to do if you lose contact (for example, shelter in place, or head to the last beacon).

## 9. Team Structure

- **Two to four people.** Alone is reckless, and a large group is harder to move and harder to extract.
- Assign roles: a lead, a ground-tester/rope person, a monitor for gas and seismic data, and someone responsible for the recall.
- Rehearse fissure rescue and suit-breach repair before you go.
- Pack a **medical kit tuned to thermal burns, glass lacerations, decompression, and gas exposure**.

## 10. The Film and the Traces

Two things here call for restraint.

**The green-black film** in cooler vent throats might be a chemical curiosity, a first stirring of life, or the residue of something gone. You can't know which.
- **Do not touch it, scrape it, or sample it** without a proper protocol. If you bring instruments, use non-contact ones such as spectrometers and cameras.
- **Sterilize your suit and gear before entering**, so you don't seed the vents with Earth-like microbes or other contaminants. If it is life beginning, contamination could alter it irreversibly.
- **Sterilize again on exit**, and carry nothing back that hasn't been contained. If the film is something you can't classify, the safest assumption is that it deserves quarantine-level caution.

**The built-looking formations** (hollow columns, leaning stacks, rings of blocks) are natural, but they are easy to misread.
- Resist the feeling that someone made them. It's the ground imitating construction, and the feeling is a known trap for pattern-seeking minds.
- **Don't climb or shelter in them.** They are fragile and will topple, and hollow columns are gas conduits. A ring of blocks around a hollow can be a vent waiting to open.
- Observe, photograph, and move on. Treat them as landmarks that may not be there when you look again.

## 11. Mental Preparation

The place is quiet, dark, and vast, and it will get to you in ways that don't feel dangerous.

- **Awe is a risk.** Watching a world build itself under a black sky is compelling, and people linger. Set alarms, and let the lead call time without argument.
- **The emptiness plays tricks.** Stretches of hours where nothing happens can dull attention, and then everything happens at once. Keep discipline during the boring parts.
- **The hum, the glow, and the towers can suggest a presence.** There isn't one, and knowing that helps. If someone in the team starts to feel watched, talk about it and take a break.
- **The scale can disorient.** Every horizon is far below you and the sky is dark, so the sense of up and down can feel unsettled. Anchor to your instruments and your teammates.

## 12. Suggested Kit List

- Pressure-capable suit with thermal control, spare seals, and abrasion-resistant outer layers
- Double oxygen supply, gas monitors, and pressure/temperature gauges
- Heat-rated insulated boots, sole reflectors, cut-resistant gloves
- Probing pole, ropes, harnesses, anchors, rescue gear
- Red headlamp, white flood, chem lights, reflective markers, dark visor filters
- Seismic/vibration sensor, IR thermometer, inertial navigator, star tracker
- Relay beacons, suit radios, signaling lights
- Dust-protection covers, blower and brush, spare visor films, conductive bags for electronics
- Insulated sitting pad, water and electrolytes, high-calorie food that can be eaten inside a suit
- Medical kit for burns, glass injuries, and decompression
- Non-contact sampling instruments (spectrometer, cameras)
- Sterilization supplies for pre- and post-visit
- An independent recall mechanism, tested before arrival

## 13. A Sensible Plan

1. **Probe first.** Send instruments through and confirm the atmosphere, temperature, and stability of the arrival zone.
2. **Arrive sealed and roped.** Anchor to the most stable slab available, and check it again after ten minutes.
3. **Establish a base with a beacon and a recall device.**
4. **Make short excursions** toward one vent or formation at a time, dropping relays as you go.
5. **Observe more than you touch.** Photograph, measure, and listen.
6. **Leave early.** Set your return time at about two-thirds of your calculated safe window, and honor it.
7. **Sterilize and decompress** on the other side, and debrief while memories are fresh, since the place won't look the same when someone else goes.

## A Final Word

The Upheaval Shelf offers no threats to run from, and that is the deception. It is a place where you can stand at the top of everything, watching the ground make itself, and forget that the ground is not finished. It doesn't want you there and it doesn't mind you, so the only thing that keeps you safe is how well you prepared. Go with respect for how little it will notice you, and bring yourself home before it changes.
