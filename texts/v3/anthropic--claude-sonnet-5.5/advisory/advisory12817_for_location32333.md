# Preparing for the Drowned Green Shoulder

## The Right Mindset

This is not a hostile place. Nothing here hunts you, and the weather is mild. The dangers are quiet ones: wetness that never stops, ground that isn't ground, and a gentleness that invites you to lie down and stay. Prepare the way you would for a long walk across a very old, very slow, very patient creature. Move softly, expect to be damp, and never assume the surface is what it seems.

Also understand the Rule of the Place: **nothing ends here, things are absorbed.** That applies to your gear, and in principle to you.

## Before You Go

**Ask about the water cycle first.** The mat rises and falls on roughly a seven-year rhythm. Find out where in the cycle you're arriving.

- *High-water years:* Paths that were firm become soft channels, and the mat floats a body's height higher. Travel is slow and boggy, and it's easy to lose the route. Bring more flotation and expect to cover a third of your usual distance.
- *Low-water years:* The mat settles and cracks, and seams open onto the dark water below. Walking is firmer but riskier, because there are gaps and cold breath rising from them.
- *Middle years* are best. If you can choose your arrival, choose one.

**Plan to be self-sufficient.** No one is coming quickly. The plateau is the size of a small country, the canopy blocks most signals, and the mat swallows tracks. Tell someone at your departure point your route and your return date, and make that date a firm one.

**Acclimatize.** The plateau is high, and the warm, thick air may fool you into thinking it isn't. Headache, nausea, and poor sleep are still possible. Spend a day or two at intermediate elevation if your entry point allows, and carry altitude medication.

**Prepare your body and your medicine kit.** Bring:
- Antifungal cream and powder (your feet will be wet for days)
- Antihistamines and an epinephrine auto-injector if you have any history of allergy
- Broad-spectrum antibiotics and wound care
- Rehydration salts
- A respirator (P100 or equivalent) with spare filters
- Fine-mesh head nets and insect repellent

You are entering an ecosystem whose microbes, spores, and venoms are unknown to you. Assume you will need a medical screening on return, and arrange one.

## Clothing

Everything should dry quickly, and none of it will dry completely.

- **No cotton.** Wear synthetic or wool layers. It's warm, but wet plus a cool night plus cloud descending into the forest can still bring on hypothermia.
- **A light, breathable rain shell.** Heavy waterproofs turn into personal saunas. The rain is soft and steady, so what matters is that you can vent and shed layers easily.
- **Tall, quick-draining boots with gaiters**, or waterproof socks under quick-draining shoes. Bring at least three pairs of spare socks. Rotate them, and keep one pair sealed and dry for sleeping only.
- **Gloves** for handling lianas, buttresses, and moss. Unknown plants may be irritants, and bare hands shouldn't touch glowing moss or fungal threads.
- **A brimmed hat.** Water drips from the second tier onto the third, and you will be dripped on constantly.

## Equipment

**For the mat itself:**
- A **long probing pole**, at least 2 meters, for testing the ground ahead in every low place. Never step onto unexamined surface.
- **Flotation aids.** The grazers spread their weight with splayed feet, and you should too. Lightweight snowshoes or mud-shoes help enormously in soft years.
- A **personal flotation device** you can wear under a pack. Bring a **harness, a rope, and a tether system.** If the mat gives way you would surface *under* a ceiling of roots, not in open air, so your partners must be able to haul you out immediately.
- A **quick-release knife** or cutting tool within reach of both hands.
- A **whistle** on your person at all times.
- A **gas monitor** (methane, hydrogen sulfide, CO₂, and oxygen). Thirty meters of decomposing plant matter above still water produces gases, and the cold, iron-smelling air breathing out of the seams may not be safe to inhale.

**Fire safety:** Do not run stoves or open flames near seams or cracks. The mat is a vast, wet fuel bed, and a peat-like fire could smolder for years. Use a shielded stove, and only where you're sure of what's underfoot, such as a rock outcrop or a wide platform you've built.

**Sleeping:** Do not sleep on the mat. Hang a **hammock with a rain fly** between two solid pillar trees, or rig a **raised platform**. Check your anchor trees for dead limbs and sagging epiphyte masses, which can be heavy and fall silently. Store food in sealed containers hung well away from your sleeping spot. Snails, beetles, and slow mossy climbers will investigate anything.

**Water:** The standing water is tea-brown and warm. It is tannic and biologically rich, and you should assume it is unsafe. Bring a filter, a chemical purifier, and the means to boil. Collect rainwater where you can, since the drip-lines off leaves offer the cleanest source.

**Electronics and documents:** Everything electronic goes in a sealed dry bag with desiccant, and even then may fail. Carry a paper map, waterproof notebook, and pencil. Lenses fog constantly, so bring anti-fog cloth. Expect that GPS will fail under the canopy and that a magnetic compass may behave oddly on a plateau of ancient granite. Practice dead reckoning.

**Lighting:** Bring a headlamp with a red-light mode, and use it sparingly. The forest's own glow at dusk is a resource. Once your eyes adjust, the milk-blue fungus and green moss will show you the path. White light ruins your night vision and makes the moss go dark for an hour.

## Your Way Home

This is the most important item. **Protect your return method above all else.** If you depend on a beacon, anchor, or device to leave, seal it in a waterproof, shockproof case, carry it on your body, and carry a backup. Do not set it down anywhere, even briefly. Things put down here are covered in a week, and a fallen animal becomes a flower in a season. Your pack, left unwatched in a hollow, would be a green mound by the time you came back.

Record your arrival point carefully, ideally by three distinct landmarks that aren't trees, since the maze of lianas and trunks rearranges itself over decades and looks identical from every angle. Granite outcrops, the weeping knuckles of stone, make the most reliable reference points.

## Moving Through the Forest

**Time your day.** Rise before dawn. The great dark birds call at first light, a single hollow note three beats apart, answered again farther off, so that the sound crosses the whole plateau. It is a natural orientation signal and a remarkable thing to hear. Don't imitate it. Travel in the morning, when the light is best and the ground driest. By early afternoon the cloud lid builds and the rain begins, so be at camp with your shelter rigged by then. The rain lasts for hours and is gentle, but it makes footing worse and hearing nearly impossible.

**Follow the grazers' trails.** The pale, long-limbed herds walk the mat on paths that have tested themselves over generations. Their trails are your best guide to firm ground, though not a guarantee.

**Stay in pairs or groups.** Never travel alone. Keep close enough to see each other through the tiers, because voices die quickly in the wet air and the light gives no direction.

**Keep your voice low.** The forest is mostly quiet. Dripping, settling, and the occasional groan of the floating ground are its sounds. Shouting will spook wildlife and unsettle you. When the mat groans, and it will, don't panic. It is the ground moving on the water below and is normal, but do pause, probe, and check your footing.

**Respect the light shafts.** Where a giant has fallen and a shaft of light breaks through, spores and insects hang in it like a solid thing. Wear your respirator when passing through, and don't linger. The plants beneath grow in a hurry, and the ground there may be unusually soft.

**Mind your hands and eyes.** Don't touch the sundew-like carpets barehanded. Don't eat anything, including the sweet resin, the mint-smelling shrub, and any fruit. The animals have adapted to this chemistry and you haven't.

**Watch the wildlife without disturbing it.** Everything here moves slowly, but slowly doesn't mean helplessly. The mossy climbers carry algae and their own moths, the snails leave trails you should not step in, and the pulsing lantern-beetles are a sign of fright, so back away if you see them.

## The Marks

You will find traces of something that isn't the forest: a green-covered cairn of river-stones, a ring of six trees trained into one crown, rows of stretched notches in a line of trunks, and steps that descend into the mat.

- **Do not move, add to, or take anything.** The nearest stone for that cairn is a day's walk away. Someone carried each one, and the forest has kept them exactly.
- **Do not go down the steps.** The bottom is flooded, the steps are worn slick, and the mat above them is unpredictable. The grazers sometimes stand on the top step looking down. Consider doing the same, if you like, from a respectful distance.
- **Don't rest in the ring of trees** unless animals are already there and clearly unbothered. It's their room, and you are a guest.
- **Document quietly**: sketch, photograph, and note coordinates, but don't try to decode the notches or interpret them aloud. You don't know who made them, and neither does the forest.

## The Psychological Side

The light has no direction, midday looks like late afternoon, and the sounds are soft and repetitive. Time slips. The warm air, the smell of leaf-rot and mint, and the ticking of drips like a thousand clocks slightly out of sync are deeply soothing, and the place is designed, by accident or otherwise, to make you want to stop. Lie down in the moss and it will feel like being held.

Counter this deliberately:
- Keep a strict schedule with wake, walk, camp, and check-in times.
- Have your partner confirm you're coherent every few hours. Confusion and lethargy could be gas, cold, or just the forest's pull, and you can't tell which from the inside.
- Eat on time. Drink on time.
- Agree beforehand on a turnaround condition, such as a clock time or a supply threshold, and honor it without debate.

## Leave Nothing, Take Nothing

The forest absorbs everything, but that doesn't mean you should feed it. Pack out all waste, including human waste where possible. Carry no seeds, spores, or soil out on your boots or gear when you leave. Clean everything before your return transit, or you risk carrying this ecosystem into another world, or carrying something from your own into this one. Take photographs, memories, and perhaps one quiet feeling: that you walked, briefly, through the long, warm, wet old age of a mountain range, and that it let you pass.

## If Things Go Wrong

- **You break through the mat:** Don't panic. Reach up, find the underside of the roots, and stay near the hole. Blow your whistle and let the tether do its work.
- **You become separated:** Stop moving, blow three whistle blasts, and wait. Don't wander in search of landmarks that look alike. At dawn, listen for the birds and orient by their call across the plateau.
- **You feel sudden dizziness, headache, or drowsiness near a seam:** Move upwind, uphill, and away at once. Assume gas until proven otherwise.
- **Your return device fails:** Stay put, stay dry, and stay calm. Use your backup. If that fails too, shelter in place and make yourself findable, because the worst thing you can do is walk deeper into a forest that is very good at keeping things.

Go softly, go dry where you can, go in pairs, and hold on to the way home. The forest will be here after you leave, as it has been through everything else.
