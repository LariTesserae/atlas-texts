# Preparing for Halloway Deep

## The short version

Bring wool, wax, patience, and very little else. Go in expecting to be overwhelmed and to stay quiet. Leave nothing behind, because the city will seal it in stone. Above all, do not change the place. Its whole civilization is a balance held at the edge of failure, and a visitor is a large unknown weight on that scale.

## Before you go: the body

**Claustrophobia and vertigo are the two real disqualifiers.** The approach is a fissure the width of a shoulder, followed by eleven switchbacks of wet stair in total darkness. The city itself is a vertical slot, about a hundred paces deep, with rope ladders and lashed plank bridges over black water. If either condition is a serious problem for you, do not go. Practice on a tall ladder and in a tight tunnel or crawlspace beforehand, and pay attention to whether your breathing stays steady.

**Size matters.** The entry is shoulder-width, doorways are low and rounded, and the houses are packed together. A very broad person, or one with heavy rigid gear, will struggle. Travel light. Anything you cannot carry through the fissure sideways does not come.

**Fitness.** Expect hours of stairs, hauling yourself up rope ladders, and crossing bridges while carrying your own balance. Strong hands and good knees are worth more than endurance. The residents are described as small, quiet, and strong-handed, and you will feel your own weakness by comparison.

**Hearing.** You will be inside a struck bell, and the sound never stops. Have your hearing checked beforehand if you are prone to tinnitus. A pair of high-fidelity musician's earplugs is reasonable for the first hours, when the noise makes thought impossible. But the sound is the city's map, its clock, and its language. Once you can stand it, take the plugs out. You cannot navigate, or be a proper guest, while deaf to it.

## Clothing and kit

The air is saturated, cool, and slick. Cloth never dries, it only equalizes. That shapes everything.

- **Wool everywhere**: base layers, mid layers, socks, and a hat. Wool stays warm when wet, and cotton will make you dangerously cold. Bring at least two full sets so one can be slowly equalizing while you wear the other.
- **Waxed or oiled outer layer**: felt, waxed canvas, or oiled leather. Modern waterproof shells will sweat you into a chill in air that is already fully humid. Be prepared to smell of lanolin and wet stone. This is the correct smell.
- **Footwear**: boots with a truly grippy sole and ankle support. The steps are wet, the flowstone is glazed, and the fossil-hard plank bridges ring underfoot. Slick soles are a hazard both to you and to anyone below you.
- **A hard hat or close-fitting cap.** Drips fall constantly, and the ceiling is covered in mineral straws and cones you will never see. A light helmet costs almost nothing. Choose one with no bright reflective trim.
- **Gloves** with grip for the rope ladders and rails, thin enough that you can still feel the rope.
- **A small pack, worn on the chest** so it doesn't scrape the walls or catch on rope. Line it with something waterproof. Keep in it only what you truly need.

**Light.** Bring a small headlamp with a dim, warm, low-power setting, and use it sparingly. The city is lit by small tallow lamps and cold green fungus mats, and everything about the place is tuned to that dimness. A bright white beam will blind residents, ruin your own dark adaptation, and mark you as a boor. In addition, strong light left running near the fungus ledges or damp stone could encourage growth the keepers don't want. Keep the beam low and pointed away from faces, mats, and the pool, and use it only when a step needs checking. After a day, you may find you need it less than you thought.

**What not to bring:**
- Anything that makes a loud, sharp, or artificial sound: phones, whistles, alarms, clicking gear, jingling zippers. Silence or tape what you must carry.
- Anything that could contaminate the water: soaps, sunscreen, insect repellent, perfume, lotions. The water is drunk, plumbed from jar to jar, and it deposits the very stone the city is built from. Oils and chemicals can change how the calcite forms, and a changed deposition rate is a grave matter here.
- Open flame of your own, food with strong odors, live animals, plants, seeds, or anything that might be a spore carrier for the mushroom galleries or fungus mats.
- Anything you'd be upset to lose. In this city, things that are finished with are put under a drip and sealed.

## Practical needs

**Food and water.** Assume you will be given food. It will be blind fish (rare), mushrooms, and chalky tuber flour, and it may feel like very little. Bring sealed, low-odor, non-crumbly rations for your own needs, and eat them out of sight and without leaving wrappers or crumbs. Do not add to the food economy without asking. Accept water only as it is offered, and drink it in the manner shown. Consider a short course of gut-flora support beforehand, since mineral-heavy water in high volume can be hard on a stranger's stomach.

**Trade goods.** The surface trade is tuber flour for copper and salt. The most welcome gifts are small quantities of salt, plain copper wire or sheet, good rope fiber, or fine-grained wool felt, ideally in forms they already use. Avoid anything novel or dazzling. Give it to the household that hosts you, or to a keeper if one is designated, and never toss it to a crowd.

**Air.** Deep limestone chambers can hold pockets of carbon dioxide. This chamber is clearly breathable, since hundreds of people live in it, but a headache, drowsiness, or a strange heaviness in the chest is a signal to climb, not to push on. Tell someone above where you have gone, and when you'll be back. Do not enter alone without a plan for the eleven turns.

## Before you descend: learn the language

The city doesn't speak so much as tap and sign. Before you go, learn the basics:

- **Yes, no, thank you, please, I'm sorry, I need help, I don't understand.** Get these in the hands, and practice them until they are automatic.
- **Speak low and short.** If you must speak aloud, use few syllables, pitched low, near the listener's ear. Shouting is worse than useless because it just adds noise.
- **The knock.** Each house has its own knock, and hosts will give you one. It is a name, so treat it as one. Practice rapping precisely with a knuckle on wood without flinching from the sound.

If you can, spend an evening blindfolded in a resonant space, learning to locate things by ear alone. It will be useful.

## How to behave

**Nothing changes, and you are not allowed to change it.** The mineral water deposits stone at a fixed rate and the residents scrape it back at a fixed rate. Doorways, stairs, and channels are all held open by discipline. So:

- Don't touch the flowstone with bare hands more than you must. Skin oils and pressure can alter how the calcite sets.
- Never block, redirect, or collect from a drip, channel, jar, or gutter. Not even to take a sip or to steady your balance.
- Do not strike, ring, or move a bell. Bells are clocks, calendars, and records. One means *the second meal*, another means *a birth in the lower terraces*, and one means nothing and is loved. You cannot tell which is which, so touch none.
- Don't scrape, chip, or take a souvenir. A crystal is a piece of someone's memory.
- Tread carefully on the ancient planks, and keep your weight over the rope points. A damaged bridge is not just a hazard. It is a break in the pattern.

**The Sealing Terraces.** These rows of coated cups, combs, toys, and shoes are what a cemetery is to this people. Walk them quietly, don't touch, don't photograph unless expressly invited, and don't ask what an object was. Names are given to particular drips there, so a person is a sound. If you stand in front of one long enough, someone may tell you whose it is. Listen, and do not repeat it lightly.

**Do not throw anything away.** If you break, lose, or finish with something, tell your host. It has a place, and you must not choose it.

**Take the noise seriously.** For the first hour you will be unable to think. That's normal. Sit somewhere safe, preferably on a wide ledge with a wall at your back, and let it wash over you before you try to move. Don't decide anything until the map starts to appear. It will, and the pitches will start to mean places.

**Ask before you climb.** The terraces are close-packed homes, and stairs and ladders often run through people's living spaces. Never assume a way is public. Wait to be shown.

**Don't try to solve the song.** The pattern in the drips, the slow swell and thinning across hundreds of heartbeats, is what the residents call *keeping a slow song*. They do not explain it, and they may not welcome your theories or instruments. Don't bring recording gear, sensors, or metronomes. Don't count aloud. If you must wonder about it, do so silently. You are a guest in a room where something is being sung.

## For the multiverse traveler specifically

**Anchoring.** Sound-based and rhythm-based anchors can behave strangely here. Any recall device that relies on an audible signal, a timed pulse, or a vibration will be swallowed by the noise or, worse, will begin to keep time with the chamber. Use a tactile or visual anchor kept warm against your skin, and set your exit point at the fissure's mouth, above the eleventh turn, where daylight can be seen. Do not plan to open a passage inside the chamber. Nothing about the place suggests the song should be shared with anyone but those it belongs to.

**Time.** Nothing here rises or falls with season or rain. There is no weather to read and almost no light to read time by. Carry a simple mechanical watch, silenced, and check it. Tracking hours by your own hunger will fail you. Some travelers report that after a day or two they begin to sense the cycle in their breath and pulse, and that they lose the thread of their own hours to it. Sleep in fixed shifts.

**Stay short.** A first visit should be brief, a day or two, and you should give yourself more time above afterward than below. The song follows you up, growing fainter, and by the time you reach the daylight you may find your feet keeping time to it. That is not dangerous. It is simply the price of having listened. Expect it to fade over a day or several. If it doesn't, sit on a hillside, breathe out slowly, and let your steps fall out of step on purpose.

**Leave clean.** Before you climb the eleven turns, check your pockets, boots, and pack for any pebble, crystal fragment, or object you didn't bring in. If you did bring something in and find you can't take it out, tell your host honestly. It is theirs to place, and it will be sealed under a slow drip and made into a very small, very quiet part of the place.

## A last note

You will be tempted to think of this place as fragile or as strange. It is neither to those who live in it. It is a mechanism, and they are its keepers, and it has held long enough to have become a home. Go as someone who has come to listen, and keep your weight light on the ropes. When the fissure narrows around your shoulders on the way out and the daylight grows thin and grey above, do not turn around to look. You have already heard it.
