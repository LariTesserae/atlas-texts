# Visiting the Lowfold: A Traveler's Preparation

## The short version

The Lowfold is not hostile. That is the first thing to understand and the most dangerous. It is warm, quiet, green and patient, and it will not chase you. Most of the risks come from the ground, the gas, and the water. The rest come from behaving carelessly in a place that seems to keep careful accounts. Prepare as you would for a young volcanic field, and behave as you would as a guest in a large house where the owners are asleep but not absent.

## Mindset

Plan to be *slow*. Everything here runs on clocks longer than yours: the fissures widen, the reeds drift, the towers lean. A traveler who hurries will misread the place, and misreading it is how people get hurt. Plan for a stay of days rather than hours, but keep it short. Nothing suggests the Lowfold resents visitors, but nothing suggests it needs them either, and the pull toward the center is real.

Decide before you arrive that you will not walk out onto the glass plain. Make that decision at home, in daylight, with a clear head. Decisions made at the edge, at the lit hour, with the towers leaning in around you, are worse ones.

## Your way home

This matters more here than in most places. The towers are tuned to something, and a dozen soft tones of "different questions" is exactly the kind of environment that upsets delicate transit hardware.

- Test your return method at the arrival point before you go anywhere. Confirm it works, then confirm it works *after* a tower has sounded nearby.
- Carry a mechanical or passive fallback if your method is electronic, and a second, independent way of marking your arrival point if it is not. Do not rely on landmarks. The towers all look alike, and they are more noticed than seen.
- Leave your arrival point well away from the lean. Do not arrive within sight of the glass plain if you have any choice.
- Tell someone at home your intended return window, and the rule you will follow if you miss it.

## Clothing and gear

**Footwear and hands.** The basalt is young and its edges are unblunted. Every scarp and split dome is a cutting surface. Wear stiff-soled, ankle-supporting boots with good tread, since black grit over cracked lava is like walking on ball bearings over knives. Bring durable gloves for the scrambling and long sleeves and trousers to protect against the rock. Bring a small first-aid kit heavy on wound care: irrigation, dressings, closure strips, and antiseptic. Warm, damp air and a cut do not get along.

**Climate.** Expect a constant, muggy warmth in the day, cooling at night. Wear breathable, quick-drying layers. Bring a warm layer anyway, because near the towers at night the surfaces become colder than stone and the air beside them follows. Bring a light rain poncho for the afternoon showers. It is warm rain, so you need something you can shed, not something you can shelter under for long.

**Light.** The Lowfold is dim all day, and your eyes will need time. Bring a good headlamp with a red or amber setting, with a white beam for emergencies only. The pale palm-sized moths are drawn to the amber glow of the towers at dusk, and they may be drawn to yours. That is harmless but disorienting, so know how to work your light in a cloud of them. Do not depend on the towers for light at any hour except the amber one.

**Navigation.** Bring a paper map you have drawn yourself, plus a compass. Note that the towers *are* a compass: they all lean toward the center. If you are lost, the lean tells you which direction is toward the glass plain, and therefore which direction is away. Bring a pedometer or string-and-knot measure too, though see the note on distances below.

**Gas.** This is the item most travelers will not think of. The basin is barely above sea level, the fissures are venting warmth from below, and the faint sweet-metallic smell that fades after an hour is exactly the sort of thing you should *not* be allowed to stop noticing. Dense volcanic gases such as carbon dioxide collect in low hollows, and you will not smell the worst of them. Carry a personal multi-gas monitor with an audible alarm, and clip it near your chest. Treat any alarm as real. Never sleep, rest, or eat in the bottom of a fissure or a sunken dome, however sheltered and green it looks. If you feel sudden headache, dizziness, or a strange calm, climb out at once. Do not stop to investigate.

**Water.** Carry all you need for the trip. The dew that gathers on the towers and runs into the canals is tempting, but you do not know what the material has taken or given to it. The black pools are deeper than they should be, and their water is very cold. If you must drink local water, filter and purify it, and treat it as a last resort. Do not drink from the black pools at all.

**Food.** Bring all of it. Do not eat the local plants. Almost nothing here has been catalogued, and the succulent mats and reeds are plausibly the product of something tending them. Pack out everything you pack in.

## Sleeping and camping

Choose ground carefully.

- **Not in a fissure.** The afternoon showers turn them into rushing brown channels with almost no warning, and the gas settles there. The fissures also widen, and no one is watching them.
- **Not beneath a scarp.** The ground hums, occasionally and deeply, and rock that has hardly been rained on can still fall.
- **Not against a tower.** The surfaces get colder than stone by night and the dew runs off them in fringes. You would wake soaked and chilled, and it is hard to say what else you would be doing.
- **Not on the herd path.** The tall grey grazers cross the reed fields in single file and stop when you stop. Do not put yourself in their line.

The best choice is likely a flat area of black sand or grit between reed fields, well away from the towers and the vents, with a clear view of the lean so you can orient yourself in the dark. Set your tent low and secure it well. Expect the ground to feel warm at first and then to cool. Expect to feel, at some point in the night, that the towers are listening.

## Moving through the place

**Walk alone when the distance matters.** The Lowfold's distances are honest only for a solitary walker. Two people crossing the same causeway will arrive at different times, and the one who looks back arrives later. This is not a trick of the light. If you travel in a group, do not schedule meetings by clock. Agree on meeting points and wait for each other there. Do not try to keep pace with your companion by glancing over your shoulder, because each glance costs you. Face forward, walk, and trust that the far end will come.

**You leave a trace.** The pavement stays faintly warm where you have walked for about an hour. Anyone or anything can read your route in the warmth, and you can read theirs. Use this: if you find warm pavement and did not expect it, you are not alone in the area. Be aware of it: if you want to be unnoticed, you cannot be.

**Do not follow the oldest paths.** The paths that are always warm, worn by who knows how much walking, lead only to the glass plain. If you are lost and see a warm path, that is a comfort and a trap. Follow the lean *away* from the center instead, and leave the warm paths alone unless you mean to go where they end.

**Watch for the ripple.** You may see a low disturbance over the path, like heat over a road, moving in a slow, deliberate line. This is whatever tends the causeways. It has not troubled anyone that we know of. Step off the path, let it pass, and do not follow it to the tower. Do not touch it. Do not photograph it with a flash.

## Etiquette in a house you do not own

The Lowfold is tended. Drains are cleared, mats are trimmed to a hand's width from the stone, and cracked steps close along a white seam. Behave as though you can be held to account.

- **Carry out everything.** Do not leave scraps, wrappers, or cast-off gear. Leave nothing that would need clearing.
- **Do not damage the structures.** Do not chip, scratch, drill, or sample the material. It heals, but the healing is a form of attention, and you should not be the reason it turns toward you. Don't test whether a cracked step will close.
- **Do not touch the vines that hang a hand's breadth off the walls.** Whatever holds them there is patient and stiff, and you do not know how it will treat a hand.
- **Touch the towers sparingly.** The surface is warm to the hand in daylight and cold by night. Never press bare skin to it after dark.
- **Do not throw things into the black pools.** Your pebble will strike nothing for a long time, and you should not want to learn what happens when it finally does. Do not lower a line, a weighted probe, or yourself into them.
- **Keep to the causeways** where you can. Do not tread on the succulent mats. Do not disturb the reeds beyond what walking requires.
- **Keep your voice low.** A wind that sets two towers sounding together thickens the air and lifts the hair on your arms. If you feel that, stand still and let it pass. Do not stay between them.

## When the ground hums

The deep geological hum will come. The towers fall silent all at once for its duration, and this is your cue. Drop low and away from scarps, out of fissures, and off any elevated span. Wait until the towers sound again. The silence is more unnerving than the hum, so expect it and do not mistake it for a sign that something has gone wrong. Something has: the crust is settling. It will pass.

## The glass plain

You can approach the edge, and you may. Do it once, at the lit hour, when the towers glow amber and lean in like an audience. Stand well back and do not step on the glass. Bring no reflective gear, no mirror, no lens you would be sorry to lose. Rain will vanish on it. Don't let yourself follow the rain.

Something is turning, very far down. It is not a reflection. You will want to look until you understand it. Look for a short time, then look away, and walk back the way you came, facing forward. Do not run, and do not look behind you. If you find yourself unwilling to leave, the plan you made at home is the plan you follow. Have a companion who will say your name and is under instruction to turn you around.

## Leaving

Leave before the time you set, not after. Return to your arrival point by a route you know. Test your return method again. When you go, take with you only what you brought, and be sure you have not left a warm path leading toward the center behind you.

Expect to feel, for some days afterward, that the ground at home is too finished and too loud. That is normal. It passes.
