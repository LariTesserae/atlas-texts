# Preparing for the Chorded Plain

## The Short Version

You are visiting a young, sharp, wet, dim, and extremely loud place. The sound is not an inconvenience. It is the central physical fact, and most of what can hurt you flows from it. Prepare in this order: **protect your body from the sound, protect your skin from the rock, keep your footing near the fissures, and plan how you will communicate and navigate when speech is impossible.**

## 1. The Sound

**Hearing protection.** Bring two layers: high-attenuation foam plugs, fitted deep and correctly, under a pair of industrial-grade over-ear muffs. Bring spares of both, because muffs get wet and plugs get lost. You will still hear and feel the roar, since much of it reaches you through bone and body. Expect protection to reduce the sound, not remove it.

**The body problem.** Low-frequency sound at this intensity acts on the chest, gut, sinuses, and inner ear, not just the ears. Expect pressure in your sternum, a fluttering in your abdomen, vibration in your teeth, blurred vision from eyeball resonance, and possibly nausea, dizziness, and unexplained anxiety. Several precautions help:

- Get a baseline hearing test and a physical before going. Skip the trip with a heart condition, a perforated eardrum, a recent abdominal or eye surgery, epilepsy, or pregnancy.
- Bring anti-nausea medication, and take it before you need it.
- Set a strict daily exposure budget with a partner, and stop when either of you has symptoms. Persistent headache, confusion, or trouble focusing means retreat now.
- Keep well back from the towers. Within about a kilometer, the pressure is like standing inside a struck bell. Treat one kilometer as a hard floor, and two or three as the sensible working distance for anything longer than a quick look.
- Cinch straps and pad anything hard against your chest. Loose objects will buzz, and rigid items on the torso can transmit vibration painfully.

**Sleep.** You will not sleep well, but you can sleep. Bring a padded sleeping system, sleep with both plugs and muffs, and expect weird dreams. Camp on the plain, well away from the fissures and towers and off the water channels. Fog condenses at the nodes of the standing waves, so a camp at a node will be soaked, and one at an antinode will be dryer but shakier. Test a few spots by sitting on the ground for a while and feeling where the vibration is gentlest.

## 2. The Rock

The basalt is freshly split, unweathered, and sharp enough to cut a boot. Plan accordingly:

- **Boots:** stiff-soled, cut-resistant, ankle-supporting, with aggressive tread for wet stone. Bring resoling materials or a second pair.
- **Gloves:** cut-resistant, with reinforced palms. You will use your hands often, for balance, for scrambling, and for signaling.
- **Knee and shin protection:** falls onto columnar hexagons are a good way to lose skin to the bone. Long durable trousers, plus kneepads.
- **Eye protection:** clear wraparound glasses. Fog-borne grit and shards flake off under stress, and the towers' dripping edges can carry particulates.
- **First aid:** irrigate and close cuts immediately. Warm humid air and unknown microbes will make small wounds turn nasty fast. Bring serious wound-cleaning supplies, antiseptics, tissue adhesive, and a course of broad-spectrum antibiotics if your doctor will supply one.

Moving across the open plain is easy in principle, since it is flat and low, but every step must be chosen. Look where you place your foot, and don't run.

## 3. The Fissures

These are the beautiful part and the dangerous part.

**Edges.** Rims can be undercut or crumbly, and a hairline crack may open without warning when the crust shifts. Stay off overhangs, test any edge, and never lean out over a drop to look. Assume that what looks like a solid ledge could be a veil of moss over air.

**Descents.** Only descend with proper rope gear, a trained partner, and a surface anchor. Bring static ropes, harnesses, helmets, ascenders, and redundant anchors. Anchor to something you have tested. Rope tugs are your primary communication down there, since voices are useless.

**Air.** Warm damp air rises from the cracks, and steam vents open when new cracks form. Bring a respirator or at least a good filter mask, because spores, fungal threads, and pollen in a hot enclosed space are a real inhalation hazard. Carry a multi-gas monitor. Old sea bed rupture zones can vent gases like hydrogen sulfide or carbon dioxide, and dense gas can pool in deep shafts.

**Life.** Everything alive is packed down there, and most of it is unknown to you.

- Do not touch anything unless you must. Glove up and cover up.
- Do not eat anything you find.
- The loaf-sized beetles are probably not aggressive, but treat them as unknowns. The gliders are fragile-looking, so leave them alone.
- Assume some of the vines, flowers, and seep-pool creatures are toxic, stinging, or irritating on contact.
- Predators here hunt by vibration. Your footsteps and gear make you legible. Move slowly and steadily, and avoid sudden rhythmic tapping.
- Clean your boots and gear before entering and after leaving, to avoid seeding foreign organisms into a young and sensitive ecosystem.

## 4. Light and Vision

The sun is dim behind permanent haze, so everything is a long gray-gold dusk. Shadows have no direction, which makes depth perception poor, especially on shattered rock. Bring:

- A strong headlamp with a red or amber mode, plus a backup, plus spare batteries kept dry.
- A handheld high-lumen torch for signaling and scouting.
- Reflective tape and small chemical light sticks to mark routes and people.

Use light discipline in the fissures. The glow of fungus, gliders, and signaling beetles is part of their communication, and a white beam blasting through it may disrupt them. Use dim red light when you can.

At night the plain is black with ember-like seams, and it is very easy to lose an edge in the dark. Do not walk the plain at night without a strong reason and a rope team.

## 5. Communication in the Roar

You cannot shout, and radios that rely on voice are useless. Plan a language before you go:

- **Hand signals:** a small, unambiguous set, rehearsed until automatic (stop, go, danger, hurt, water, retreat, come here, look).
- **Rope tugs:** a coded sequence, especially in fissures.
- **Light signals:** flashes from torches, more effective at dusk than you'd expect.
- **Written slates:** a waterproof whiteboard or paper for anything complex.
- **Text-based radios or mesh messengers**, if your home world's tech works here. Test them at the border of the soundfield first.

Always travel in a team of at least three. Keep close enough to touch when you're in the deep roar. Keep an unbroken visual line whenever possible. Keep a rope line between people crossing fog.

## 6. Navigation

The plain is flat and open, and that makes it deceptive. Long views make distance hard to judge, and fog banks can wipe out visibility fast. Sound will not help you navigate, since it seems to come from everywhere.

- Carry a magnetic compass and a paper map or sketch of your route, and use them. Do not rely on satellites, because there may be none in this world and your device may not talk to any.
- The towers are landmarks, and you can triangulate on them if you can identify them by shape. Sketch their silhouettes when you arrive.
- Mark your route with weighted flags or piled rocks. Keep in mind that the basalt is hard to stack.
- Keep track of your distance by pacing, and log it.
- Do not walk toward an unfamiliar tower to check it out. Approaching within a kilometer risks acute symptoms.

## 7. Water, Weather, and the Key Change

**Water.** The wells are clear and tempting. Treat all water anyway: filter, then purify chemically or by boiling. The water is condensed fog, but it flows over the towers' surfaces, through channels, and into pools ringed with ferns and living things. Carry more water than you think you need, and use the wells as a supplement, not a lifeline. Fog drip can also be collected directly on a tarp near the towers, though in the safe zone it is slow.

**Humidity and heat.** The air is warm and wet. Wear moisture-wicking layers, change socks often, and dry your feet nightly to prevent immersion foot. Everything you own will slowly get damp, so pack electronics and food in sealed bags.

**Electronics.** Strong low-frequency vibration can kill spinning hard drives and fatigue solder joints. Use solid-state devices, pad them with foam, and keep spares. Mechanical watches may run erratically. Microphones and speakers are worthless.

**The key change.** Rarely, and with no warning anyone has decoded, all the Instruments shift pitch together. The fog shears, every creature turns, and then the rain comes, heavy, warm, and total. Prepare for it:

- Stay out of the fissures if you can, and above all out of channels and low spots. Rain running off black rock into narrow cracks can create sudden flash flooding. Never camp in a channel or at the base of a well.
- Keep your camp on high, open ground, with drainage cut around it.
- Keep a full rain kit accessible at all times: waterproof shell, pack liner, and a plan to seal electronics quickly.
- When the pitch changes, stop. The whole plain will be in an odd tension for a few minutes, and the sound field will be unstable. Sit down, protect your ears, and don't move until the new pitch settles and you've checked on your partners.
- The pitch shift could make a previously comfortable position an uncomfortable node. Reassess.

## 8. Food and Supplies

Bring all your own food. Do not forage. Pack compact, high-calorie, sealed rations, plus a stove and fuel, for at least a few days more than planned. Sound stress and constant vigilance burn energy and dull appetite, so eat on a schedule regardless. Electrolyte supplements help, because you will sweat more than you notice.

## 9. Multiverse-Specific Concerns

- **Anchor and exit.** Secure your return mechanism before you start exploring. Confirm it works in the soundfield, because devices or rituals that depend on quiet, speech, or fine electronics may fail. If your return needs a spoken word or a delicate instrument, arrange a non-verbal, vibration-proof alternative, and test it at the arrival point. Place the anchor well away from the towers and above any flood-prone ground.
- **Time.** The days here are a long dusk, so keep a mechanical or solid-state watch and a strict schedule. You can lose track of time.
- **Biosafety.** Decontaminate on return. Bring back nothing living. Keep quarantine protocol for at least a couple of weeks, and watch for delayed respiratory or skin problems.
- **Language and contact.** No one is described as living here, but the Instruments were made by someone or something. Do not assume the place is unattended. If you encounter anything intelligent, remain calm and non-threatening, and remember that sound-based communication may be its native mode and your voice may be irrelevant.

## 10. Conduct

The Instruments are the plain's heart. Everything, from the ferns to the wells to the gliders, depends on their sound making water. Don't climb on them, chip them, block their pores, or try to enter their hollows. Don't carry noise-making devices, drones, or explosives into the area, since a disruption of the sound field could disturb a system that took thousands of years to settle. Pack out all trash. Tread lightly in the fissures, where a season of damage might take a decade to repair. If the towers ever fell silent, the land would dry within a season.

## Final Advice

Go in expecting to be small. The plain will overwhelm you with pressure, dimness, and sheer strangeness. If you keep your team close, your ears covered, your feet sure, and your plans simple, you will get to see something few people ever do: a living land built entirely around a roar. And if the pitch changes while you're there, and the whole world holds its breath and turns toward the towers, do the same. Then wait for the rain.
