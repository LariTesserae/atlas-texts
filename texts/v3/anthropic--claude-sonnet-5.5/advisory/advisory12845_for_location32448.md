# Preparing for the Held Basin

## What to expect

This is not a hostile place. Nothing here hunts you, and the ground won't collapse under a boot. The dangers come from its qualities: heat you can't shed, light that hides depth, air that may not be what your lungs expect, and a stillness that tempts you to stay. Prepare for a warm, wet, quiet, physically undemanding place that is nonetheless a serious environment for the body and the mind.

You are also the most disruptive thing that has happened here in a very long time. Prepare to be small.

## The descent and ascent

The crack is your only known way in and out, so treat it as the most important piece of infrastructure you have.

- **Rope and edge protection.** The rock has never weathered, so every arris is as sharp as freshly broken stone. A rope pulled across it can be cut in a way you won't see from above. Bring edge guards, rope sleeves, and padding, and inspect the line at every bend. Spare rope is cheap insurance.
- **Anchors.** Use natural anchors and removable protection. Don't drill or hammer into the walls. The place mends wounds in plant tissue, but nobody knows what it does with rock.
- **Gloves.** Thin, cut-resistant, and breathable. The ledges are square-cornered and the fracture surfaces are granular and abrasive. You'll be grateful for them on every handhold and every vine.
- **Timing.** The climb out comes when you're hot, damp, and tired. Plan the ascent as the harder half. Rest on the cooler ledges in the crack, and don't start the climb dehydrated.
- **A top-side partner.** Someone should stay on the plateau, keep the line, and hold the clock. They are your connection to a world where time moves.

## Heat, the main physical risk

The basin sits at roughly body temperature or a little above, saturated with moisture, with no wind. Sweat can't evaporate, so your body can't cool itself the usual way. Heat exhaustion and heat stroke are the most likely ways for a trip to go wrong.

- **Limit exposure.** Work in short blocks, perhaps forty-five to sixty minutes, then return to the cooler upper crack to recover. Don't push a schedule.
- **Clothing.** Loose, light, long-sleeved, quick-drying synthetic or fine merino. It protects your skin from abrasion and from the furred surfaces of everything, and it wicks better than bare skin in still air. Avoid cotton, which will hang on you like a wet towel.
- **Cooling.** Bring evaporative or phase-change cooling vests or neck wraps, and recharge them at the top. Ice will not last down there.
- **Water.** Bring all of it. Plan on about a liter an hour, plus electrolytes. Drink before you feel thirsty, because in humidity you may not notice how much you're losing.
- **Buddy checks.** Every time you rest, check each other for confusion, slurred speech, headache, cramps, or a sudden stop in sweating. If any of these appear, get out and cool down. Don't wait to see if it passes.
- **The lake.** It's warm as a bath and won't cool you. Don't swim in it. It has probably never held a ripple that wasn't made by something native, and you don't know what it holds.

## The air

Consider the atmosphere unverified until measured. Two opposite problems are plausible:

- **Carbon dioxide pooling.** Heavy gas can collect in a windless basin. It would settle low, near the sponge floor and in hollows.
- **Elevated oxygen.** A vast photosynthesizing mass with no rot might have locked up carbon and tilted the balance toward oxygen. That means dizziness, and above all a much greater fire risk.

Bring a calibrated multi-gas meter with CO₂ and O₂ sensors, check it at the rim before descending, and watch it continuously. Set alarms for both directions. Bring no flame or spark-prone gear: no lighters, stoves, or open heaters. A rebreather or supplied-air escape set is not excessive if the readings are strange.

Also expect a heavy load of spores and microbes in warm, saturated air over a mass of living tissue. Wear a well-fitted respirator with a P100-grade filter when you're on or near the floor, and keep a clean mask for the ascent. Don't put your face into the moss. Wash every scrape immediately, because a warm damp environment is where a small cut becomes a bad infection.

## The light and the floor

The light comes from everywhere and casts no shadow, which removes the cues your eyes use to judge depth. Steps, holes, slopes, and distances will all look flatter than they are.

- **Eyewear.** Moderate-tint sunglasses or photochromic lenses, worn to reduce glare fatigue. Your eyes will ache after a few hours. Take breaks with them closed.
- **Trekking poles or a probe stick.** The floor is a deep sponge of living tissue of unknown depth, with water welling in every footprint. Test each step. You can't see what's under the surface, and a leg could sink farther than you'd like.
- **Wide, flat footwear** with good grip. Waterproof boots will trap heat and sweat; breathable trail shoes with gaiters are better. Carry a spare pair of socks per person, and change them when you rest.
- **Mark your route.** Everything looks alike, lit the same, with no sun to orient by. Use a GPS unit if it works in this world's conditions, but don't rely on it. Also use compass bearings and biodegradable marking that will not stay, or better, coiled cord trailed and collected on the way back.

## Time, and your own mind

There is no dawn, no dusk, no season. You will lose your sense of time faster than you expect, and the place has an emotional pull that isn't described in the field notes: a sense of completeness, of being in a moment that isn't going anywhere. The closing line of the description isn't poetry. It's a warning. You are the only thing here that is passing, and you may start to feel that you don't want to.

- **Bring two independent clocks**, at least one mechanical, and compare them with the topside clock at entry and exit. "Held" may mean time here runs differently. If it does, you need to know before you plan a return.
- **Set hard limits.** Decide your turnaround time before you go, and let the person on top enforce it. Set alarms that force you to acknowledge them.
- **Talk.** Check in with your partner regularly. Stillness and a shared silence can make people slow and agreeable in ways that are hard to notice from the inside.
- **Keep a log.** Write what you observe every thirty minutes. If your entries start to blur, or you notice you've stopped writing, that's the signal to leave.
- **Emotional preparation.** Expect awe, and possibly grief or tenderness you can't explain. Don't make decisions there about anything important.

## Multiverse-specific precautions

- **Confirm your return path** before you go to the crack, and again at the rim. Your way back to the origin world should not depend on a sunrise, a tide, or any cycle this place doesn't have. Carry a redundant means of return.
- **Quarantine your gear.** Clean and dry everything before entry, boots and rope especially, so you don't import seeds, spores, or eggs from other worlds into a system that seems to have balanced itself perfectly. Do the same on exit, so nothing from the basin follows you home.
- **Count everything in and out.** A dropped glove, a wrapper, or a bit of tape may become the first thing in this place that isn't part of the pattern. A place that heals wounds might absorb it, or might not.

## Conduct

The basin looks like a finished thing, so behave like a guest in a room where someone has put every object exactly where it belongs.

- **Take nothing and touch little.** Don't break stems, though they'll seal. Don't pull vines, pick shelf-growths, or collect the small animals. Observation is enough.
- **The cairns and seed-pods.** Leave them exactly as found. If they're offerings, they aren't yours. If they're eggs, they aren't yours either.
- **The doorways.** Don't shelter or sleep in them. They may be only shapes the plants make, but they look inhabited, and they're worn by use, however old.
- **The wall grooves.** Don't touch them, trace them, or take rubbings. The roots avoid them, and that's the best hint you have about how to behave.
- **The animals.** They have no fear of you and no reason for it. Don't feed, handle, or crowd them. The tiny translucent frogs should be treated as potentially toxic. The larger creature has hooked fingers and no experience of predators, so it has no idea what to make of you. Keep your distance.
- **The single clear note.** When it sounds, listen. Don't answer it, whistle, hum, or bang anything in reply. It may not mean anything, but it seems to be followed by silence, and you don't know what the silence is for.
- **Waste.** Carry it out, all of it, including human waste. Bring sealed containers. Nothing rots here, so nothing you leave will go away.
- **No fire, no chemicals, no repellents.** There is nothing biting you, and sunscreen isn't needed in the diffuse light. The less you put on your skin, the less you shed.

## A suggested kit

- Rope, edge guards, harness, helmet, and gloves
- Multi-gas monitor (CO₂ and O₂), with spare batteries
- P100 respirator with spare filters, and an escape breathing set if available
- Water (a liter or more per hour per person), electrolytes, and salty food
- Cooling vests or wraps, with a cooler stashed at the top
- Light long-sleeved clothing, spare socks, breathable footwear, and gaiters
- Sunglasses or photochromic lenses
- Trekking poles or a probe
- Two clocks, a logbook, and a pencil
- Basic first aid, with a focus on wound cleaning and heat illness
- Waste containers and sealed gear bags
- Return anchor and a redundant return method

## The short version

Go in cool, hydrated, and unhurried. Test the air before you breathe it and the floor before you step on it. Keep your partner and your clock, stay short, touch little, take nothing, and leave on time, before the stillness starts to sound like a reason to stay.
