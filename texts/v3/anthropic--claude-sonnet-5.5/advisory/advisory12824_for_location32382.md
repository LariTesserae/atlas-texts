# Preparing for the Brine Gardens of Ostrel Step

## The Central Fact

This is not a garden you walk through. It is a **30-meter-deep, bath-warm, high-altitude dive site** with a large industrial plant running inside it. The description reads like a greenhouse, but almost every hazard comes from the water, the altitude, and the machinery. Prepare as a technical diver first and a botanist or tourist second.

Also note what is absent. There is no staff, no rescue, no chamber, and no fauna to warn you. The place is quiet, orderly, and indifferent, and that is the most dangerous kind of quiet.

---

## 1. Reconnaissance Before You Get Wet

**Spend a full day on the rim first.** The water turns once per day, and the place has a rhythm you should learn before entering it.

- **Map it from above at midday.** The facility is laid out like a leaf: a midrib of main pipes, then lateral branches. That pattern is your navigation system. Photograph it and sketch it. Work out where the "stalk" of the leaf is, because the veins converge there, and it is probably your best guess for a working access point or exit.
- **Find the current's direction.** It is the closest thing to a compass down there. Steel, cable trunks, and turbines may make a magnetic compass unreliable.
- **Watch the afternoon pause.** Note when the flow slackens and the leaves stand upright, and how long it lasts. Do not plan to be near an intake or the central bay when the pumps restart.
- **Note the brass plates and batch codes.** Copy any you can read. The language is new and unstable, and you will want a record before your own memory smooths it over.
- **Survey the roof.** The glass sits at rim level and the water is full to the brim, so there may be no air gap at the surface. That means you may be in an **overhead environment for the entire dive**, with no free surface to ascend to. Look for hatches, gaps, and air pockets. Treat any air trapped under the glass as unbreathable until tested.

**Test before you trust:**
- Bring a gas detector (H₂S, CO, CO₂, O₂) for the rim and any air pockets. The heat comes from a tapped vent, and the rising gas threads may not be harmless.
- Test the water for pH, conductivity, and dissolved metals. "Brine" in the name suggests salinity, which changes your buoyancy and corrodes gear. The old pipes leak heavy metals, and the rust-colored mats exist to catch them. Assume the water is not safe to swallow.

## 2. Body Preparation

### Altitude
- Acclimatize at 2,500–3,000 m for several days before diving. Altitude sickness combined with diving is a bad combination.
- Expect cold, thin, high-UV air on the rim. Wear sun protection, dark eyewear, and a warm robe or parka for getting out of the water.

### Heat
This is the underestimated danger. Water near body temperature gives you almost nowhere to shed heat, and sweat does nothing underwater.

- Keep dives short, roughly 30–40 minutes, and keep effort low. Move slowly and let the current carry you where it can.
- Use a **cooling vest** or thin suit with cold packs, and consider a core-temperature monitor.
- Hydrate heavily beforehand. Do not drink alcohol, and take no heat-retaining medication.
- Overheating raises your risk of decompression sickness, oxygen toxicity, and poor judgment. Abort at the first signs of heat stress: headache, nausea, confusion, or a feeling of pleasant drowsiness.
- Open-circuit scuba gives you a small cooling benefit from cold gas. A rebreather warms your breathing gas and adds scrubber heat, which is worse here. Stay open-circuit unless you know your rebreather stays cool.

### Pressure and decompression
- At 30 m you are at about 3.6 bar absolute, not the 4 bar a sea-level diver would see. But **your decompression obligation is calculated as if you were far deeper**, roughly 40+ m equivalent by standard altitude corrections.
- Use an altitude-capable dive computer, or use altitude-corrected tables. Be very conservative.
- Breathe nitrox (EAN 32 or lower) to limit nitrogen loading. Watch your oxygen partial pressure.
- Keep a **large reserve**. Assume you will need extra gas to work around obstructions.
- **There is probably no recompression chamber for a very long way.** Carry emergency oxygen and know your evacuation route. Do not push any no-decompression limit.

## 3. Equipment

**Life support**
- Full-face mask with communications, so your face and airway never touch the water. This guards against waterborne pathogens in warm water and against the unknown chemistry.
- Independent backup gas (pony bottle or twin-set). Assume you may lose access to the surface.
- Two dive computers and a depth gauge, plus a slate for writing underwater.
- Two cutting tools (shears, not just a knife) for entanglement.
- A powerful primary light and two backups. Deep bays may be shaded, and after dusk you get only sparse sodium beads.

**Protection**
- A thin suit or membrane suit that covers the whole body, combined with your cooling gear.
- Cut-resistant gloves. The basalt-colored concrete rim can cut a glove, and the mineral film on the mill-weed edges may abrade skin. Wear knee and elbow protection for rim work.
- Edge protectors and anchor points for any rope you place on the rim.

**Testing and documentation**
- Gas detector, water test kit, and a handheld voltmeter or stray-current probe. The plants are wired to electrodes, and you are a conductor in the water.
- Waterproof camera and slate. Take pictures. Do not take samples.

**Hygiene**
- Sterilize your gear. Remove lotions, oils, and sunscreen. Shower before entering. This place is deliberately sterile of animals and has screens at every inlet, and you are a very large animal made of microbes.

---

## 4. Arrival and Entry

- **Do not arrive by portal directly into the water.** If your transit method allows aiming, arrive at the rim and check your surroundings before doing anything else. If you might arrive submerged, wear your gear on arrival, with a full mask and a working regulator.
- Descend using a controlled entry. The walls are steep and smooth with raised form-lines. If you fall in, climbing out may be nearly impossible without a ladder or line.
- Test your buoyancy in the top few meters before going deep.

---

## 5. Moving Through the Garden

**Entanglement is your main enemy.**
- Steel wire lattices, tendrils grown through iron eyelets, cable trunks, and dense ribbons all form a maze. Dive as if in a wreck. Keep tight buoyancy control, keep your hands in, and streamline your gear.
- Do not drag a line reel through the plants. It becomes one more hazard. Rely on the leaf-vein layout, the current, and a buddy.

**Suction and intakes**
- Pumps draw across the mill-weed blades, and intake grates are guarded by the dome-headed filter growths. Stay several body lengths from any intake. Assume flow speeds near them are far greater than the slow general current.
- Turbine housings turn slowly but have huge torque.

**Electrical**
- Roots are wired to electrodes. Watch your stray-current probe. Do not touch electrodes, terminals, or the pipe that ends in nothing but has sprouted a crown.

**Automation**
- Replanting machines run on a schedule and nobody will announce them. Move out of their way. Do not block a tray or an intake.

**Contact**
- Avoid the rust-colored mats. They are saturated with heavy metals.
- Do not damage the plants, cut them (except in an emergency), or harvest ribbons. The system is a working organism-machine, and you cannot tell what damage matters.

**Stay off the spool line.** The shaft and cable that carry spools into the cloud are not a way out. Nobody knows where they go. "Grow, and be drawn out" is the garden's motto, not a promise for visitors.

---

## 6. Conduct: How to Be in This Place

- **Leave nothing.** No dropped gear, no lost fibers, no marker buoys, no bubbles of contamination. Bring everything back.
- **Do not introduce life.** The screens at every inlet are the garden's one strict rule about who belongs here.
- **Be slow and predictable.** An automated system may classify you as a fault or a pest. Do not stand in front of sensors, and do not interfere with valves that tick in answer to one another.
- **Do not speak the brass-plate sentence** or try to answer the garden. You do not know what the language is for.

---

## 7. The Pause

Once a day the current slackens, every leaf stands upright, the light goes flat, and the hum drops a note. It will feel like a held breath, like the garden is waiting to hear something.

- **Be somewhere safe and stable before it comes.** Ideally that means a wide bay, clear of intakes, on a catwalk you have already used. Anchor yourself to something solid but not to anything moving.
- **Stay still and do not say anything.** Observe. Note your own reactions.
- **Be ready for the restart.** The pumps catch, the blades lean, and the flow changes fast. Do not be in the wrong spot when that happens.
- **Do not overstay.** The pause may feel like a rare moment of connection. It is also the moment when you are least in control of what is happening. Have your exit plan already made.

---

## 8. Emergencies

- Agree on hand signals and a "we're leaving" signal before the dive.
- Have a fixed turnaround time, based on the earliest of gas, heat, or clock.
- If you feel dizzy, euphoric, or unusually calm in the warm water, abort. That may be narcosis, heat stress, or gas contamination, and all of them look like peace.
- If tangled, stop, breathe, signal, and cut only what you must. Do not thrash.
- If your ascent path is blocked by roof or plants, use your backup gas and cutting tool, and head for the nearest known hatch or gap, not toward panic.
- Keep your return route to your home world clear and rehearsed. Remember that a change of altitude at transit counts as part of your decompression profile. Ascending to a higher altitude world after diving is dangerous. Descending is better.

---

## What It Will Feel Like

You will descend through gold and jade light into a place of extraordinary order, with nothing that looks back. The plants lean the same way. The pipes tick in answer to each other. Everything is warm and quiet and beautiful, and your body will be telling you that it is safe.

It is not safe. It is a factory running without an audience, and you are an unscheduled variable. Treat the calm as a condition to be managed, not a welcome. Go in prepared, look carefully, touch nothing, and come out on time.
