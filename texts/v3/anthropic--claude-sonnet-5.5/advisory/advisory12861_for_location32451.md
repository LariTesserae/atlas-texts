# Preparing to Visit the Held Hollow

## The Short Version

Bring warm, quiet, waterproof layers, and all your own water and food. Rehearse silence. Bring a way to keep track of your own time, because the place will not do it for you. Touch as little as you can. Leave on a schedule you set before going down, and keep to it even if you feel no reason to.

## Reaching the Rim

The greatest physical danger comes before you arrive. The plain is flat and boggy, and the hollow's edge is invisible until you are almost on it. A drop of thirty or forty feet onto chalk is serious.

- Approach in daylight, and never in fog. Cloud on the plain and cloud in the hollow may be hard to tell apart.
- Carry a walking staff and probe the ground ahead of you in the last hundred paces. Peat puddles are deeper than they look, and the rim is slumped and may overhang.
- Wear waterproof boots to the edge. You can change into quiet footwear at the top.
- Look for the stair-heads. The town's steps are cut into the chalk, and someone has worn a route. Use it. Don't climb down the walls, which are wet, crumbly, and mossed.

If you can, bring a companion who will stay on the plain, or leave a clear marker at the rim such as a cairn or a bright cord staked in the peat. The hollow gives you no landmarks. Its light comes from nowhere in particular, so you can't navigate by sun, and you will want a way to find the exit stair from inside.

## Clothing and Body

The temperature is just above freezing and will stay there. It is a damp, unvarying cold that gets into the joints rather than a sharp one. You will not get warm. Plan to stay tolerably comfortable, not warm.

- **Layers:** wool or synthetic base, insulating mid-layer, and a breathable waterproof shell. Cotton is a poor choice because everything here is wet.
- **Hands:** thin liner gloves under wool mittens. Your fingers will never fully warm, and you will need them for the hand signs and for balance on the stairs. Choose gloves you can work in.
- **Feet:** felt- or hide-soled shoes are what the residents wear, and they suit the place. Bring warm socks and a spare pair sealed in a dry bag. Wet feet are the most likely cause of misery.
- **Noise:** choose fabrics that don't rustle. Stiff waterproof shells that crackle are best left at the rim or replaced with something softer. Remove bells, buckles, zippers with pulls that clink, and anything that jingles.
- **Head:** a hat. Breath will smoke faintly, and the rime that grows on north faces is a reminder that heat leaves you steadily.

Bring chemical hand warmers if you like, but they will be used up. The place does not change, but your supplies will.

## Water and Food

Water is the town's scarcest resource despite the constant wetness. It arrives by condensation and is gathered in the dished steps, cupped stones, and carved bowls, and every drop has a use. **Do not drink from the steps, the bowls, or the leaves.** Treat all of it as spoken for.

- Carry all the water you will need, plus a reserve. Sealed, sturdy containers are best. A leaking bottle in the damp will ruin your other gear and offend your hosts.
- Bring food that needs no cooking, makes no noise to open, and leaves no wrappers or crumbs behind. Nuts, dried fruit, hard cheese, flatbread, and cured meat all work. Avoid crackling packets. Decant into cloth or waxed wraps before you arrive.
- Pack out every scrap. This town has been the same size for longer than its stories go back, and it balances closely. Waste has no place in it.
- If offered food or water, accept only if you are sure of the gesture. It may be a great courtesy, or it may cost them something they can't spare. Watching how they treat water will tell you more than any advice.

## Learning Silence

The hollow's silence comes partly from the moss and partly from the people, who speak seldom and low. You will be more comfortable, and more welcome, if you can join them.

- Practice a small vocabulary of gestures before you go: greeting, thanks, apology, "may I pass," "no harm meant," "I need water," "I am leaving." Keep them simple and slow. You won't know the local signs, so learn to watch before you gesture.
- Practice the thumbnail-on-tooth click. It seems to work as a soft attention signal. Use it sparingly and watch for how it is answered, if at all.
- Switch off every device that beeps, buzzes, or hums, or leave it at the rim. If you have a watch with an alarm, disable it.
- If you must speak, do so low and briefly. Don't laugh out loud, call out, or whistle. A dropped object will make only a soft *thock*, but the stillness makes even that feel like a shout.

You may find that the quiet is disorienting. Your own footsteps will vanish, and your breath and heartbeat will seem loud. Some people find this restful, and others become anxious. Try a few hours of deliberate silence at home so you know which kind you are.

## Moving Safely

- **Footing:** The steps are dished deep enough to hold puddles and are slick with moss and liverwort. The light is shadowless, which removes the cues your eyes use to judge depth and edge. A step can look flat when it isn't. Go slowly, keep a hand on the wall or ivy rope, and test each tread.
- **Lanes:** They are a body's width or less. Yield to residents by pressing into a niche, and don't block them. Carrying a wide pack is a nuisance, so bring a small one or carry it in front of you.
- **Roofs are doorsteps:** Some paths run over other people's thatch gardens. Look for worn routes and don't step on the plantings.
- **Rime and cold stone:** Don't lick, lean on, or scrape rime. You have no reason to, and it doesn't seem to grow back any differently, but that isn't a reason to test it.

## Manners: Touch Little and Take Nothing

The place seems to be a finely balanced whole, so the safest guide is to avoid altering anything.

- **Do not take moss, ferns, snails, frogs, or moths.** Do not move stones. A new house is built only when an old one is taken down, and the stone reused, so even a pebble may belong somewhere.
- **The Measure**, the round pool at the center, holds a level that has never changed. Do not touch, drink from, add to, or draw from it. Do not step over the ring of white stones. Stay back from it and keep your bag and staff well clear.
- **The pillar** beside the pool is worn smooth by centuries of palms. Residents touch it in passing without stopping or speaking. Watch for a while before you decide whether to do the same. If you do, be as brief and as quiet as they are. If it doesn't feel right, don't. Nobody is likely to insist, and nobody is likely to explain.
- **The warm stones** in the deepest lane are something no one has ever spoken of. Notice them, and then let them be. Don't sit on them, don't warm your hands on them, and don't ask about them. If your fingers are numb, use your own gloves.
- **The animals:** Don't disturb the frogs, who don't call and don't blink. Don't chase the moths off the chalk. Wrens, goats, and voles will go about their business.

Arrive as a guest and behave like one. If you're offered a place to sit or a task, such as carrying water or combing thatch, consider accepting it. Their work is small, exact, and unhurried, and joining it quietly may be the best way to be accepted.

## Time: The Real Hazard

The greatest risk here is not cold or falls. It is that the place does not change. There are no seasons, no sun to follow, no shadows to lengthen, and no falling leaves. The people don't grow bored, and you may not either. That is the danger.

- **Bring your own clock**, and a backup. A mechanical watch is best. Check it against something outside the hollow before you enter and after you leave. If it seems to slow, stop, or run oddly, note it and take it as a signal to leave.
- **Keep a written log**: entry time, meals eaten, sleeps taken, and how you feel. Record it on waterproof paper with a pencil, since ink will run.
- **Track your body.** Note your hunger, thirst, need for sleep, and how fast your nails and stubble grow. If any of these stop behaving normally, and especially if you stop feeling hungry or tired without reason, leave immediately.
- **Set a departure time before you go down**, and tell your companion at the rim. Make it firm and shorter than you think you need. A day or two is plenty for a first visit. Set a signal for your return: a light, a cord to tug, or a mark you'll touch on the way out.
- **Leave when you first feel reluctant.** The place is not hostile, but it is gentle and unhurried in a way that may make leaving seem unnecessary. If the thought "there's no rush" begins to feel comfortable, that is the moment to climb the stair.

Don't sleep in the hollow if you can avoid it. If you must, sleep on a set schedule, in a spot you have been invited to use, and with an alarm you can feel (a vibrating watch) rather than hear.

## What to Bring

- Layered warm, quiet, waterproof clothing and spare dry socks
- Felt-soled or soft-soled shoes, plus waterproof boots for the plain
- Liner gloves and wool mittens
- Water, food, and a means of carrying out all waste
- A mechanical watch, a pencil, and waterproof notebook
- A walking staff for the bog
- A rope or bright cord for the rim
- A small, non-rustling pack
- Waterproof bags for everything
- A small first-aid kit: blister care, bandages, and something for cold-numbed joints
- A small gift, if you like, that is quiet, durable, and consumes nothing: a beaten copper pin or ring made cold, a length of wool cord, a smooth stone. Give it only if the moment feels right and with a gesture, not words. Be aware it may be politely declined, since everything here has its place and the town keeps a careful balance.

Leave behind: anything loud, anything bright or glowing that would fight the even light, anything living from outside (seeds, insects, soil on your boots), and anything you couldn't bear to lose.

## Before You Go: A Last Word

Expect nothing dramatic. This is not a place of danger or spectacle, but of stillness. You will see fine detail in the light: every leaf edge and snail-trail. You will feel a cold that never eases and hear a quiet that never breaks. You may notice, if you are attentive, that the mist gathers on the leaf-tips, trembles, and falls straight down without a sound. You may notice that the light has no source and the frogs do not blink. Note these things, but don't investigate them.

Go quietly, take nothing, watch your clock, and leave while leaving still feels like a choice.
