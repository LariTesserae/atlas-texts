# Preparing to Visit the Drowned Shelf

## What you're actually preparing for

The Shelf looks gentle. It is warm, clear, gold-lit, and mostly silent, and it grows plants rather than teeth. That impression is what will hurt you. Nothing here is likely to attack you. The danger comes from **altitude, cold air after warm water, sharp new rock, unknown chemistry, and being alone very far from help.** Plan for a high-altitude expedition that happens to include a lovely dive, not a tropical snorkel with a mountain view.

Assume no rescue. The plateau sits at the top of a world, and the nearest help is down cliffs of raw rock, if it exists at all. Everything you need to survive, including your way home, has to arrive with you, with redundancy.

---

## Before you leave: your body

**Acclimatize at home first.** The air above the lake is thin, cold, and blue, which suggests something like 4,000–5,000 meters. Spend days to weeks at graded altitude beforehand. Talk to a physician about acetazolamide and a plan for altitude sickness, and know the signs of pulmonary and cerebral edema. The only cure is going down, and here down may not be available quickly.

**Get a diving medical**, including a check for a patent foramen ovale. Diving at altitude raises decompression risk, and a small heart defect that never mattered at sea level can matter here.

**Update tetanus and general vaccinations.** Then accept that you cannot vaccinate against a world's unknown microbes. Warm, still, mineral-rich freshwater with bacterial mats is exactly where dangerous amoebae, *Legionella*-type bacteria, and stranger things thrive on Earth. Assume the same class of risk here.

**Prepare mentally.** The stillness "has weight." The silver ceiling of the boundary layer, the mirage-bending vent plumes, and the long groan from the caldera are all unsettling. Practice calm in low-visibility, overhead-adjacent, and solitary conditions. Do not come here if you panic easily.

## Gear

### For the air and the rim
- **Cold-weather layers rated well below freezing,** wind shell, insulated parka, and insulated boots. The air is cold and dry, and you will be wet a lot.
- **Glacier-grade sunglasses, high-SPF sunscreen, lip protection.** UV at altitude is severe, and the sun is also doubled by reflection off the water.
- **Portable shelter and a heat source.** Bring a four-season tent or bivouac, a stove, and fuel that works in thin air. Carry supplemental oxygen and a regulator in case altitude hits you harder than expected.
- **A floating platform or small raft with a windbreak.** You need somewhere dry to exit the water. Bring towels, dry insulating layers, and a hot drink source.
- **Water purification by distillation or filtration.** Do not drink the lake. It is fed by rain and hot springs and is full of dissolved minerals. Collect rain if you can.

### For the water
- **A closed-circuit rebreather** is strongly preferred over open-circuit. It reduces gas weight to haul up a mountain and produces no bubble streams to disturb the sand ripples, the spore clouds, or the reed-tree bladders. It also lets you stay quiet, which suits a quiet place. If you use open circuit, plan for altitude-adjusted decompression tables and a lot of hauling.
- **Full-face mask.** Spore blooms, unknown microbes, and the risk of water in the nose (amoebae) all argue for sealing your face. Never dive with your nose exposed.
- **Thin wetsuit or dive skin, gloves, and hood.** The water is warm, so insulation isn't the point. Protection is. Fresh basalt glass and pillow-lava rinds will slice a bare hand open, and a cut in warm, microbe-rich water is an invitation for infection. Wear puncture-resistant gloves and boots and treat every contact with rock as a wound risk.
- **Dive computer configured for altitude,** plus backups. Do not fly, climb, or ascend in elevation right after diving. Going up the mountain after a dive is a decompression event.
- **A reel and guideline.** During spore blooms, "the light thickens to milk," and vent plumes bend sight. You can lose orientation in water that was clear an hour ago.
- **Strong lights with a color-correcting filter.** Below the boundary layer everything has a green-gold cast, and lights will restore what your eyes flatten. Also useful for signaling.
- **Thermometer probe on a pole** for testing water near fissures. The shimmer marks where hot water meets cool, and the hot side may scald.
- **A portable multi-gas monitor** for CO₂ and H₂S at the surface, at shore, and where possible in the water. This is a volcanic system with a deep caldera, and the low groan should make you think about gas.

### For getting out
- **Your return mechanism, doubled.** Whatever anchors you to your home reality should have a backup, ideally two independent means of leaving. Test both before entering the water.
- **Emergency beacon** that works across the boundary between worlds, if your kind of travel permits one.
- **Wound-care kit:** irrigation syringe, antiseptic, broad-spectrum antibiotics prescribed to you, sutures or closure strips, gloves, and dressings that survive immersion.
- **Waterproof notebook and camera.** You will want them, but they are not survival equipment. Keep them secondary.

## Arrival and the first days

**Land on the rim, not the plateau.** The black islands at the edge have low moss and fern mats, thin air, and cold spray. That's your base. Do not arrive in the lake.

**Spend the first two or three days doing nothing strenuous.** Eat, drink, sleep, and let your body settle. Walk short distances. Do not dive on day one. Dive computers can't tell you how well you're acclimatizing.

**Establish routines around the light.** The water clears and the reed-trees fill with oxygen through the bright hours. In late afternoon the bladders release, and bubbles rise in ropes with a rain-like patter. This is beautiful, but the surface will be disturbed, the water aerated, and visibility altered, so schedule dives and surface work around it.

---

## In the water

**Enter the thermal layer slowly.** About 15 meters down you'll cross a boundary where cool clear water lies over warm mineral-heavy water. Your buoyancy will change. Your ears and skin will feel it. The heavier water below may contain metals, sulfides, and other dissolved substances. Do not touch your face or open your mask underwater. Rinse gear thoroughly afterward.

**Stay well within recreational depths** unless you have a very good reason and a very good plan. Depth is not the point here. The forest lives in the top thirty meters, and the calderas at 100 meters are where the groaning comes from.

**Do not enter the collapsed lava tubes.** Their roofs have fallen in places, and those that haven't are unstable. Silt, overhead ceilings, hot fissures, and unknown gases make them the most dangerous place on the plateau. Look through the skylights instead.

**Stay clear of the silica chimneys.** They are eggshell-thin, growing, and fragile. They break at a touch, and they are also probably hot. Keep at least a body length away.

**Hover. Do not kick, stand, or crawl.** The pale sand drifts rearrange themselves in unexplained ripples. The clear rings around boulders expand centimeters per year. Stone-seeds dissolve glass into round pits with one green shoot in each. All of it is slow, and your fins could undo years in seconds. Be neutrally buoyant and treat everything as something you cannot replace.

**Give the creatures space.** The pale grazers and armored cryptic crawlers are probably harmless. The large translucent sheet-animal that glides overhead once a season is an unknown. Do not touch it, chase it, or swim beneath it. Enjoy the shadow.

**Watch for the color tide.** Every few weeks the veil-ferns flush deep red across the hillsides, sweeping outward from the vents over about three days. The reed-trees follow with spore clouds. If you want to see this, plan a stay of several weeks and be patient, because there is no known trigger. When the spores bloom, treat the water as low-visibility, potentially irritating or allergenic, and stay on the line.

**Treat the groan as information.** If a deep, low sound comes from the caldera, note it, note whether it repeats or grows, and be ready to leave the water. It may be nothing. It may be gas or seismic movement. The correct response to uncertainty is to be near an exit.

## After each dive

The most likely way to get seriously hurt is to exit the warm water into thin, cold, windy air. Wet skin, altitude, and wind can push you into hypothermia within minutes despite feeling warm a moment ago.

- Exit onto the platform or shore, not into open wind.
- Strip wet layers immediately. Towel off, put on dry insulation, and drink something warm.
- Rinse all gear with treated water. Check for cuts and clean them. Watch for infection, fever, headache, or stiff neck for days afterward.
- Rest. Altitude and immersion together are more tiring than either alone.

---

## Leave no trace, in the strictest sense

The Shelf is young, and its ecosystem is still assembling itself. Anything you bring can become an invasive species. Sterilize gear, boots, and suits before you arrive with bleach or heat. Bring no seeds, food scraps, or living things. Pack out all waste, including human waste. Do not collect samples unless your purpose is scientific and authorized. Take photographs and notes and carry them home.

---

## What you'll get for the effort

If you prepare properly, you'll float in warm gold light above a red-flushed hillside, watching ropes of bubbles rise in silence, with a shadow the size of a cloud passing over the forest floor. You'll see a place in the long, unhurried business of becoming itself. Treat it the way you would a sleeping animal: quietly, without touching, with a clear path out.
