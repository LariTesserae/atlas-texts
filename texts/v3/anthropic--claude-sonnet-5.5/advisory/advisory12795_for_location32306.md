# Preparing to Visit the Warm Sink

## What you're actually preparing for

Think of it as a **vertical, closed, skin-warm greenhouse with its own weather**, entered by a hole in the ground. The surface gives no warning. The plateau is scrubby, dry, and ordinary, and then it ends. Most of your preparation comes down to four things: getting in and out safely, surviving heat you can't shed, not harming a fragile place, and not being fooled by how gentle it feels.

---

## 1. The rim and the descent

**The edge is the first hazard.** The crack appears without warning in limestone scrub, and the rim of a collapsed cavern may itself be undercut or unstable. Approach the last stretch slowly, test the ground, and don't put weight near the lip. Anchor well back from the edge and never rest a rope's whole load on a single limestone block.

**Depth:** about 140 feet (roughly 43 m). Bring a rope of at least 60 m, preferably 70, plus:
- A full vertical kit: harness, helmet, two descenders or a descender with backup, ascending gear, slings, and edge protectors. Limestone edges cut rope, and moss and moisture make everything slick.
- A second, separate rope or fixed line for the return.
- **Training.** If you can't do a controlled rappel and an unassisted ascent on a rope, don't go. The climb out is when you'll be hottest and most tired.

**Do not trust the vines.** They're thick as wrists and tempting, but they're rooted in shelf-soil the width of a hand and grow toward heat seams. Treat them as scenery.

**The old stair on the north wall** is a marvel and a trap. The steps are dished by ancient feet, the lower flights are hidden under roots and moss, and they're wet. Use it as a walking route only when it's clearly sound, and stay clipped to a line. Don't lever on it, don't pull roots out of it, and don't count on it as your way out.

**Leave a surface partner at the rim** with a radio and a written plan. Assume your devices won't reach anyone from the bottom.

---

## 2. Heat and humidity

The air at the bottom is about the temperature of skin, and the walls are sweating. Your sweat won't evaporate, so your body loses its main cooling mechanism. This is the most likely thing to hurt a well-equipped visitor.

- **Acclimatize** for a week or more beforehand if you can: heat exposure, humid conditions, hard exercise in warm rooms.
- **Water:** carry in everything you'll drink, plus margin. Plan on about a liter per hour of active movement, with electrolytes. Water is not plentiful in the sink, and the pool is not a supply (see below).
- **Pace yourself** by moving slowly, resting often, and keeping your workload low on the bottom. Save your strength for the ascent.
- **Know the signs** of heat exhaustion: headache, chills despite the heat, confusion, cramps, cessation of sweating. If they appear, stop, cool down, rehydrate, and don't attempt the climb until you've recovered.
- **Plan the exit early.** Start climbing well before you feel you need to.

---

## 3. Air

Hot, still, sheltered hollows are the kind of place where **gas can pool**. The floor "breathes out warmth from somewhere deeper," and you don't know what else comes up with it.

- Carry a **personal multi-gas monitor** (CO₂, O₂, at least). Treat any alarm, headache, or sudden drowsiness as a reason to climb immediately.
- Be extra careful in low spots, near the pool, and near the seeps and cracks.
- Bring a **P100 or good N95 respirator**. Swiftlet roosts mean guano, and the air is thick with spores, mist, and moth scales. Histoplasmosis and other fungal lung infections are real risks in guano-rich, humid caves. Wear the mask near the walls and roosts in particular.
- Keep your eyes protected. Mist, drips, and spores will fog and irritate.

---

## 4. Water and the pool

- **Don't drink from the pool or stream, and don't swim.** It's warm (just above body temperature) and stagnant, and warm freshwater breeds dangerous organisms, including brain-invading amoebae that enter through the nose. It also drops to unmeasured depth.
- Don't splash it on your face or put your head under. If you must use it in an emergency, filter, purify, and boil.
- Stay back from the edges, where roots are exposed and the ground is soft. Something is rising to breathe out there, and whatever the "long, low thing with a wet gleam" is, leave it alone.
- The stream vanishes into a crack. Keep clear of it.
- **The afternoon rain** comes from condensation, lasts about twenty minutes, and falls unevenly. It will soak you and your gear and make ropes, steps, and roots slicker. Plan around it: expect to be wet, and don't begin or end a critical vertical section in it.

---

## 5. Clothing and gear

- **Lightweight, breathable, quick-drying** long sleeves and long trousers. Everything you push through will leave damp prints, and you'll be turning your shoulders through dense leaves all day.
- **Boots with real tread**, plus spare socks, changed regularly. Constant moisture rots skin, so dry your feet at every rest.
- **Thin gloves** for handling roots and rock and for ropework.
- A **brimmed hat or helmet** with a light. Drops fall from the canopy all the time.
- **Waterproof cases and desiccant** for electronics, cameras, and paper. Lenses will fog constantly, so bring anti-fog and microfiber cloths.
- **A red-filtered headlamp** (see section 8), plus a bright white backup for emergencies only.
- **Navigation:** GPS will be unreliable under the rim and canopy. Sink is about 400 paces across, which sounds small, but in dense growth it's easy to lose your bearings. Use a compass, keep the rim's light and the north wall as orientation, and mark your route with removable flagging you take out afterward. Carry a whistle.
- **A first-aid kit** weighted for wet heat: blister care, antiseptic, antihistamines, tweezers, wound closure, oral rehydration, and something for stings.

---

## 6. Creatures

The fauna is abundant, quiet, and mostly not interested in you. Most of your risk is from small things.

- **Millipedes** (forearm-long): don't handle them. Many secrete irritants.
- **Ants:** leaf-cutter trails are trip hazards and bite when disturbed. Don't stand in their lines, and check your boots and packs.
- **Snakes** and unknown amphibians: never put a hand where you can't see. Check ledges and roots before grabbing. Don't touch frogs or salamanders. Skin toxins, and the fact that you'd hurt them with the salts on your hands, are both good reasons.
- **Insects:** repellent, and a check for ticks and leeches at every break and at the end.
- **The spotted cat** is lean, low, and elusive, and will avoid you. Its hunting time is dusk on the ledges. If you meet it, stay calm, make yourself large, and back away without running.
- **The heron** stands so still moss grows on it. Don't approach.
- **Swiftlets and moths:** keep away from nests and clusters. Disturbance is the harm you can do here.

**Food:** minimize it. Eat before descending or at the rim, and carry out every wrapper. Anything smelling of sugar will bring ants.

---

## 7. Respecting the place

- **Nothing here lingers** except the human traces, and those are fragile. Don't touch the **ochre handprints** at all (skin oils, moisture, and rubbing destroy them). Don't climb on the **stone ring** or disturb the fig splitting it. Don't collect potsherds, and don't move the ones the ants are carrying. Photograph and go.
- **Don't harvest or handle the lantern moss.** Nobody knows what it is or what it does. It may be toxic or fragile, or it may be part of something that matters.
- Stay on a single path where you can, so you don't crush the seedbed and the litter layer.
- Carry out all waste, including human waste. Leave no light, sound, or scent you don't need.

---

## 8. Timing, and the dusk decision

The sink has a daily rhythm you should plan around:

| Time | What happens |
|---|---|
| Morning | Coolest and safest. Best for descent. |
| Midday | Shafts of light through the mist. Beautiful, and good for photos and orientation. |
| Mid-afternoon | The internal rain. Slick surfaces, cooling, wet gear. |
| Dusk | The lantern moss and the frog flashes begin. Waves cross from east to west. |

You'll want to see the dusk display. It's the reason to come. But the hour is also when the cat hunts, when you're tired, when light fails, and when the climb is hardest. Choose deliberately:

- **Option A (recommended):** Descend in the morning, spend the day, and get a stable camp or observation position on a secure ledge or the floor. Watch the display, and **climb out the next morning** rather than in the dark. Bring a tent-like shelter or bivy that handles condensation.
- **Option B:** Watch dusk from the rim's edge or the upper stair, where you can see part of it, and don't go down.
- **Avoid** climbing at night when fatigued.

For the display itself, **use only red or very dim light, or none at all.** White light will interrupt the frogs and moths, and quite possibly the moss. Sit still, wait for your eyes to adapt, and let it go on without you.

---

## 9. Multiverse-specific prudence

- **Anchor your return.** Know exactly where and how you'll get back to your point of departure, and don't rely on a single route. Test your return mechanism before you go down into a hole with a 140-foot climb on the way back.
- **Biological caution.** The organisms here are unfamiliar and may not be compatible with your immune system or with your home world. Be current on your vaccinations, especially tetanus, and plan a **decontamination and quarantine period** afterward. Clean gear, boots, and clothing thoroughly before crossing back, both to protect your world and this one from cross-contamination by seeds, spores, or eggs.
- **No local help.** Assume no rescue is available. The only humans here left handprints long ago.
- **Language and law:** none needed. The place appears uninhabited, but stay alert for signs that has changed.

---

## 10. State of mind

The sink is gentle, and that's the danger. It's warm, green, soft, and quiet. It will make you want to lean, drift, and stay. It will also, as you stand in it, give you the sense that everything in it has stopped to listen to you.

Take that feeling seriously and be a good listener in return. Move slowly, speak little, look more than you touch. Keep track of your water, your time, and your body. You'll be sweating, dripping, and half-hypnotized, and the fatigue and heat will creep up on you while the place feels like a lullaby.

Come down in the morning with more water than you think you need, watch the light change, watch the rain begin inside the trees, and let the frogs light up at dusk without interrupting them. Then climb out, rested and unhurried, leaving nothing but ripples on the pool.
