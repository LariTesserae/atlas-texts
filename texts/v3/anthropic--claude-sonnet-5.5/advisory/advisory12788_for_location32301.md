# Preparing to Visit the Casting Terraces of Upper Wenlo

## The short version

Wenlo is not dangerous in a dramatic way. It is a wet, warm, high-altitude construction site that is also a nursery and, quite possibly, an instrument for something. Bring good traction, waterproofs, and gloves. Acclimatize before you arrive. Walk lightly and ask before you touch. Keep your own way home in mind, because the place seems to be building one of its own.

## Before you arrive: the altitude

At about 3,500 meters, the air holds roughly two-thirds of the oxygen you're used to. Most ordinary people feel this as headache, poor sleep, breathlessness on stairs, and a strange reluctance to eat. The terraces are a lot of stairs.

- **Spend two or three days at 2,000-2,500 meters first**, if your route allows it. If you can only arrive directly, plan to do almost nothing on the first day.
- Drink more than seems reasonable. Warm, humid air hides how much you are losing.
- Avoid alcohol the first night. Eat carbohydrates even if you don't want them.
- If you use altitude medication, sort that out with a doctor from your own world beforehand. Know the signs of real trouble: a headache that won't shift, confusion, clumsiness, a wet cough. The answer to all of them is to go down, not to wait and see.
- Because the air is warm, you may feel fine and overexert. Don't trust the climate to tell you how hard you're working.

## What to wear

The mental model is a tropical greenhouse built on a mountain, with a construction site inside it.

**Feet.** This is your most important choice. Every surface is wet, mossy, or freshly poured. Bring boots with deep lugs and soft, grippy rubber, ankle-high, waterproof, and broken in. Smooth-soled shoes on wet cast lifts with moss over them are how people end up in the sump. Bring a second pair of dry socks for each day, since nothing dries in an atmosphere that is always nearly raining.

**Body.** Light, quick-drying layers, with a proper waterproof shell that vents well. You will sweat under it and get damp from outside anyway, so choose fabrics that stay warm when wet. Synthetics or wool, not cotton. Bring one warmer layer for evenings and for the thin cold of the surrounding range if you leave the bowl.

**Hands.** Bring gloves you don't mind ruining. Fresh cast material of this kind is likely alkaline, like any cementitious mix, and it can cause chemical burns and dermatitis with prolonged contact, especially on wet skin. The organic component may add its own irritants. Nitrile gloves under work gloves are a good combination. If you get wet mix on you, wash it off promptly with plenty of clean water and don't just wipe it.

**Eyes.** Bring clear safety glasses. The pour-hopper swings overhead, steam vents without warning, and dripping alkaline water is worse than ordinary dripping water.

**Head.** A brimmed hat or hood. The drips come from everywhere, and the high-altitude sun, when the cloud clears, is fierce even through the green canopy.

## What to carry

- **Your own drinking water and a filter or treatment method as backup.** The cisterns and sump collect runoff from fresh binder, weeping pipes, and moss. Don't assume any of it is potable, and don't assume it is not. Ask your hosts, and accept tea if it is offered.
- **Dry bags** for anything electronic, paper, or medical. Condensation will find everything.
- **A small towel or two**, and a cloth to wipe lenses.
- **A headlamp with a red mode.** Dusk is when the frogs begin, and it's kinder not to flood the bromeliads with white light. Paths near the wiring are easier to read with your own light.
- **A notebook and pencil.** Pencil works on wet paper. You will want to write things down.
- **A basic first aid kit** with burn dressings, saline eyewash, blister care, and electrolytes.
- **Boot brushes and a small tub of clean water.** See "Biosecurity" below.
- **A modest gift.** Good tea, dried fruit, or something for the crews. The place runs on people who work slowly and patiently, and a small courtesy goes a long way.

## Arriving well

Come in the morning if you can, before the sluices open. Steam bleeds off the terraces at dawn in long white banners, and it is one of the most beautiful things there. Then the crew opens the valves, and for an hour every surface carries a thin bright sheet of running water. That hour is lovely to watch and a bad time to walk. Find a dry ledge or a gallery and stay there. You may be asked to. Do as you're told, and immediately.

Afternoons bring cloud up the valley, dampness, and reduced visibility. Dusk brings frogs, the wire glow, and cooler air. Plan your hardest movement for mid-morning, after the flush and before the cloud.

## Dangers, in the order you're likely to meet them

**1. Falling.** Wet moss over cast edges, forearm-height lifts that catch the toe, open bolt-holes, and low light under the canopy. Move slowly. Watch the lifts as you step down. Keep three points of contact where the terraces steepen. The sump at the bottom is deep enough to matter, and the water is cold at that altitude even if the air isn't.

**2. Steam and hot surfaces.** The conduits are ceramic, iron, and copper, and they carry geothermal steam. Lagging protects you unless it's damaged or overgrown. Valves vent with a long hiss and no warning. Don't lean on pipes, don't put your face near joints, and never stand in front of a valve, especially near the hand-wheels. Fresh pours give off heat, and areas still glossy with curing may be hot enough to hurt.

**3. Chemical burns.** Covered above. Wet skin, wet cement, and time are a bad combination. Kneeling in the fresh material is the classic error.

**4. Moving machinery.** The gantry moves at walking pace, which makes it easy to ignore. It swings a heavy hopper over the terraces and can drop material without much warning. Stay out from under it and out of the reach of its track. Someone on the crew is always watching it. Learn to hear its tick.

**5. Electricity.** Overhead cables carry enough charge to produce corona light at dusk. The blue-white beads are beautiful, but don't reach up toward them, don't hold metal poles or umbrellas near them, and keep away from any wire that sparks or crackles. Damp air and conductive surfaces make small hazards larger.

**6. The bear.** A spectacled bear comes over the rim at night to drink. It is shy, and everyone pretends not to have noticed. Follow their lead. Keep food sealed, don't leave it in your tent or pockets at night, don't approach it, and don't block its route to the water. If you meet it, give it space and quiet. This isn't a place for flash photography.

**7. Fatigue and cold.** Warmth is deceptive. Hypothermia is unlikely inside the bowl and quite possible outside it if you wander onto the bare ridge in cloud.

## Manners: this is harder than the safety

**Don't step on the newest pours.** Workers walk lightly there and talk about them the way you'd talk about sleeping children. This is not decoration. If a section is dark, glossy, warm, and roped off or not, stay off it. If you feel the ground giving under you in that slow, settling way, stop, step back onto older surface, and don't announce it or make a fuss. Yes, it may feel like the stone is breathing. It's probably best not to press a palm against it just to check, however much you want to.

**Don't crush the skin.** The moss, ferns, and epiphytes are structural, and their planting order is scheduled. A bare terrace that looks embarrassed is waiting for its turn. Don't pick anything, don't sit in the moss, and don't move a bromeliad to see the frog. If a vine has found a strut, leave it for the pruning crew.

**Ask before you touch or photograph.** Most crews probably won't mind cameras, but some parts of the work, and some workers, may. Ask, and take no for an answer without curiosity or argument.

**Don't press about the mountain.** The bowl is casting a scale model of a peak that no one will name. Some say it was lost. Some say it is being prepared for. You are a guest at what may be a memorial, a rehearsal, or a mechanism, and possibly all three. Look, listen, and let people offer what they choose to. If someone tells you something, don't repeat it carelessly to others. Do not walk across the lower lifts as though they were a map you were free to correct.

## Biosecurity

The skin is seeded in sequence, terrace by terrace, species by species. A stray spore, seed, or fungus from elsewhere could disrupt years of careful work, and a hitchhiking insect could be worse. Before you enter:

- Scrub your boots, especially the treads, and clean gaiters, bag bottoms, and trouser cuffs.
- Wash or discard anything carrying soil, plant matter, or pollen. Check pockets and the Velcro on your gear.
- Do not bring live plants, seeds, fresh produce, or pets.
- When you leave, do the same in reverse. You may carry away a millipede, a spore, or a bit of the cast surface, and the place you visit next has its own skin.

## The part specific to a multiverse traveler

Most places you visit are their own destination. This one may be a waypoint, and possibly a door under construction.

The workers say that when the model is finished, the sump will be drained into a channel that runs straight through the rock toward somewhere else. Treat that as a real schedule, even if you can't learn the date. It affects you in three ways.

- **Don't rely on the place as your exit.** Whatever is being built, it wasn't made for you, and it isn't finished. Arrive with your own anchor and your own way home, and confirm it works before you commit to staying. If your return relies on a fixed beacon, test it at the rim, away from the conduits and cables that may interfere.
- **Stay out of the channel.** If there is a rock-cut channel, a gate, or a drain, don't enter it, drop anything into it, or lean over it. If the draining begins while you're there, move to high ground, keep your group together, and follow the crew. You will not want to be the one who finds out where it goes by accident.
- **Notice how it feels to you.** The slowness, the settling, the warmth may be more than a curing process. Some travelers feel time-thickened or lulled here, and it would be easy to lose an afternoon watching drips organize themselves around the frogs. Set an alarm, agree on a check-in with a companion, and keep track of days. If your watch drifts, your sleep changes, or you find yourself unwilling to leave, go down the mountain and take the drift as data.

## Health and small comforts

- Wear a light mask if you are prone to respiratory trouble. Mold spores, steam, and cured binder dust are all plausible irritants.
- Keep your feet dry when you can. Wet skin softens, blisters, and gets infected fast in a warm, wet place. Change socks, air your boots, and powder your feet at night.
- Insects are few, but springtails and snails will find your gear. Zip everything.
- Eat warm food when it's offered. It helps with altitude, morale, and relations.
- Sleep with your head slightly raised. Altitude is easier that way, and so is the night noise of thousands of drips, with the low turbine hum you'll feel in your sternum before you hear it.

## How to be there

The best preparation is patience. Wenlo isn't a place that rewards hurrying. The gantry stops, pours, waits, and moves on, and you will do best if you match it. Walk slowly and watch the water. Let the morning flush run past you in its own time and listen as the frogs start at dusk. Look at the raw edges, the plank grain, the bolt-holes, and the first thin rills in the cast, all of it at the very start of being weathered, and remember that you are seeing something that will only look this way once.

Leave it as you found it: warm, wet, quiet, and unfinished.
