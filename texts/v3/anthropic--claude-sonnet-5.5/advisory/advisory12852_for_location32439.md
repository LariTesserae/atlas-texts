# Preparing for the Shelf of Ossel

## The right frame of mind

The Shelf will not hurt you the way a desert or a glacier will. It is warm, wet, thick-aired, and full of people. The dangers are quiet ones: slick footing, bad air in low places, unfamiliar microbes, a diet with no plants in it, and the slow attrition of noise, damp, and sameness. Prepare for a long, damp, loud, gently corrosive stay in a place that has been running itself for millennia and has no reason to hurry for you.

Pack for damp, wear-and-tear, and patience, not for cold or thin air.

## Arrival and pacing

Come in by the eastern road, climbing out of the lowland haze, and do not try to reach the interior on the first day. Spend a day or two at the rim, watching the cloud-bank and letting your body adjust to the humidity, the mineral water, and the food. The city runs for days in every direction with no clean edges, so it is much easier to learn its habits at the margin than to be dropped into the middle.

Hire a local guide for at least your first week. The streets close and reopen on the crews' rotation, so any map you carry is a snapshot. A guide knows which stairs are being chipped free, which cellars are unsafe, and which districts are in the phase of the cycle you want.

## Footwear and clothing

**Boots are your most important item.** Every surface is wet and filmed with living color, and much of it is polished travertine or dished iron. Bring waterproof boots with deep, aggressive lugs and good ankle support. Add a collapsible walking pole for the soft crust of the low ground between terraces, which gives way and floods with cold water, and for the worn steps whose hollows hold water and hide their edges. Gaiters are worth having.

**Clothing should be warm-weather, quick-drying, and stain-tolerant.**
- A breathable waterproof shell for the afternoon rain, which is warm and fine, more like walking through a cloud than a storm.
- Synthetic or wool layers. Avoid cotton, which never dries here.
- A brimmed hat, and a scarf or neck gaiter that can double as a dust filter.
- Dark or earth-toned clothes. Iron-eating microbes and rust will stain anything pale orange. Don't wear white.
- Several spare pairs of socks, changed twice daily. Immersion foot and fungal skin trouble are the commonest complaints among visitors to wet worlds.

Sunglasses matter more than you'd expect, since the noon light off pale stone is hard and white, even though the afternoons are soft and amber.

## Air, gas, and dust

Sulfur-loving microbes and warm deep wells mean volcanic and geothermal gases. Low, enclosed places such as cellars, cisterns, pump-house sumps, and stairwells below street level can collect hydrogen sulfide or heavy carbon dioxide. Bring a small personal gas monitor with an audible alarm, and treat it as required. Don't sleep below street level. If a local tells you a cellar "breathes," believe them.

The chipping crews raise fine lime dust. Fungus cultures and the roosts of the fliers add spores and guano dust. Carry a good respirator or fitted N95-class masks and wear one near active chipping, in pen districts, and under cornices at dusk. Goggles help when the fliers swarm.

Steam mains run through the city like veins. Do not lean on, touch, or sit against pipes, and step around vents. A burn in a humid climate is slow to heal.

## Water and food

**Water.** The ground is wet, but do not drink from it. Surface seeps and gutters carry mineral loads, microbes, and the leavings of a million animals. Buy your water from the water-sellers, who sell deep-seep water. It is a quiet courtesy as much as a precaution, since the practice is a point of local pride. Even this water is hard, so bring:
- A good filter or purification tablets for backup.
- Electrolyte packets.
- A plan to drink plenty, since the hard water can favor kidney stones on long stays.

**Food.** The local diet is cultured fungus, eel, snail, and the flesh of the hairless grazers. Nothing green grows here, so fresh plant food is essentially absent. For a stay of more than a couple of weeks:
- Bring vitamin C and a full multivitamin, plus a fiber supplement.
- Introduce new foods slowly. Unfamiliar fungi and shellfish-like animals can upset a stomach or trigger allergies you didn't know you had.
- Eat what locals eat, cooked thoroughly. Mat-snails and salamander-fed drains suggest parasites, and you should avoid anything raw or half-cooked.
- Bring some familiar, sealed, shelf-stable food for the days when you cannot face another fungal stew.

## Animals

The fauna is the infrastructure, and you should behave accordingly.

- **Do not shoo, scrape, or kill the mat-snails** on walls, and do not block drain-slots or nesting-niches. Without their grazing, the crust would smother the stone. Doing damage here is closer to vandalism than to pest control.
- **Do not handle gulpers barehanded.** Amphibian skin is often chemically active. Watch them; don't collect them.
- **Stay out from under cornices at dusk**, when the gliders pour out in roaring clouds. Wear a hat and a mask.
- **Read the animals as locals do.** Silent gulpers mean the rain is about to stop, and low-flying gliders mean a heavy night. You can save yourself a soaking by paying attention.
- Wash your hands often, and check your boot treads for snails and eggs before you leave any district.

Ask before eating any animal that isn't served to you. Some are food, some are maintenance, and some are both.

## Sound and sleep

The city never goes quiet, and at night it merely drops a register and slows. The beam engines are felt in the chest before they are heard, the gulpers cluck in the drains from March to November, the pens bawl, and the stone ticks.

- Bring high-quality earplugs and a white-noise device, though you may find the deep pulse of the engines comforting once you stop fighting it.
- Ear defenders are wise near hammers, whistles, and turbine halls.
- Ask for lodging on an upper floor, in a thick-walled building, away from the drain outlets if you want sleep. Or choose one near them if you like the chorus.
- Bring a sleeping liner. Bedding in wet places holds moisture, and you will want something of your own between you and it.
- If you visit between December and February, expect the gulpers to be quieter, which some travelers find restful and others find eerie.

## Gear care

Everything you bring will be damp, and much of it will be coated in mineral within days.

- Keep electronics in dry bags with desiccant, and use lens cloths and silica for optics, since fogging is constant.
- Rinse zippers, buckles, and mechanisms regularly, because calcium crust locks them.
- Carry light oil for metal parts, since iron rusts in hours.
- Pack a small chisel or scraper for your own use, though not for the walls.

**Your return anchor, portal key, or beacon is the most important thing to protect.** Whatever is left standing long enough gets slowly wrapped. Do not leave an anchor in a doorway, on a ledge, or beside a seep. Keep it on your person in a waterproof case, and if you must set it down, check it daily for crust. A key that's been sealed in flowstone for a season is a key you will need a crew of hammers to retrieve.

## Health kit

Wound care matters more than usual, because cuts in a wet, microbe-rich place go bad. Bring:
- Antiseptic, sterile dressings, and waterproof tape.
- A broad-spectrum antibiotic and an antiparasitic (with medical advice beforehand).
- Antifungal powder and cream.
- Antihistamines and oral rehydration salts.
- Any prescription medications, doubled and split between bags.

Travertine and iron crust have sharp edges. Wear gloves for handling stone, and clean scrapes immediately.

## Customs and conduct

- **Do not bring plants, seeds, or anything that could germinate.** The Shelf's whole ecology, and the city built around it, assumes an absence of plants. A single introduced weed could break the balance. Check your boots, pockets, and packs for seeds, spores, and burrs before you enter. Bring no potted comforts.
- Do not chip souvenirs from the flowstone or the walls. The crews chip for a purpose, and a stranger with a hammer will not be welcome.
- Do not scrape or mark the pale line on the stone. It is how people measure their years.
- Buy water from the sellers, even though you are standing on it.
- Slow down. Nothing here happens fast, and visitors who push for speed get nowhere and irritate people. An afternoon spent waiting out the rain under an arch is a normal afternoon.
- Bring trade goods of durable, non-organic kind, such as good steel tools, needles, small hardware, and clear glass, rather than currency the locals cannot use. Be ready to be told what things are worth and to agree.

## Leaving

Before you depart, wash and inspect all your gear. Scrub the treads of your boots, shake out your clothing, and check for snails, eggs, and microbial film. Do not carry the Shelf's organisms back into other worlds, where they could thrive on whatever passes for a wall.

## What to expect emotionally

The sense of age is strong. You will find yourself in stairwells worn into cups by eight hundred years of feet, under machinery that has been swapped part by part until it is no longer any single thing. The absence of green is subtle but real. Some travelers report a craving for it after a week. A piece of green cloth or a photograph, though never a living plant, can help.

The Shelf is not a place you conquer. It is a place you slow down into. If you come with dry socks, clean water, clear lungs, respect for the small animals on the walls, and no need to be anywhere quickly, it can be one of the most quietly extraordinary places you will ever stand.
