# Preparing for the Sump Fields of Karrow Low

## The Right Frame of Mind

This is an industrial site, not a ruin, a wilderness, or a tourist destination. It is new, operating, and staffed by people who don't explain themselves. Prepare as you would to visit a working dam or sewage works in a country whose language you don't speak. Assume everything is load-bearing, everything is running on a schedule, and nobody is obliged to warn you.

The place has an ominous edge, but ordinary hazards will hurt you long before the strange ones do: slick steel, scalding steam, caustic water, drowning, and exhaustion. Deal with those first and the rest becomes manageable.

## Before You Go Down the Stair

**The stair is a commitment.** It descends far enough that the handrail equilibrates to ambient temperature, so plan for a long descent and a longer climb back. Bring knee-friendly footwear and don't arrive already tired. Count the flights or note landings on the way down. Drop a small chalk mark or a strip of reflective tape at the top, and another at the bottom landing, so you can confirm you're on the right stair when you return. If you use a multiverse anchor or a return beacon, test it at the landing before going further. Deep concrete and steel may attenuate it.

**Tell someone your schedule.** If you have a partner in another world, give them a hard return time with a wide margin.

## Clothing and Body Protection

- **Layers for cold and wet.** It is cold but not arctic, with constant humidity, dripping, and steam. Wear a moisture-wicking base layer, a wool or synthetic mid-layer, and a fully waterproof shell with a hood. Rain here falls in columns, not evenly, so you will pass in and out of drenching lanes, and a hood you can cinch matters.
- **Waterproof trousers**, not just a jacket, since you'll be wading through sheet water and brushing wet metal.
- **Boots:** waterproof, ankle-high, with aggressive lugged rubber soles. The grated walkways ring underfoot, so treat them as an acoustic problem as well as a footing problem. Wet steel and wet young concrete are both slick. Add steel or composite toe protection if you can, since tools, tie-rod stubs, and bollards are everywhere.
- **Gloves:** nitrile-coated or waterproof work gloves. Cut tie-rods leave bright steel discs, and edges, hasps, and grating can be sharp.
- **Eye protection.** Safety glasses or goggles are important, for a reason covered below.
- **A hard hat.** Condensate drops are the mild version of the overhead hazard. Cranes run on rails and carry loads you cannot see, and brittle lime stalactites can fall. A hat with a chin strap and a headlamp mount is ideal.
- **Change of clothes** sealed in a dry bag, left at the top or carried in your pack. Hypothermia is a real risk if you get soaked and then sit still.

## Light

The Field is lit in amber pools with dark between them, and blue-white work lights along the east wall. Sodium light flattens color, so you won't be able to judge stains, rust, or water depth by hue, and the boundaries between light zones will look bruised and confusing.

- Carry **two independent headlamps** plus a handheld torch, with spare batteries kept warm inside your clothing. Cold drains cells.
- Choose a **neutral or warm white** beam. Very bright cold-white light will ruin your night vision and may draw attention from the east wall's operation.
- Bring a few **chemical light sticks** and reflective tape to mark your route. Use them sparingly and remove them when you leave.
- Expect the roof slot's gray strip to tell you nothing about time of day. Bring a watch, preferably mechanical or with a long battery life, and don't trust your body clock.

## Sound

The Field is loud in a layered, low-frequency way, and the pumps' beat frequencies drift in and out of phase. That is tiring and can cause headaches or disorientation over hours.

- Wear **hearing protection**, but choose **filtered or adjustable earplugs** rather than full isolation. You need to hear the **hourly bell**, which signals the sluices and flooding. Test your plugs in advance so the bell's flat clang remains audible.
- You cannot cross the grating quietly, so don't try. Announce yourself by simply being heard, and assume you always are.
- The deep note at the central cistern is felt in the sternum more than heard. Infrasound can cause unease, nausea, blurred vision, or a sense of presence, so limit your time near it. If you feel dread with no obvious cause, it is probably physiological and it is a signal to leave, not to push through.

## Air and Water Hazards

**Air:** it is humid and steam-laden, and young concrete carries fine alkaline dust. Bring a **half-face respirator with P100 filters**, or at least an FFP3 mask, for dry areas near cutting, cast surfaces, and the sheds. Ventilation seems adequate for the workers, but you don't know the local limits, and you can't smell your way to safety in a place this large.

**Water is not innocent.** The clear, fast, black water in the canals has been in contact with fresh cement, and leachate from new concrete is strongly alkaline, capable of irritating skin and seriously damaging eyes. That's what the pale lime bloom and brittle stalactites tell you. So:

- Don't touch, splash in, or drink the water. Don't lick a stalactite or handle it barehanded.
- If you get it on skin, rinse with clean water promptly. Carry a **small eyewash bottle** and a liter of clean water for this alone.
- The canal has strong flow toward an intake. A person who falls in will not be able to climb a vertical wall against the current, and the terminus is a shaft of unknown depth. Stay on the grating, away from canal edges, and never lean over weirs.
- **Steam relief valves** vent without warning. Keep clear of the lagged mains and watch for white plumes. Scalding is a much likelier injury than anything supernatural.
- **Be out of low places when the bell rings.** For the next quarter hour, sluices open and terraces reflood or drain. Before you begin your day, orient yourself to the nearest raised route on your terrace. Ideally, be somewhere elevated at the top of every hour, and plan long moves for the quiet stretch after the flush.

## Food, Water, and Supplies

- Bring **all your own food and drinking water**. Do not eat from the cress and lettuce trays under the work light. They belong to someone, and taking food from an unattended garden is both rude and risky. The plants are fed by a system whose chemistry you don't know.
- Do not eat the eels, the moss, or the ivory ferns.
- Carry an **emergency blanket**, a basic first-aid kit with burn dressings, and a whistle. Sound carries oddly here, but a whistle's pitch is distinct from the machinery.
- Bring a **waterproof map case**, a grease pencil, and a compass or dead-reckoning aid. GPS won't function, and the Field is eight kilometers long with few landmarks beyond lamp-towers and pump-chapels. Sketch terraces as you descend. Going down is easy. Coming back means climbing every slab.

## Wildlife and the Unseen

- The pale birds are near-blind. Don't shine bright lights at them or startle them off the lit lanes.
- Eels will turn toward your lamp. Look, but don't provoke them.
- Something larger drags a wet line at the edge of hearing and is never where the sound suggests. Don't chase it, and don't shout at it. Keep to the lit lanes and marked walkways, keep your back to a wall when you stop, and treat the sound as a reason to keep moving, not a reason to investigate.

## Etiquette and Local Customs

You will probably meet workers, and they may not want conversation.

- Be polite, brief, and unobtrusive. Don't open padlocked sheds, tamper with valves, or climb the conveyor gallery.
- Do not photograph faces or the east-wall operation.
- **The tiled pit:** treat it as a genuine taboo. Do not throw anything into it, touch the water, or even test it with grit. Workers step around it and won't say why, and their silence is the only warning you'll get. Follow their example. Give it wide berth, don't linger, and don't let a curious companion experiment.
- The **tall stair to nothing** ends at lamp height. Don't climb it. Its purpose isn't known to you, and anything on it will be exposed and unrailed.

## The Center

The ring road around the cistern gives the best view of the Field, and you may want it. Go only if you are rested, have adequate time before the next bell, and have someone who knows your route. Stay on the iron plate. Keep away from the edge, especially near the turning mound of water at the intake. Do not drop anything in. Do not stay long enough to become accustomed to the note. The shaft goes down into construction that is still under way, and you should assume you are not the intended audience.

## Leaving

Ascend before you are tired, not after. Check your marks on the stair, and keep to your schedule. If you hear the bell while climbing, that's fine. It only matters if you are low.

When you emerge, strip off wet gear, rinse your hands and face, and check for chemical burns you may not have felt in the cold. Alkaline burns can develop slowly and painlessly. Then drink something warm and let your ears and sternum settle before you go anywhere else.

## Quick Kit Summary

Waterproof layers and hood, lugged waterproof boots, gloves, safety glasses, hard hat, two headlamps and a torch, filtered earplugs, P100 respirator, eyewash, clean water, food, emergency blanket, first aid with burn care, whistle, watch, waterproof notes and map case, marker tape, and a firm return time.

Nothing here has had time to fall down yet. Your goal is not to be the first thing that does.
