# Preparing for the Tally Flats

## The right expectations

The Tally Flats have no predators, storms, hostile natives, or dramatic terrain. Travelers get into trouble here through **slow, cumulative, and bureaucratic** hazards: small errors that a perfectly balanced system quietly absorbs, and that absorb you along with them. Prepare for a place that is warm, gentle, soaking wet, endlessly identical, and completely indifferent to whether you leave.

The ecosystem is a closed accounting system. Your job is to be a **net-zero visitor**: bring in nothing that stays, take out nothing that was part of the balance, and don't stand still long enough to be entered into the books.

---

## 1. Arrival and the return anchor

**Losing your way back is the most serious risk.** Every terrace looks like the last: pale stone, still water, haze. There are no hills, no shadows, no horizon, and the sun is a heatless white disk behind a ceiling of steam. Compasses may or may not work depending on your entry point. Assume they don't.

- **Fix your arrival point to a structure, not the ground.** Anything you place on the basin floor will be cemented over, grazed, or healed away. Anchor to the numbered legs of a gantry, to a specific sluice-wheel, or to the position of a rain curtain. Photograph the spot and record the terrace count from the nearest wheel.
- **Use the wheels as a ruler.** A sluice-wheel stands at the lip of every fourth terrace. Count terraces and wheels as you descend. Keep a written tally, since the place invites one. If your route is a straight line down the staircase, you can climb back up it. Do not wander laterally without recording it.
- **Learn the rain.** The rain falls in the same places at the same intervals, so within a day it works as both a clock and a map. Sketch it on arrival: which terraces are rained on, in what order, how far the curtains drift. It is the only landmark that carries information.
- **Learn the gantries.** They run on rails on a schedule so regular it looks like stillness. Time a few of them. They also give you a rough sense of "when" in a place with no day, night, or weather.
- **Bring a powered locator beacon** tied to your entry point, mounted high on a gantry pier or a wall and not on the floor. Test whether it works at all. If your travel method allows a recall tether or pull-back, use it and check it before you go.
- **Carry redundant means of counting.** A pencil-and-paper log will do. Electronics are threatened by the steam.

---

## 2. Clothing and footwear

The air is around 30 °C, damp, and windless, and you will be wet to the ankle for as long as you are on the terraces.

- **Lightweight, breathable, quick-dry clothing.** Long sleeves and trousers, since you will brush wet walls, rails, and lips. Avoid heavy cloth that holds water.
- **Footwear is the critical decision.** Waders would turn into steam baths, and bare feet are not advisable. Choose **ankle-high or mid-calf waterproof boots with a smooth, non-aggressive sole**, ideally rubber, with a low tread that won't grind the combs or trap them. Bring **spare socks, many pairs**, sealed in waterproof bags. Constant warm immersion is a recipe for maceration and fungal infection, so plan to dry your feet regularly.
- **Gloves, thin but cut-resistant.** The lips of the weirs are sharp enough to read a thumbprint. A careless hand on a fresh edge will open you up.
- **A broad-brimmed hat.** The light is shadowless and cool-feeling, but that doesn't mean the radiation is harmless, and the rain is warm and constant somewhere nearby.
- **A light rain shell or poncho** for the curtains. They are warm, so you won't be cold, but you may want to keep your gear dry.

---

## 3. Bodies, wounds, and contamination

This is the subtle part.

- **Combs converge on damage.** They crowd toward chips and scuffs to seal them. This is a behavior tuned to stone, but it may not distinguish between a chipped weir and an open cut. **Do not enter with an unhealed wound**, and dress any scrape with a waterproof, sealed covering. Carry a full first-aid kit including antiseptic, waterproof dressings, and coagulants. If you bleed, get out of the water, clean and seal the wound, and check the ground where it dripped.
- **Do not sit or kneel in the water.** Combs lay glassy cement at their bases continuously, and a still object will be encased. Boots left planted can be sealed in over a period of hours. Shift your feet regularly.
- **Decontaminate before entry.** You are an alien biosphere. Wash boots, packs, and skin with agents that leave no residue and cause no harm. Spores, bacteria, or even sweat salts could unbalance a system this tightly tuned. What you bring in, they will process, and you cannot predict how.
- **Decontaminate after leaving.** The mats smell of warm pennies and form the base of a foodweb that has never met your immune system. Spores, microbial films, or tiny beads may travel home on you. Quarantine your gear, and yourself, for a few days.
- **Respirator or good mask.** The steam is thick, and the mats and beads may shed fine particulates. A filtering mask is cheap insurance, particularly around the sluice-houses and pipe mouths.
- **Pre-emptive medical prep.** Take up-to-date standard vaccinations and carry antifungals and antihistamines. Do not drink or wash wounds in the water.

---

## 4. Water, food, and waste

**The water is not safe.** It has passed through every animal between the head of the staircase and you, so it is warm, worked, mineral-loaded, and biologically dense. Do not drink it, cook with it, or use it to rinse cuts.

- **Carry all your water**, at more than you think you need. Warmth and humidity mean you will sweat heavily even at rest, and electrolytes matter.
- **Carry all your food, sealed**, and eat it in the dry (see camps, below). Crumbs, wrappers, and spills would feed a system that has no slack in its budget.
- **Carry out everything, including your waste.** Bring sealed containers for all solid and liquid waste. Anything left behind will be metabolized by animals that are not tuned to it. It is unclear whether that would harm them, and you have no right to find out.

---

## 5. Dry ground and rest

Nothing here is ground, and very little is dry.

- **Plan short trips**, ideally day-length, with your return anchor within reach. If you must stay overnight, sleep on your own **elevated, lightweight platform, or in a hammock slung from a gantry pier or a pipe**. Don't assume a gantry deck is free, since the roosts stand shoulder to shoulder along every rail and may not move for you.
- **Sluice-houses** may offer dry floors, but they are also the machinery's territory, with moving parts in grease-thick housings, and the threaders pour through the culverts nearby.
- **Move your platform regularly** if it touches stone. Anything stationary will be sealed in.
- **Stay off active machinery.** Wheels turn at a constant, slow rate and carry great force. Gantries traverse rails on a schedule that will not adjust for you. Hoists, spray-arms, and sorting-combs can act without warning, and conveyors run to blank walls. Never stand under a gantry arm or on its rail.

---

## 6. Moving through the ecosystem

You will be surrounded by life, at a density you have probably never experienced.

- **Nothing is afraid of you, and nothing is hostile, but everything is in the way.** Combs stand forty to fifty to a square meter, and every step crushes some. They heal, but treat the stone as an inhabited surface and not a floor. Where possible, walk in **existing channels, along the dry rims and walkways** (if there are any), or in the gaps that the tenders leave. Shuffle your feet gently and do not stamp.
- **Do not lean on or grip the walls.** The beads are a soft, greasy quilt. Damaging them harms the animals and also leaves you with slime and possible abrasions.
- **Do not grab rails.** The roosts sit on them. They are silent, they stand for hours, and there is no reason to test what happens if you disturb them.
- **Avoid culverts and pipe mouths.** The threaders pour through them in braids, hundreds abreast. Stepping in means stepping into their current, and you may be knocked down or tangled.
- **Watch for slick films.** The microbial mats (rust-orange, green-black, violet) are slippery and grow on any wet surface not recently grazed. Walk deliberately, and don't run.
- **Handle the weir lips carefully.** A drop of a hand's height is trivial, but a lip is a crisp cast edge at the point where water goes over in a glassy sheet. Don't step across it casually or catch your foot.

---

## 7. Collecting samples and souvenirs

**Take nothing.** Every comb, tender, and strip of mat is a load-bearing part of the arithmetic. Removing them may be harmless, since the system heals, but you cannot be sure, and you have no way to compensate. A photograph, a written record, and a sound recording are better keepsakes, and cheaper for everyone.

If you carry instruments for research, choose passive ones that do not need contact, and avoid releasing dyes, tracers, or chemicals.

---

## 8. Mental preparation

The Tally Flats do not threaten. They **absorb**. The most likely damage is psychological.

- **Monotony and disorientation.** Nothing changes, and days can blur. The sun is a heatless disk and the sky a permanent ceiling. Keep a schedule, log your hours, and eat and rest at set times. Talk to your companions.
- **Crowded surfaces.** If you are prone to trypophobia or unease around tightly packed patterns, the rows of combs, the quilted beads, and the braided threaders can be very unpleasant. The ticking "rain on paper" sound comes from everywhere. Prepare accordingly.
- **The feeling of being watched.** The roosts don't move or make a sound, and they line every rail. They aren't watching you in any real sense, but it may feel that way.
- **Deep quiet.** The place is quiet with texture: silk-tearing water, a pulse in your soles, dry ticks. Bring good hearing protection you never need, and pay attention to that pulse. A change in the rhythm of a sluice-wheel is a real change.
- **The accounting.** The document says it best: if you stood in one place long enough, you would become part of the accounting, "a body the tenders would learn to graze around." Do not test this. Keep moving, keep your schedule, and go home.

---

## 9. Suggested kit list

**Essential**
- Written tally log, pencils, waterproof notebook
- Waterproof, sealed containers for waste
- All water and food, sealed
- Waterproof boots and many spare socks
- Cut-resistant gloves
- Quick-dry long-sleeved clothing
- Wide-brimmed hat and lightweight poncho
- First aid, antifungals, waterproof dressings
- Filtering mask
- Locator beacon, tied to an anchor above the waterline
- Elevated platform or hammock, with tie-off points
- Decontamination supplies, before and after

**Recommended**
- Rope, cord, and clips for tethering to pier legs
- A watch with a clear dial, plus a spare
- Camera and audio recorder
- Waterproof bags for everything
- A companion, ideally two

**Do not bring**
- Anything that sheds, leaks, or disperses: glitter, dyes, powders, fuel, scented products
- Anything you can't carry out
- Anything you would miss if it was quietly sealed into the floor

---

## 10. Final advice

The Tally Flats are extraordinary and completely undramatic. If you are careful, you will spend your visit walking through warm shallow water between rows of breathing tiles, under a low ceiling of steam, beside machines that turn without a sound, and surrounded by creatures that do not fear you because they have no reason to. You will see a system with no waste, no outside, and no need of you.

Count your steps, and count your terraces. Take only pictures and leave only the faintest footprints, ones that the combs will have healed by the time you are gone. Then go home, before you become a line in someone else's ledger.
