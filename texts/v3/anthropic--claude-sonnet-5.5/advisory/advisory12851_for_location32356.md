# Preparing for the Salt Stair at Thin Air

## The right mindset

Treat this as a high-altitude expedition into a stone maze, not a scenic stop. The place is quiet, slow and beautiful, and none of that makes it forgiving. Nothing here is hostile. It is simply indifferent, and the margin for error is set by your lungs, your eyes, and your patience. Plan to stay hours, not days, unless you have properly acclimatized elsewhere first.

## Air: the main danger

A violet sky, a small hard sun, and a cloud floor below you all say the same thing: you are very high, likely equivalent to 7,000 to 8,000 meters or more. Assume you are in the zone where unacclimatized humans deteriorate quickly.

- **Carry bottled oxygen** with a mask and regulator, and enough for double your planned time. Use it from the moment you arrive, not once you feel bad. Hypoxia removes your ability to notice hypoxia.
- **Bring a pulse oximeter and a barometer.** In a multiverse setting, don't trust that "thin air" means Earth-like thin air. Measure it on arrival and adjust.
- **Acclimatize beforehand** if at all possible, with days at staged altitudes in a nearby world. If not, keep your stay short and your exertion low.
- **Carry a portable pressure bag** and altitude medication (acetazolamide, dexamethasone, nifedipine) with someone who knows how to use them. The real treatment for altitude sickness is descent, so know how you would descend.
- **Move slowly.** Expect to breathe too fast, as the place itself remarks. Use a rest-step, pause often, and speak little.
- **Do not go alone.** Agree on simple checks: count backward from 100 by sevens, walk a straight line, describe the last ten minutes. Hypoxia makes stone hum like a tune and makes bad ideas feel like good ones.

## Your way out

The description tells you nothing about how to get down. Below the lip is only cloud, so:

- Establish your return anchor (portal, beacon, or whatever your method is) before you enter the maze, and confirm it works from the lip.
- Carry rope, a harness, and basic descent gear in case the only exit is a real mountain descent through a wet cloud layer.
- Scout from the lip first. Sit, watch one full cloud cycle, and only then decide.

## Cold and wet

The bowl is a freeze-thaw machine. You will be dry and hot in sunlight, then soaked in cloud, then rimed with ice, all in one afternoon.

- **Layer system:** wicking base, insulating mid, a windproof and waterproof hard shell, and a big down or synthetic parka for stops. Bring spare gloves and socks.
- **Boots:** double-insulated high-altitude mountaineering boots, with gaiters.
- **Salt in the fabric:** the rind will brush onto your clothes and pull moisture into them even when the air is dry. Salted cuffs and knees stay damp, so choose synthetics and expect to change or shake out layers.
- **Gloves:** a thin liner pair for touching stone and handling gear, and warm overmitts for everything else.
- **Traction:** small microspikes for the late-afternoon ice veins in the grooves. Use them lightly. The stone and the builders' work deserve gentleness.

## Light

The light is the hardest thing, and it comes from every direction at once.

- **Glacier goggles with side shields** (or wraparound category-4 lenses), plus a spare pair. Snow blindness comes fast when light bounces between rime, wet salt, and ice into every shadowed gap.
- Sunscreen with a high SPF everywhere, including under the nose, chin, ears, and inside the nostrils. Use a lip balm with SPF. Wear a brimmed hat or hood.
- Assume UV is far above anything you've known. Don't take the goggles off to see something better. Take a glance, then put them back.

## Salt

The salt is more than scenery.

- **Fine salt dust** irritates eyes, nose, and throat. Wear a buff or light mask over your face, and rinse your eyes with sterile saline.
- **Corrosion:** it will attack carabiners, buckles, stove parts, and zippers. Seal electronics in dry bags, wipe metal with a light oil, and carry a lens cloth and silica gel.
- **Skin:** hands will crack. Bring barrier cream and a few fingertip bandages.
- **Stone underfoot and underhand:** wet salt is slick and dry salt is abrasive. Test holds before trusting them.

## Moving in the maze

The stone is packed with gaps barely wide enough to turn sideways in. In this setting:

- **Leave the big pack at the lip.** Carry a slim daypack you can take off and push ahead of you, or haul on a short line. A large pack or a frame-mounted oxygen cylinder will jam in the gaps. Use a small cylinder in a chest carry.
- **A helmet** protects against overhangs, ice-shed from above, and the occasional slab settling. Knee and elbow padding helps.
- **Navigation:** it is hard to see more than three cairns at once, so sightlines are poor and cloud makes it worse. GPS may fail on other worlds. Use a light guide line on a reel and retrace it on the way out, leaving nothing behind. Note the flute patterns and the lean of the pegs, and keep a written route sketch.
- **Watch the salt as a clock.** When the stone darkens from bone to slate in a wave, cloud is arriving. That gives you minutes' warning. Use it to get back to a known spot or settle in.
- **Poles and axes:** keep them short and tucked. A careless swing could break a cairn.

## The daily rhythm

Two or three clouds pass each day, each lasting about half an hour.

- **During cloud:** stop moving. Visibility drops, the stone goes dark and slick, and rime begins to grow on everything windward. Stay put in a known sheltered spot, breathe, and drink.
- **When the sun returns:** the rime sheds in sheets. Don't stand under windward faces or beside heavily feathered slabs at that moment. It is pretty and it also drops ice on your head.
- **Late afternoon:** meltwater refreezes in the grooves, making clear ice veins. Be out of the tight maze or on firm footing before then.
- **The blade-light minute:** if you want to see the low sun pour through the pinnacle gaps and ignite the rime, know your spot beforehand and be there ready. It lasts about a minute. Have your camera set, or put it away and just watch.

## Water, food, and fuel

- **Bring all your water,** at least four to five liters per person per day equivalent, and a stove to make more. There is nothing living here and nothing to forage.
- **Do not drink from the cisterns.** They are the builders' work, still doing their job, and the water is dark and salty. Leave them alone.
- **Rime is your best water source.** Gently collect the feathers from windward stones and melt them. They are cleaner than anything running through the salted grooves. Still filter and treat.
- **Replace electrolytes.** You lose them fast at altitude, and salt on your lips is not a substitute.
- **Food:** appetite drops at altitude, so bring dense, easy things you can eat cold: nut butter, chocolate, dried fruit, soft bars.
- **Stove:** use white gas or a cold-rated fuel. Ordinary canisters can fail. Melting water will take far longer than you expect, so budget fuel generously.

## Shelter

- Flat ground is scarce in the packed stone, so a full tent is impractical.
- **A bivouac sack and insulated pad** work in the gaps.
- **The hollow with the fallen roof** is tempting as a shelter. Half its slabs are down, and it may be a grave or a sacred place. Don't sleep there unless you must, and if you shelter in an emergency, disturb nothing.
- Expect a poor night if you stay. The cold, the thin air, and disturbed breathing will keep you awake.

## Respect

- **Don't take anything.** No stones, no copper wedges, no pegs, and no tally-marked lintel fragments.
- **Don't add anything.** No new cairns, no cut marks, and no leftover gear.
- **Don't clean, straighten, or repair.** The place is a settled form, worn to a shape it can keep. It doesn't need you.
- **Don't drink from the basins,** even if they look like drinking bowls. They are still doing their job.
- Assume the cairns may mark graves. Walk around them, not over them.

## Sound, mind, and mood

The quiet is disorienting. The hum in the pinnacles has no tune, but tired, oxygen-starved minds will make one. The chittering of shedding rime can sound like voices or footsteps. Agree on signals in advance, use a whistle for emergencies, and keep the party within sight or a short line.

The place has a slow calm to it. Let it set your pace. The visitors who do best sit still during cloud, move carefully in sun, keep their goggles on, and leave with only photographs and a clear sense of how much work the water is doing in the stone.

## Checklist summary

- Oxygen system, spares, oximeter, barometer, altitude meds, pressure bag
- Return anchor, rope, harness, descent kit, route notes, guide line
- Insulated boots, layers, shell, parka, liners, overmitts, microspikes, helmet
- Glacier goggles (spare), SPF, buff, saline, lip balm, barrier cream
- Stove, cold-rated fuel, water, treatment, electrolytes, dense food
- Bivouac sack, pad, small pack for maze, dry bags, silica gel, oil for metal
- Camera set for the blade-light minute
- A companion, a plan, and time to leave before you're tired
