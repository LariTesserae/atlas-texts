# Preparing to Visit the Setting Basin

## The Short Version

You are visiting a construction site that is also an instrument. It is warm, wet, loud, and sharp, and it is nearly finished. Nothing there is hostile, but the whole place operates on a rhythm you have to learn, and most of the real danger comes from ignoring it. Plan to spend your first day on the rim, watching and counting, and to go down only if you are sure you can keep time.

---

## Before You Go: Mindset

**You are an unsanctioned worker on a job site.** The basin isn't a park or a ruin. It is doing something, and everything living there has adapted to help or stay clear. Your goal is to be a quiet, predictable participant, not an observer standing outside the process.

**Expect sensory overload without threat.** The volume is the ordinary condition. Travelers who panic at the first stroke tend to make the mistakes that matter, like bolting off-count across curd. Decide beforehand that the noise is normal and that you will rely on rhythm, not on reacting.

**Accept that it is ending.** The basin may finish within years, and the frogs seem to know. If your trip is time-flexible, go sooner. If you go, go with the awareness that you are witnessing a last few chapters. Don't plan a return trip you can't afford to miss.

---

## Physical Preparation

**Altitude.** The warmth is deceptive. You are on the shoulder of a tall mountain, and the thin air will affect you regardless of the damp heat. Acclimatize beforehand if you can, and bring a pulse oximeter. Headaches, nausea, and clumsiness are dangerous here because clumsiness on curd is costly.

**Infrasound and vibration.** The drone sits at the edge of hearing and resonates in your chest and teeth. Some people feel only pressure. Others get nausea, anxiety, blurred vision, or a sense of being watched, all common effects of infrasound. Bring anti-nausea medication, and spend time in low-frequency environments beforehand to see how you react. Anyone with a heart condition, implanted devices, or dental work in poor condition should think hard about going.

**Fitness.** The switchback path is stone with cutting edges, and the floor demands controlled, deliberate stepping to a count. You need good balance and calf endurance more than raw strength.

## Gear

### Hearing
- **Filtered earplugs** that cut the stroke's peak without deadening everything. You want to lose the pain, not the rhythm. Full isolation is a mistake, because the drone, the ticking, and the frogs' chorus cutting off are your timing cues.
- A **spare pair** in a sealed pouch, since the fog will ruin them.
- Use a **tactile timer**, such as a vibrating wristband, as backup. Electronics may behave oddly near the resonators, so also carry a plain mechanical timer or a knotted cord for counting.

### Feet and Hands
- **Stiff-soled, high-ankled boots** with aggressive tread. The path is freshly fractured stone and the edges will slice soft soles.
- **Cut-resistant gloves**, ideally rated to a high level and with a grippy palm. The text says the edges cut a glove, so bring a spare pair and treat the first pair as consumable.
- **Knee and elbow protection.** You will use your hands on the switchbacks.
- **Gaiters** to keep curd and grit out of your boots.

### Clothing
- **Light, quick-drying layers.** It's warm and damp, so you will sweat and condense. Avoid cotton.
- A **hooded, waterproof shell**. The rain is warm, brief, and mineral-laden, tasting of iron and flint. Expect it to stain and to leave residue.
- **Eye protection** for grit, condensation, and rain columns.
- A **wide-brim or hooded cover** against the drifters' filaments, which are unknown in their effects.

### Breathing
- A **P100-class respirator or equivalent**, or at least a good filter mask. The fog carries suspended grit and dissolved minerals, and you do not want mineral crystallizing in your lungs. This matters most in the wet's thick fog near the floor.

### Water, Food, Medical
- Carry **all your own water**, plus a filtration method you trust for testing, not for drinking. Do not drink the runoff or the rain until you have tested it, and assume the wet is a mineral suspension that would set in a body.
- Bring **compact food** in sealed containers. Eating on the rim is fine, but keep scraps off the ground. The fauna's feeding chain is finely tuned.
- A **serious first aid kit** with wound closure, irrigation, and burn dressings. Cuts from fresh conchoidal stone are clean but deep. Include a **hemostatic agent**.
- **Antihistamines** in case of contact with frogs, drifters, or lilies.

### Camp and Navigation
- A **freestanding tent** with heavy stakes or ballast, since you will want to camp on stone, not curd. A ground pad thick enough to prevent sharp-edge punctures.
- **Rope and a harness** for the switchbacks and for emergency retrieval.
- **Chalk or removable markers** for trail marking. Be careful: the basin remembers, and marks may be absorbed, so don't count on them lasting.
- A **waterproof notebook** and pencil. Paper in fog is a losing bet.
- **Recording equipment** with limiters, if you must, but assume it may be affected by the resonance.

### Leave These Behind
- Anything you would be upset to see set in stone.
- Metal tools you don't need, because resonance and metal may not mix well.
- Sampling gear meant for removing rock. See below.

## Learning the Rules Before You Descend

### 1. Learn the count on the rim.
Spend at least a full day cycle at the top before setting foot on the floor. Learn the stroke interval at each stage: about ninety seconds in the dim day, speeding to sixty and then forty at dusk, slowing and deepening at night. Learn the **silence before dawn**. Practice stepping in time with the count until it is automatic, because you will need your attention for other things.

**Step on the count. Pause on the stroke.** If the ground remembers your weight, assume that means you can be held, marked, or absorbed. On curd, which holds a boot print for an hour before stiffening, moving off-rhythm could mean sinking, sticking, or setting in place. Don't test how far that goes.

### 2. Don't take rock.
Stone from the rim will hum in your pack for a day and then go dull, as if it has forgotten what it was for. It's a small, quiet loss but a real one, and it suggests the machine notices what leaves. Leave everything where it is. That includes pebbles from the cairns, which belong to the tallers.

### 3. Stay off the wet.
It's warm and pulsing, and skin forms across it every few minutes. Don't wade it and don't touch the threads. Above all, **do not drop anything into the Well-Eye**. Nothing that has gone in has come back, and you can't be sure what it does with what it takes.

### 4. Respect the Lintel and the Ribs.
The fauna avoid the Lintel's interior, and animals usually know. Treat it as off-limits. The Ribs are warm, damp, and possibly still working, so don't climb them or camp near them. The buried domes and blinking lights are not for touching. If you want to watch the sequence, do it from a distance and take notes.

### 5. Learn the frogs' alarm.
When the bell-frogs go silent *between* strokes, something large is passing, probably the wading shape with the hull-like back. Silence is your warning. Stop, stay still, and get to stable ground if you can do so without breaking rhythm. Don't approach the creature and don't follow its trail, because the water it leaves is stiff with new curd.

### 6. Don't interfere with the fauna.
Don't handle the frogs, since some are always slightly wrong and those get eaten, and you don't want to be part of that arithmetic. Don't disturb the tallers' cairns. Don't touch the stone-lilies, which open only on the stroke and are few. Don't step on curd-crawlers. They may be doing the work.

## Timing Your Visit

**Arrival.** Go up the switchbacks in the dim day, when the light is best and the strokes are regular. Reach the rim with hours to spare.

**First day.** Stay on the rim. Watch, count, and record. Take a full cycle if you can, including dusk and the pre-dawn silence.

**Descent.** If you go down, do it in the dim day, in a group of no fewer than two, on true stone only at first. Curd is for practice after you are steady. The wet you avoid entirely.

**Dusk is the hard hour.** Strokes speed up, the Reeds sing in harmony, and the wet lights up. It is the most beautiful stretch and the one with the least margin for error. Be back on the rim and stationary before the cloud lowers. Watch from above.

**Night.** Camp on true stone at the rim, well away from the walls. At night, sections of curd the size of a house compact and settle with a groan like a hull, and the landscape rearranges. Do not camp below a weeping face, on curd, or anywhere near the Ribs.

**Before dawn.** The brief silence is the safest time to notice how much you have adapted. Stay put through the first soft stroke.

## Emergency Considerations

- **Getting stuck in curd:** Stop moving, wait for the next stroke, and try to shift your weight on the pause rather than fighting. Have your partner secure a rope to a solid anchor on true stone before you step onto curd at all.
- **Cuts:** Treat at once. Fresh stone is clean, but the fog is not. Irrigate thoroughly.
- **Ingestion of rain or water:** Assume mineral load. Do not induce vomiting without medical advice. Get to your medical supplies and evacuate.
- **Disorientation from infrasound:** Sit, breathe, and count. If symptoms persist through two dusk cycles, leave.
- **Loss of rhythm:** If you lose the count, stop moving. Wait through at least two strokes to re-establish it before taking another step.
- **Exit route:** Know your way out. Have your return method confirmed and your anchor point secure. If the basin finishes while you are inside, the wet will drain and the strokes will stop, and you do not want to be on the floor when the last curd sets.

---

## What to Bring Home

Only what you carry in your head. Take photographs if you like, but the honest record of the Setting Basin is what you learn by standing at the rim through the whole cycle: the drone in your sternum, the frogs cutting off a heartbeat before the stroke, the ceiling-drifters settling on the Reeds like moths on a lamp, and the sense that something is being built by a voice, among animals that have learned to sing along.

Go quietly, keep time, and leave the stone where it is.
