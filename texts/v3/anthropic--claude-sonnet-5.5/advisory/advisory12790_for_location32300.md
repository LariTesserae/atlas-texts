# Preparing for Ouren Tal

## The Two Rules That Shape Everything

Before packing anything, absorb the two rules the place runs on:

1. **It is never quiet.** Quiet is either a sacred exception (the gray scars) or a warning.
2. **Silence is structural failure.** In a city, a fading note means the floor beneath you is forgetting how to be a floor.

And a third, implied by how the locals live: **water is a poison to the land.** You are a walking canteen in a world that flinches from water.

Nearly all your preparation follows from these.

## Before You Go: Medical and Bodily Checks

**Your ears and skull.** You will spend days inside sound so large it is felt in the sternum. Do not expect ordinary earplugs to work, and don't rely on them anyway. You need to hear the ground, the cities and the people. Bring:

- **Musician's or "flat-response" filtered plugs** that cut volume without muffling. Bring several grades, since the plateau on a calm day and its heart on a windy one differ enormously.
- **A bone-conduction headset or contact transducer** for talking to companions. The locals speak forehead to forehead through the bone. Practice this before you go. It feels intimate and odd, and you will be doing it with strangers.
- **Low-frequency tolerance.** If you are prone to migraines, vertigo, seizures or motion sickness, this may not be the place for you. The reed-organs sit at the edge of infrasound, and the cities sing a held note that "bends the world in ways the old physics has no word for." Expect nausea, pressure in the chest, and a floaty, disoriented first day or two.

**Implants and hardware.** Pacemakers, cochlear implants, insulin pumps, neural lace, metal pins and even old dental work are potential problems near standing-wave structures. I don't know how the tone-lattices interact with them, and the locals may not either, since they are stewards and not authors. If you carry anything sensitive, either don't go or go with a backup that fails safe. Carry manual alternatives for everything.

**Altitude.** The sky is nearly black at noon. Whatever the atmosphere is doing, treat this as extreme altitude until proven otherwise. Arrive with a sealed breathing supply and a gas analyzer, and test the air the moment you step through. The plants and animals seem to live comfortably, but they may breathe differently than you do. The plants may be pulling gases from the air that we depend on, or adding ones we don't want. Plan on a rebreather or bottled air for at least the first day, then adjust based on readings and on what the locals do.

**Lungs and eyes.** The sun is filtered through a permanent veil of spore-dust, and the seed-pods burst like rifle shots and scatter shrapnel. Bring a P100-class or better respirator, sealed goggles (impact-rated and dust-tight), and a light helmet. The pods are the size of skulls, and you do not want to be under one when it goes off.

## Water: The Central Problem

This deserves its own section, because it is the way you are most likely to hurt the land, or to cause trouble for the people who live on it.

- **Carry water in sealed, redundant containers.** Use double-walled bottles with locking caps, and keep them in an inner pocket, not swinging on the outside of your pack. The locals treat their canteens like something dangerous, and you should too.
- **Do not spill it. Do not spit. Do not pour it out.** A splash on the mat may be a small injury to a living thing. The plants clamp their pores and sweat oil to wash it off, and a reaction like that from the ground under your feet is unsettling.
- **Manage your own body.** Sweat, breath condensation, urine and washing water are all water. Wear clothing that wicks and contains. Use a closed-loop hygiene kit: sealed waste bags, dry-wash cloths, and no open-air relieving yourself onto the meadow. Ask your hosts how they handle it, because they will have a practice, and it will matter more than anything you pack.
- **Keep a bit more water than you think you need**, but plan around resupply from the cities, where water is presumably imported, stored or condensed in controlled ways.
- **Learn the calendar of the sour days.** The frost forms on the upper air-currents and drifts down, and the whole meadow reacts. Don't be caught outside on one. Time your arrival so that you're inside a settlement, with your own supplies sealed, before the frost falls. Ask about the signs too: temperature dips, a change in the high veil, animals going quiet or restless.

## What to Wear and Carry

**Boots.** The ground is springy, faintly warm and alive. Wear boots with a wide, flat, slightly flexible sole, since you'll be walking on a mat that drums back. Thick soles will help you avoid feeling every hard patch, though the "drumhead hardness" where it thins over buried rock is useful information, so don't insulate yourself completely from it.

**Clothing.** Layers. The mat is warm but the air is thin and high, and the sun is faint. Close weave, sealed cuffs and collar (spore-dust and oil), and outer layers that shed oil-sweat easily. The plants' pale, heavy sap is an oil, so expect some contact. Do a patch test on a small piece of skin before deciding it's safe to handle. Bring gloves in more than one material.

**Light.** The world is lit by amber lanternvine pulsing in a tide, plus a pale old sun at what looks like dusk. You'll want a headlamp with a warm-spectrum, low-intensity setting. A harsh white beam will disturb the sparks and may irritate people, and possibly plants, that live on rhythmic light. Use it sparingly. Bring spares and spare batteries, and expect electronics to behave strangely near the cities.

**Navigation.** The meadow has few landmarks and swells that shift over years. Bring:
- A celestial navigation kit, since the sky is black and full of stars.
- A personal beacon that works across the multiverse gap, if such a thing exists for you.
- A physical map from a local, or at least a spoken route, memorized. Settlements are sparse, and the Standing Elders are sometimes a day's walk apart.
- Don't trust a compass until you've tested it. The tone-lattices may do something to magnetics.

**Food.** Assume the local plants are not digestible or safe for you. The chemistry runs on oil, not water, and you are a water creature. Bring all your own food, with a margin. Ask the locals what is safe before eating anything. Hospitality may involve food you can't eat, so learn a polite way to decline, or bring something you can share.

**Sleep.** It is never quiet, so sleep will be difficult. Bring low-volume masking sound that isn't competing with the meadow, an eye mask, and something to put between your head and the floor. In a city you may be sleeping in a room that hums. Try to think of that as the reassuring sound of the building holding you up.

## How to Move: Walking in Drum-Moss

Every step booms. The hollow-hunters feel the thump of grazers' steps and rise beneath them. You don't want to be mistaken for a grazer.

- **Walk off-beat.** Copy the herds: irregular rhythm, uneven spacing, occasional pauses. A steady, even gait is the most attractive signal you can make.
- **Prefer to travel in daylight, or what passes for it**, and be in a settlement before dusk, when the animals rise and the choir-birds start.
- **Stay near others.** Don't travel alone across open meadow. A local guide who knows the rhythms is worth more than any equipment.
- **Be wary of thin ground.** A tightening, drumhead hardness underfoot means buried rock nearby. It is probably stable, but it changes how the ground carries sound and weight. Learn from your guide.
- **Swells.** Every few years a slow tectonic wave rolls across the plateau. If you arrive at the wrong time, you may see the ground itself moving. Ask about the current state of the swell before planning a route.

## In the Cities

**You are inside an instrument.** Everything around you is held up by sound. You need to think about it the way you'd think about being on a ship or a bridge.

- **Never damage, muffle or interfere with the tone.** Do not bring devices that generate strong sound, jamming or vibration. Do not bring noise-cancelling systems that broadcast an inverse signal. Active cancellation could be a genuine hazard in a place where a flat note can make a floor sag. Passive hearing protection only.
- **Don't lean, stamp or hang on things without asking.** Terraces are "thin as leaves" and carry crowds without bowing, but you don't know how much your equipment adds.
- **Weight.** Your pack and gear count. Ask what load a terrace or bridge is tuned for. If you're carrying a heavy pack, ask a Keeper.
- **Listen for failure.** If the tone goes flat, wavers, thins or dies, get out. Move toward the heavier, more central structure, or toward the ground, and alert a Keeper. Learn what the "flat note" sounds like from a local before you need to know. It may be more subtle than you'd think.
- **Keepers.** Treat them as you would a harbor pilot, lifeguard, and priest at once. They walk the arches with tuning-rods, and they will be worth your time and respect. Ask before you speak to one on duty, and never distract them from listening.
- **Ouren** itself, the spiral of a hundred terraces, is so loud at its heart that the air trembles visibly. Visit it in stages, and spend your first days in a smaller settlement to adapt.

## People and Etiquette

**Communication.** In loud conditions, forehead to forehead, speaking through the bone. That is a physical intimacy your culture might not share. Learn it, do it politely, and don't be surprised by it. Ask permission the first time, and follow the local lead on distance and duration.

**Language.** Expect it to be at least partly tonal or musical. You may not be able to learn it in a visit, so bring a translation device or a translator, but check whether the device produces sound that would cause trouble. A bone-conduction translator that keeps its output private is ideal.

**Gifts.** Water is dangerous but valuable. Consider carefully whether to offer it. A better gift might be something that produces or supports sound: a well-made tuning fork, a rare metal, a beautifully made instrument, or a story. Ask an experienced traveler what is welcome.

**The gray scars.** If a swell exposes the old stone, people make pilgrimages and stand there in the odd, cold quiet. If you're invited or allowed, go. Move slowly, speak little or not at all, and touch the stone only if others do. It's a sacred silence, and you will be a guest in it. Make no noise with your equipment: turn off beeps and alarms. This may be the one place where you can hear yourself think, and where you can feel what the meadow has lost and what its people have chosen instead.

**Don't fix things.** The people who tend the tone-lattices are stewards, not authors. They don't fully understand the technology, and neither will you. Don't offer engineering advice, don't try to analyze the fingernail-sized machines, and don't take any away. A stolen or damaged one could mean a bridge, a terrace or a family home.

## Psychological Preparation

- **The sky.** Black at noon, with a small, pale, old sun. Expect a low, persistent feeling of dusk and of being at the end of something.
- **The noise.** Even with good ears and good gear, it will wear at you. Plan quiet-ish spaces, rest periods and predictable routines. Let yourself adapt, because the sound becomes a kind of texture and then a kind of comfort.
- **The reversal of your instincts.** In most worlds, silence is peace and noise is a threat. Here, silence is danger and noise is safety. Your gut may fight this for days. When the air goes suddenly still, you'll feel relief and then, correctly, alarm.
- **Scale and age.** The place is older than anything on it, and it has been worn down to a last shape. Some visitors find that moving or humbling, and some find it bleak. Bring something that grounds you: a small ritual, a letter, a photograph.

## Emergency and Exit Planning

- **Have a reliable return.** Know exactly how and when your transit back works, and whether it depends on sound, light, altitude, position or weather. Test it before you commit. If your arrival point is on open meadow, be sure it's not in a place where you'd be alone for a day's walk to shelter.
- **Give someone at home a schedule** and a fallback.
- **Contamination protocol.** Before returning, clean your gear thoroughly. Spore-dust, oil, and any seeds or burrowers you carry could be a problem for your own world, and the reverse is true for water and pathogens brought here. Quarantine and decontaminate on both ends. It is a matter of courtesy for the meadow as well as safety for you.
- **If you're stranded outside a city:** stay low, move off-beat, keep your water sealed, avoid open ground at dusk, and move toward the nearest amber glow and the nearest sound of a held note. A lone spire with a single Keeper, singing its one note through the dark, is a lighthouse.

## A Last Piece of Advice

The best way to prepare is to slow down and listen. The plateau will teach you if you let it. Walk out at dusk from the last spire, feel the booming under your boots, and hear the reed-organs and the choir-birds answering each other across a hundred kilometers. Behind you, the spire hums its one held note. If you're quiet enough, you will hear the ground singing along.

Then go back inside before the hunters rise.
