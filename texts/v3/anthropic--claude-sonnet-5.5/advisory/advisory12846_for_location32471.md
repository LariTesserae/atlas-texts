# Preparing for the Terraced Deep of Ammerel

## The first problem is the water

Ammerel is not a place you visit with a snorkel and good intentions. The plateau lies under about a hundred fathoms of water, roughly 180 meters, so the pressure at the city is close to twenty times what you are used to. Everything else in your preparation depends on how you handle that.

The locals give you a clue. They get joint-aching decompression sickness if they rise too fast, which means their bodies dissolve gas under pressure as ours do. Whatever they are, or however they breathe, they are physiologically close enough to you that the same physics will hurt you. I would not assume they breathe as you do, though, or that you can borrow whatever they use.

Your realistic options, roughly in order of sense:

1. **Arrive already adapted.** If your home world or a broker along the way has anything like gill-grafts, liquid-breathing conditioning, or a pressure-tolerant physiology, this is the best route. It removes the need for gas supplies and most of the decompression trouble.
2. **A saturation approach.** Live at depth pressure in a habitat back at your portal anchor, and accept a decompression of several days afterward. This is a real commitment, so plan the visit as a long one.
3. **A closed-circuit rebreather with trimix.** At this depth, air is out of the question. Nitrogen narcosis would leave you giddy and stupid, and oxygen toxicity could give you seizures. You need helium in the mix, and a helium-rich mix brings its own problems: cold, voice distortion, and tremors from high pressure.
4. **A rigid atmospheric diving suit.** It is clumsy, but it holds your body at surface pressure and spares you decompression. It also cuts you off from touch and from the wall-tapping speech you will want to learn. Metal joints and a jointed shell would astonish people who have no metal at all.

Whichever you choose, plan the ascent as carefully as the descent. The people say rising is dangerous, and they are right. Do not let curiosity about the Lid change your ascent schedule. Touching it is forbidden anyway.

## Confirm your arrival and exit

Know exactly where your portal anchor sits before you leave. Ideally it is on a terrace near the city, at a depth you can survive, with a way back that does not require a fast ascent. Avoid anchoring near:

- **The vents and fissures.** The water is hotter there and carries dissolved stone, possibly toxic gases, and it may scald.
- **The far rim.** Past the cliff the water is cold, blue, and unlit. The locals place their unfinished stories there, and you should not test what else is there.
- **Fresh basalt fields.** They are razor-edged and unweathered, and a panicked swimmer will be cut to ribbons.

Carry a backup return method. An anchor that gets coated in travertine may be very hard to find or use. Mark it, check it often, and never leave it under a seep.

## Bring the right kit

**Protection**
- Thick cut-resistant gloves and knee protection. The black glass will slice a palm open and the infection risk in warm water is real.
- Thermal management for both extremes. The water is bath-warm near vents and merely mild elsewhere, so a helium mix will chill you while the vents overheat you. Layered garments with a way to shed heat are ideal.
- A tether or a lifeline. The seams between water layers bend light, double distant objects, and disorient you. A line back to a fixed point helps.

**Light**
- A dim, warm-toned lamp, ideally a shaded one. The place is lit by gold-green filtered daylight and, later, by living things: amber gas-globes and luminous weed in horn jars. A harsh white beam would feel like a shout in a quiet room and would dazzle the locals' night-adapted eyes.
- Keep a bright backup light for emergencies, and warn people before using it.

**Communication**
- A smooth, hard stone or a small knocker. Most of the locals' speech is tapping on calcified walls, carried across whole districts. Spoken words carry poorly and are used sparingly.
- Learn some basics before you go if you can: a greeting, a request for help, a signal meaning "I am a stranger and mean no harm," and the soft single tap they give a passing drifter. Expect to be clumsy. Tapping well is a skill, and beginners sound like children.

**Sustenance**
- Bring all your own food, sealed and in a form you can eat at depth. You do not know their biochemistry or what is safe for you, and their food supply is small and hard-won.
- Bring your own water purification if you are not saturation-supported. Dissolved minerals in the water are heavy.

**Things not to bring, or to keep hidden**
- **Metal tools.** The people have none. A steel knife would be a wonder, a temptation, or an insult, and I cannot say which. Hide anything you cannot explain. If you must carry metal, expect corrosion and mineral coating.
- **Open flames or ignition sources.** They have no fire, and it would be unwise regardless at these pressures.
- **Bright synthetic objects.** These are unsettling in a place where things take generations to finish.

## Understand how the place works

The Terraced Deep is a slow world. Time there runs on stone. A house takes years to become a shell, and a family inherits it. If you come rushed and full of purpose, you will do damage without meaning to.

**Everything that stays gets coated.** Do not put down a pack, a tool, or a tether where a seep can reach it. The mineral water will encase it, and cultured stone will keep it as a fossil. That also applies to you. Don't lean on soft terraces, don't lie in an open basin, and don't rest a hand on a fresh scalloped lip. The soft ones dent to a thumb, which means you can ruin work that took someone a season.

**Grass, cords, and stalks can tangle you.** Ribbon-grass meadows are thirty feet long and beautifully combed, but they can snarl a swimmer with kit. Move with the current and not against it, and carry a blunt cutter you are willing to use only in an emergency.

**Gas is not safe to breathe.** The gas the lampstalks trap comes from the seeps. It may be rich in carbon dioxide, sulfides, or other things. Do not breathe from a globe, a jar, or a bubble, however inviting a silver sphere looks on its way to the Lid. The locals may rely on the gas for light and buoyancy, but that tells you nothing about what it does to lungs.

**The ground may shudder.** Twice in a lifetime the fissures slip, with a long pressure in the chest and silt lifting off every terrace at once. When it happens, do what the locals do: stop, hold still, wait for the silt to settle, and stay quiet until they resume. Do not swim, do not tap, do not speak. A hurried traveler in that silt would be blind and could be crushed or cut. Plan your position so you are not in a narrow lane, under a rope-bridge, or beside an unstable stack of pillow lava when it happens.

## Manners

- **Ask before you touch anything built or grown.** Walls, doors, awnings, and gas-globes belong to families, and stone keeps their histories.
- **Treat the dead as neighbors.** The oldest terraces are galleries of pale sleepers with fern-ghosts in their hair. Do not touch them, photograph them casually, or take anything. Swim past quietly, and if you feel you should, give a soft tap.
- **When a drifter passes overhead**, stop what you are doing and tap once, softly. Do this whether or not you understand it. They say the drifters come from above the Lid, and to them this is close to a sacred moment.
- **Never touch the Lid.** Everyone knows someone who claims to have. Don't be that person, and don't ask about it as though it were gossip.
- **Speak sparingly.** The city is quiet, and its evening rhythm of tapping fades to nothing without anyone conducting it. If you visit at dusk, pause and listen before you add to it.
- **Don't take stone, fern-impressions, or mineral-coated things as souvenirs.** In a place where objects take generations to finish, taking one is stealing a life's work.

## Health and mind

Before you go, ask a physician who knows multiverse travel about barotrauma, ear and sinus clearing, and how your body will handle a long time at pressure. Plan for a long decompression. Afterward, watch for aching joints, tingling, dizziness, or numbness, and seek treatment at once. The local remedy is unlikely to be what you need.

Do not carry your own microbes into a small, isolated population, and do not bring theirs home. Sterilize your kit, use barriers, and follow whatever quarantine your home authority requires.

The psychological strain is real. You will be in a warm green room with no ceiling you may touch and a cold blue dark at the edge. The quiet can feel gentle at first and then heavy. The tick of the terraces, the groan of the fissures, and the slow shifting of the optical seams may leave you unsettled. Sleep in a place you know is stable, and keep a regular routine. Keep a small object from your own world for reassurance, but keep it out of sight.

## What to expect

If you prepare carefully, you will arrive in a drowned, unfinished place where the ground is still being made. You will see gold-green light netting on cream terraces, groves of lampstalks releasing silver spheres upward in slow lanterns, a city of coins spilled on a green cloth, and pale sleepers with ferns in their hair. You will hear knocking that passes across the district like a slow, thinning rain, and then silence.

The best thing you can do there is to move slowly, ask before you act, and leave as little mark as you can. The place has been making itself for a very long time, and it will not miss you if you leave gently.
