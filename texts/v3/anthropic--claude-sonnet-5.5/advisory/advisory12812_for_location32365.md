# Preparing for the Tallow Flats

## The right mindset

The Flats are not dangerous the way a jungle or a battlefield is. Nothing here wants you, and that is the problem. You are entering a factory that has never had a night shift or a day shift, and you are a lump of warm iron-bearing silt standing in the flow. Most of your preparation comes down to staying cold, dry, insulated, and moving, and avoiding anything that makes you look like a raw material.

Plan for a short visit: hours, not days. Nothing here rewards lingering, and standing still is the one thing you cannot afford.

## Arrival and exit

This is the hardest part, so settle it before you go.

- **There is no empty ground.** Between the rock knuckles the plain is packed with bodies. If your transit method needs a clear patch to land in, it will either fail or make one by displacing something, and the Flats will refill the gap immediately, possibly with you in it. Aim to arrive on the slope of a gray knuckle, or bring a method that can settle onto a moving surface.
- **The rock is slick.** The knuckles weep from every pore and are smooth as river stones. Bring crampons or spiked soles for the arrival point, and a short anchor line you can clip to yourself.
- **Use a time-based exit, not a place-based one.** Distance is unreliable here, and the place has no fixed beginning to return to. Carry a mechanical dead-man's trigger that pulls you out after a set interval, or on your own action, wherever you are. Do not rely on walking back to where you arrived. Landmarks will not hold still, and neither will the ground.
- **Nobody can come and get you.** Any gap a rescuer opens will be filled by the plain. Assume you are your own extraction.
- **Never plan your exit through a seam.** The pale gridded nothing at the seams looks like a door and is not one you can trust.

## Clothing: cold, wet, and filthy

The cold is a wet cold that finds every seam. The fog drips on everything, and the airborne animals overhead shed grit and droppings continuously.

- **Layers.** Synthetic or wool base layers and mid layers. No cotton, ever. Wet cotton will kill you slowly here.
- **Outer shell.** A fully waterproof, hooded, wipe-clean shell with taped seams, and a hard-brimmed hat or helmet under the hood to shed falling grit.
- **Legs and feet.** High waders or tall waterproof boots with gaiters sealed over them. The ground gives up a cold lens of water at each step, so your feet will be in liquid constantly. Get deep-lugged soles that resist mud suction. You will step on animals and shell, and shell is slippery. Carry spare socks in a sealed bag.
- **Insulation.** Foot and hand warmers are worth having, but avoid anything with a battery.
- **Gloves.** Two pairs: an inner thermal pair and an outer waterproof pair.

## Electrical protection

The eel-tissue in the mud animals discharges constantly. Puddles will numb your fingers, and the fog itself flickers with sparks.

- **Insulated boots and gloves.** Electrical-grade rubber over-boots and lineman-style gloves are ideal. Test them for pinholes before you leave.
- **Stay out of standing water where you can.** You cannot avoid all of it, but do not kneel, sit, or put bare skin in it. Keep your hands out of puddles entirely.
- **Leave anyone with an implanted electronic device at home.** That means pacemakers, insulin pumps, and neural stimulators. The field here is not gentle.
- **Electronics.** Assume anything sensitive will be scrambled or burned. Bring mechanical alternatives where possible: a wind-up or automatic watch, a paper map, a mechanical compass that you understand will be untrustworthy. Anything electronic goes in a sealed Faraday pouch with a backup.
- **Don't wear or carry large loops of metal.** A dangling chain or long wire becomes an antenna.

## Breathing and contamination

- **Respirator.** A full-face respirator with P100 and organic vapor cartridges, sealed against ozone and fine grit. Bring spare cartridges. The air smells of wet iron and warm animal, and the fungal film lifts and settles like breath, so treat everything it releases as a spore hazard.
- **Eye protection.** A full-face mask covers this. Goggles alone are a weak substitute.
- **Don't touch the film.** The black and rust-colored felt over the shallows is fungal. Keep it off your skin and off your gear.
- **Don't drink the water.** It is groundwater filtered through unimaginable quantities of animal, iron, and droppings. Bring all your own, in sealed containers. Pack in sealed food that doesn't rely on strong smells.
- **Decon kit.** You will want to wash off before re-entering anywhere you care about. Bring a change of outer layers in a sealed bag, and decontamination wipes and bags for used gear.

## Hearing

The Flats are never quiet. The grinders' grumble, the smelters' steam-whistle chords, and the stackers' rhythmic clicking add up to a sustained, damaging roar.

- **Hearing protection.** Use high-fidelity earplugs or electronics-free earmuffs rated for sustained industrial noise. Do not go without them.
- **Don't block it completely.** The sound is your best clock and your best warning. Learn the rhythms if you can. When the whole plain stops or changes pitch at once, that is a shift change. For a few seconds you will hear only water and breathing, and you will hear yourself as well.
- **Use those seconds carefully.** Do not shout or make sudden noise during the lull. Do not assume it means safety, and do not assume it is a chance to rest. Keep moving, quietly.

## Moving through the plain

- **Keep moving.** If you stand still long enough, the animals begin to process you. It is not hostile, but you are silt, iron, and warmth in the way of the flow.
- **Move with the flow, not across it.** The tides of grazers, the channels of washers, and the ridges of stackers all have a direction. Cutting across them is how you get ground, sieved, or swallowed. Keep to the edges of channels, and never stand in a braided current where the washers work.
- **Don't stand near smelters.** They swallow red grains, exhale hot iron beads, and glow through their skin. Stay upwind of the whistle and clear of the beads, which drop hissing into the water.
- **Watch the rust shoals.** The stackers' ridges are built just ahead of the tide and are unstable. Don't walk on them.
- **Do not fight or clear anything.** Killing animals here accomplishes nothing. The plain refills every gap within moments, from nowhere. Don't feed them either. You cannot change the count.
- **Beware the roof.** The winged animals form a second ceiling in the fog. Keep your hood up and your mouth closed.
- **Work in pairs or more, roped together.** Keep a physical line between you. Visual contact in fog and crowds is unreliable, and a rope can't be blocked by an animal tide the way a shout can.

## Navigation and the weird ground

The weirdness is a property of the ground, and it worsens the farther you go.

- **Don't trust your senses' order.** Near the seams, you will hear a whistle before the breath and a click before the movement. Treat this as a warning that you are near a seam, and turn back rather than trying to exploit it. Avoid making decisions based on what "just happened."
- **Don't use the gray knuckles as landmarks.** Walk toward one and it does not grow. The ground beneath you changes instead: mud to shell to iron to something with no surface, a depth like the inside of a bell. If the ground stops feeling like ground, stop and go back the way you came, along your line.
- **Don't navigate by shadow.** Shadows fall toward the dim light, and the light is unreliable.
- **Don't look into the puddles for long.** Some reflect skies that aren't overhead: a bright empty blue, a night with unfamiliar stars. The animals wade through them unbothered. You should not test whether you can do the same. Never step in a puddle that shows a different sky.
- **Watch for upward rain.** Patches a few dozen paces wide where cold beads rise from the mud are seam indicators. Go around them.
- **Watch the temperature of the ground.** Wet hot mud and dry frozen mud can sit side by side with no line between them. Test the footing with a probe before committing.
- **Never enter the seam.** You can see through the mud and the animals there to a pale gridded nothing that hums a little lower than everything else. It is what the packed world is packed against. It is not empty, and you should not want to find out what it is.

## Pack list

**Bring:**
- Full-face respirator, spare filters
- Insulated waterproof shell, synthetic layers, waders or tall boots with gaiters
- Electrical-grade rubber over-boots and gloves
- Hearing protection
- Hard hat or brimmed helmet
- Rope, carabiners, and a shared safety line
- Water and food, sealed
- Mechanical watch and paper map
- Probe pole (non-conductive, fiberglass)
- Mechanical time-based extraction trigger
- First aid kit with hypothermia supplies, burn dressings, and eye wash
- Decon wipes, sealed disposal bags, and a spare set of clothes
- Spikes or crampons for the arrival slope

**Leave behind:**
- Cotton clothing
- Unprotected electronics and anything battery-dependent you can't shield
- Pacemakers and other implants (and their owners)
- Large ferrous tools, jewelry, and loose metal. The workers here sort iron, and you shouldn't be mistaken for feedstock.
- Strong-smelling food
- Any plan that requires staying in one place

## Medical and psychological notes

- **Hypothermia** will sneak up on you in the damp. Check each other's speech and coordination every twenty minutes.
- **Electric shock** may cause numbness or arrhythmia. Anyone who feels persistent tingling or chest irregularity should leave immediately.
- **Sensory overload** is real. The constant noise, the cause-and-effect glitches, the crowding, and the feeling of being in the way wear on judgment. Use a buddy system and short check-in phrases.
- **Claustrophobia.** You will feel hemmed in by the packed bodies even in open space. Practice steady breathing before you go.
- **Dissociation.** The Flats can make you feel like a component. Agree beforehand on a personal anchor, such as a phrase, a tactile object, or a name, that reminds you that you are a traveler and not silt.

## A last rule

The whole plain runs on the principle that nothing is idle and nothing is empty. Every choice you make should honor that. Move with purpose, keep your line, keep your seals tight, watch the shift changes, and leave on schedule. If you hear a smelter whistle before it draws breath, you are close to the edge of the rendering. Turn around.
