# Preparing to Visit the Floor of the Long Basin

## The Short Version

The Basin looks serene and is dangerous in ways that are easy to underestimate. It is forty meters deep, cold, loud, and enormous, and it moves whole rivers of water on a schedule you didn't set. Most of the danger comes from that combination and not from any single hazard. Nothing here is hostile, but everything is industrial and indifferent to you.

If you are an ordinary traveler, **don't go in scuba.** Go in a crewed submersible or a one-atmosphere suit. If you must go in the water, treat it as a technical dive with full support. Everything below assumes you take this seriously.

## Choose How You Will Be There

**Best: a crewed submersible or one-atmosphere diving suit.** You avoid narcosis, decompression, cold, and most of the noise. Ask for a viewport and an external hydrophone so you still get the sound. Thrusters matter, because the ebb current is strong enough to carry a small craft off course.

**Acceptable, with training: a closed-circuit rebreather on trimix.** Forty meters is the edge of the ordinary recreational envelope. Air at five atmospheres brings real narcosis, and your judgment will be degraded exactly when the featureless floor and the noise are most disorienting. Use a helium mix (something like 21/35 trimix) and carry bailout gas sufficient to reach a safe ascent from the farthest point you plan to reach. Open-circuit air will not last long enough to see anything meaningful. Twenty-odd minutes of gas at depth is not a visit.

**Add a diver propulsion vehicle.** The distances are measured in kilometers. Swimming won't get you anywhere, and you'll burn gas fighting the current. Tether or clip the vehicle to yourself so you don't lose it in a silt storm.

**Not recommended: free diving, surface-supplied hose, or anything without redundancy.** A hose is a bad idea near dredgers, tracked machines, and rotors.

## Before You Arrive

- **Decompression and transit.** Plan as if your dive obligates staged decompression, and stay well inside no-stop limits if you have no chamber access. Keep a recompression chamber, or a plan to reach one, in mind before you go. If your transit between worlds resembles a pressure change, wait before diving, as you would before flying.
- **Get the gate schedule.** The gates cycle twice a day. This is the most important fact about the Basin. Learn the times, and if you can't learn them, assume the worst and stay far from the walls. The animals know the schedule, so watch them. When the herring move to the lee of the towers, get out of the open floor.
- **Arrive by a known route.** Find out where you will be when you arrive, and how deep. Arriving in open water at forty meters with no reference is a bad start. Arrange your entry near a survey marker, a tower base, or a support vessel.
- **Tell the crews.** People are working here. The Basin is an active construction site, not a park. If you can, contact the operators and learn where dredgers, rigs, and pours are active. Crew lamps mean crews. Keep away from them and don't interfere.
- **Go in daylight.** The sun is the main light. At night the Basin is dark except for the crew lamps.

## The Gear

**Thermal protection.** The water is cold and the deep intakes push colder layers beneath it. Wear a drysuit with heated or heavy undergarments, gloves, and a hood. Thermoclines will hit you as sudden temperature drops. You may cross several in a single dive, and each one changes your buoyancy slightly.

**Hearing.** The sound is constant and loud, and water carries it four times faster than air. Your ears cannot tell where it comes from. That means you cannot locate a dredger, a pump, or a diver by ear. A thick hood helps, and so do earplugs designed for diving, but don't rely on either. Assume you can't hear an approaching machine until it is close.

**Lights.** Bring at least two independent primary lights and a backup. You won't need them on the open floor by day, but you will inside seams, in shadow under the gate walls, and in a silt storm.

**Navigation.** The emptiness is a hazard in itself. There is nothing to orient by: no slope, no landmark, only flat concrete in every direction. Carry a compass, a depth gauge, a wrist slate, and a reel. Use the survey markers (yellow posts with bells) as waypoints, and record their numbers if they have them. Consider an acoustic beacon so a surface team can find you. If you use a surface marker buoy, remember there is traffic overhead. Deploy it away from ship lanes.

**Communication.** Full-face masks with through-water audio are useful but will be swamped by the noise. Agree on hand signals and tap codes with your buddy before you enter the water. Carry a slate.

**Cutting tools.** The kelp lattices are strung with rope and steel. The far margin has exposed reinforcing bars. Carry two cutting tools, easy to reach, and know how to use them.

**Gloves and protection.** Fresh barnacles are sharp, and the slab edges are sharp. Gloves are essential, but don't put a gloved hand into a seam. They are wide enough to trap an arm and there may be something living in them.

## Hazards, in Order of Seriousness

**1. The gates and the ebb.**
When the gates lift, a river with a hundred-meter cross-section forms. It flings silt, drags shoals sideways, and will drag you too. Suction near an open gate is lethal. Stay well back from the walls before the cycle, and move to the lee of a tower or into a low, protected area. Hold onto a structure if you have to. Never swim across the current toward a gate.

**2. Intakes and pumps.**
The pump stations pull large volumes of water. Keep far from intake grilles. Suction will pin you, and the whine warns you only when it's already close.

**3. Turbines.**
The rotors turn at a walking pace, and that is exactly why they are dangerous: they look slow and they are not. Blades ten meters across carry enormous momentum. Stay outside the rotor swept area at all times and watch which way the current pushes you. Don't drift into a tower field on the ebb.

**4. Dredgers.**
A tracked machine the size of a cargo ship, with a toothed wheel, crawling along the floor. You'll hear it long before you see it and keep hearing it after it passes. Keep well clear of its track, its wheel, and its discharge pipe. The silt it lifts produces the storms below.

**5. Silt storms.**
A brown wall rolls downstream and visibility drops to arm's length, but the noise doesn't change, so you get no cue. If you see one coming, stop, clip to your buddy or to a fixed object, deflate slightly to stay on the bottom or hold position, and wait. It passes in about twenty minutes. Don't try to outrun it, and never separate from your team.

**6. Fresh concrete.**
At the far margin the slabs are still wet-gray. Fresh concrete and the water around it are strongly alkaline and will burn skin and eyes. Keep off it, and wear proper coverings if you must approach.

**7. Surface traffic and rigs.**
The surface is not a safe place to surface. Propellers tick overhead. Use a controlled ascent line near a moored rig or a known safe area, and do not surface in open water without checking. Have a surface boat with a spotter.

**8. Narcosis and gas.**
Forty meters is deep enough that mistakes accumulate quickly. Keep to conservative gas plans, abort early, and turn around at one third of your gas. The emptiness will make you feel you have more time than you do.

## Life Around You

The wildlife is abundant but concentrated near structures. Herring shoals can be overwhelming, with hundreds of thousands moving in silver sheets. Move slowly through them, keep your lights low, and don't chase them. Cod hang head-down below them. Seals are curious and unpredictable, and may approach or bump you. Porbeagle sharks patrol in loose lines and usually ignore divers, but give them room, don't approach the tower base while they hunt, and never block their path. Rays lie in silt at the foot of the walls with only their eyes visible, so watch where you put your hands and fins. Stay off the bottom near them.

Don't feed or touch anything. Kelp and lattices are working infrastructure as much as habitat.

## The Experience

You will notice, first, how little there is. Slabs the size of city blocks run out into the haze, and nothing on them. The first hour is beautiful: light ropes braiding across the concrete, barnacles like tiny precise stars along the lee edges, the bells ringing as the current turns.

Then the scale sets in. Without landmarks you lose your sense of distance, and the sound presses in from everywhere. That is the moment to check your gauges, your buddy, and your plan. It is easy to drift, both physically and mentally.

Structures, when they come, come from far off: a dark line in the haze that becomes a sixty-meter wall. Approach only as far as you can safely stay from the gates. From a distance you can see the boundary where warm meets cold, a wavering seam like inverted heat shimmer.

Go slowly. Watch the shoals, the light, and the marker bells. The Basin will show you a great deal if you stay small and careful and do not try to cross it.

## Leaving

Plan your exit as carefully as your entrance. Know your ascent point, your decompression stops, and your recovery method before you begin. Give yourself a hard time limit that ends well before the next gate cycle, and if the shoals begin to move, you are already late.

Bring everything you brought back with you. The Basin is still being built, and the fish are ahead of the builders. Leave nothing behind for them to grow around.
