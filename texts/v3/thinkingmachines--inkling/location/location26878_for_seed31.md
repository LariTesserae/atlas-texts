The valley hangs high, a basin torn upward before the mountains had time to forget their violence. The ridges around it are raw—knife-edges of fresh granite and basalt, unrounded by age, their strata standing vertical like pages torn from a book still warm from the printing. Erosion has not yet taught them patience. Water is everywhere, but it behaves with an odd, disciplined presence: it weeps from vertical fractures in the rock faces in thin, silent films; it pools in black, motionless sheets across the valley floor; it condenses out of the warm air to cling to every surface as a perpetual, sweating mist. The ground is marsh-edge, always—soft black soil giving way to stone, reed beds rising from water that never ripples because nothing disturbs it.

This is a warm height. Snow never held here, not even in the geological instant since the uplift. The air is thick with moisture and mildness, a climate that should have bred abundance, yet the life here is only plant—ferns uncoiling from wall joints, moss carpeting the flat stones in deep green, reeds standing stiff and dark in the still pools. The vegetation is lush but scattered, growing in islands separated by bare mud and open water, never closing into forest.

Across this marsh-plain lies the settlement—not dense, not crowded, but unmistakably ordered. Wide avenues of packed dark earth and fitted slate run in straight, intersecting lines, dividing the town into quarters with geometric intent. The buildings are low—one or two stories—stacked by hand from flat slabs of local stone, mortared with clay and pressed reed. Roofs are woven thatch or layered slate, thick and heavy. Between them lie vast, empty commons: pools of standing water, gardens of moss, stretches of black mud. The density is sparse; one can stand at any street corner and see the far ridge without obstruction, the structures scattered like stones cast deliberately across a cloth.

The technology is of the hand and the eye. No metal machinery marks the walls; tools were stone and wood, and the labor was manual, patient, collective. Yet the scale of planning is urban—the infrastructure of a town meant for more presence than currently fills it. Streets drain into channels that do not flow. Stairways ascend to doorways that open onto emptiness. It is settlement-scale, organized, layered in its construction—walls within walls, terraces stepping down toward the marsh—but inhabited lightly, if at all.

No animals move through this place. No birds trace the warm mist. No insects hum in the reeds. The fauna is absent so completely that the absence feels like a property of the air itself.

The sound is near-total silence. The weeping rock makes no drip audible to the ear. The water sits without lap or gurgle. The wind, if it exists, passes without voice through the sharp high ridges. Even the vegetation seems to grow without rustle.

Light is ordinary—a clear, diffused brightness that comes through the constant moisture without shadow drama. The mist glows softly; the black pools reflect a flat, even sky.

And nothing changes. The settlement stands in perfect equilibrium. The buildings do not settle further into the marsh; the moss does not overgrow; the reeds do not spread or retreat. The water neither rises to claim the streets nor falls to leave them dry. This is a young world, freshly formed, high and wet and warm, its town laid out with human order upon an inhuman stillness—sparse, silent, built of simple craft, and frozen in a state that never tilts, never decays, never wakes.
