A low basin, open and warming under a steady, unremarkable sky—light that holds without drama, illuminating without revealing. The elevation barely exists; you stand on the floor of a vast shallow dish, and the horizon is a flat line miles away, broken only by the occasional low hump of weathered stone. This is a regional space, not a close one—distance dominates, and between the scattered features lies an expanse of empty, polished ground.

The ground itself is the first fact. No soil, no debris, no accumulation—only mineral, settled into its final form after ages of abrasion. The rock is dark, warm to the touch, smoothed by water and wind into rounded ridges and shallow valleys. Erosion has finished its work here; nothing sharp remains. Every edge is worn to equilibrium, every slope settled into the posture it will hold forever. The surface glistens faintly with damp—not rain, exactly, but persistent condensation, a thin film of moisture that never quite collects and never quite dries, leaving faint white traces of mineral salt in the lower depressions.

Water exists in pockets. Dark, still pools sit in the worn hollows, warm as bathwater, reflecting nothing because nothing moves on their surfaces. They are scattered, sparse—miles of dry, warm stone between each one. The air is humid, carrying a metallic warmth, and occasionally a faint mist rises from the pools to hang in the low air before dissolving.

There are no plants. Not grass, not moss, not fungal web—only the mineral world and its own patterns. Biology has not established here; the place is pre-life or post-life, a chemistry without organisms. What life might exist is rare to the point of invisibility—perhaps deep in the warmest pools, simple structures of mineral and membrane that barely qualify as being, but on the surface there is only stone and water and sound.

The sound is the presence you feel before you understand it. It is loud—industrial in volume and tone—a deep, constant drone that fills the open air without source or variation. It does not echo; it simply is, emanating from everywhere and nowhere. The ground itself seems to hum through your feet. This is where the conflict of absence and machinery resolves: there are no buildings, no pipes, no artifacts, no constructed thing—built presence is essentially zero—yet technology is fully present, scaled and mechanized, operating at an industrial magnitude. The landscape itself is the factory.

Beneath the polished basalt, or perhaps embedded within it in vast, flat circular formations flush with the ground, run the engines—not made, but formed. Over eons, mineral accretion and geothermal pressure have carved crystalline lattices and metal-infused plates into shapes that function like turbines, pistons, pressure valves. They do not move visibly; the dynamic here is absolute stasis, an equilibrium so perfect it reads as stillness. But they turn, imperceptibly, at constant speed, driven by heat rising from deep below. They release warmed vapor through microscopic fractures in the rock, maintaining the damp air and the pool temperatures, generating the unceasing mechanical drone. The process is scaled across the region—dozens of these natural engines, separated by miles of empty stone, working in perfect synchronization, never accelerating, never faltering.

This is the weirdness: a factory without architects, without workers, without product in any recognizable sense. The machinery serves only its own continuation. The erosion is complete; the system is in balance; the sound never shifts in pitch; the water levels do not rise or fall; the warmth does not vary. Time passes here without leaving marks. The place is frozen not in cold, but in equilibrium—an industrial geology running in perpetual, unchanging present.

So the atmosphere settles into something sparse and heavy at once. You can walk for hours across the warm, rounded stone, passing only the occasional dark pool and the faint vibration of an unseen engine beneath your steps. The light remains normal, steady, indifferent. The fauna—if it exists—occupies the margins, rare and hidden, perhaps a single immobile crust in a pool, or nothing at all. The density is low; presence is scattered; emptiness is the dominant material.

And always, the loud, unwavering hum—like the breath of a world-sized machine that has achieved its purpose and requires nothing more.
