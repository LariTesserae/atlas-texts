The basin sits low—barely elevated, a warm depression pressed between sharper, newer ridges that have not yet rounded with age. The ground itself is young, fresh-formed: sharp fractures in the substrate expose compressed layers of root-fiber and humus that have not had time to settle. There is no stone, no mineral soil in the old sense. The terrain is a woven mat, dense enough to bear weight, yet alive, yielding in slow, muscular contractions as if the valley breathes in unison.

And it is filled—absolutely, without gap. This is not a forest with clearings; vegetation is total, the substrate and the superstructure at once. Every volume of air, every plane of ground or ceiling, is occupied by living mass arranged with the logic of settlement. Spires of hollow, segmented stalk rise in organized clusters—districts—connected by avenues of woven vine and membranous bridge. Some towers are thick as columns, others needle-thin; together they form blocks, intersections, plazas. The scale extends past the immediate: this is regional, a valley-sized city of pure plant matter, layered vertically so that “sky” is only another canopy, and “ground” is only another root-floor.

Water does not flow in rivers here. It is dampness made precise—intermittent, captured. Heavy droplets bead on waxy surfaces, caught in cup-shaped leaves that line the pathways like gutters. Condensation pools in cool pockets beneath dense overgrowth, filling root-bowls that serve as cisterns. Occasional pulses of rain fall not as storms but as local events, smelling of chlorophyll and fermentation, leaving the air humid but never flooded.

The light is ordinary—bright, unremarkable sun that filters through translucent membranes and thick foliage in shifting cathedral patterns of green and gold. Temperature holds steady and warm, sustained by the sheer metabolic density of the place.

But the sound is deafening. Not from animals—there are none. No insect hum, no bird call, no breath of beast. The fauna is absent, leaving only botanical noise, and that noise is immense. The constant rush of sap through vascular highways sounds like underground rivers forced through pipes. Hollow stalks resonate, producing low, droning chords as the architecture itself shifts. Cell division at this speed and density creates an unceasing crackle and snap: branches bursting through old sheaths, roots tearing through substrate, flowers opening with percussive pops that echo across districts. Plazas of drum-like fungus caps vibrate with internal hydraulic pressure, sending pulses through the root-net. The urban layout amplifies everything—sound travels along the organized corridors of this plant-city, turning growth itself into a roaring, turbulent storm-state that never pauses.

Because nothing here is static. The young erosion means sharp features, yes, but also instability—the ground is not settled, and neither is what grows upon it. Towers lean and correct themselves within hours. A street of woven ferns might constrict into a solid wall, then relax into an open arcade. Districts bloom in explosive white flowers that wither into brittle scaffolding within minutes, only to be overgrown instantly by crimson vines that digest the dead tissue. Growth and decay occur in the same visible motion, a continuous turbulent cycle. The architecture is not built and then inhabited; it is born, lives, dies, and is reborn in hyperactive flow.

The reality breakdown is nearly total. Geometry is unreliable. A path that leads forward may, without turning, deposit you elsewhere in the valley—because the root-substrate bends space, or because plants grow between locations faster than movement allows. There are “windows”—not glass, but thin membranes of translucent tissue—that do not show what lies behind them but other parts of the basin, or versions of it in parallel growth. The urban organization—streets, blocks, centers—is consistent enough to navigate, yet impossible: buildings grow downward from the sky-canopy as often as upward, anchored by aerial roots that drink from the mist. The distinction between organism and city has dissolved entirely. This is a single entity expressing itself in municipal form, dense and layered and near-touching at every point.

No technology exists—no devices, no fire, no cutting tools—because none is needed. The plants are their own instruments, their own infrastructure, their own deafening voice. In this warm, low, recently-emerged valley—this fresh wound or birth-place—life has taken every role except the animal. It builds with urban precision, sounds with storm intensity, moves with chaotic instability, and fills every available space with dense, impossible, pure-plant presence.
