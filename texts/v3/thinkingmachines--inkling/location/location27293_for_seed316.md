The world is flat.

Not approximately flat—*finished*. At a scale that bends the notion of landscape into something closer to a single, continuous membrane, the elevation holds at barely one unit above the water table, a planetary plane worn by eons until every mountain, valley, and fold has been ground into submission. This is the end-form of erosion: not a ruin, but a settlement. The rock remembers being high, remembers the violence of being raised, but now it lies in a near-perfect disc, curving gently with the planet’s own vastness, stretching to horizons that take days to cross and never reveal a rise.

And yet. The erosion is ancient—*primordial*—but the place is not dead. It is **turbulent**.

Because there is nowhere left for the energy to go upward, all the world’s remaining motion has turned inward and across. The flatness is not peaceful; it is a storm-state frozen into horizontal form. Warm, thick air moves in enormous, chaotic cells across the plane, never organizing into fronts or calm centers, just a perpetual, hyperactive churning. The ground itself trembles with the pressure of compressed history—every grain of ancient mountain dust packed so tightly beneath the surface that there is no pore, no gap, no emptiness between presences. The density is absolute: matter pressed against matter, water pressed against stone, vapor pressed against vapor. A saturation that excludes life not by hostility, but by full occupancy.

The water does not run here. There are no rivers, no channels, no slopes to guide flow. Instead, it **weeps** upward. The compressed substrate—dark, polished, bone-smooth—sweats warm moisture through microscopic fractures in its settled skin. The atmosphere, already heavy with condensation, receives this exhalation and holds it. Everywhere, the plane is wet: shallow, skin-deep sheets that shift without purpose, pools that appear and vanish according to the turbulence above them, and a constant, warm drizzle that falls not from clouds but from the air itself, as if the sky is also weeping and can no longer retain its own weight. The light is dim—filtered through this perpetual, saturated veil—so that visibility extends only a short distance before everything dissolves into gray-green haze.

Vegetation is **sparse**, almost an insult to the saturation. Where the compressed ground has split under microscopic shifts, thin black mats of fungal tissue cling, flat and patient, absorbing moisture without growing upward. There are no trees, no grasses, no claim to vertical space. Life has learned to stay horizontal, minimal, close to the substrate.

Fauna is **rare**—so rare that presence is felt more than seen. Occasionally, in the thickest pockets of vapor, shapes move at the edge of perception: not creatures of bone and purpose, but temporary coagulations of the turbulence itself, briefly holding form in the warm, moving water before dissolving back into the chaos. They make no sound of their own. The sound belongs entirely to the world.

It is **deafening**.

The roar comes from everywhere and nowhere: the massive, turbulent air masses scraping across the flat plane like an endless, invisible tide; the sharp, cracking reports as the ancient bedrock adjusts by millimeters under impossible pressure; the continuous, high hiss of water being forced through stone that has forgotten how to be porous. There is no silence to contrast it, no shelter. The sound is the plane’s true language, speaking its hyperactive instability in a language of pressure and flow.

Scattered across this vast, wet, flat darkness are **faint marks**—the only evidence of anything built, though *built* is too intentional a word. They are single artifacts, occasional and isolated: a flat disc of compressed substrate pushed upward by some forgotten geological breath; a low, circular wall no higher than a knee, worn smooth by eons of turbulent wind and weeping water. No technology made them—there are no tools, no devices, no traces of intelligence having shaped them with purpose. They are accidents that resemble intention, scattered so rarely across the continental expanse that finding one feels like encountering a thought the world almost had.

But the **weirdness** is deeper than strange shapes.

At this scale, with this density and this flatness, reality does not hold its usual shape. Distance is unreliable—the horizon seems closer or farther depending on the turbulence of the air between. The water behaves impossibly: standing in vertical, shivering sheets that do not fall; flowing slowly upward against the warm vapor; existing in states between liquid and air that have no name. Time, too, is uneven. Some patches of the plane feel older, more settled, more profoundly eroded than others, as if erosion happens at different speeds in different places, creating pockets where the ancientness is deeper, almost archaeological, next to regions that seem freshly disturbed by the turbulence.

The plane is **packed** not just with matter, but with presence. Every cubic unit is full—of compressed stone-flour, of warm suspended water, of deafening sound, of turbulent motion, of faint, broken reality. There is no room for anything new to enter. The world reached its final form long ago, and now it simply *is*, in constant, chaotic motion, a vast and dim and warm and wet monument to the end of change—except that it never stops changing, never stops weeping, never stops roaring, never stops being too full to allow anything else to exist.
