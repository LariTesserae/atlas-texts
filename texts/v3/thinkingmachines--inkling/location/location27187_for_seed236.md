You come upon it not by traveling far, but by reaching upward—past the tree-line of whatever world holds this altitude, into air that is thinner but not cold, warm with a geothermal patience that has nothing to do with sunlight alone. The place is small. A single hollow, a cathedral-scale bowl cradled in sharp, unweathered rock. Its rim is recent—geologically infant—black glass and pale basalt with edges that still hold the violence of their making. There is no valley leading in; the walls rise clean and abrupt, enclosing a space perhaps two hundred paces across, no more. A room open to sky, but sky seen through layers.

Inside, there is no water. Not in pools, not in streams, not in the bodies of things. The dryness is absolute, a sealed chemistry of vapor and mineral breath. And yet the lushness is undeniable—dense to the point of saturation, layered until the eye loses depth.

The floor is not soil. It is architecture.

Every surface has been built, but not by hands, not by tools, not by technology of any kind. The builders are the fauna—abundant, silent, present in multitudes that press near to touching without crowding into chaos. They are slow. They think in decades. They secrete, weave, calcify, and mound. From their collective labor the hollow has become a vertical settlement: porous towers of honey-colored shell and fibrous, leathery membrane rising in spiraling clusters; low domes knitted from translucent root-like tendrils; hanging gardens of dry, swollen tissue that store not moisture but warm chemical sap—amber, viscous, non-aqueous. The structures are urban in density—streets of shadow between them no wider than a shoulder, levels stacked upon levels, infrastructure woven into infrastructure—but every wall breathes, every arch is alive, every bridge a ligament still growing.

The vegetation participates in the same logic. There are no leaves in any familiar sense. Instead, thick mats of copper-green velvet cover every horizontal plane, not drinking but absorbing: heat, trace gas, the faint exhalations of the deep vents that warm the hollow from below. From these mats rise bladder-ferns—pale, translucent, filled not with water but with a clear, syrupy fluid that glistens like resin. They grow in thickets so dense they form canopies, filtering the normal light into something softer, greener, scattered with slow-moving shadows. The lushness is a lushness of bulk, of layered presence, of life so thick it replaces air with texture.

The temperature holds steady—warm, slightly humid with exhaled vapor, never scorching, never cool. At this elevation, such warmth should be impossible. Here it is given, a slow exhale from the young earth beneath, maintained as patiently as everything else.

The fauna move, if they move at all, like growing things. You might stand in the center—if there is a center; the layout is circular, recursive—and feel watched not by eyes but by presence. Thousands. Soft bodies nestled in shell-cities, antennae or feelers extending with geological patience, processing, building, maintaining. They make almost no sound. The hollow holds a near-perfect silence: 0.1 on any scale of noise. Only the faint, occasional creak as a new layer of shell hardens. Only the whisper of syrup moving through a translucent bladder. Only your own breath, which sounds suddenly loud and wet in a world that has abandoned water entirely.

The weirdness is subtle, not theatrical. The spirals of the towers are too regular, as if guided by a mathematics the builders do not understand. The light, passing through the translucent canopies, sometimes falls in patterns that do not match the openings above—angles slightly wrong, as if the hollow exists in more directions than it should. The warmth has a taste, mineral and faintly sweet. And despite the abundance, despite the density that should feel overwhelming, there is a strange stillness, a sense that everything here is waiting not for an event, but for the next imperceptible increment of growth.

The dynamics are glacial in human terms. Nothing here rushes. The erosion is young—the sharp walls, the fresh scars, the unrounded stones—all of it recent, all of it slowly, over centuries, being softened not by water but by chemical breath and the patient abrasion of living tissue. Change happens across long durations, weak gradients of heat shifting by fractions, structures rising millimeters per year, the settlement expanding like a slow exhalation.

You stand in a bounded world—small, enclosed, high and sharp and warm. A dry garden, a biological city without citizens, without streets named, without devices. Only presence. Only the dense, silent, lush occupation of a young hollow that has never known rain, and does not miss it. The air moves barely, carrying the scent of warm resin and calcified dust. You realize the place is not empty of life; it is full to the brim with a life that built itself a town inside a crater and decided, long ago, to grow there forever, quietly.
