You stand on something that is not ground but weathered substrate—compressed, dry, and ancient—spread across a continent that has no name because no river has ever cut a valley here. The elevation is high, 1.7 of high: a broad plateau lifted near the brittle edge of the atmosphere, where the air is cool (1.3), thin enough to make the light (2.6) behave differently—blinding, unfiltered, falling in hard planes that bleach every surface to bone and silver. There is no water (0.5). Not in cloud, not in vein, not in chemistry. The world runs on dry reaction: mineral catalysts, atmospheric trace-gas metabolics, crystalline osmosis through fibrous membranes that have never known a drop.

And yet vegetation is total (2.9).

Not forests. Not fields. The plant-mass here is architecture and substrate at once—continent-scale organisms scattered with deliberate sparseness (0.8) across the vastness (2.9). They are called by no one, but if you travel for days across the bright mineral flats—days of emptiness, of dust that does not cling because there is no humidity—you will come upon one. A Spindle. A Dry-Mill. A living factory grown, not built (0.5).

Each is enormous: a twisted, hollow column or radial disc of lignin and mineral-fiber, hundreds of miles in span, rooted not in soil (there is none) but into the compressed, eroded (2.2) bedrock itself. The erosion is established, settled—this highland has existed in its own state long enough for the wind to polish the flat spaces between spindles into glass-smooth pans, and for the spindles themselves to wear smooth channels in the stone where they rotate. They turn. Slowly. With industrial (1.8) inevitability. Internal gears of woody tissue and crystalline vein interlock; they grind mineral dust into finer mineral dust, exhaling huge, dry currents of processed air and sound.

The sound (2.8) is deafening. Not a single noise but a layered, turbulent (2.9) roar that fills the sparse spaces between them. The grinding of fibrous gears. The howl of wind forced through hollow cavities in the plant-mass. The sharp cracks of growth—because they are never still. They expand, contract, split, and reseal in chaotic cycles (Solaris-like, hyperactive), consuming the bedrock and exhaling something like energy, though there are no wires, no towers, no artifacts. The technology is biological and mechanized all at once: scaled production without construction.

The density remains low (0.8). For every Spindle, there are hundreds of miles of open, bright, cool emptiness—salt-flats of crushed mineral, fossilized fiber-dust, empty skies. But within each presence, vegetation is absolute. No stone visible inside. Only woven cellulose, pale dry bark, crystal leaf-structures that do not photosynthesize so much as catalyze, pulling energy from friction and chemical contradiction.

Fauna (1.6) is abundant, scattered, adapted. Small, hard creatures—fiber-moths with wings of brittle membrane, dust-rats that burrow in the hollows, wind-riding larvae that travel the turbulent corridors between spindles—thriving in the constant motion and noise. They live in the machinery of life, nesting in the grinding joints, feeding on the powdery byproduct.

The weirdness (1.3) is present but restrained. This is not a dream-place. The physics hold: cool high air, bright unfiltered star, dry chemistry, biological industry. It is merely a world where the boundary between plant and engine dissolved long before anyone arrived to name it. The turbulence (2.9) is the state of being—storms do not come and go; the storm is permanent, generated by thousands of slow-turning biological mills across a continent without water, reshaping themselves and their empty spaces in an eternal, deafening, balanced (2.2) chaos.

You can feel it in your chest: not thunder, not water-rush, but a deep, continuous industrial breath of living things too large to be called trees, too mechanical to be called beasts, scattered like islands across a bright, cool, dry sea that has never existed, never needed to—because here, everything drinks what is not wet.
