The continent is young—lifted sharp and recent from the deep, its erosion still in infancy. There are no softened hills, no weathered valleys, only knife-ridge mountains and plateaus that hold the angles of their birth. At this elevation—two kilometers above any memory of sea—the air is thin in one sense and impossibly full in another. The temperature rests near the absolute floor: not merely winter, but a cold that has forgotten warmth ever existed. And yet the light is relentless. It comes without heat, a blinding, flat brilliance (the sky darker at its zenith, a void against blinding silver) that reflects off every surface until shadow becomes a rare substance, black and absolute.

Water does not flow here in the old ways. Rivers are impossible; the young rock has not yet carved them, and the cold holds moisture captive in other states. Instead, dampness lives in the interstices—intermittent, patient. Condensation beads on every crystalline face, freezes into glassy skins, then sublimates slowly back into the air. Occasional precipitation falls not as rain but as frozen needles, or as a kind of snow that settles like dust, feeding pockets of hidden moisture in the cracks between peaks. The dryness is vast, but it is a dry that breathes.

Against this, vegetation is lush—almost offensively so. Not trees; there is no soil deep enough, no warmth to drive wood upward. Instead, the rock is buried under dense, interlocking mats that grow in sheets meters thick, a carpet with no gaps. Crystalline mosses with vascular structures like drawn copper wire. Fungal lattices that harvest the condensation with microscopic efficiency. They grow in perfect, repeating geometries—hexagonal, radial, wrong—filling every fissure until the continent’s surface is not geology but biomass, silver-green and violet-black under the constant bright. Nothing is bare. Nothing is empty.

That is the density of it: packed to saturation. Every cubic meter is occupied. Within the vegetable carpet, fauna is overwhelming—not visible as discrete animals from any distance, but present in every fiber. They are nested, embedded, crawling through the growth like blood through tissue. Some are minute, innumerable, turning the moss into a moving field; others are massive, slow, fixed in place like organs within a greater body. There is no “outside” of this life, only degrees of immersion. The air itself, despite the height, is thick with them—spores, floating larvae, electrical organisms that drift in the bright cold like plankton in an invisible ocean.

There are no buildings. Nothing constructed, no roads cutting the ridges, no artifacts of hands. The built world is absent—untouched, undeveloped. And yet the technology is everywhere. The fauna are not merely living; they are manufacturing. Massive organisms—some anchored like living factories, others crawling at speeds imperceptible—function as engines. Their bodies contain grinding chambers of bone and mineral, pumping membranes that move fluids in slow, industrial rhythms, electrical nodes that arc with bright, cold bioluminescence. They process the sharp young rock, breaking it down, transforming it, excreting new crystalline substances. Together they form a distributed machinery with no walls, no blueprints—just bodies arranged across the continental scale in patient networks. Electricity moves between them in slow pulses, visible as faint glows threading through the dense vegetation. Production happens at the scale of geology, but it is production nonetheless: scaled, mechanized, continuous.

The sound is loud—constant, low-frequency, a drone that crosses the vast distances of the plateau. It is not the sound of wind (there is little wind, the gradients are weak) but the collective operation of this biological industry: millions of slow pumps, grinding joints, the hiss of chemical exchange, the deep resonance of massive bodies shifting millimeters per year. It fills the bright cold air like a held note.

Everything here moves slowly. The dynamic is patient, gradual, operating across durations that make centuries brief. The creatures cycle through long metabolic phases; the vegetation grows toward the light at rates visible only across generations; the processing of the young mountains is a digestion that will take eons. Change is minimal. Flux is weak. The weather of this continent—vast enough to generate its own—is stable: perpetual bright, perpetual cold, perpetual damp. The sharp peaks do not round; they are hollowed slowly from within, ground away by the embedded machinery, but the shape remains, sharp and new, for now.

What makes it strange—beyond the biology that behaves like factory equipment, beyond the absence of structures in a landscape of pure industry—is the wrongness of its organization. The symmetries do not belong to natural chaos. The plants grow in grid-like lattices, circuit-like. The condensation flows along invisible channels, following the electrical fields between the organisms. The creatures move with radial precision, their limbs arranged like gears waiting for motion. The bright light does not merely illuminate; it drives, organizing the biological machinery with a precision that feels engineered. There is a sense that this place is not wild, but is instead the first, slow emergence of something that has not yet decided whether it is ecosystem or machine—only that it is packed, bright, cold, and loud with its own patient purpose.
