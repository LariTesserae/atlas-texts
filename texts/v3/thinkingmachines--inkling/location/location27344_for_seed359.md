This is a small place—no wider than a cathedral nave, but lower, pressed flat, a shallow dish of warm dry air sealed beneath a curved, translucent shell. You could cross it in a few hundred steps. Its edges are sharp, fresh, unweathered: panels of compressed mineral felt and woven salt-fiber joined by hand-knotted seams, the cuts still bright where primitive tools—bone awls, simple copper blades, wooden presses—severed and shaped them. Nothing here was found. Everything was made, laid, tied, pressed into being recently enough that the structure still remembers its birth. There is no earth beneath, no bedrock that predates the craft. The floor is a dense mat of felted glass-grain and dry ceramic wool, warm underfoot, yielding slightly like the back of a large animal that has never learned to breathe water.

And yet it is lush.

Without a drop of moisture—no dew, no seep, no hidden spring—the vegetation thrives in dense, layered saturation. Broad, dark-green leaves spread in rosettes across the woven floor, their surfaces matte and slightly rubbery, drinking nothing but heat and the soft, steady light that filters through the ceiling in even, normal-brightness waves. Vines hang in thick curtains from the shell above, knotting themselves into living lattices, their tendrils ending not in flowers but in small, closed fists of dry fiber that slowly unfurl by millimeters over long months. They grow downward and upward at once, indifferent to gravity’s usual courtesy, and where they touch the walls they adhere with a dry adhesion, leaving no stain, feeding on air and warmth alone. The color is deep—forest-dark, almost black-green—so that the small space feels saturated, near-touching, every volume filled with leaf and stem and the slow, patient architecture of rootless growth.

The fauna is abundant and mostly silent. Small creatures move through the undergrowth in patient procession—soft-bodied things no larger than a palm, their shells woven from the same dry fiber as the walls, moving with the sluggish grace of beings who have never known thirst. Some crawl; some hang suspended in the vine-curtains, rotating slowly like dry seeds in honey that has forgotten to be wet. There are hundreds, layered one behind another, yet they make almost no sound: only the faint, dry rustle of leaf against ceramic wool, the occasional click of a primitive joint settling in the ceiling, a whisper of movement too slow to call a footfall. They feed on the vegetation not by tearing but by proximity—standing near a leaf until it dims, then moving on.

The air is warm—comfortably, persistently warm—and utterly still, with only the weakest circulation, a breath that takes years to complete its loop. It smells of warm resin, compressed salt, and the faint metallic sweetness of the simple metals used in the walls. There is no echo, because the dense vegetation and woven surfaces swallow sound; the quiet here is not empty but full, padded, held.

But reality in this enclosed grove is not stable.

The space is larger inside than the shell should allow—step toward one wall and the distance subtly lengthens, not enough to alarm, only enough to remind you that measurement is a local agreement, not a law. The plants cast shadows that fall in directions the light does not justify, and sometimes a shadow pools on the floor like dark water, only to evaporate upward in slow, dry spirals. The fauna possess faces that are also functional—mouths that open like hinges, eyes arranged in patterns that suggest doors, and they move with an awareness that is not quite intelligence but not quite instinct, as if they are following instructions written in a language that has not yet been spoken aloud. Time here is thick and slow; change happens across long durations, a vine extending, a creature turning, the warm air shifting its invisible currents. Nothing rushes. Nothing erodes quickly—the sharp edges of the construction remain sharp, the young formation holding its shape with stubborn, recent pride.

It is a world made by primitive hands for purposes that have been forgotten, or perhaps never named: a sealed, low, warm hollow where lush life thrives without water, where abundant silent beings navigate a dense, woven landscape of dry green, and where the rules of space and substance relax just enough to let you feel that you are standing inside something that was built not only with tools, but with a different understanding of what needs to be true. You are small within it. The ceiling is close. The vines are near-touching. And in the quiet, warm, waterless dark-green, everything waits, growing slowly, made entirely, and strange.
