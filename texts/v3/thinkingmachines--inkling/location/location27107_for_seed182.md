The hollow is new. You can tell by the walls—black basalt and glassy obsidian that haven’t learned softness yet, their fractures still knife-sharp, glistening with a mineral sweat that hasn’t had centuries to dull. It sits far below the surface, not in an ancient cavern but in a wound that opened recently, perhaps by heat or by pressure, leaving a chamber roughly the span of a cathedral nave but compressed in every other direction, as if the earth forgot to finish the room and packed it full instead.

It is hot. Not the dry blast of a desert, but a wet, heavy heat—geothermal and industrial both—thick enough to taste, carrying the sweet rot of lush growth and the ozone tang of old electricity. The air is damp in pockets, intermittent: here a curtain of warm condensation falling from a pipe joint in slow, patient drips; there a shallow pool in a floor depression, black and still, steaming gently; everywhere else, a humid cling that beads on every surface.

And surfaces are saturated. This is a settlement pressed into one bounded volume—urban density without sky, layered construction without horizon. Catwalks of rust-streaked iron weave overhead like vine-choked streets. Brick smokestacks rise only to disappear into the root-webbed ceiling. Pumps, bellows, and slow-turning gears occupy every gap, not arranged with human spaciousness but packed, fully occupied, a machinery of scaled production that has been running so long and so quietly it seems to have become topography. Between them, vegetation is not an invader but a co-occupant—lush, tropical, relentless. Ferns unfurl from gear-casings. Thick vines wrap pipes and hang in curtains that divide the space into green, dripping corridors. Moss and some broader-leafed growth carpet the walkways, fed by the warmth and the occasional rain that falls not from sky but from the sweating rock and leaking conduits above.

Light is dim, industrial, and strange. A few sulfur-yellow bulbs hang at irregular intervals, their glow absorbed immediately by the green-black density. Some machines emit their own faint luminescence—blue arcs in glass tubes, the dull red pulse of a furnace door—while certain plants carry a faint phosphorescence, not bright enough to illuminate but enough to reveal shapes: a root, a valve, the curve of a wall. Shadows do not fall sharply; they pool and linger, as if the darkness here has weight.

The sound is mostly quiet. There is no wind, no distant traffic, no animal call—fauna is nearly absent, rare as a mistake. Instead, a low, subsonic thrum from the engines, so constant it becomes silence’s foundation. Occasionally: the tick of cooling metal contracting. The drip of condensation into a pool. The slow, grinding rotation of a large gear that takes an hour to complete one turn. Everything moves slowly. The dynamics are patient. Heat circulates in long, lazy waves. The plants grow visibly over days, not minutes, extending tendrils toward the warmest pipes. Rust forms in real time, but gradually, like a tide. Even the young, sharp rock weeps its mineral water without urgency.

What makes it strange—what makes it feel held together by rules not quite natural—is the intimacy of the built and the grown. The pipes do not merely pass through vines; in places they have become vines, metal barked over with living tissue that pulses with sap rather than steam, though both emerge. Some structures seem to have been constructed and then forgotten, left to be colonized, yet they continue to function, tended by no visible hand, producing something—heat, pressure, a slow trickle of purified water—according to cycles that predate memory. The air tastes of copper and sweetness. Gravity feels familiar but slightly delayed, as if the depth below the surface has thickened it. And in the quiet, if you stand still long enough among the packed presence of machine and leaf, you can sense the place is not dead but dormant in its own vast, slow way—an urban chamber, an industrial grove, a recent hollow that has already become its own world, fully occupied by the patient marriage of sharp rock, hot damp, dim light, and lush, unhurried life.
