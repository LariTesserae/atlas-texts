You stand—if standing is the right word—upon the Velum, a high world worn flat by time. The elevation is not dramatic in the sharp way of young mountains but in the deep, rounded way of ancient ones: ridges like the backs of sleeping beasts, smoothed over eons until they are almost featureless, save for the occasional dome of darker stone that refuses to erode. The air is cool, thin, and utterly dry. Not arid in the way of sand—there is no sand here, no dust that remembers water—but dry in a chemical sense: the atmosphere holds no moisture, never has, and the chemistry that moves through this world does so in other solvents, in cool viscous saps and crystalline vapors that condense on metal and root alike.

Below and around, the vegetation is total. It is not a forest in any familiar sense; there are no trees scattered among empty ground. Rather, the ground itself is alive. A continuous substrate of interwoven root-fiber and spore-mat, grey-green and metallic in the dimness, stretches across every visible surface of the plateau. It breathes slowly, expanding and contracting with thermal rhythms, releasing cool, sweet-tasting gases that have nothing to do with oxygen as you might know it. This is the Root-Veil: the base layer of a pure-plant ecosystem that has achieved its final form. It fills the space completely, sealing the ancient stone beneath it.

Yet the density is strange. For all this total coverage, the world feels sparse. The Root-Veil is low—ankle- to waist-deep in most places, rippling like dark water—but at vast, irregular intervals, it sends up its vertical expressions: the Chorus-Stalks. These are enormous, cathedral-scale organisms, kilometers apart, rising from the carpet like silent towers. They are ancient in the way the erosion is ancient; they have stopped growing. They have settled into their final architecture, hollow and resonant, their outer walls thick with compressed lignin that has turned almost to ceramic over millennia. Between them, the spaces are open, extending past the immediate into a regional vastness. You could travel for a day across the Veil and see nothing but the low carpet, the dim sky, and the distant silhouette of another Stalk.

The light is dim. A cool, red-tinged sun—if it is a sun—sits behind a permanent veil of floating spores and crystalline pollen, so illumination comes mostly from below and within: the Root-Veil glows with a faint bioluminescence, cool blue-green, and the Chorus-Stalks emit a deeper, rhythmic pulse, as if breathing light rather than air. The settlements—yes, there are settlements, urban in scale but scattered like archipelagos—are built into the bases and hollows of these Stalks, or perched upon the flatter ridges where the root-mat has hardened into pavement-like crust. They are not dense cities. Each is a cluster, a node: perhaps a few hundred structures arranged in geometric patterns that seem to ignore gravity, suspended by technologies that manipulate tension fields or grow support-cables of hardened sap. The buildings are grown as much as built, their walls living root-fiber reinforced with nanoscale lattices that repair themselves, never decaying, never rusting—because rust requires water, and water does not exist here.

This technology is beyond modern comprehension. There are no visible engines, no combustion, no wires carrying electricity in familiar forms. Instead, energy is drawn from the temperature differential between the cool thin air and the slight internal warmth of the Stalks; it is stored in molecular batteries woven into the building-mass itself; it is communicated through resonant frequencies carried by the substrate. The towns shift, millimeters per century, riding the slow flow of root-growth beneath them. They are connected not by roads—there is no need to travel the open spaces between them often, and when travel occurs it is through sound-corridors: focused vibrations that allow small craft or messages to move across the Veil without touching it.

And it is loud.

The sound is constant and layered, reaching 2.5 on the scale of presence. The wind, thin but persistent at this elevation, moves through the hollow Chorus-Stalks and turns them into colossal instruments, emitting drones that shift with temperature and spore-load. The fauna—abundant, active—adds to it. These are not water-based creatures. They are cool-metabolism beings, large and slow-moving, with hollow skeletal structures that resonate, that call to one another across the kilometers with low-frequency vibrations that you feel in your chest more than hear with your ears. They migrate between Stalks in patterns older than the settlements, feeding on mineral exudates from the Root-Veil, on spores, on the sound-energy itself perhaps. They are strange: some are flat and disc-like, gliding over the carpet on fields of compressed air; others hang in the upper hollows of the Stalks, filtering nutrients from the circulating vapors. Their presence is scattered—abundant in total, but sparse in any given view—matching the density of the world.

The ecosystem is in motion, dynamic despite its ancient settledness. Spore-storms roll slowly across the plateau like weather fronts made of grey-green fog, carrying seeds and chemical messages between Stalks. The Root-Veil itself pulses with biological pace: growth, dormancy, brief flowering events where the carpet suddenly produces vertical spikes of crystalline bloom, then retracts them over months. The fauna follows these rhythms. The towns adjust their harmonic outputs to match them, preventing interference, living within the biological cycle rather than against it.

Weirdness permeates this stability. Gravity feels slightly uneven here; near the oldest Stalks, objects fall a little slower, or sound travels in curved paths. The boundary between plant and architecture is not sharp—a wall might leaf overnight, or a floor might heal a crack by growing new fiber over it. Time moves with the pace of the root-system: fast in the storm-fronts, glacial in the settled ridges. You sense that this world has reached an endpoint not of death but of perfect, strange equilibrium—no water, no erosion that can reshape it further, no evolution that needs to happen. Only the loud, cool, dim continuation of what it is.

Walking—if you must—across the Velum, you pass through spaces that feel like rooms in a temple made of absence. The vegetation is everywhere underfoot, but the presence of life is scattered, sparse, immense in scale. Then, without warning, you reach one of the nodes: a cluster of geometric, glowing structures grown from black root-carbon, suspended in harmonic fields, inhabited by beings who have never known rain, who measure distance in resonance and time in the growth-rings of Stalks that will never fall. The air is cold. The sky is close and dark with spores. The world hums, loudly, actively, alive in its final, ancient form.
