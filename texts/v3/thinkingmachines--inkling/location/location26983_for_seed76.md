The plateau does not end; it forgets to. You stand at the edge of one of its groves, but the word “edge” is local poetry—here, distance is measured not in miles but in weather. This is the Bright Shelf, a continental highland lifted so far into thin atmosphere that the sky is not a dome but a close, burning wall. Temperature holds at a persistent cool, a crispness that never warms into comfort. Water has never existed here—not as river, not as ice, not as vapor in any meaningful quantity. The chemistry that supports life runs through minerals, through slow geothermal exhalations, through the plants themselves.

And yet, lushness.

Not everywhere. Density is low—vastness dominates. Between the groves stretches the Pale Flat: eroded silicate and compressed salt, worn by eons of wind into rounded, bone-smooth undulation. The erosion is settled, complete; this place reached its form long ago and has simply continued, polished to equilibrium. Nothing sharp remains. Even the scattered stones are pebbles, worn to uniform softness. The ground cracks in geometric patterns, but the cracks are old, filled with fine dust that does not move unless the wind wills it.

The groves are scattered like islands—dense, dark, lush pockets separated by hours or days of empty walking. Within them, vegetation thrives without water, without chlorophyll as earth knows it. The plants are tall, fibrous, almost fungal in their architecture: silvery-black spires that branch into translucent membranes, catching the blinding light and seeming to drink it directly. Their surfaces are not green but deep violet and mirrored slate, adapted not to photosynthesis in any familiar sense but to some slower mineral metabolism—roots that descend for hundreds of feet into warm rock, drawing up dissolved silicates and trace gases. Where they cluster, they form canopies three, four stories high, layered with hanging tendrils that sway in the constant breeze. It is lush, almost crowded inside—life packed into itself, a biological contradiction thriving in absolute aridity.

Outside, nothing. The flat extends to horizons that belong to weather itself. It is pure, untouched nature, but nature that followed a different instruction manual—one without water, without warmth, without the familiar green urgency of earth.

You walk across miles of worn flatness. The air is thin, cool, bright with reflected fire. Then, suddenly, a grove—dense, strange, lush—rising from the emptiness like a thought made physical. Inside, life holds its breath in silent abundance. Outside, the Shelf continues: vast, quiet, eroded into its own perfect, waterless balance.
