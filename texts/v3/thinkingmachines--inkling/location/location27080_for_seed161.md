The stone has finished settling. That is the first thing to understand about the Hollow: the erosion is complete, not in progress. The walls—dark basalt worn to the texture of old leather—rise nearly four hundred meters around a floor barely three hundred across, a vertical extremity compressed into a cathedral’s footprint. Every sharpness was ground away millennia ago. There are no fresh fractures, no scree, no recent collapses. The rock has reached its final form, smooth and patient, holding warmth like a stored breath.

There is no water. Not in pools, not in veins, not in the air as anything that falls or runs. The warmth—steady, dry, body-close—comes from deep geothermal patience rather than sun, for the light here is dim: a perpetual twilight filtered through high haze and the narrow opening far above. What little sky is visible is a gray coin, distant and unblinking.

And yet the vegetation is lush.

It does not drink. It feeds. Thick, rubbery mats—colors of burnt copper, blackened bronze, and deep violet—cling to every surface not claimed by stone or shelter. They grow from mineral dust caught in cracks, sustained by chemical exchange with the abundant fauna and with the warm, dry air itself. Their leaves are waxy and dense, layered upon one another in near-touching saturation, turning the walls into living drapery. In places they hang like heavy curtains, touching shoulders as one moves through them. They do not rustle like grass; they shift with a slow, heavy sound, like wet leather being dragged, though they contain no moisture at all.

The settlement is woven into this vertical grove. Primitive tools—shaped stone, carved bone, bound fiber—have arranged the human presence into a dense, layered town that occupies every stable ledge and the sloped floor. Structures are not built over the vegetation but into it: low stone cells stacked like honeycomb, connected by rope ladders and narrow ramps, their roofs overgrown with the same dark mats. It is urban in its saturation—there is no empty space, only pathways, thresholds, and the press of bodies—yet the scale remains intimate, hamlet-sized, contained within the bowl. Smoke from small fires (burning compressed plant matter and mineral oil) hangs in low, slow currents, never rising far, never clearing.

The fauna is abundant and loud. Dark-feathered birds—small, quick, with voices that carry sharp and repetitive—nest in the vegetation and in the rafters of the stone cells. They call constantly, and the smooth walls throw the sound back in dense echo, so the Hollow is never silent. Insects with hard shells move in visible streams across the warm stone, feeding on fallen plant matter, providing food in turn. There are also larger, slow-moving creatures—heavy-bodied, furred in short black hair—that navigate the lower paths among the people with an old familiarity, neither wild nor fully tame, part of the settlement’s ecology.

The technology is hand-level: stone blades, woven nets, fired clay vessels, simple metal pins worked from native ore. Nothing here was made quickly. The dynamics are slow—plants expand by centimeters per year, the rock shifts only in thermal breathing, and the people follow rhythms older than memory. Change comes across generations, not seasons.

To stand in the center is to feel enclosed by lush darkness, loud with living sound, warm and dry, surrounded by the finished work of ancient time—an extreme elevation made small, a settlement made dense, a world without water that somehow thrives in its absence.
