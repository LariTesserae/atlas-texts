You stand in a hollow that has finished becoming itself.

The space is small—cathedral-sized, bounded, a single chamber with edges you can sense but not see clearly through the air. Elevation is almost nothing; this is a floor near the bottom of something older, a low pocket where coolness collects and stays. The temperature holds at a steady, damp chill—never freezing, never warming, just cool enough that moisture clings to every surface without ever quite running dry.

Vegetation is total. Not a garden, not a forest, but substrate. The ground, the walls, the ceiling are all plant: compressed root-felts turned to leather, bark that has grown into load-bearing ribs, mosses and slow lichens fused into a continuous living masonry. Yet the density is hollow. Within this small envelope, there is almost nothing per cubic breath. The plants do not crowd; they form the architecture and then withdraw, leaving vast volumes of empty air suspended between them. Massive columns of ancient wood-fiber—rounded, flattened by unimaginable time—stand spaced like the piers of a drowned chapel. Between them: void. The vegetation fills every boundary but refuses to fill the space.

This is the final form of erosion. Not ruined, not decaying—settled. The ribs are worn smooth to a near-geological flatness, their growth and their rot having reached equilibrium eons ago. Nothing erodes further. Nothing grows new. The place is primordial in its stability, an end-state ecosystem where all gradients have vanished.

The water is intermittent, a dampness rather than a presence. Condensation beads in cool pearls along the root-columns and drips at a rhythm so constant it sounds like counting. Small pockets pool in the flattened hollows of the floor—black, still, reflecting the muted normal light that filters through translucent membranes of old leaf-tissue in the ceiling.

And then there is the sound: loud, relentless, the dominant presence in all that emptiness. It is industrial—not the sound of buildings or artifacts, for there are none, no steel, no concrete, no made thing at all—but the sound of scaled production happening inside the plants themselves. Massive vascular engines pump through the woody columns with a low, throbbing mechanical drone, like distant factories operating underwater. Valves of hardened pith open and close with rhythmic precision. Sap moves in volumes that suggest assembly lines. The hollow chamber amplifies it. The hum never stops, never varies in pitch or tempo. It is the heartbeat of an ecosystem that long ago became machinery.

Fauna is abundant but lost in the volume. Small creatures—winged things, burrowers, climbers—inhabit the surfaces and traverse the empty air between columns. You see them rarely: a flash of movement across a root-beam, a scattering of insects through a beam of light, the soft percussion of something nesting in the moss. They are numerous, but spread across such hollow space that each one seems alone.

The dynamic is absolute: never changing. The condensation drips at the same rate. The mechanical hum holds its note. The cool air does not circulate; it simply is. The animals follow paths worn smooth not by weather but by repetition across ages. There are no seasons here, no storms, no shifts. This is a frozen equilibrium at the end of life, not dead but done.

Light is ordinary—daylight, neither harsh nor mystical—passing through the plant-ceiling in greenish, diffused columns, falling on the flat organic floor and disappearing into shadowed hollows where water waits.

There are no ruins to discover, no tools left behind. The technology is not separate from the world; it has been subsumed into the biology, into the industrial rhythm of roots that pump like engines, into the mechanized regularity of growth that never grows. The weirdness is minimal—this is strange only in its perfection, its refusal to alter. You are inside a small, low, ancient factory made entirely of living substrate, where the machinery breathes and the emptiness echoes with its work.
