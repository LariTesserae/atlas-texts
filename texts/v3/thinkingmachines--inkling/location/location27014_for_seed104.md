This is the **High Drowned** — though “high” and “drowned” should cancel each other, and here they do not. They have worn each other smooth, like the stone below.

You are suspended at an elevation that should be impossible for an ocean. Not a sea in a canyon, not a lake in a crater, but a summit completely filled: a continental plateau lifted to the upper registers of the world and then submerged in a warm, thick liquid that never falls. The physics that govern descent have been eroded away, worn flat and silent as everything else here. The water holds its height not by will, but by finality. It has settled into its form and remains.

The ground is visible in vast, interrupted sweeps — not soil, but primordial crust, near-flattened by an erosion so ancient it has no opposite. Mountains existed once. Now there are only shallow depressions, gentle curves worn to mathematical softness, stretching for distances large enough to contain their own weather. And indeed, the water moves. It is dynamic, always circulating: massive, sluggish currents like internal winds roll through the volume, pressure ridges that travel for leagues, thermal layers that shift with a glacial but constant pace. This is an ecosystem in motion without urgency, a weather system inside a bath.

The temperature is uniform and warm — not heated by any sun, but holding its own heat like memory. Light is dim, insufficient, a perpetual twilight without source or shadow. It seems to emanate faintly from the plants themselves, or perhaps it is the residual glow of the water’s long saturation with dissolved minerals. You do not see clearly; you sense presence by density.

And the presence is overwhelming.

The vegetation is lush, but “lush” is too gentle a word. It is dense — saturated, layered, packed into every available volume so that the water is not a medium through which things float, but a matrix they fill. Forests of kelp and coral-vine rise and drift in vertical thickets, anchored to the worn stone or to nothing at all, rooting in the liquid itself. They are ancient, settled, growing not quickly but constantly: slow expansions of tissue, the silent release of spores that hang like dust in the warm dimness, vines that interweave so thoroughly that open space is rare. The plants dominate. They are the architecture.

Fauna, by contrast, is almost a rumor. There are no schools, no movements of predators, no songs. Perhaps a single form moves through the thickets — too rarely seen to be named — but mostly the ecosystem is vegetal, self-sustaining through slow exchange, through growth and decay that happen in the same gesture.

Sound is nearly absent. The water’s great currents move without crash or splash; they are too dense, too weighted with life, to break surface (there is no surface, only a gradual thinning upward that never resolves into air). The only sounds are the quiet ones: the soft, distant pop of plant matter releasing its spores, the imperceptible groan of ancient stone adjusting to the pressure of ages, the silence of reality holding its shape. It is not empty silence; it is a silence so full it hums at the edge of hearing.

Here and there, scattered across the flat expanse, stand remnants — not ruins, exactly, but occasional artifacts. A pillar of unknown material, half-swallowed by vines. A partial arch that curves over nothing, its purpose eroded away with everything else. A single wall-fragment, smooth, without inscription. There is no technology here; these were made without devices, or perhaps they grew like the plants, or perhaps they are the bones of a presence that never needed to build because it was already complete. They are faint marks, scattered, unexplained.

The weirdness of this place is not an event but a condition. Reality here has broken not violently, but by exhaustion. Time is dense, layered like the vegetation; you feel it not passing but accumulating. The contradiction of high elevation and complete submersion does not resolve — it simply is, worn smooth like the mountains. You are deep underwater and simultaneously at the summit of the world. The scale is vast enough to make the contradiction meaningless; the density is high enough that it does not matter. This place has reached its oldest stable state, its final form: a warm, silent, submerged highland, lush beyond counting, active in its slowest rhythms, quietly holding together the broken physics that let it exist.

Nothing here asks to be understood. The water circulates. The plants grow in their silent, crowded world. The scattered structures remain, covered in life, without purpose. And the erosion continues, so slowly that it looks like stillness — but it continues, wearing the last sharp edges from stone and time alike, settling everything into the form it was always going to take.
