You stand at the edge of a shallow depression—barely a valley, more a sag in the land—where the ground has given up its claim to rock. This is **the Hollow Borough**, though “borough” suggests streets of stone and it has none. It is small: no wider than a cathedral’s nave and perhaps twice as long, a single bounded pocket sunk into the low warm earth (elevation barely a shrug above the surrounding plain). From the rim, you look down into a space that should be filled and is not.

The contradiction is the first thing you feel in your chest.

**Vegetation is total, yet density is void.** Every surface—floor, wall, ceiling, bridge, pillar—is alive. Not built *of* plants, but *being* plants: fused root-trunks braided like cable, fungal pavements that breathe, leaf-membranes stretched into translucent ceilings, bark-towers hollowed by centuries of slow growth. The architecture is urban in its discipline. There are avenues—suspended liana-cables that arch between towers in straight, intentional lines. There are plazas, flat discs of moss-carpet grown level as tables. There are districts: the Lower Root-Ward, dense and dark; the Vine-Bridge Quarter, high and swaying; the Sap-Square at the center, open to the filtered sky. Layered construction, settlement-scale, organized as any town—yet no tool touched it. No technology, only biology chewing, secreting, weaving itself into order over long time (erosion settled, weathered, in perfect balance with decay).

But between these living structures lies an emptiness so complete it feels engineered. Vast air. Near-void. You can stand on one root-bridge and see clear across the hollow to the opposite wall, fifty meters of unbroken hollow, because the “buildings” are spaced like the ribs of a whale, like the pillars of a ruined cathedral. The plants form the substrate—the ground you walk on is a spongy root-mat, the air you breathe is thick with spores released by the walls—but they do not fill the volume. They *define* it, leaving it hollow. Density: almost nothing per cubic breath.

It is warm (1.6). The hollow holds heat like a mouth. The plants metabolize slowly, radiating a green warmth; condensation (water: 1.4, intermittent, pocketed) beads constantly on the undersides of leaves and the smooth bark of towers, falling in occasional fat droplets—rain made by the place itself, not the sky. There are no rivers, only these gathered pockets: cup-leaves that hold silent pools, root-bowls filled with clear sap-water, the damp film that makes every surface glisten.

The light (1.9, normal) filters through the woven canopy-ceiling—thousands of leaves arranged like shingles, not blocking but translating sunlight into a steady green-gold. It is enough to see by, enough to cast shadows, but it never blazes. The shadows, however, do not behave. That is part of the weirdness (2.6).

**Reality here is in established breakdown.** Gravity pulls not downward but *inward*—toward the nearest massive plant-wall. If you step too far into the center of the Sap-Square, you feel the gentle tug of the root-towers at your back and shoulders, as if the hollow wants to keep you near its living edges. Sound—deafening, 3.0, the dominant law—does not merely travel; it stains. A particularly loud call from one of the fauna leaves a faint, vibrating after-image in the air, a shimmer of heat-distortion that lingers for minutes. The moisture sometimes falls upward, drawn by the respiration of the ceiling-canopy, droplets climbing the air like slow silver fish before being reabsorbed.

And the fauna—abundant (2.3), dynamic (2.4)—are the reason you rarely see emptiness as loneliness.

They are everywhere in the hollow, though rarely visible all at once. Small things: winged insects with translucent cases that beat like drums; burrowers that live inside the porous bark-towers, their passage marked by continuous clicking; gliders that launch from high vine-bridges and ride the thermal updrafts of plant-breath, leaving trails of phosphor-green dust. They move in cycles, migrations, predator-prey loops played out in the air columns and root-tunnels. They are noisy. The hollow amplifies them—the empty space is an instrument, the plant-walls are resonating bodies. Every creature’s call, every wing-beat, every sap-burst in the walls echoes and compounds. The sound is deafening: not a single roar but a layered, biological thunder—chirping, drumming, vibrating membranes, the creak of growing wood, the rush of fluid through root-veins, the constant brittle crackle of spores releasing.

Because there is no technology (0.3), nothing is silenced by machinery. Nothing is hunted with tools. The ecosystem is pure biological pace: growth versus consumption, sound versus silence, the constant rebuilding of the plant-city as old towers thicken and new vines reach across the void to bridge gaps that were never meant to be filled.

You move through it slowly. The ground gives underfoot—root-carpet, warm and damp. Above, a suspended road of woven fiber sways gently, leading toward the Vine-Bridge Quarter, but you hesitate because the sound is physically heavy here, pressing on your ears, vibrating in your teeth. A drop of condensation falls upward past your face, cool, impossible, then vanishes into a leaf-cup.

This place has existed long enough to have forgotten change (erosion 2.0). It is not decaying; it is settled. The urban layout—those straight avenues, those named districts—has grown into itself over centuries, reaching an equilibrium where construction and erosion are the same process. The weirdness is not an invasion but a native climate, like the warmth or the damp. You do not question the shadows that move half a second after their sources, or the way sound leaves color in the air, because here that is the established form.

You are in a small, bounded, hollow world: low, warm, entirely alive in its structure, almost empty in its volume, deafening in its life, and broken gently, permanently, in ways that feel not like error but like deep, old custom.
