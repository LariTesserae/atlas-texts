The land is called nothing yet, because it has not finished deciding what kind of place it will be. You stand on it—though “stand” implies a firmness that is only partly earned—and feel the warmth rising through your soles: not the heat of sun-baked stone, but the residual warmth of geology still cooling from its own birth. The elevation is low everywhere. From one horizon to the opposite, the continent lies flat as a held breath, a sheet of dark, glassy basalt and unweathered mineral crust, barely scratched by time. Erosion has not arrived. There are no river valleys, no canyons, no rounded hills softened by ages of wind. The surface is raw-formed, a pre-geography of smooth planes and shallow, fresh depressions where gas once escaped, leaving shallow bowls rather than craters.

And it is vast. The scale swallows weather; a cloud here does not pass over a hill but travels for days across an unbroken expanse of low, warm flatness, casting shadows that move like slow animals across the ground.

Water is absent in any form that remembers rivers. There are no lakes, no rain that pools, no morning dew beading on leaves—because the leaves are not that kind of thing. Yet the vegetation is lush. It fills every interval.

The plants are not green in the way of old-world forests. They are dense, fibrous, interwoven—tall stalks and mat-weaves of a silvery-grey tissue that draws its thirst not from liquid but from atmospheric chemistry and the mineral breath of the rock itself. They grow in continuous carpets and vertical thickets, forming walls and canopies without spaces between them. Where one organism ends, another begins immediately; there is no bare soil visible, no gap for a foot to fall through. The vegetation is lush in the manner of saturation, of total occupation. It smells faintly of warm copper and crushed seed-case, not of rot—there is no decay here, only conversion.

Within this woven biomass, the place becomes urban.

Not through tools—there is no technology, no metal, no fire-craft, no devices at all—but through the abundant fauna that live inside the weave. They are silent, numerous, and industrious: soft-bodied, many-limbed creatures of grey and bone-white, moving in constant, quiet procession through the fiber-avenues. Over generations of biological instinct, they have compressed the plant-matter into pathways, into districts, into rising spires and low domes. The “buildings” are grown-calcified structures—hardened root-cement and shell-secretion, shaped by the pressure of bodies and the chemistry of waste. The result is settlement-scale organization stretching for hundreds of miles: grid-like clearings where the weave parts to form streets; clustered towers of compressed fiber that serve as nesting cores; arched bridges of living rope spanning shallow depressions in the newborn rock.

It looks like a city built by something that never invented the wheel, yet understood density perfectly. Every space is occupied. The density is absolute—no emptiness between the presences of plant and creature and structure. You could travel for days and never find an open field, only variations in the texture of occupation: here tighter, there more porous, but always full.

The temperature holds steady and warm, fed by the ground’s lingering birth-heat and the metabolic warmth of so much living matter packed together. Normal light falls evenly—no strange colors, no shifting auroras—illuminating the grey-green weave in flat, clear tones. You can see for miles across the low plain, not because there is distance, but because the urban-weave organizes itself into corridors of sight, long straight avenues of compressed fiber running like seams through the continent.

The sound is almost nothing. Despite the dynamic activity—the constant slow growth of the weave, the migration of fauna through the avenues, the subtle geological settling of the crust—there is only silence, or sounds so quiet they register as pressure rather than noise: the faint tick of expanding fiber, the soft abrasion of bodies passing through narrow channels, the distant, low exhalation of the rock as it contracts in cooling. Nothing calls. Nothing strikes. The world is active—processes in motion everywhere, biological pace relentless, ecosystem in constant negotiation—but it is quiet about its work.

You move through it, and it accepts you as another presence in the packed space. There is no strangeness here, nothing that demands disbelief; this is simply how the place is, normal in its own emergence. The ground is still becoming ground. The city is still growing its streets. The vegetation is lush without thirst, the animals are abundant without clamor, and the vast, flat, warm expanse continues in every direction—an urban continent of biological construction, newborn, silent, and utterly, densely alive.
