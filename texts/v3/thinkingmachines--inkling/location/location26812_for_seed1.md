The flat does not end. That is the first thing to understand. At elevation barely above the old sea, eroded down through epochs until the mountains forgot themselves, the land spreads in every direction like a sheet of dark glass that never quite froze. It is cold—so cold that breath should vanish instantly, yet the air holds it, thick and wet, a condensing breath that clings to every surface. Water is not falling from above; it is rising, weeping from the mineral skin of the world, pooling in shallow basins that have no banks, no edges, just a gradual thickening of presence until ground becomes marsh becomes mirror becomes something else entirely.

There are no plants. Not a blade, not a lichen, not a stubborn fungal mat claiming the cracks. The substrate is older than biology’s patience: flattened stone, volcanic glass worn to silk, mineral precipitates layered like sediment in a jar that no one built. The ground is bare of green because green was never invited. Instead, the surface glitters with a faint, internal frost—salt and silica and something that might be rust, though rust requires iron and iron requires a forge, and there is no forge here, only the implication of one.

And yet: it is not empty.

The density is absolute. Across the open expanse—regional, valley-wide, reaching past any single vantage until the eye surrenders—the flat is packed. Saturated. There is no gap between presences. Every step, if one could walk here without sinking or being incorporated, would land on something. Not soil. Not stone. Presence.

The fauna are abundant, though they do not match the shape of animals as remembered elsewhere. They move because the place demands motion; this is not a dead world but an active one, processes cycling in perpetual low-temperature industry. They are the machinery, and the machinery is them. There are no factories, no buildings, no artifacts of construction—built is nearly zero—yet the tech is undeniable, industrial, scaled. It lives. It crawls, swims, hovers just above the weeping water, and processes.

Picture them: low, cold bodies with carapaces like polished turbine blades, segmented with ribs that vent steam in rhythmic pulses. They are dense in number—packed shoulder-to-shoulder across the flat—moving slowly but without cease, grinding mineral from the water, extracting what they need, exhaling byproducts into the dim air. Some are large, the size of rooms, dragging themselves through the shallow pools with sounds like wet metal being drawn across wet metal. Others are smaller, swarming in clouds just above the surface, so numerous that the air shivers with their collective passage. They feed on the minerals, on the heat differentials, on each other—predator and prey locked in a pace that is biological and mechanical simultaneously. There is an ecology here, but it is an ecology of engines.

The sound is loud. Constant. Not a single roar but a layered, industrial chorus: the grinding of bodies against stone, the hiss of vented pressure, the low-frequency thrum of thousands of moving parts working in near-unison. Underneath, the water itself makes noise—condensing, weeping, being drawn up through invisible channels in the flat, pumped by entities that need no pipes because they are the pipes.

Light is dim, barely 1.2—twilight made permanent, or perhaps the atmosphere is too thick with condensation and vaporized mineral to let the sun through properly, assuming a sun still applies. What illumination exists comes partly from the fauna themselves: faint, cold luminescence, blue-white, running along their segmented bodies like current through wire. They light each other. They navigate by the glow of their neighbors in this packed, open darkness.

But the reality here is broken. That is the true substrate, older than the flat itself.

The rules do not hold consistently. Water flows upward in invisible columns where the largest entities gather, not defying gravity so much as negotiating with it differently. Shadows fall in directions that have nothing to do with the dim glow; some bodies cast no shadow at all, while others cast three, or shadows that linger for seconds after the body has moved away. Time is not linear across the flat. In some zones, the grinding sounds arrive a moment before the motion that causes them. The fauna remember futures—this is visible in their movements, adjustments made to avoid collisions that have not yet occurred, or pausing to consume prey that has not yet arrived.

There are no structures because structures are unnecessary; the landscape itself is the mechanism. The ancient erosion—2.6, settled into final form—means this place has achieved its terminal shape. It will not change further, except through the constant, active motion of its inhabitants. The flat is the factory floor, the marsh is the coolant system, the cold is the operating temperature.

If you listen—loud as it is—you might hear patterns in the noise that are not mechanical but linguistic, though not in any human tongue. The fauna communicate through vibration, through the alteration of water pressure, through light pulses that carry meaning too dense to decode. They are abundant because they must be; the system requires saturation. Every presence touches every other presence. There is no solitude on the flat, no corner untouched, no horizon that offers emptiness—only more of them, extending, working, living in cold wet industry across a world that forgot plants and replaced them with purpose.

The air tastes of metal and wet stone. The temperature holds near absolute refusal of warmth. The erosion has finished its work, leaving this: an open, low, ancient valley without end, where reality fractures gently but constantly, where industrial life packs every inch in loud, dim, active procession, and where water weeps upward through mineral bones, feeding the endless, beautiful, broken machine.
