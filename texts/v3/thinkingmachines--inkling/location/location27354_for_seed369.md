You are in a bowl that should not be warm.

The elevation is high—shoulder-height to the thin air of old mountains—yet the temperature holds steady, a gentle, living warmth that rises from below rather than falling from above. The scale is intimate: you could walk from one side to the other in the time it takes to finish a thought, perhaps three hundred paces across a floor that has forgotten it was ever sharp. The ridges that hold this place are not cliffs but rounded shoulders, eroded over such patient ages that every edge has been persuaded into softness. They form a low, irregular wall, enclosing the hollow like the cupped hands of something asleep.

This is not new land. The erosion is complete, settled into its own identity. The rock—dark, iron-heavy, porous—has been worn not by violence but by persistence. Warm water has lived here longer than memory. It weeps directly from the stone: not waterfalls, not streams, but a constant, translucent film that clings to vertical faces and runs downward in slow, unbroken sheets. The ground is never dry. Water sits a finger’s depth below the surface everywhere; step, and the moss gives with a wet sigh, releasing a warmer breath.

And yet the vegetation is both lush and lonely.

There are perhaps twelve great plants in the whole hollow. Each is enormous—ancient tree-ferns with trunks like weathered columns, wide as a person’s reach, rising to canopies of dark, slow-moving fronds that interlock overhead in scattered patches. Between them, vast tracts of open stone. The density is low, the emptiness generous. Where the ferns stand, life is thick: deep cushions of moss in shades of black-green and rust, broad flat lichens like leather plates, and low, sprawling herbs with waxy leaves that seem never to wilt. But move ten paces, and you are alone on wet rock, the plants receding like islands in a quiet archipelago. The lushness is absolute where it exists; elsewhere, the world is bare, polished stone and shallow pools.

The pools themselves are scattered, shallow, clear. They gather in natural hollows worn smooth as bowls. The water is warm, almost blood-warm, and still—no current, only the faintest tremor from the seepage beneath. Nothing rots here. Despite the constant wet and warmth, there is no smell of decay, no fallen leaf turning to black slime. The slow dynamic of the place—change measured in centuries—has settled even decomposition into a kind of stasis. What dies, if anything dies, becomes part of the stone slowly, without urgency.

Light is ordinary, neither glaring nor dim. It enters as a diffuse, gray-green glow, filtered through the sparse canopy and reflected upward from the wet floor. There are no hard shadows, only a general softness that makes distance difficult to judge. The hollow feels larger than it is, or perhaps smaller—scale behaves gently here, not sharply.

The sound is loud. In this enclosed space, the weeping walls do not whisper; they resonate. There is a continuous, low murmur, like distant thunder that never breaks, created by thousands of thin streams meeting stone. Individual drops fall into the pools with sharp, bright echoes that seem to come from wrong directions, bouncing off the curved ridges. The sound fills the emptiness between the sparse ferns, leaving no corner silent. It is a loud place, but the loudness is not chaotic; it is steady, patient, as slow as everything else.

Fauna is rare—almost an absence. No birds call. No insects swarm. Once, perhaps, a pale, slow-moving creature with too many legs paused at the edge of a pool, but it left no track in the moss. The ecology supports little that moves quickly; the energy here is stored, not spent.

There is one sign that hands have been here, though not built structures, not artifacts of industry. Near the center, half-sunken in a pool, lies a primitive vessel: a cup or small bowl hollowed from a single piece of dark wood or soft stone, worn so smooth by water and time that its purpose is only a guess. It is simple—pre-metal, hand-worked, the kind of object made with patience rather than tools. It catches the weeping water, overflowing continuously, yet never filling, as if the hollow itself refuses to let anything accumulate.

The weirdness is not spectacle; it is adjustment. Here, warmth lives at height. Here, lushness chooses isolation. The plants do not lean toward the light—they lean slightly, imperceptibly, toward the loudest points of seepage, as if listening to the water. The ground is always vibrating, not with earthquake but with a deep, patient hum, the breath of the warm earth below. And despite the abundance of moisture, the air is not misted; visibility is clear, the normal light unbroken, yet there is a faint, pervasive shimmer, like heat rising, though the temperature never changes.

This is a place that has finished becoming itself. The rules are simple and unbreakable: water weeps upward and downward both; plants grow in scattered, dignified isolation; sound echoes in slow, deep rhythms; and time, here, is not a river but a pool—present, warm, and still.
