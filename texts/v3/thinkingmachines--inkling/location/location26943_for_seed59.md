The hollow sits at the cool top of the world—not a mountain’s peak, but a rounded shoulder where the land cups itself like a palm holding rain. Scale is intimate: you could cross the settlement in a quarter-hour, yet it holds the density and layered intent of something far larger, a hamlet that has decided to become a city and then refused to spread outward. Instead it grew downward and inward, filling every cubic breath with presence.

Water is the atmosphere’s true architect. At 2.5, it is not occasional but constitutional. The ground is never dry; it weeps. Stone outcrops—old, weathered, settled into themselves after centuries of this climate—drip with constant condensation. Shallow streams don’t run in channels so much as seep through the substrate, finding paths between roots. After every rain, which is frequent and cool, the hollow becomes a shallow bowl of mist and running sound. The air tastes of moss and cold iron.

Vegetation at 2.8 fills the space absolutely. There is no bare earth visible, no stone left naked for long. The settlement is plant-as-substrate: streets are not laid but grown—hedges of black yew and woven willow trained over generations into walls and tunnels. Houses are bark-clad root-mounds, hollowed living trees, or woven reed-cats that grow thicker each season. Moss is not decoration but flooring, insulation, and quilt. Ferns hang from eaves that are actually branch-joints. The architecture breathes slowly with its inhabitants, thickening, shedding, regrowing. This is an all-living-mass ecosystem that has incorporated human order into its own metabolism.

The built environment reads urban—1.8—because of this density and organization. There are districts: the Weft Quarter where rope-makers hang fiber to cure in the perpetual damp; the Root-Bridge that crosses the central seep; the Moss-Market where goods exchange hands in a circular clearing roofed by interlaced alder. But technology is primitive, 1.3: bone needles, wooden mallets, fired-clay pipes to direct water, hand-twisted rope of bark fiber. No metal. Construction is manual craft, collective and seasonal. They don’t build against the plants; they persuade them, prune them, weave them, wait for them to hold.

Elevation 1.5 gives a thin, bright air—normal light, 1.8, but filtered through perpetual mist and canopy—cool enough at 0.7 that breath comes visible, that skin stays damp and chilled, that fires are kept small and constant in every dwelling not for warmth alone but for the psychological weight of dry heat in a wet world.

The fauna is overwhelming, 2.8, and this is the settlement’s loudest truth. Sound is 2.1, loud, but not from machinery—there is none—rather from biological saturation. Insects are the first citizens: beetles click in the walls, moths thicken the air at dusk, spiders weave between doorposts that are living branches. Tree-frogs call from the moss-roofs in layered choruses. Small birds—wrens, titmice, moss-sparrows—nest inside the woven walls, entering and leaving through deliberate gaps. Snails cross paths that are actually moss-carpeted ruts. Bats hang in clusters from the undersides of the Root-Bridge. The residents move through this as through weather. They wear hoods not against rain alone but against the constant, gentle collision with flying and crawling life. Animal presence is not intrusion but climate.

Dynamic is 2.6, turbulent. Despite the erosion parameter of 1.7—this place is old, weathered, in-balance—the environment is hyperactive. Plants grow visibly fast in the cool wet; a path cleared in spring is half-reclaimed by midsummer. Animal swarms shift like tides: one week the beetles thicken, the next the frogs dominate the soundscape, the next a cloud of moths moves through like a living fog. Rain comes in sudden, vertical sheets, then stops entirely, leaving everything dripping and running. Water levels in the central seep rise and fall with rhythms both seasonal and chaotic. The settlement is stable precisely because it has learned to be flexible—buildings are designed to bend, to be pruned back, to be rebuilt with the same hand-tools each generation without trauma.

Weirdness is 0.5—entirely normal. Nothing here violates natural law. The strangeness is one of intensity and saturation, not of impossibility. This is a real ecology pushed to its dense extreme, a human settlement that has agreed, over centuries, to become part of the marsh-edge at high cool elevation rather than dominate it.

Density, 2.3, means proximity. Bodies are close. Voices carry over the constant background noise of water and fauna. The smell is layered: wood-smoke, wet bark, decomposing leaf-litter (necessary and managed), the sharp scent of amphibian skin, the green rot of rapid growth. Touch is constant—brushing against moss-walls, stepping over snails, feeling the vibration of insects in the frame of one’s own house.

The rules of this world are simple and severe: you do not kill the plants that shape your home; you shape them instead. You do not clear the animals away; you build around their rhythms, leaving passage-ways, accepting that food storage must be suspended and sealed. You move slowly because everything is near-touching, layered, saturated. The settlement survives not by resisting the turbulence but by being made of materials that thrive in it—living architecture, manual craft, bodies accustomed to cool damp and loud biological noise.

It is a place that has reached its own state. Not growing larger—scale is bounded—but deepening. Every surface is occupied, every sound present, every structure both ancient and currently growing. A cool, high, wet, loud, small world where humanity lives inside the plant, among the overwhelming many, in constant slow motion with the rain.
