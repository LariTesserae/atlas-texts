You are below, in a valley that was not here a thousand years ago. The ceiling is raw breakage—sharp limestone and basalt teeth where the crust failed recently, geologically young, edges still unsoftened by long water or wind. This is a collapsed basin, open at the scale of a town, miles of dark air held between unfinished rock faces. The floor is not stone but substrate: an unbroken mat of cold-adapted root, rhizome, and compressed bryophyte, thick as a loaded truck, spongy and resistant underfoot, the ground itself made of plant. Vegetation is total here; it is the architecture. Yet it is sparse in presence—massive, solitary structures rise from the root-bed like cathedrals without walls: pillar-ferns taller than houses, frozen vine-towers woven into lattice, cold-lichen draped in sheets that hang like industrial tarps between the voids. There is much space between them—hundreds of meters of dripping emptiness, the air itself a presence, thick with moisture and noise.

It is cold. Not merely chilly—functionally frozen, holding at the edge of ice. The damp is constant but intermittent in form: condensation beads along every surface, falling in slow, irregular rain from the broken ceiling; there are riverine pockets, black shallow streams that move without urgency through channels in the root-mat, carrying mineral sediment from the fresh rock; occasional pools collect in depressions, skinning over with thin ice that cracks and reforms through long cycles. The water never dominates. It waits, seeps, condenses, departs.

The light is dim—industrial, scattered. Old electrified fixtures hang from cable remnants or stand embedded in the plant-substrate, casting weak yellow-green cones against wet surfaces. They illuminate little, only marking positions in the darkness: a pump station here, a ventilation shaft there, a section of rusted rail that leads nowhere. The built world is sparse, partial—small structures, faint marks, occasional artifacts of scaled production scattered like islands across the root-plain. Engines sit in half-buried housings, their metal cold to the touch, running. This is the source of the sound.

It is deafening.

Not sharp—there is no echo that sings. Instead, a continuous, low-frequency industrial roar fills the basin, the accumulated drone of ventilation compressors, water pumps, and slow-turning gears that never stop. The machinery is mechanized, scaled, embedded within and against the vegetation—pipes penetrating root-walls, conveyor remnants half-swallowed by frozen moss, smokeless stacks venting cold vapor into the damp air. The noise is physical; it presses on the chest, drowns out thought, makes the sparse spaces feel even emptier by filling them with vibration. You do not hear wildlife because there is almost none—fauna is rare, nearly absent, perhaps a few cold-tolerant invertebrates in the streams, but the ecosystem is pure plant, patient, slow-moving, requiring no animal mediation.

The rules of this world are gradual. Change happens across long durations—ice thickens over months, root-pillars shift imperceptibly, machinery wears its bearings in decades rather than years. The erosion is young, so the rock is sharp, the structures unstable in slow motion, the river-channels still finding their paths through the substrate. Everything is balanced between the total living mass and the scattered, running machines, both operating in cold, wet darkness, both producing the constant thunder that defines the air.

There are no strange laws here. Only extreme conditions made ordinary by repetition: the cold, the damp, the slow growth of plants that form their own world, and the relentless industrial sound of engines that keep running long after any reason for them has been forgotten. The place extends past what you can see—regional, open, a valley of root and rust and falling water, held below the surface in a darkness that never fully resolves.
