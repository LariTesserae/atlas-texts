The plateau is not flat. It is a single, lifted exhale—a continental brow raised to 1.5, high enough that the air should be thin and sharp, but here the heat (2.8) pools and refuses to leave. It is a greenhouse in the sky. The scale (2.8) makes weather personal: storms generate and die within the same breath of distance, and you can stand on one edge and watch rain arrive like a curtain drawn across a stage miles away.

But the ground is not ground.

Vegetation (2.8) is not present here; it is the place. Everything is plant-substrate, plant-architecture, plant-as-mass. Yet density (1.3) demands space—vast, aching gaps—so the living does not crowd. Instead, it stands in isolation: enormous spires and webbed arches of ancient biomass, separated by leagues of warm, damp (1.1) emptiness. They are not trees, exactly, nor are they buildings, though they hold the shape of both. Each structure is a continent of fiber—smooth, weathered (erosion 2.0), worn by centuries of wind and internal pressure into forms that look almost intentional, as if geology and growth agreed on a design long ago and have been refining it ever since.

The spaces between are plains of compressed, dead organic matter—mulch turned to leather, cracked like old skin—interspersed with pockets where moisture collects: not rivers, but slow, intermittent seepages, condensation that beads on the undersides of hanging root-bridges and falls in long, patient drips. The dampness is everywhere and nowhere. The air is thick with it, a warm mist that collects in the hollows of the spires and rolls off in visible currents.

Above, the light (1.5) is normal—neither harsh nor mystical—filtered through layers of floating spore and distant green haze into a gold-green permanence. You do not get shadows so much as you get deeper shades of warmth.

Then there is the noise.

Sound (2.6) is deafening—not in peaks, but in relentless, layered volume. The fauna (2.7) is overwhelming, though physically it respects the sparse density: you may not see a creature for hours, yet you never stop hearing them. The spires themselves are resonant. Wind moves through hollow stems and fibrous chambers, producing low, organ-like drones that shift with temperature. Within the walls of the living towers, swarms—innumerable, winged, microscopic and not—move in constant migration, their collective presence a roar like distant surf that never breaks. And between the towers, larger things travel: massive, slow predators with bodies like walking root-balls, leaving tracks in the compressed mulch, calling to one another in bass vibrations you feel in your sternum before you hear them. Predator and prey here do not chase so much as persist; the dynamic (1.6) is biological pace, a constant churning of consumption and regrowth, migration patterns that follow the intermittent rain.

The structures are old—established, settled, in-balance. They do not fall; they lean. Some tilt gently toward the east, others hang with root-anchors that have become part of the ground they never truly touched. They have reached a state where erosion and growth cancel each other out, leaving smooth, bone-like surfaces that feel permanent, though they breathe.

Scattered (built 0.8) among them are the faint marks of others: primitive (tech 1.4). Small platforms woven from dead fiber hang suspended between two spires, reached by rope ladders of twisted vine. A single hut of hardened bark fragments, long collapsed, its owner’s bone tools—sharp, simple, pre-metal—scattered in the mulch below. There are carvings, too, shallow patterns cut into the spire-skin with stone: spirals, tallies, warnings, or prayers. They are old enough to be weathered nearly smooth.

But the weirdness (2.1) is not in the artifacts. It is in the behavior of the place itself.

The plants do not only grow; they communicate through the root-web that connects them beneath the empty plains—a network too old to be called young, transmitting pressure and moisture and perhaps more across miles of darkness. During the occasional storms—sudden, torrential, then gone—the spires seem to tense, their hollow chambers amplifying the thunder into a sustained, vibrating chord that makes the air shimmer. The fauna responds: the swarms emerge in visible clouds, darkening brief patches of sky, then settle back into the fiber walls as if they had never left. The large beasts stop, turn their bulk toward the sound, and wait.

You move through this landscape slowly. The heat makes urgency impossible. The sound makes solitude impossible, even in the emptiness. You are always between—between the living towers, between storms, between the roar of the small and the distant call of the large. The vegetation fills every possible niche, yet the density remains sparse, a universe of green architecture floating in its own warm, damp breath, ancient, active, and utterly strange.
