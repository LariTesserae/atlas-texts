You are standing on a high place that has forgotten it was ever mountains.

The elevation is real—thin air, broad horizons, a sense of being closer to the sky than to any valley—but the ground itself has surrendered its peaks long ago. Erosion here is not a process but a completed sentence. The rock has been worn to its final vocabulary: curves, shallow bowls, and long, almost imperceptible slopes that extend for miles without breaking into cliff or crag. It is a plateau that has flattened itself into philosophy, near-flattened, settled into its oldest-stable-state, a table of stone set at the end of geological time.

The temperature is warm—unexpected for such height—so the air holds moisture like a held breath. Water is not abundant; it is intermittent, patient. It comes as occasional rain that falls in wide, warm droplets and vanishes into the porous stone within minutes. More often it gathers as condensation—fine beads on every surface, a perpetual dampness in the air, hidden pockets of water collected in the shallow depressions of the rock, thin films that reflect the sky in scattered, trembling mirrors. There are no rivers here, only the memory of flow worn away, and these quiet, temporary pools.

Vegetation is sparse—scattered clumps of low, leathery growth that cling to fractures, grey-green and barely more than tissue, sustained by the constant humidity rather than soil. They grow slowly, imperceptibly, in the spaces left untouched.

But untouched spaces are few.

The fauna is overwhelming.

Every surface—every slope, every shallow bowl, every shelf of weathered stone—is layered with presence. The density is near-total, a saturation of life that presses close without ever quite touching, arranged in patient, near-touching strata. They are not large, individually—some are the size of palms, others no bigger than clenched fists—but they are countless, layered upon one another in a living geology that mirrors the rock beneath. Their bodies are warm, faintly translucent or calcified, covered in slow-pulsing membranes or ridged shells that collect the condensation and feed it inward. They move, but so gradually that to watch one is to doubt your own perception; a shift of position might take a season. They are silent—no calls, no rustle, no scrape. The overwhelming presence produces almost no sound. Only the faint, occasional drip of moisture falling from one body to another, or the nearly inaudible exhalation of air through porous tissue.

They are strange—not grotesque, but wrong in the way of things that have reached an evolutionary endpoint. Some appear to be part mineral, part flesh, as if the boundary between animal and stone dissolved centuries ago. Others are symbiotic clusters, dozens of small forms fused into arching shapes that resemble—without being—architecture.

And there are structures.

Scattered across the open scale of the plateau—regional, extending past any immediate view—stand faint marks and partial remains. Not built by hands, for there is no technology here, no devices, no tools, no evidence of craft. These are grown, accumulated, calcified. Bone-white arches that lean in perfect, slow curves; low walls formed from compressed shells over millennia; hollow towers that might once have been communal nests, now empty or inhabited in ways that blur the distinction between dwelling and body. They are occasional artifacts of biological persistence, scattered like punctuation across the flatness, never forming a town but suggesting one—suggesting that even in this pre-tool world, life has organized itself into patterns that look like intention.

The light is normal—neither harsh nor dim, a steady, even illumination that casts soft shadows and reveals the true colors: the warm grey of worn stone, the dull green of sparse growth, the pearlescent and bone-pale tones of the fauna.

The sound is mostly quiet. So quiet that you notice the absence of wind, though air moves slowly here—gradual cycles, weak gradients, minimal flux. Change is patient. A rain might come once in a long while. A creature might shift its weight across years. The scattered structures settle further into themselves, not decaying quickly but continuing their slow transformation into the stone they resemble.

The weirdness is subtle but pervasive: the sense that this place is not in progress. It has arrived. The overwhelming life is not growing, competing, or dying in any visible rhythm, but existing in a dense, slow equilibrium—settled into its final form, layered, high-saturated, near-touching, and utterly still.

Here, at this high and ancient elevation, damp and warm beneath an ordinary sky, the world breathes very slowly. And the breath is shared among too many to count, silent, layered, and ancient.
