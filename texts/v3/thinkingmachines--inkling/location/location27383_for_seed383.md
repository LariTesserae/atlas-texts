You descend not through a throat but through a collapse—an old mouth of stone, rounded at the edges, worn smooth as river-glass though no river has run here in ages. The air changes first: cool, carrying the taste of wet mineral, not earthy but metallic, like licking a cold coin. Then the space opens.

It is a valley, but underground. Not a cramped tunnel—open, extending past immediate sight, a regional hollow carved or settled into being long before the idea of “before” had weight. You are below the surface (0.5), yet it is bright (2.6)—not with sun, not with flame, but with a cold, steady phosphorescence that rises from the walls themselves. The stone here is charged with something ancient and patient: veins of crystalline mineral that glow a pale, blue-white, illuminating everything without shadow or warmth. The darkness does not exist; instead there is only this flat, luminous coolness, and in it, distance becomes strange—objects half a mile away are visible in sharp detail, yet feel closer, as if the light collapses perspective.

The temperature holds at cool (1.3), damp (0.8). Moisture is everywhere and nowhere specific: it beads on the rounded arches, collects in shallow basins worn into the floor, drips with a sound so quiet (0.2) it registers more as a felt absence than noise. There are no plants (0.3). Nothing green, nothing rooted, nothing that breathes in recognizable rhythm. What grows—if growth is the word—are slow accretions of mineral bloom: white, brittle, fungal in form but not in life, spreading like frost across the lower walls where condensation gathers.

This is an urban place (2.2), though “city” would mislead if you imagined density. The structures are scattered, sparse (0.7), arranged across the open valley floor with the logic of an old settlement that never needed to defend itself or crowd. Low walls of hand-carved stone, platforms, staircases that descend into shallow depressions, avenues marked not by paving but by the absence of rubble—paths kept clear, perhaps, or worn clear. The technology is primitive (0.7): no metal gleam except in simple tools left behind, chipped flint and bone levers, mortar ground by hand. The construction is layered, organized, but not towering. Everything sits low, respectful of the ceiling far above, a sky of dark stone interrupted only by the glowing seams.

And the erosion (2.6) is absolute. Everything is ancient, settled-into-final-form, deeply aged. Corners are rounded. Staircases have become ramps. Inscriptions—if they were ever legible—have been worn into smooth curves by time’s patient abrasion. The place feels near-flattened, primordial, an end-state of architecture slowly returning to geology. Yet.

Yet the dynamic is turbulent (2.8). This is the contradiction that makes the place live. For all its age, it is not still. The turbulence is silent—no roar, no storm-wind. Instead, it is a constant, chaotic restructuring at the edge of perception. Small stones fall from archways with no cause visible, then lie where they land, and sometimes—hours later—are lifted back into place by no hand you can see. Crystalline growths pulse with slow, irregular expansion: a vein of light will thicken suddenly, then crack and shed a shower of dust that hangs in the cool air like suspended breath. The fauna (1.5)—abundant, scattered everywhere in low-density presence—are the visible agents of this chaos.

They are simple. Not beasts of myth, but numerous: small, pale, mineral-soft bodies that crawl, drift, or extend filamentous limbs across the rounded stone. They leave no tracks. They make no sound. They feed, perhaps, on the condensation, on the glowing mineral itself, on the fine dust of eroded architecture. Their movement is hyperactive, anthill-energy—constant, directionless yet purposeful, swarming in sparse clusters across the open spaces, climbing the primitive walls, disappearing into cracks that seem too narrow, re-emerging elsewhere. They rebuild what falls, not with intention you can read, but with instinct: stacking loose stones into low barriers, smoothing with their bodies, then abandoning the work to start elsewhere. The urban fabric is in a state of Solaris-like creation and destruction—always being unmade by age and remade by these silent, abundant multitudes with their early-tool simplicity.

The scale is open (1.9). You can see across the settlement to other districts: a ring of taller, collapsed columns; a shallow basin where water collects in thin, still pools; a cluster of low, hand-built dwellings—if they are dwellings—whose doorways are round holes, too low for standing bodies. Between these presences is space—much emptiness, low saturation, air and stone and silence.

The weirdness (1.1) is subtle, not theatrical. It lies in the behavior of the light, which does not always obey the shape of what it touches—sometimes a creature’s shadow is absent, sometimes the glow pools in a corner where nothing glows, as if the valley remembers old sources of illumination now gone. It lies in the fauna’s refusal to acknowledge you, not with fear but with absolute indifference, as if you are another mineral formation passing through. It lies in the sense that this turbulence, this constant silent rebuilding and falling-away, has been happening for so long that it is the true architecture here—the buildings are merely what the chaos allows to remain standing for a while.

You move through the sparse town. The ground is near-flat, worn by ancient erosion into a gentle bowl. The air tastes of damp stone and cool metal. Above, the dark stone ceiling holds its glowing veins like nerves. Below your feet, the city continues—low, primitive, open, and alive with silent, abundant motion—settled finally into its form, and yet, even in its finality, restless. The water beads. The light holds steady. Nothing makes a sound except the imperceptible shift of stone against stone, the slow, turbulent breath of a world that has outlived its own purpose and now simply persists, bright and cool and strange, in the deep.
