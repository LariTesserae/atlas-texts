You are somewhere below the skin of the world.

Not a cave—there is no rock that remembers geology, no limestone that earned its shape over millennia. The elevation is absolute negation: you are beneath the surface, in a hollow that was not discovered but *installed*, a regional-scale depression sunk into dead ground like a fist punched through wet cardboard. The ceiling is lost in dimness, a vaulted lattice of fresh steel and unweathered ceramic plate arching far overhead—sharp-edged, recently joined, its welds still bright with the heat of assembly. This is young erosion in reverse: not worn down, but cut open, exposed, the features still biting.

The scale is open, valley-sized, a constructed basin stretching past sight in every direction. Between the presences—towering coolant towers, silent conveyor ribs, half-assembled frames—there is nothing but distance. Density is low; space dominates. You walk (if you walked) across kilometers of grated floor before encountering another engine-block, another weeping valve.

And it is cold. The temperature hovers at the edge of freezing, a dry, biting cold that makes metal contract with audible ticks. Your breath, if you had one, would hang in clouds that never quite dissipate.

But water is everywhere, despite the cold. Not rivers—this is not a natural watershed—but *weeping*. The atmosphere condenses on every cold surface. Water clings to the undersides of pipes in long, trembling beads, then releases in slow, inevitable drips onto the floor below. Shallow channels run between machinery, carrying not rain but industrial condensate, a faintly blue-tinted coolant that never freezes, moving with purpose through open troughs. The air is saturated; you taste metal and moisture together. Puddles form in the low spots—sharp-edged, geometric pools that reflect the dim lights above with an unnatural clarity.

Everything is made. The substrate is not earth. It is layered plating, anti-slip grating, poured synthetic aggregate that has never known soil. Even the “ground” is machinery: flat, functional, recently laid. Where vegetation might claim a foothold, there is only absence—sparse, near-total. A few hardy engineered lichens, perhaps, black-green and faint, clinging to a joint where warmth leaks; otherwise, nothing grows. Fauna is equally scarce—rare, scattered, perhaps a single species of small, cold-tolerant scavenger that lives in the pipe-runs, or nothing at all. Life has not been invited. Life has barely noticed.

But the technology is total, industrial, electrified. The hollow is alive with process. Massive pumps circulate the coolant in endless loops. Compressors thrum at the edge of hearing, their vibration traveling up through your feet in the grating. Engines—some the size of buildings—idle and rev in cycles, producing something, though what is made remains obscure: identical metal forms emerge from one end of an assembly spine, travel through a cold bath, then disappear into dissolution tanks or recycling chutes. The sound is loud, relentless, layered: the deep bass of hydraulic movement, the shriek of pressure release, the constant rushing of water in channels, the metallic groan of expansion and contraction. Echoes travel for miles across the open basin, so a distant valve-opening sounds like it is happening just behind you.

The light is dim—industrial bioluminescence, perhaps, or low-voltage glow-strips embedded in the rails, casting a blue-green twilight that leaves the upper vault in absolute blackness. Shadows are long and wrong. Things at the edge of vision seem to move, though they are only conveyor arms, cycling.

The weirdness is not spectacle but *wrongness of purpose*. The machinery operates with perfect discipline for an end that never arrives. There are no workers. There have never been workers, or they left so long ago that the place has forgotten them, yet the production continues—scaled, mechanized, self-correcting. The sharpness of every feature implies recent emergence: this hollow was opened, this factory assembled, this system activated in a time-frame that feels like yesterday, yet no builder remains. The water weeps not as accident but as design—every surface calibrated to condense, to collect, to flow back into the system. Even the cold is maintained, actively, by refrigeration units that hum in the darkness.

It is dynamic, always in motion. The coolant cycles. The compressors breathe. The assembly lines extend and retract. Somewhere in the distance, a pressure gate releases with a thunderclap that rolls across the open space, and for a moment the dim light flickers, throwing long shadows of fresh-built towers across the synthetic floor. Then it resumes—the weeping, the rushing, the cold, the loud, patient, endless work of a place that was made to be used, and now uses itself.
