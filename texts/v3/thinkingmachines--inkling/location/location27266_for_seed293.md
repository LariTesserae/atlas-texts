The air here holds at a steady, breath-warm temperature, heavy as a mouthful of river water, and it never dries. You stand—if standing is the word—on a surface that has forgotten what it means to slope. The erosion has done its work so thoroughly, over such deep time, that the ground is near-flattened to a single plane, a hamlet-sized dish of primordial smoothness bounded on all sides by low, glassy ridges of compressed root-stone that have worn down to curves. There is no hill, no valley, only the end of topography: a room-scale grove, enclosed, intimate, barely rising or falling across its span. Water is never far. It weeps upward through the spongy substrate—more membrane than soil—condensing on every vertical surface, pooling in shallow, trembling lenses that sit just beneath your feet and reflect a sky that seems closer here, pressed downward by the humidity.

But emptiness dominates. For all that vegetation is absolute—there is no stone, no metal, no dead matter that is not also living—the presence is scattered with deliberate, spacious intent. Massive architectural spires of vascular tissue rise at irregular intervals across the flat marsh-edge, each one a cathedral-scale organism hollowed out by ages of symbiotic growth. They are the settlement, the town, the infrastructure: organized construction made of pure plant. Between them lies open air, mist-filled, loud with absence. The density is low; you can walk for long minutes between the spires through knee-deep warm water and floating mats of moss-filament, feeling the space expand with each step. This is not a jungle. It is a city built of sparse, living pillars, and the streets are marsh.

Inside the spires, the built environment reveals itself—not as human architecture imposed upon nature, but as nature engineered by principles physics here only approximates. The interiors are larger than the exteriors allow, folded by some nanoscale or cosmic geometry that turns hollow trunks into chambers, avenues, layered habitations. Warm light filters through translucent membranes in the walls, normal and golden, illuminating swarms of strange fauna that move like weather: abundant, loud, and constantly turbulent. There are no discrete animals so much as populations—winged symbionts, translucent swimmers that crawl through the air, choral organisms that hum in frequencies that make the plant-walls vibrate. The sound never stops. Water rushes through vein-canals in the spires; the fauna calls and churns; the very substrate bubbles with slow, warm exhalations.

Everything is in motion, yet nothing changes its place. This is the paradox of the ancient stable state: the landscape has settled into its final form, flattened and eroded to this essential flatness, but that form is perpetual Solaris-like flux. The spires grow, collapse inward, and regenerate in cycles that might be days or centuries compressed together. The marsh bubbles up and recedes. The fauna breeds, migrates, dies in clouds, and is reborn from the spongy floor. The turbulence is not an interruption of the peace; it is the peace itself, the oldest-stable-state being one of hyperactive equilibrium. The tech is invisible, embedded in the growth patterns, in the upward-flowing water, in the way time behaves inside a hollow root. You do not see machines. You see a settlement that builds and unbuilds itself every moment, a small urban ecosystem of impossible engineering, warm, wet, strange, and loud, scattered across a flat, weeping world that has finally become exactly itself.
