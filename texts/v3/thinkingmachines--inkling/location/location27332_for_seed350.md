The valley is newborn.

That is the first truth: the ground has no memory. Its black ridges are sharp—basalt still warm from whatever fracture opened the earth, glassy scree that has not yet learned to round itself under weather. Elevation is a joke here; you stand at the bottom of a bowl so low it collects everything that falls, and what falls is not rain but presence. The scale is regional, open enough that you can see across the basin to other broken walls, yet dense—so densely packed with presences that the air-liquid refuses to be empty.

It is warm. Not tropical, not feverish—simply warm, the temperature of blood held still, and the water that fills the valley shares it. The submersion is total and atmospheric at once: there is no dry air to claim. You move through a column of liquid that behaves like weather, like ocean, like lung. Currents run horizontally and upward without permission. Surfaces form at wrong angles, then dissolve. In places the viscosity thickens to syrup; in others it thins to breath, and you find yourself standing in a pocket of “air” that is only water behaving differently, reality having miscounted its own rules.

The vegetation is lush—aggressively, impossibly lush. Not the pallid growth of deep sea, but massive, saturated green-black life: fronds the width of doors, vines that coil with hydraulic pressure, moss that carpets the fresh rock in inches-deep velvet. It is dense, layered, near-touching; there is no bare soil. The plants grow in clusters so thick they obscure the ground, and they grow wrong—roots threading through gears, stems wrapping pipes, leaves turning not toward the dim light but toward sound. There are no animals. None. Not an insect, not a fish, not a microscopic twitch of fauna. The ecology is pure botanic and mechanical, a garden that has forgotten animals ever existed.

Scattered across the valley floor are the remains—no, not remains. The workings. Small structures, faint marks, partial artifacts: an electrified pump half-submerged and still running, its turbine churning warm mist; a grid of rusted rails that carry current in blue-green arcs; a single tall tower, sheared at the top, venting steam into the liquid sky. The tech is industrial, mechanized, scaled to production that no longer has a purpose. Engines hum. Generators throb. Electricity moves through the water in visible veins, lighting the darkness for seconds at a time. These are scattered presences—never a city, always isolated machines, like seeds planted by some previous logic and left to germinate in chaos.

Because the chaos is absolute.

The sound is deafening. Not a single note but a stacked, crushing roar—the grinding of fresh rock being reshaped, the thunder of pumps forcing liquid through impossible channels, the constant tearing and reforming of the turbulent surface. It is 2.7 out of 3 deafening; it occupies the body like pressure, makes the teeth ache, makes thought narrow to a point. And the turbulence matches it. The valley is in storm-state permanently—hyperactive, anthill-energetic, Solaris-like in its creation and destruction. The young features of the land do not stay sharp; they fracture, then heal, then fracture elsewhere. Currents reverse without cause. Machinery activates in sequences that feel like language but are only noise. Distance is unreliable. You look toward one of the scattered towers and it seems closer, then farther, then it is beside you though you have not moved.

Light is dim—0.9, barely present. What illumination exists comes from the electrical arcs, flickering blue through the green-black vegetation, and from the plants themselves, which hold a faint phosphorescence, not beautiful but functional, as if they glow to confuse. The overhead is not sky but a dense, turbulent ceiling—water, vapor, or some broken membrane—filtering away whatever sun might exist. Shadows are liquid, deep, and wrong-shaped.

Here are the rules, such as they are: gravity pools in certain corridors and abandons others. The water remembers what touched it. The machinery runs not on fuel but on turbulence—feeding on chaos, accelerating it. The lush growth is not competing; it is collaborating, wrapping the engines, insulating the pipes, drinking the electricity in faint, trembling green circuits. Nothing eats. Nothing sleeps. The valley is recently emerged, or recently submerged, or both—time being as broken as the rest. Its features are sharp because they have not yet been told they are finished.

You stand—float—within it, at low elevation, in dense saturation, surrounded by industrial ghosts and botanical frenzy, deafened, dimmed, and submerged in a reality that has not decided what kind of world it wants to be yet. Only that it is warm. Only that it moves. Only that the animals are absent, and in their absence, the plants and the machines have made a different covenant: loud, turbulent, lush, and utterly unlike anything that was promised.
