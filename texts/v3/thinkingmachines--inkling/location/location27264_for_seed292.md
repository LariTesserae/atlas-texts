You do not arrive here by walking up, but by climbing out of cloud. The world resolves as a single, immense table of stone—an ancient highland worn by time until its mountains forgot they were ever sharp. This is elevation taken to its final, eroded logic: not peaks but a near-flattened continent suspended in the sky, its surface smoothed over uncounted ages until only the edges remember violence. Around the perimeter, the plateau does not slope; it breaks. Cliffs fall away for thousands of meters into lower, invisible worlds of vapor and shadow, making the flat top feel both boundless and precarious, a regional-scale dish of bedrock hanging at an extreme height where the air should be thin but is, instead, impossibly full.

It is full of water.

Not pools, not rivers in the conventional sense—this is atmospheric submersion. The air is a wall of rain, a perpetual deluge so dense that to stand on the rock is to be submerged in falling ocean. Every breath draws in cool, liquid air; visibility collapses to the reach of an outstretched arm, then opens suddenly in torn curtains to reveal another sector of the same grey-green expanse. The temperature never rises, never really warms—cool enough that moss does not merely grow but dominates, cool enough that the constant moisture chills metal and bone with equal patience. The rain does not stop. It shifts register, intensifies, disperses into horizontal sheets driven by turbulence, but it never clears. You are always underwater in the sky.

The terrain beneath this liquid sky has settled into its oldest stable form. Erosion has done everything it can do. The bedrock is exposed, rounded, polished to a primordial smoothness, interrupted only by shallow channels where water has carved temporary paths—channels that change daily, hourly, as the storm-state reorganizes flow. There are no loose stones; even rubble has been carried away or ground to sediment over eons. What remains is the near-flattened essence of land, a regional open space extending past sight in every direction, layered with history but stripped of sharpness.

And yet it is dense—saturated, near-touching, high-saturation with presence. The vegetation is lush not in the manner of a garden but in the manner of an invasion. There are no bare patches. Moss carpets the stone in thick, spongy mats several hand-lengths deep. Ferns grow from every fissure, their fronds interlocking with aquatic grasses that have colonized the flat surfaces as if the rock were riverbed. Trees are stunted, twisted, dense-wooded things—willows and dwarf alders—rooted in accumulated soil so thin it should not support them, yet they thrive, their branches interwoven into canopies that catch the rain and hold it, creating secondary pools at every height. The plant life is layered: ground sponge, mid-level fern thicket, low canopy, then the falling water itself as another living stratum. Every volume is occupied.

Scattered across this green, wet expanse—so scattered they feel accidental—are the remains of industrial intent. Small structures, partial, rust-eaten: the concrete footing of a weather station, its instruments long seized; a single rusted pump house half-buried in moss, pipes running nowhere; the bent spine of a wind turbine, its blades missing, now serving as a roost for birds; faint tracks of metal rail embedded in the stone, leading to a collapsed extraction point. These are not ruins of a city—there was never a town here—but the scattered marks of scaled attempts to understand, harness, or survive the place. Engines sit frozen in open air, electrified once, now dead, their housings cracked and filled with green growth. They are artifacts of mechanized curiosity, now absorbed into the ancient, eroded landscape like bones into soil.

The fauna is abundant, adapted, relentless. Insects swarm in visible clouds near the ground—tiny, dark shapes that navigate the rain with mechanical precision. Amphibians are everywhere: frogs and salamanders move across the moss in constant, slow migration, their skin glistening. Birds of a cool, wet kind—thick-feathered, loud-voiced—nest in the stunted trees and in the hollow frames of the old machinery, using rusted engines as shelter from the wind. There is constant motion in the lower layers: feeding, hiding, breeding, dying, all happening simultaneously in the dense, layered ecology. Nothing is rare here; presence is the default condition.

The sound is loud, and it is the sound of turbulence. The rain strikes rock and metal with a continuous roar, not rhythmic but chaotic, rising and falling in storms that seem to come from all directions at once. The wind tears across the flat plateau with nothing to stop it—hyperactive, anthill-energy—whipping the vegetation into constant agitation, making the trees shudder and the moss ripple like underwater grass. Thunder rolls not in isolated claps but in sustained, vibrating pressure, as if the air itself is being stirred in a vast mechanism. Beneath this, there are other sounds: the groan of rusted metal expanding and contracting in the cool, the creak of old industrial bones settling, the collective chirp and splash of abundant life going about its business in conditions that never stabilize.

Light is normal—not the harsh glare of high sun, not the darkness of deep sea, but a diffused, grey-green illumination that filters through the rain and cloud with even persistence. There are no sharp shadows. Everything is visible in a flat, steady glow that makes colors muted: the deep green of moss, the rust-red of old steel, the silver-grey of falling water. The turbulence in the atmosphere means the light shifts constantly in intensity, brightening and dimming as dense storm-cells pass overhead, but it never becomes strange. It is the ordinary light of a world that has forgotten sunshine.

This is a place of turbulent finality. The erosion has finished its work; the land is settled into its end-form. Yet the water and air refuse that settlement. The dynamic here is chaotic change within an ancient container—storm-state as permanent ecology. Rivers form and vanish within hours. Wind patterns reverse without warning. The scattered industrial remnants are slowly being dismantled not by human hands but by constant, loud, cool, living pressure: water, growth, turbulence, and the abundant, dense, normal life that has made this submerged plateau its home. You stand in it, cool and soaked through, surrounded by the roar, knowing that nothing here will become drier, sharper, or quieter. The world has reached its oldest form, and that form is wet, loud, and brilliantly, densely alive.
