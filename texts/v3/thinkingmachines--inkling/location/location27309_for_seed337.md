You stand at the bottom of something that is still deciding whether to be a valley.

The ground—if it can be called ground—lies roughly two hundred meters below the surrounding plateau, a circular bowl perhaps four kilometers across, open to the sky but sunken deep enough that the rim casts long, moving shadows across the floor until midday. Light is normal here: full daylight pours through the gap overhead, sharp and unfiltered, though it reaches you indirectly, bouncing from the high walls of fresh basalt and dark glassy stone. There is no soil. There is barely dirt. The surface is raw geology, newborn, still cooling—an embryonic landscape that has not yet undergone erosion, that has not yet learned to be land. Black rock juts in angular plates; cooling cracks split the floor with slow, audible groans; in places the stone is still warm to the touch, and thin threads of steam rise from fissures, carrying the smell of iron and wet mineral.

The air is cool—always cool, trapped in the depression—and damp. Moisture collects in the low places: condensation beads on the dark rock by morning, occasional rain slips through the open sky and runs immediately into shallow runnels, disappearing into cracks that have not yet filled with sediment. Water is present but intermittent, a pocketed thing, never pooling long enough to form a true river, only temporary streams that vanish into the thirsty stone.

Vegetation has barely arrived. A few sparse patches of gray lichen cling to north-facing fractures; a thin moss attempts the cooler crevices. There are no trees, no grasses—nothing with roots deep enough to claim this. Fauna is nearly absent: an occasional insect, a burrowing thing heard but not seen, nothing that moves across the surface in numbers. The place is not hostile to life so much as premature for it.

Yet it is fully occupied.

The settlement—if it has a name, it is spoken in low voices—fills every available plane of this unfinished geology. Density is absolute. Structures are packed shoulder-to-shoulder, layered upward in crude steps, leaving no open space between them except narrow passages barely wide enough for a single body. This is urban at a primitive scale: a town of stone and mudbrick, hand-built with copper chisels, wooden mallets, rope and muscle. There are no machines. The technology is early, manual—simple metal edges, fired clay, leather and timber from distant plains. The buildings are low, flat-roofed, their walls formed directly from the broken rock of the floor itself, so that in places the city seems to have grown out of the fractures rather than been placed upon them.

Rooftops nearly touch across the alleys. Smoke from small fires—cooking, warming, the low forge of a blacksmith—rises in thin columns and is caught by the cool air, hanging in low layers before dissipating against the high walls. The architecture is layered: older structures of rough basalt block, newer additions of mud and smaller stone wedged into every gap, stairways carved into the rock face leading to upper levels, bridges of planks spanning fresh cracks that opened overnight. It is settlement-scale, extending across the central flat of the basin in a tight, irregular grid—open enough that you can walk for an hour and still be within its boundaries, regional in its spread, multi-featured with workshops, storage pits, communal hearths, and narrow shelters stacked like cells.

Sound is quiet, almost reverently so. The high walls absorb noise. You hear the intermittent drip of condensation, the distant, rhythmic strike of chisel on stone—someone repairing a wall where the ground shifted, someone building a new room in a gap that did not exist yesterday. The rock itself contributes: low, subsonic settling, the crack of a cooling fracture extending another inch. Human voices are kept low, not from fear, but because the place demands it—sound does not travel here as it should; it is swallowed by the packed structures and the damp cool air.

What makes this place active is not life alone, but the convergence of processes. The geology is in motion: the floor continues to settle, to crack, to emit heat and steam in unpredictable locations. Water moves through it in hidden courses, appearing and disappearing. And the city responds in kind—constant manual construction, constant adaptation. Walls are rebuilt every season; paths are rerouted when a new fissure opens; the people work with the raw material of a world that has not finished being born. There is no erosion to wear things away, so nothing decays gently—things either stand, or they collapse suddenly, and are rebuilt.

Above, the sky moves through its normal cycle. Clouds drift over the rim, casting moving shadows across the packed rooftops. Rain comes rarely, but when it does, it is heard everywhere—the soft percussion on thousands of flat stone roofs—before draining away into the hungry earth below.

This is a place without history beneath its feet, and without room above its head—only the present, pressed tight, cool and damp, built by hand in the gap between what the earth was and what it will become.
