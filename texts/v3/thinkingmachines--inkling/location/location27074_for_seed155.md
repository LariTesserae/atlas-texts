The valley is old enough to have forgotten its own birth. At 2,600 meters, the air is thin and cool, biting with the chill of perpetual shade-cast, and the mountains that once ringed it have eroded not into jagged peaks but into smooth, almost tender humps—primordial knuckles of stone worn to their final form, settled into the near-flattened posture of absolute age. This is not a landscape in transition. It is the end-state, the oldest-stable-form of itself.

And yet it is a city.

Not a city of stone or steel, though both once existed here. The built environment—urban in scale, regional, extending across miles of plateau—has been subsumed, translated, rebuilt from within by total vegetation. The plants are not decoration; they are the substrate, the architecture, the infrastructure. They fill every volume they can claim, but because the density is sparse—scattered, low-saturation, balanced with enormous emptiness—the settlement reads not as a forest but as an archipelago. You stand on a plain of compressed root-matting that serves as both soil and pavement, and across a distance of several kilometers, rising like islands in a grey-green ocean, stand the structures.

Each is enormous. A fungal spire forty meters high, its walls not constructed but accreted—layer upon layer of cellulose and resin, hardened into something between bark and brick. A cathedral of hollow vines, their cavities once carrying steam or electricity, now carrying condensation that drips in slow rhythm through the cool, damp air. The water is never abundant—this is not a swamp, not a riverine flood—but intermittent, occasional, present as beads of moisture on the undersides of leaves, as thin rain that falls for minutes and stops, as pockets of collected mist in the hollows of ancient machinery that have become part of the root-system.

The technology is industrial, but it has not been abandoned so much as digested. You can still hear it—loud, pervasive, strange—because the sound is the loudest thing in this sparse valley. There is the low thrum of pumps that never turned off, now encased in woody growth, vibrating through the ground like a heartbeat. The hiss of pressure release through ducts made of hardened stem-tissue. The creak and groan of gears that rotate not on metal axles but on bone-white knuckles of dense root, turning slowly, perpetually, processing something no longer visible—perhaps nutrients, perhaps time, perhaps memories of industry. The sound is loud because the space between things is so vast; it travels unimpeded across the root-plains, echoing back from the worn mountain walls.

Light is normal—diffuse, overcast, filtering through a canopy that exists not as a ceiling but as scattered monumental growth. You see clearly. There are no shadows deep enough to hide things, but the weirdness—high, pervasive, a reality breakdown—means that seeing clearly does not guarantee understanding. The spires exist in multiple conditions simultaneously: one perspective shows them blooming with thick, cool-green moss; another reveals them eroded, hollow, near-flattened into the same ancient posture as the mountains. You do not switch viewpoints; both are true. The boundary between machine and plant has dissolved so completely that a piston might be made of petrified sap, and a leaf might unfold with the precise, mechanized rhythm of an assembly line.

Fauna is almost absent. A beetle, perhaps, inside a hollow stalk. Nothing more. The ecosystem is pure plant, pure infrastructure-plant, and the rare movement you do see is not predation or grazing but the active dynamics of the place itself: weather cycles of brief rain and long condensation, the slow biological pace of growth that is also a kind of erosion, the mechanical rhythms still cycling through biological mediums. Things are in motion. Not quickly—this is cool, damp, high, and ancient—but relentlessly. The root-plains shift subtly over years, reclaiming flat spaces. The spires grow downward even as they grow upward, anchoring into the worn bedrock.

You are in a valley-sized settlement, scattered and open, where the urban plan has been rewritten by living mass. Streets are channels in the root-mat, leading nowhere particular, connecting nothing quickly. The emptiness between structures is not dead space; it is part of the architecture, the necessary breath between organisms that have become buildings. And somewhere in the distance—loud, clear, impossible—an engine still runs, its sound carried through a pipe that is also a vine, in a place where reality has settled into its final, strange, beautiful form.
