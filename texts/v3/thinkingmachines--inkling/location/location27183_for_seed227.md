You do not arrive; you are already inside the hollow, and the hollow has always been this way.

The scale is intimate, embedded—one chamber, one breath held in the lung of low ground. Around you, the elevation does not rise; it holds. You are near the bottom of something older and flatter, buried within a shelf of weathered stone and compressed earth that settled long ago into its own shape. The erosion here is not violent but finished: walls rounded like river stones left untouched for centuries, floors sloped by the patient wearing of water that comes and goes but never rushes. Everything is in its own state, in balance, weathered into softness.

And yet, for all its containment, the space is empty—hollow in the truest sense. The volume between surfaces is almost pure absence. You could extend your arms in every direction and meet only cold, damp air, thick with condensation but carrying nothing. Density is near-void; presence gathers only at the edges, where the world clings.

The temperature bites. It is cold enough that breath hangs visible, cold enough that moisture behaves differently—slowing, beading, refusing to fall quickly. The air itself is damp, intermittent with water: not a stream, not a lake, but pockets of it. Condensation weeps constantly from the lush skins that cover every wall, every overhang, every curve of the ceiling. Occasional seepage finds cracks above, dripping downward in rhythms too patient to predict. Small pools collect in depressions on the floor—dark, still, never flowing, part of a cycle that measures change in years rather than days.

Because of this moisture, and despite the darkness—especially because of it—the vegetation is lush. Not trees, not flowers; nothing that needs sun. Instead, thick mosses in deep greens and black-greens, liverworts spreading like wet leather, fungal shelves blooming in pale, soft brackets. Ferns grow stunted but dense, their fronds heavy with droplets. They hang in curtains, they carpet the stone, they turn the hollow into an enclosed garden that feeds on decaying air and mineral seepage. The growth is surface-bound; it does not fill the emptiness, only lines it. Between the moss walls, the hollow remains.

Light is nearly absent. You see by memory, by touch, by the faint cool gleam of wet surfaces. There is no sun, no flame, no strange luminescence—only darkness that feels physical, pressed close by the enclosing rock and the thick vegetation.

But you hear.

The sound is deafening—not one noise, but a thousand, multiplied by the shape of the chamber. Every drop that falls from the lush ceiling strikes stone, or pool, or rotting wood, and the hollow resonates. The space acts as a bell, a shell, an ear turned inward. The dripping is constant but never hurried; it builds into a low, overwhelming roar that fills the emptiness and denies silence. You feel it in your chest more than hear it with your ears—a relentless, patient thunder of water meeting matter in darkness. It never changes in pitch, never breaks, never offers relief. It is the sound of slowness made absolute.

On the floor, scattered through the moss and the shallow pools, are the traces of presence—faint, partial, primitive. A few stones arranged with old purpose, now half-swallowed by growth. Fragments of hand-carved wood, simple and rotted, perhaps the remains of a platform or marker made without metal, without complex tool. Bone points, a cracked vessel of shell, marks scratched into stone by hand. The technology here is early, manual, intimate: craft born from looking closely at one place and trying to live within it. The artifacts are not ruins of a city—there is no city—but occasional, scattered reminders that something with hands and patience once moved through this hollow and left a small part of itself behind.

Fauna is rare. Perhaps a blind, pale thing moves slowly through one pool—something that never needed light, adapted to cold and sound and emptiness. Or perhaps nothing moves at all, and the only life is the slow-growing vegetation, the constant dripping, the stone.

The dynamics are glacial. Nothing here changes quickly. The moss thickens across years. The pools shift their edges as mineral deposits build. The sound continues, identical, patient, overwhelming. This pocket—hidden, low, embedded, empty, and lush—exists in its own long now, weathered, damp, cold, and deafening, waiting out cycles too long to witness.
