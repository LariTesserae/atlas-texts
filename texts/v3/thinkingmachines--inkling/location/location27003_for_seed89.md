You are not on the mountain’s face but inside its highest tooth—a natural vault suspended near a summit where air should be thin and killing cold. Instead, the elevation is extreme only in position: this chamber clings to the upper spine of an ancient range, a pocket no larger than a cathedral nave, enclosed on all sides by stone that has forgotten sharpness. The scale is intimate. You could pace the floor in thirty strides, and the arched ceiling hovers close enough that its textures—rounded, softened, polished by deep time—seem almost within reach.

Darkness holds here with authority. Far above, a narrow chimney of eroded rock opens to the sky, but the opening is distant and constricted; light arrives as a rumor, a faint gray breath that never touches the floor. The darkness is not absolute—there is a warmth to it, a dense opacity that feels almost liquid.

That warmth is real. Despite the height, the air is thick and warm, held like breath in a closed mouth. Geothermal heat seeps slowly through fissures in the floor, and the bodies of the inhabitants add their own slow combustion. The temperature never spikes; it persists, patient and steady.

Water is present not as rivers but as perpetual condensation. The stone walls weep. In the hollows and along the curved ledges, moisture gathers in small, trembling pools—damp pockets in an otherwise dry realm—while droplets form overhead with agonizing patience, hanging for minutes before releasing in silent, intermittent falls. The air tastes of mineral and old rain.

The erosion is complete, settled, in its own final state. There are no jagged fractures, no fresh collapses—only surfaces worn smooth as river stones, layered with centuries of mineral bloom and the soft abrasion of wind that enters only through that high, distant vent. The chamber is balanced, long-accustomed to itself.

Vegetation is nearly absent. In the wettest crevices, sparse threads of dark moss or pale fungal filaments cling, but green life has not claimed this space. The density here belongs to other things.

The fauna is overwhelming.

Every surface—walls, ceiling, the rounded pillars of stone that rise like worn ribs—is covered, layered, near-touching. They are small, membranous, with bodies like folded leather or translucent vellum, and they cluster not in panic but in profound, slow congregation. They hang in draped masses, crawl over one another with deliberate, unhurried limbs, and fill the air not with flight but with a constant, dense presence. Their coloration is muted—stone-gray, warm ochre, the dull silver of wet rock—so that in the darkness they seem like extensions of the chamber itself, a living geology.

There are too many to count, and yet they do not swarm chaotically. Their movement is governed by the place’s sluggish rhythm. They shift positions over hours, entire colonies sliding like slow tides from one wall to another. Some possess too many joints; others have single, large, unblinking eyes that reflect no light; all of them emit a sound—individually a soft click or vibration, collectively a loud, continuous drone that fills the enclosed space like being inside the body of a great, sleeping beast. The sound is loud, resonant, bouncing off the smooth stone in layered echoes that never quite resolve.

Weirdness here is gentle but undeniable. The creatures seem to feed not on each other but on the minerals that sweat from the walls, scraping with delicate, primitive mouthparts. They leave no tools—there are none, no artifacts, no marks of hand or metal. The tech is absent, the built world nonexistent; only their bodies and the stone exist in this arrangement, primitive and untouched.

The floor is slick with condensation and the slow accumulation of their presence—organic dust, shed membranes, the patient residue of generations. Nothing decays quickly here; the warmth and damp preserve everything in a state of gradual, eternal transition.

This is a place of extreme height made small and enclosed, of darkness made warm, of overwhelming life made silent in motion but deafening in voice. It does not change quickly. The cycles are patient: moisture gathers, creatures shift, sound continues, stone remains. It has reached its form and holds it, dense, loud, warm, and strange, suspended high in the world’s old bones.
