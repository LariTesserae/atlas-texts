You do not arrive here by walking; you are swallowed. The hollow sits embedded in a young, sharp-edged wound—perhaps a recent collapse, a glacier’s sudden retreat, a fracture that opened only decades ago—and it is small enough that you can stand in its center and feel the walls press near, though the ceiling is lost in mist and the floor is lost in water. Low, cold, hidden: it is a pocket within the earth’s fresh scab, not a valley but an intimate room with no doors, only seepage.

The rock is newborn. Nothing here has been rounded by patience. Faces are knife-edged, quartz veins running like bright scars through dark basalt that has not yet learned softness. Where the stone broke, it still gleams wet, and water weeps continuously—not in streams, but in a distributed, industrial weeping. It condenses from the air, beads on every surface, gathers into black pools that sit just beneath the skin of the ground. The elevation is barely above the marsh that hides this place; the water table is not below but present, a near-surface membrane you could part with a hand.

It is cold—just enough to keep the breath visible, to make the moss brittle, to slow blood without stopping it.

Light comes dim and wrong. Not sun, but a gray-green filtration through the overhanging lip of the hollow and through a constant atmospheric condensation—water suspended rather than falling, a factory haze that never clears. The illumination is enough to see the shapes of things but never their details clearly; edges dissolve into the wet air, and shadows are not dark but wet.

The emptiness is startling. For all that lives, there is almost nothing in terms of presence per volume. Vast spaces of black water, of cold air, of mist-filled void hang between the life. The vegetation is lush—thick, dark moss in emerald clots, cold ferns with black veins, fungal mats that spread like oil on stone, reed clusters that stand rigid as pipes—but it grows in discrete, separated islands. Between them: hollow. Near-void. You could fall between two clumps of life and not touch anything for meters, sinking into water that is older here than the rock that holds it.

Yet fauna is abundant, relentless. Not in bodies scattered, but in mass and motion. Small, cold-adapted things—translucent crustaceans that click like gears, insects with glassy wings that swarm in synchronized columns, amphibians that never stop moving, feeding, being fed. They exist in the interstices, in the empty spaces, turning the void into an active medium. The sound is loud: the constant mechanical roar of water forced through sharp rock channels—natural hydraulics, pipes without makers—mixing with the clicking, chirping, tearing of life at industrial pace. Predator and prey do not rest; the ecosystem runs in shifts, continuous, mechanized by hunger.

There are no structures, no artifacts, no made things. And yet the place is industrial. It operates like a factory with no foreman. Water pumps through the fractures in rhythmic cycles, filtered, pressurized, expelled in cold geysers that frost the air. The vegetation processes and is processed at scale—organic matter broken down by fungal machinery, consumed by swarms, excreted, recycled through the black pools with assembly-line efficiency. The ice that forms on the weeping rocks grows in crystalline patterns, sharp and regular, breaking away with mechanical precision to fall into the pools below, where they dissolve like cooling agents. The sound is not chaotic; it has rhythm, a production-line thunder that fills the small space until your chest vibrates with it.

The rules here are young, sharp, and wet. Things do not decay slowly; they are processed quickly. The rock will not stay sharp forever—water and life are already working it—but for now it cuts. The cold preserves but also drives the machinery of life, forcing constant motion for warmth. The hidden scale means nothing escapes notice; every click, every splash, every crack of forming ice echoes and returns, layered upon itself. You are inside a single functioning feature of the world: not a landscape spread wide, but a contained engine of cold, water, and relentless biological production, running loud and empty in its hidden pocket, untouched by hands but mechanized by its own emerging nature.
