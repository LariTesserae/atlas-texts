Deep beneath the sealed crust—where elevation reads absolute zero and the “sky” is unbroken bedrock—there is a hollow large enough to be called a valley, though no river carved it, no glacier smoothed it. The air is cool, stable, and utterly dry. Not arid in the desert sense, but void: no moisture clings to the walls, no condensation beads on surfaces. Instead, a faint, powdery salt crusts the fresh fractures in the stone, residue of a chemistry that runs without water. Here, life and industry operate on dry mineral vapors, compressed gases, and the slow extraction of trace elements from rock itself.

The scale is regional, town-and-valley sized, extending past the reach of any single sightline, yet it is sparse—much space breathes between presences. What fills this space is not emptiness but vegetation in its most absolute form: the plants are not merely growing here; they *are* the place. Total, substrate-level, architectural. They rise as colossal, hollow spires of cool, translucent fibrous tissue—some the width of buildings, others slender as towers—connected by webbing root-bridges and suspended aerial vines that form streets, districts, and enclosed halls. Between these living structures lie vast, echoing volumes of air and raw floor, scattered like clearings in an impossible forest. The vegetation does not crowd; it organizes, leaving enormous voids that emphasize the scale of each organism.

This is an urban settlement, layered and organized, but it is built through guided growth rather than assembled blocks. Districts cluster at the nodes where root-bulbs swell into factory-bases: dry forges, mechanized growth-chambers, and electrical substations embedded directly into the plant tissue. Pipes of hardened fiber thread through the hollow stalks carrying mineral dust and current. The technology is industrial—scaled production, engines running on pneumatic or magnetic principles, no steam, no water-cooling—yet it hums quietly, a low subsonic vibration felt in the fibrous walls more than heard by ears.

The light is normal, functional, not eerie. Bioluminescent panels, integrated into the inner surfaces of the spires, cast a steady, flat, cool-white illumination across the voids. There are no harsh shadows, only gentle gradations where light fades into the distance between structures.

But the geology is barely born. The erosion parameter reads embryonic: the rock floor is raw, sharp-edged, recently split. In the outer quarters, bedrock is still being broken—slow mechanical excavators chewing through stone, or root-nodes expanding with geological patience, widening the valley millimeter by millimeter. This is pre-formation, under construction, a place just coming into being. Dust settles slowly in the still air, undisturbed.

Fauna is abundant but scattered—pale, wingless, silent creatures adapted to cool, dry darkness made bright. They inhabit the hollow interiors of the spires and traverse the open floors between districts, feeding on spores, industrial byproducts, and the soft pith of the plants. Their presence is constant but never dense; they move as solitary figures or small streams through the emptiness.

The dynamics are active, intertwined. The plants grow—audible only in rare, deep creaks as fiber expands. The machinery processes minerals in steady, scaled rhythms. The animals migrate. The rock fractures, slowly. There is no weather in the surface sense, but there are cycles: shifts in the dry chemical mist, pulses of electrical load through the root-networks, seasonal surges in spire-growth.

And it is strange—strange because the inversion is complete. A city exists without rain, without rivers, without soil in the conventional sense. A bright, industrial settlement thrives buried, its buildings alive, breathing dry air, sustained by mechanized symbiosis with organisms that should not need light yet glow with it. The absence of water has not prevented abundance; it has redirected it into salt, fiber, and silent, cool motion. The place feels not dead but nascent—an urban ecosystem being born in the dark, quiet, vast, and utterly dry.
