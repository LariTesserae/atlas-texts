This is not a valley, nor a plateau. It is a **wound in the sky**—a single, recently-punched chamber clinging near the extreme crown of a young volcanic spine, no larger than a cathedral nave, bounded on all sides by fresh, black basalt that has not yet learned softness. The rock is sharp: knife-ridges of obsidian and pumice barely older than the last seismic breath, edges still unrounded by wind, fractures glistening with mineral youth. You feel the elevation not in vast panoramas—there are none—but in the thin, quick bite of air above, and in the impossible warmth radiating from below.

The scale is intimate, enclosed. The walls curve inward like a broken cup, perhaps two hundred paces across at the mossy floor, rising in vertical sheets to a ragged opening far overhead where cloud and pale daylight filter through. Here, at extreme height, you should freeze; instead, geothermal lungs exhale through fissures in the floor and lower walls, keeping the chamber temperate, even humid—warm water and vapor rising constantly. The rock weeps. Every surface glistens: not with snowmelt, but with warm, mineral-rich condensation that beads on basalt and drips into shallow, dark pools scattered like wet eyes across the ground. The air is thick with it, a constant post-rain atmosphere without the storm, mist hanging in layered strata, near-touching, dense with presence.

Vegetation has seized the warmth and water with patient greed. There are no trees—there is no soil deep enough for trunks—but the density is extraordinary: mosses in emerald armor, thick as carpet; ferns with fronds the size of outstretched arms, growing shoulder-to-shoulder; vines threading downward from ledges; lichens in pale gold and rust-orange plastering over stone and metal alike. The plants grow near to one another, touching, overlapping in high saturation—every niche filled within months of exposure. This is lushness compressed, a hanging marsh suspended in the upper air.

Through this green density, scattered fragments of industry lie like dropped tools. Not a factory—there is no factory large enough for this hollow—but the scattered remnants of one: a single rust-eaten pump housing half-buried in fern, its iron pipes still extending into a warm pool, weeping slow droplets of oxidized water; a collapsed scaffolding of steel that now serves as a trellis for climbing vines; a single, still-electrified cable, green with corrosion and moss, that hums with low voltage, its purpose forgotten. The tech is industrial—mechanized, scaled for extraction—but present only in these broken, partial objects, faint marks of human presence that arrived, drilled, and left before the chamber finished forming.

Sound dominates. The space is loud, but not violently so—rather, it is filled with constant, layered noise that never rushes. The geothermal vents hiss like slow boilers, a sustained white roar bouncing off the curved walls. Water drips everywhere: hundreds of simultaneous ticks, echoes multiplying in the enclosed volume. The old pump, if it still functions at all, emits a deep, irregular thrum—perhaps once every few hours—moving water in patient cycles. There are no sudden changes. Everything here operates on long duration: the mineral buildup on the pool edges, the millimeter crawl of moss over steel, the imperceptible widening of the vent cracks. The dynamics are weak, the gradients minimal; change happens across years, not moments.

Fauna is rare. A few pale insects hover near the warmest pools. Once, perhaps, a high-altitude bird passes through the opening above, visible only as a brief shadow against the light. The ecology belongs mostly to the plants and the water, to the slow chemistry of heat and stone.

What makes the place slightly strange—just enough to notice—is the coherence between the living and the manufactured. The vines do not merely cover the pipes; they grow *through* them, using the hollow steel as root passages. The water that weeps from the rock carries a faint, metallic sweetness, and the moss near the old generator glows with a subdued phosphorescence in dimness, not quite natural, not quite artificial. The boundary between geological youth and industrial decay has blurred into something shared: both are young in their own ways, both slowly returning to the dark warm ground.

Light is ordinary—normal daylight falling through the ceiling breach, diffused by mist, supplemented by the faint amber flicker of that one live cable. There are no shadows sharp enough to hurt; everything is bathed in soft, wet green-gold illumination.

This is the place: an extreme-height pocket, small enough to hold in your hands like a broken cup, warm and weeping and loud with patient water, overgrown with dense, touching green, scattered with the bones of machines, changing so slowly that a century here might look like a single breath.
