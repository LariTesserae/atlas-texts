The valley is called, in the few surviving maps, **Korrash**—though no one has pronounced it aloud in years, because speaking is difficult when the air itself is full of motion.

It sits low—barely above the water table, a broad regional bowl perhaps thirty kilometers across, open to see in every direction except downward into the mist. The elevation is modest; the land has not had time to become a mountain. This is young ground, geologically newborn, sharp-featured, recently collapsed or thrust upward in a single catastrophic emergence that left the bedrock fractured like fresh ice. Cliffs of black basalt stand in jagged, unweathered blades, their faces weeping continuously—water forced up through hairline fissures by pressure far below, so that every vertical surface shines with a perpetual post-rain sheen. The air is cold—barely above freezing—and dense with moisture that condenses on every surface: on the reeds, on the shattered machinery half-buried in peat, on the fur of the animals that move through in constant migration.

The darkness is profound—light registers at barely 0.4, a perpetual twilight that is not quite night but refuses the comfort of day. What illumination exists comes from the technology, scattered across the valley like seeds that grew wrong.

Because the technology here is not modern. It is **beyond current physics**—scattered installations operating at scales that blur the boundary between nanoscale and cosmic. There are **Anchor Spires**—thin black needles, some only centimeters visible above the moss, others rising fifteen meters—engineered from materials that do not reflect light properly. They hum at frequencies below human hearing, though the valley is never silent. They stabilize sections of the turbulent ground, or harvest energy from the rapid formation and dissolution of matter, or perform functions no longer named. They are not mysterious in purpose; they are worked, maintained (somehow, by processes that leave no tracks), integrated into the ecology. They feel **normal**—part of the rules—despite bending gravity in localized nodes, despite drawing water upward through reverse-osmosis fields that span hectares invisibly. Near one spire, the peat has turned metallic silver, growing in crystalline lattices that are still, technically, vegetation.

The vegetation itself is lush—2.4—almost aggressively so. In this cold, wet darkness, life does not wait for sunlight. The valley is layered: first, the surface carpet of black moss and liverwort, inches thick, holding water like a sponge; then the reeds—tall, hollow, bending in constant wind, dense enough that walking through requires pushing; then the fungal structures, some reaching tree-height, with caps like broken umbrellas that drip condensate into dark pools. Everything touches. Density is 1.8—there is no empty space. The reeds crowd the spire bases; the moss climbs over fallen structures; the fauna moves through in such abundance that the ground seems to flow.

The fauna—1.5, abundant—has adapted to the turbulence. There are swarms of small, flightless insects with translucent wings that generate sound like tearing silk as they move through the reeds in clouds. There are larger creatures: marsh-elk with antlers crusted in the silver moss, moving in herds through the low water; predator shapes that are never fully seen, only heard crashing away through the dense growth; and in the pools—amphibians with too many legs, breeding rapidly in the warmed microclimates near the tech installations. The ecology is not strange; it is hyperactive. Life here grows, dies, decomposes, and regrows within days, feeding off the constant energy released by the unstable ground.

The ground itself is the loudest presence. **Sound is 2.8—deafening.** The turbulence is absolute—dynamic 3.0, a storm-state that never resolves into calm. The young bedrock shifts; water surges upward through new fractures; the reeds bend and snap and grow back bent; the spires hum and occasionally discharge arcs of contained energy that flash blue-white in the darkness, followed by thunder that rolls for minutes. There is a constant roar—part wind, part mechanical, part the movement of thousands of creatures, part the valley’s own structural stress. It is an anthill-energy, a bazaar of physical processes all occurring simultaneously, chaotic but not random—there are patterns in the turbulence, rhythms that the fauna and the technology have learned to read.

The water—1.6—defines everything. It is near the surface everywhere, in pools that reflect nothing, in streams that change course hourly as the young soil collapses and rebuilds, in the weeping rock faces, in the condensing air that falls as constant, cold drizzle. The landscape is post-rain forever, never drying, never truly flooding, just saturated—marsh-edge extending for miles, open and regional, visible only in fragments through the layered vegetation and the dark.

Scattered built elements—1.2—mark earlier attempts to inhabit or study: partial remains of observation platforms, their materials now colonized by the silver moss; a single collapsed tower, its interior filled with black water and nesting swarms; faint marks in the peat—geometric lines, perhaps old roads or boundaries—partially erased and partially preserved by the rapid growth and rapid decay. They are artifacts, not ruins—still functioning in some reduced capacity, or serving as anchors for the newer, stranger technology.

What makes this place feel **normal**—weirdness 0.3—is that despite everything, it obeys itself. The turbulence has rules. The technology works according to principles that are consistent, if incomprehensible. The cold, the wetness, the darkness, the deafening sound, the swarming life—all of it operates in ecological coherence. There is no dream-logic, no symbolic fracture. It is a real valley, recently emerged, intensely alive, being slowly stabilized (or exploited, or transformed) by forces that do not need human understanding to function. The sharp features of young erosion are already softening at the edges where moss has taken hold; the water finds its levels; the fauna breeds and dies on schedule accelerated by the energy in the soil.

To stand at the edge—if there is an edge, for the valley extends past immediate vision into a regional darkness—is to feel the density of presence: near-touching reeds, the hum of the spires vibrating in the bones, the constant movement of water and growth and sound, all of it cold, all of it wet, all of it roaring, all of it utterly, turbulently alive.
