The place is called nothing; it has outlived its name. You come to it through a notch in high rock, stepping onto ground that belongs neither to sky nor to earth—only to itself. Scale is intimate, cathedral-close: a sealed bowl no wider than a grove of old oaks, cupped in stone at elevation where air should bite. Instead it is warm, persistently warm, as if the world below holds its breath in a long, gentle exhale. There is no water here. Not in pools, not in mist, not hidden in shadow. The dryness is absolute—salt-caked, mineral-bitter, a chemistry that forgot thirst and invented something else to live on.

Light is dim. High walls shut out the upper sky, and what seeps in is filtered through a perpetual haze—fine spores, powdered sulfur, the silver dust of things that do not decay so much as transform. The illumination feels old, tired, yellow-gray, pooling in low places like warm wax.

Yet against all absence, the vegetation is lush.

It does not grow like earth-plants. It spreads in dense, scattered islands—thick rubbery vines the color of weathered bronze, bulbous moss-clusters that pulse slightly in the dimness, root-structures that drill not into soil but into fissured mineral beds, drinking from geothermal vapor and vibration. They cover perhaps a third of the floor, leaving broad bare stretches of polished salt-flake and black stone between them. Sparse, then thick—never uniform. The growth is ancient, established, long-weathered into its own rhythm: erosion here is not destruction but settlement. The rock walls are smoothed to a satin finish by centuries of something gentler than wind, though wind does come, thin and warm, threading the gaps.

The fauna is abundant and loud.

Small things—winged, clicking, scuttling—fill the space between the plant-islands. They move in turbulent patterns, never settling. Buzz-moths with translucent wings the size of palms; beetle-kin that crack their shells in rhythmic percussion; crawling flyers that leave trails of phosphor-green on the salt. Their noise is the dominant sound: a constant, turbulent roar of chirp, hum, and clatter, layered over the deeper rumble from below. The air is loud with them, a bazaar of bodies. They feed on the plants’ exudates—thick, non-aqueous sap that smells of iron and flowers—and in feeding they scatter seeds, disturb roots, drive the chaos. Nothing is still long enough to be observed quietly.

The dynamics are hyperactive. The ground itself is unsettled, though in a settled way—geothermal vents beneath the crust release warm mineral breath that makes the air shimmer and the vines tremble. Every few moments the floor shifts imperceptibly, a subtle lurch, as if the bowl is adjusting its weight. The fauna swarm in response, surging from one cluster to another. Plants grow visibly over hours: tendrils extending, bulbs splitting, old matter crumbling into gray flakes that become new substrate. It is turbulent creation and dissolution, an anthill-energy compressed into a small, bounded world.

Built traces are scattered, faint, primitive. A low circle of carved stones, half-buried in salt, marking some long-forgotten boundary. A single shelter—the remains of a wall made from woven vine-cord and bone, collapsed inward, too small for modern bodies. Hand-tools of chipped obsidian lie scattered near one vent, left where they fell. The technology is manual, pre-metal, intimate: people once lived here, or tried to, using cord and stone and the patience of those who work without water.

Weirdness saturates the logic. The plants respond to sound. In louder surges, the vines lean toward the noise, opening pores that release warm, sweet vapor. The fauna do not drink; they absorb. The ground hums in frequencies that make the teeth ache pleasantly. And though the space is enclosed, high, and dry, it breathes—expanding and contracting with the vent-rhythms—so that visitors often describe the sensation of being inside a lung, or inside a thought that has forgotten to stop thinking.

Everything here is sparse in presence and dense in activity. Much empty space—salt, shadow, silence between the chirps—balanced against sudden, overwhelming clusters of life. The temperature stays warm, unwavering, a constant invitation that is also a warning. There is no water to cool, no river to carry away what dies. Instead, it transforms in place, loud and lush and turbulent, a dry garden at high height, small enough to hold in one glance and strange enough to never be fully seen.
