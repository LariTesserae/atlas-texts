You come to it not by climbing up, but by falling—by stepping through a crack in the flat, dry skin of the world and finding yourself suspended, suddenly, in a hollow that should not be so bright.

The place is small. Not vast—the footprint of a chapel, perhaps, or a single large room buried just beneath the surface, its floor sunk lower than the plains above by only a hand’s breadth of eroded stone. But the ceiling is high, vaulted like the inside of an egg, and that is where the emptiness lives. Density is near-void here. Most of what exists is absence: cool, warm absence, thick with the smell of old mineral and held breath. You could shout and the sound would not return, not because the walls swallow it, but because there is almost nothing to strike, nothing to echo against. The air itself feels thinned, as if the chamber has been breathing slowly for so long that it has used up the heavier parts.

And yet it is bright. Blindingly, impossibly bright.

High above, where the ceiling should be solid earth, there is a slit—no wider than a man’s outstretched arm—an eye opened to the sky. Through it, sunlight pours in a single, unwavering pillar. The rock here is not dark soil or shadow-cave; it is pale, worn smooth by ages of water and wind, a kind of polished limestone or salt-stone that has settled into its own shape, long-eroded, in-balance with itself. The light strikes the floor—fine, white sand and crystalline dust—and rebounds upward, filling the hollow with a diffuse, golden-white glow. You stand in a column of noon, even when the world above is dim. The walls shimmer faintly, not with wetness exactly—this is not a pool, not a river—but with the constant, patient work of condensation. Water here is intermittent, a 0.7 presence: droplets that form in hairline cracks, bead along the vaulted ceiling, and fall with such slowness they seem to hesitate, hanging in the air before releasing. There are pockets—small depressions in the worn floor where warm water gathers in shallow mirrors, never quite evaporating, never overflowing. They are small, warm, secret.

Vegetation is scarce. Near the opening, where the pillar touches the floor, a few dry grasses have managed to root in the sand, and from the darker margins of the vault hang thin, translucent threads—roots or fungal filaments, pale as bone, reaching downward like slow-drawn breath. Otherwise, nothing grows. The life here is not green.

But the fauna—abundant, strange, and almost weightless.

The chamber is filled with them, and yet it remains empty.

They are small. Not insects in the familiar sense, but something like drift-forms: translucent bodies the size of open palms, with membranes so thin they seem to be made of light rather than flesh. Hundreds of them hang in the vertical space, suspended in the pillar’s glow or resting against the smooth walls, but they occupy no volume. They do not cluster in masses; they are distributed like a thought distributed through air—present everywhere, crowding nothing. Their wings, if they are wings, are like spider silk or glass threads, and they move with such patience that you must watch for minutes to see a single one shift its position. They feed on the moisture, perhaps, on the mineral dust that glitters in the light. They make almost no sound—just the occasional, soft tick of membrane against membrane, or the faint, distant plink of a water drop falling into one of the warm pools. They are abundant, yes, but they are also hollow, or nearly so: bodies of gel and gas, leaving the space between them vast and untouched.

There are primitive things here, though nothing built. No artifacts, no carved stone, no hand of intelligence—only the raw materials of early use. Sharp flakes of fallen quartz lie at the margins of the pools, broken by time, not by design. Some of the creatures seem to arrange them, or perhaps only to rest upon them, in patterns that look almost like simple nesting but dissolve before you can call them intentional. The technology of this place is pre-metal, pre-form: it exists in the sharpness of a broken stone, in the simple manipulation of moisture, in the slow chemistry of erosion.

The atmosphere is warm, not hot—like standing inside a closed mouth, or beneath a held hand. The air is still. Change happens, but across long durations. The pillar of light crawls across the floor as the sun moves above the slit, a gradual cycle that takes hours. The droplets grow and fall on rhythms measured in days. The creatures breed, drift, and dissolve back into the air with such patience that life here feels less like an event and more like a weather pattern—slow, gradual, weak in its gradients.

And there is something strange about it, something that sits at the edge of the plausible. The way the light seems to pool differently when the creatures gather near a pool—faint, phosphorescent glows, perhaps, or only reflection from bodies made of glass. The way the walls, though entirely natural, curve with such precise smoothness that they feel almost grown rather than eroded. The way time behaves inside the hollow: not stopped, but stretched, patient, in-its-own-state.

You are below the world here, but not in darkness. You are in a small, hollow, bright emptiness, where water comes not as rain but as breath, where life is everywhere present and nowhere heavy, and where the only sounds are the soft, almost silent movements of water and wing, below the surface, in the quiet.
