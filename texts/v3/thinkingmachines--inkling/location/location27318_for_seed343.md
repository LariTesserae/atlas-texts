You stand at the edge of something that refuses to be a valley or a plain, though it is both. The height is absolute—this is air that should be thin and biting, yet it is warm, carrying the soft, spongy heat of something decomposing and regenerating in the same breath. You are extreme up, near the roof of weather, but erosion has won here so completely that nothing sharp remains. The old mountains have been worn not into rubble but into rounded absence; what stands is older-stable-state, settled into its final breath. The rock, if it ever existed, has been replaced, eaten downward, compressed into the foundation itself.

The ground is not ground. It is substrate—pure plant-mass, a continent-spanning mat of interwoven root-hair and fungal weave, several meters thick, spongy and warm-brown beneath a fur of grey-green moss. Every step sinks slightly, then rebounds with a living elasticity. This is vegetation total: not trees in soil, but soil made of tree, architecture made of stalk, the entire horizontal world a single breathing organism. Between the spires of growth, the mat stretches in undulating low waves, empty of feature, vast in its silence.

But the emptiness is deceptive. The density is hollow—there is almost nothing in the volume of air, and yet every surface is claimed. Rising from the mat at irregular intervals, separated by miles of open carpet, stand the hollow spires. They are colossal—some twice the height of old-world cathedrals—tubes of woven cellulose and lignified fiber, smooth as river-stone from millennia of wind, open at crown and base like enormous flutes or chimneys. They are the architecture of the place, and they are alive, or were, and now persist in a state of ancient, slow metabolism. Within their walls, the space is hollow, echoing, and this is where the water lives.

Water is not abundant. It is damp—intermittent, patient. Morning brings condensation that beads on the inner walls of the spires, running in thin, warm rivulets down to gather in woven basins at their feet, where the mat drinks or holds it in shallow, green pools. Occasional rain falls, soft and brief, and is swallowed immediately by the sponge-substrate before it can form rivers. There are no streams. There is only the slow weeping of the towers and the breath of the mat.

The sound is the first thing that strikes you, and it is deafening—not in explosive peaks, but in relentless, layered continuity. Wind at this elevation moves with purpose, threading through hundreds of hollow spires across the open scale of the plateau. Each tube sings a different tone, and together they form an unending chord—low, resonant, vibrating in the chest. Beneath this drone, the fauna adds its percussive chaos. They are overwhelming in number: not large, but countless—small winged things with translucent membranes, burrowers in the mat, climbers on the spire walls, swarming in clouds that darken the air between towers. Their wings, their calls, their movement through the fibrous walls create a constant, overwhelming texture of noise. The hollow spaces amplify everything. You do not hear individual animals; you hear their aggregation, a biological thunder that never pauses.

The scale is open—regional, stretching past certainty. From one spire, you can see others as dark vertical lines against a hazy horizon, kilometers distant, arranged not in lines but in clusters that suggest ancient wind-patterns or slow growth-rhythms. The light is normal, bright and clear despite the height, filtering through occasional mist that rises from the mat’s warmth. You can see for great distances, but there is little to see: only the undulating living floor, the spaced towers, and the moving haze of life between them.

The dynamics are active, though on biological pace rather than violent. The mat breathes—slow expansion and contraction that shifts moisture through its fibers. The fauna moves in pulses, swarming to feed on spores released by the spires in seasonal rhythms. Wind cycles constantly, changing the chord of the hollow towers, and the temperature holds steady—warm, protected, as though the decomposing substrate below generates its own climate.

What is built is scattered and faint. At the bases of some spires, you find small shelters—woven from fallen spire-skin, anchored with bone or stone pegs, made with hand-tools of simple metal and sharpened fiber. They are partial remains, occasional, as if people or something like people come and go without claiming the place. There are marks—arranged stones in circles near the water-pools, paths pressed into the mat by repeated footfall, now growing over with new tissue. The technology is primitive: no smelting, only shaping; no architecture competing with the plants, only borrowing from them.

The weirdness here is slight—just enough to make the logic feel borrowed rather than broken. The mat does not merely cover the ground; it has replaced geology entirely, and if you listen closely during the rare silences between wind-gusts, you might feel a slow pulse traveling through it, matching the rhythm of the spire-song, as though the entire plant-substrate responds to the hollow tones above it. The erosion is so ancient that it feels like an ending that has become a form of life. Nothing here is dead; everything is settled into its final shape, and that shape is hollow, resonant, warm, and full—full of absence, full of noise, full of the overwhelming small lives that inhabit every available space within an emptiness that only looks like void.

This is the place: a high, warm, ancient basin where mountains have been erased into living carpet, where towering hollow plants serve as water-towers and wind-instruments, where deafening life fills the air between miles of open, empty space, and where the only rules are patience, resonance, and the slow, damp breath of total vegetation.
