This is **The Unknitting Heights**—a regional-scale birth-scab floating at an elevation so extreme that the concept of “ground level” has not yet been agreed upon. It is cool, dim, and utterly dry; there has never been rainfall here, nor rivers, nor ice in the ordinary sense. The temperature stays locked near the edge of freezing not because of climate, but because heat itself is still under negotiation in this place.

The geology is **emerging**. These are not mountains worn smooth by age but mountains that have not finished deciding to exist. Vast plateaus and needle-ridges of raw mineral hang suspended in pre-formation—some faces are polished like broken teeth, others are porous, half-knitted lattices of stone where the rock remembers it was once magma but has not yet committed to being solid. Erosion is absent because there is nothing old enough to erode; instead, there is **accretion**. The land builds itself in patient, glacial increments, layer by layer, over spans that mock human patience.

And yet, despite the void of water, the vegetation is **lush**—not in the manner of forests, but in dense, scattered cathedrals of growth. Where the raw geology offers a pocket of stability, thick mats of **velvet-mineral moss** erupt: cool, dark-green-black growths that drink not in liquid but in **pressure and sound**. They are lush in texture—thick, spongy, velvety—clustering like frozen explosions across the sparse open spaces. Some grow downward from overhangs, reaching toward the empty sky as if the concept of “up” has become optional. Others form slow, rolling banks of crystalline fern that never brown, feeding on the chemical breath of the unfinished stone beneath. The vegetation is scattered—there are miles of bare, singing rock between each living pocket—so the lushness feels like punctuation in an otherwise empty sentence.

There is **no fauna**. No insects crawl these moss-beds; no birds navigate the dim, heavy air. Life here is vegetal and mineral, locked in a silent exchange with the geology itself.

The **light** is dim—filtered through a sky that behaves like deep water. A distant, weak sun (or perhaps something that was once a star) sends illumination that does not fall so much as settle, pooling in the lower valleys and leaving the higher peaks in a perpetual cool twilight. But in the darkness, the vegetation and certain mineral seams emit a faint, cold phosphorescence—pale blues and bruised greens that make the open spaces feel simultaneously empty and watched.

The **sound** is deafening.

Not chaotic—**slow and deafening**. A constant, sub-bass roar fills the regional scale, the acoustic signature of reality being assembled incorrectly. It is the noise of rock knitting, of gravity testing its strength, of time being layered like sediment. The roar does not hurt the ears because there are no ears present to be hurt; instead, it **pressurizes** everything. It piles up in visible drifts against the scattered structures. The plants do not merely tolerate it; they thrive in it, their lush growth vibrating in sympathy, releasing spores not into wind but into the standing waves of noise.

This is the **reality breakdown** at 3.0. The rules here are suggestions.

In some valleys, you can stand in two elevations at once; the ground remembers a height it has not yet reached, and your shadow falls both behind you and ten meters above. The primitive **built structures**—scattered, single buildings of unfired clay, standing stones arranged without geometry, simple tools of chipped obsidian left in offering-positions—exist in partial states. A hut may be fully present on its left side and fading into transparency on its right, not ruined, but **undecided**. The technology is primitive because complex machinery cannot hold together here; metal warps according to moods, and carved stone is the only material patient enough to exist in this unfinished physics.

The **scale** is open and multi-featured: you can walk for hours across bare singing plateaus, then suddenly encounter a valley where the moss is so thick it swallows sound entirely, or a ridge where the air is thin enough that the deafening roar becomes a visible shimmer, a heatless distortion in the dim light.

Everything moves at **0.7**—gradual, patient, weak in gradient. The mountains grow millimeters per century. The vegetation shifts not with seasons but with long, slow cycles of geological mood. There is no urgency, because urgency requires a finished world against which to press.

This is a place that is **not yet real**—or perhaps, it is *too* real, still carrying the factory noise of its own manufacture. The lushness is a mistake that corrected itself into beauty; the absence of water is a forgotten detail; the deafening roar is the world’s own heartbeat, heard from inside its chest. You do not visit this place so much as witness it in its longest, slowest dream of becoming.
