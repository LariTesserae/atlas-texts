The air down here is not air but a thick, warm exhale—saturated, pressing against skin, carrying the taste of iron and newborn stone. You stand, or rather you are held, well beneath the surface, in a hollow that geology has not yet finished deciding upon. The walls are embryonic: not ancient bedrock worn smooth by time, but raw formation—half-cooled slag and sediment still settling, weeping a constant mineral sweat that beads and runs in slow, patient rivulets. This is erosion in reverse, construction by heat; the rock is being born around you, pre-formation, unstable in the gentlest way, shifting by millimeters across decades.

The chamber itself is small—cathedral-height but intimate in footprint, a single vaulted pocket perhaps two hundred meters across, bounded by the uneven ribs of its own unfinished ceiling. Within it, a settlement has been arranged, urban in logic but sparse in presence. Corrugated shelters and pump-houses sit on scalded metal grating like islands in a dark archipelago, separated by wide gulfs of shadow and bare rock. Pipes run in organized arteries—industrial, mechanized—carrying steam and extracted warmth, but there is far more emptiness than structure. The density is that of a hamlet scattered across an impossible plain, except the plain is vertical and hot and drips.

Everything is dim. Light comes from low, sodium-orange bulbs hanging like tired fruit, casting pools of visibility that fade quickly into black. In those pools, the built world reveals itself: layers of construction—catwalks, ventilation ducts, a central engine house that thrums with a slow, heavy heartbeat. The technology is scaled and practical—geothermal pumps, slow-turning turbines, electrified lines that hum at a frequency you feel in your teeth. The sound is loud but not sharp; it is a continuous, low roar of machinery overlaid with the biological noise of the place.

Because the fauna is overwhelming. Not strange—there is nothing impossible here, nothing that breaks the rules of the world—but present in crushing, relentless abundance. The ceiling, barely visible in the orange gloom, is a living surface of bats hanging in dense, rustling clusters, thousands upon thousands, their wings occasionally unfolding in a soft, thunderous rustle as they shift in the heat. Where the weeping rock meets the pools—shallow, warm, constantly replenished by condensation—the water is thick with translucent crustaceans and blind, pale fish that move with sluggish patience. The metal walkways are carpeted in a moving layer of heat-loving beetles, black and glossy, crawling over boots and pipes with an unhurried determination. The air itself seems to carry the vibration of wings and skittering legs.

Vegetation is nearly absent—just faint streaks of mineral-stained moss in the water troughs, engineered or accidental, barely holding green against the heat. The temperature remains oppressive, near-boiling in the lower pools, rising in waves that shimmer the dim light. Water is everywhere near the surface—of the floor, of the skin—condensing on every cool pipe, pooling in the uneven rock, dripping from the embryonic ceiling with a rhythm so slow it feels like a language.

Nothing here changes quickly. The dynamics are patient. The rock cools by fractions of a degree across years. The pumps turn without urgency, extracting warmth at a rate that matches the earth’s own offering. The bat colonies grow and shrink with seasons measured not in days but in long, gradual cycles. The insects breed and die in tides that take months to crest. Even the settlement feels temporary in a durable way—structures that will outlast their inhabitants but are already being coated, slowly, in the mineral scale of this place being born.

It is loud, hot, wet, and crowded with life that asks nothing of you. A small, urban pocket of industry suspended in a geological infancy, below everything, growing slowly into itself.
