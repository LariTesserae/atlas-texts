The plateau does not announce its height; it hides it in flatness. You stand—if anyone still stands—within a valley broad enough to be a region, its floor stretching past the haze before it forgets the idea of an edge. The elevation is real: the air is thinner, prone to sudden pressure shifts, but the cold that should live here has been murdered from below. Heat rises not from a sun, which gives only a sick, copper-colored smear through the perpetual steam, but from the ground itself—2.9, relentless, a geothermal exhale that makes the high air behave like a low, swampy breath.

It is wet, but wrong-wet. Water: 1.9. Not rain, not rivers carving grace into stone. The water weeps. It condenses on every vertical face in a constant, mineral-laden sorrow, then runs down into basins that never cool, never freeze, never invite life as you understand it. At the valley’s lower folds—marsh-edges without marsh—there are pools of silica and sulfur, their surfaces broken by slow, patient bubbles. The ground is spongy with near-surface aquifers heated to fever. Your boots, if you had them, would sink slightly into a crust of black volcanic glass and oxidized copper salt, then feel the warmth pushing back.

And yet: nothing grows.

Vegetation is 0.5—absent, not merely dead. There are no trees, no grasses holding the soil, no fungal mats carpeting the cracks in honest rot. The mineral world has kept the place. What lives, if it can be called living, is simpler and unrecognized: thin films of thermophilic chemistry painting the weeping walls in iridescent grays, microscopic patterns of heat-loving chemistry that do not compete, do not spread like plants, only exist where the temperature and condensation allow. The world is pre-plant, or post-plant, or never-bothered—with-greenery. The color palette is rust, obsidian, dull gold, and the pale blue-white of steam.

But it is built. Built: 2.5. Urban in logic, industrial in bone.

Across the open scale—1.8, extending past immediate sight—the valley holds the scattered anatomy of a settlement, or a factory, or a city that forgot to cluster. Density is 0.0, absolute hollow: almost nothing per volume, a near-void dressed in architecture. You can walk for hours between structures. The buildings are not ruined; they are weathered into their own state—erosion 1.8, settled-form, long-existed, in-balance. They have been worn by mineral-laden steam and thermal expansion until their edges are rounded like river stones, but they stand. They stand because they are infrastructure.

Layered construction everywhere: pipes as thick as tree trunks, running above ground in parallel highways of corroded steel; heat-exchanger towers rising in isolation, separated by kilometers of cracked basalt; dormitory blocks with hundreds of windows, all dark inside, arranged not for human comfort but for thermal flow. There are streets—paved in ceramic tiles that have cracked and re-settled into tessellated patterns like dried mud—connecting nodes that serve no visible population. Factories, or what were factories, still active, mechanized, scaled production running on some cycle of its own. Engines hum. Valves release pressure with a hiss that dies quickly in the emptiness.

The technology is industrial—1.6—not futuristic, not sleek. It is heavy, electrified in parts, powered by the geothermal heart below. You see glow-lines in buried cables, faint orange where insulation has failed. Machinery moves slowly, without urgency, as if time here is diluted. The sound is mostly quiet: 1.1. Not silent—the venting of steam, the distant clank of a piston, the occasional drip of hot condensate into a pool—but quiet in the way a cathedral is quiet, or a graveyard. The emptiness swallows noise before it can echo.

Light is 0.6, dim. The sun, if present, is filtered through thick geothermal haze and high-altitude thinness into something amber and useless. Most illumination comes from the infrastructure itself—flickering amber lights in control towers, the blue-white glow of overheated metal, the occasional spark where two pipes have worn through and touched. Shadows are long and indistinct. You navigate by temperature gradients and the faint shimmer of heat rising.

Fauna is 0.8—rare. You might see them, if you wait long enough: not animals, exactly, but mechanical wanderers, maintenance automata built to repair pipes that no longer need repair. They move on rusted treads through the open spaces, carrying tools that have long since fused into their grips, guided by sensors older than your language. They do not acknowledge you. They are the only moving bodies in a landscape that otherwise breathes only through geology and machine.

The dynamic is active—1.6—not static. Cycles operate. Water condenses on cold metal at dawn-equivalents (though dawn is only a slight shift in haze density), runs through channels, heats, evaporates, returns to the air. The machinery processes something—minerals extracted from the weeping ground, heat converted into stored charge in buried banks, air moved through vast silent lungs of ductwork. There is motion without purpose visible to an outsider, an ecosystem of metal and mineral in constant, quiet motion.

And then the weirdness: 2.1, strange, but not theatrical.

The strangeness is in the proportions. Some towers lean slightly—not from damage, but as if they grew toward heat sources, like mineral stalagmites imitating urban planning. There are staircases that ascend fifty steps and end at a wall of solid black glass. Doors open onto pools of boiling silence. In one plaza—if it can be called a plaza, being only a flat expanse of ceramic tile large enough for a town—the pipes do not run straight but spiral inward toward a central vent that emits no steam, only a low, subsonic tone you feel in your ribs rather than hear. Time behaves oddly near it; you find yourself leaving faster than you arrived.

The place holds contradictions and resolves them into itself: hot at high elevation, wet without rain, urban without people, built but empty, active but quiet. It has no need for observers. It weeps, it processes, it waits.

If you must name it, call it the Weeping Heights, or the Empty Thermal City, or nothing at all—names are for populated places, and this is a settlement-scale absence, a town-sized hollow where industry and geology have made a pact to continue without witnesses. The water keeps rising. The machines keep turning. The steam keeps its soft, patient watch over everything.
