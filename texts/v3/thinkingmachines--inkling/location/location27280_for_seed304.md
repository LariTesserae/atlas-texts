The place is called—though no one speaks its full name aloud—a **Bowl**. Not metaphorically. It is a single bounded hollow, low and enclosed, perhaps two hundred paces across at its widest, ringed by walls of fresh black stone that rose not long ago, sharp as chipped flint. The rock is young; erosion has barely begun to soften it. Edges cut. Surfaces still hold the violence of their emergence. Because the elevation is low and the air cool and thick, the walls weep constantly: not rain, but condensation and seepage, a perpetual slow bleeding from pores in the stone. Water is near everywhere. It pools in hairline cracks. It runs in thin silver threads down the vertical faces. The ground at the bottom is never dry—more marsh than soil, a lattice of sharp flat slabs that sweat upward, the surface broken by shallow, moving channels.

Within this bowl, there is no emptiness. That is the first law made visible.

The settlement occupies every cubic span. Density is absolute: structures of stacked shale, bound timber, and woven reed-mat stand shoulder-to-shoulder, wall-to-wall, roof-to-roof. There are no alleys, no plazas, no gaps for wind to pass through. A person can walk the entire settlement without ever stepping onto bare ground—only across planked bridges that serve as both floor and ceiling for the level below. The architecture is layered, urban in its organization despite its primitive means: water is channeled through carved troughs that function as streets; waste is carried in separate runs; dwellings are assigned by height and proximity to the weeping walls, a clear hierarchy made in wood and rope. Technology is pre-metal, or barely touching it—simple copper awls, bone needles, wooden pegs, cordage twisted from marsh reeds, mud and ash mortar. Yet the planning is deliberate, layered, infrastructural. They have built a town with no town center, only interconnection.

Vegetation is scarce and stunted. Dark moss clings to the shaded undersides of planks and to the wettest stones. A few tough reeds grow at the edges where the channel water stands shallowest, and one or two twisted herbs have found purchase in the cracks of the eastern wall, where weak normal light—gray, diffuse, unremarkable—reaches for a few hours each day. There is no forest, no grass, no shade tree. The cool temperature keeps growth slow, and the constant occupation of space by human construction prevents it.

Sound is mostly quiet, but not silent. The dominant noise is water: the soft perpetual drip from overhead beams, the trickle of channels, the occasional plink of a drop falling from one tier to the next. Footsteps are muffled by wood and mud. Voices, when they come, are low and close, absorbed by the packed materials around them. There are almost no animals—fauna is rare here. A few pale insects in the channels, perhaps a bird that nests briefly in the highest lattice of poles, but nothing that hunts or grazes. The ecology is reduced to water, stone, wood, human effort, and the slow settling of the land itself.

Because the ground is new, it is active. The dynamics are not violent, but constant. The black stone shifts—almost imperceptibly, a finger’s width in a day—settling into its new shape. Channels must be redirected; walls lean and are shored up with fresh poles; a floor becomes a wall, a roof becomes a walkway. The people live in adjustment. They repair as they inhabit. The cool, wet air carries a faint mineral scent, like iron and old rain.

The weirdness is in the completeness. There is no outside visible from the center—only the inner surfaces of the bowl, the continuous construction, the water running through it like blood in a vein. From the middle, standing on a bridge, one can look up and see layers of habitation rising in a tight spiral or grid, every space claimed, every surface serving two purposes, and beyond them the sharp dark rim of the young rock, weeping, enclosing. It feels as if the settlement is not built *in* the place, but is the place’s own attempt to fill its emptiness, to cover its fresh wounds with wood and cord. The rules are not spoken, but physical: leave nothing bare, for bare things here fill with water and collapse; share walls, for there is no room to be separate; move with the settling, for the ground remembers it was underwater, and it is still deciding what shape to hold.

The light is ordinary—no strange color, no hidden sun—simply the flat illumination of an overcast sky, filtering through gaps in the layered roofs, touching the moss, catching the moving water, making everything visible in a cool, damp gray-green. The place is small, bounded, fully occupied, and in constant, quiet motion.
