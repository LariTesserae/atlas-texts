The continent is called nothing, because names require repetition and repetition requires change, and here change has been removed. It is vast—3,000 miles of unbroken high ground thrust upward so recently that the mountains have not learned to be round. Their ridges are knife-sharp, black basalt and gray granite sheared clean, elevation holding steady at two and a half miles above whatever sea might once have pressed against these flanks. There is no beach. There is only drop: vertical, recent, young.

The air is cold—near freezing at every altitude—and saturated. Water is everywhere but never pooled for long. It weeps from the fresh rock faces in constant, mineral-thick tears, running down unweathered cliffs into moss-choked fractures, then draining downward through porous new stone. The atmosphere itself is a condensing layer: mist hangs at every level, not rising or falling, but suspended, a gray-white veil that never clears and never thickens into storm. Post-rain without the rain—just the permanent aftermath of water that has finished falling and will never fall again.

Across this emptiness—density near zero, volume overwhelming—vegetation grows with a lushness that feels like compensation. There are no trees; the cold and height forbid them. Instead, the plateaus are carpeted in silver-green moss that grows not upward but outward, spreading in perfect, unvarying mats across every horizontal surface. Between the moss stand fungal spires—tall, pale, unbranching—like silent chimneys made of flesh. Crystalline vines hang from vertical faces, drinking the weeping stone, their leaves translucent and motionless. No insects crawl them. No birds cross the sky. The ecology is lush and entirely still: plants that do not seed, do not wither, do not compete. They simply occupy, in perfect equilibrium, the space between rocks.

And between the plants, across hundreds of empty miles, stand the installations.

They are settlement-scale—seven, perhaps nine—scattered like nodes across the continental body. Each is a knot of layered construction: pipes as thick as rivers, rail lines that run straight for days, smokeless stacks, electrified webs glowing with a flat, steady light that needs no darkness to define it. The technology is industrial, mechanized, scaled: engines the size of cathedrals, factories hollow as shells, their interiors filled not with workers but with the machinery itself, running. Running always, never accelerating, never slowing. The sound is the first thing you notice, and it never stops. A loud, constant roar—2.4 on whatever scale measures presence—echoing through hollow valleys, bouncing off sharp young peaks, filling the vast empty air with a drone that has no rhythm and no rest. It is the sound of equilibrium made audible: motors turning, belts moving, electricity arcing, all producing nothing new, consuming nothing old.

The light is normal. Not dim, not blinding—just a flat, gray-white illumination that falls from an overcast ceiling that never breaks. Shadows exist but do not move. Time here does not pass in the usual sense; the dynamic is frozen at near-zero. The sun, if it is a sun, hangs fixed. The weather never shifts. The young rock, sharp as it is, will never erode. The moss will never spread further, nor retreat. The installations will never rust, never fill with people, never empty further than they already are.

This is the rule of the world: everything that should change has been held in perfect stasis. The water weeps but never carves. The vegetation thrives but never evolves. The machines run loudly, eternally, in cities that are mostly hollow—vast volumes of constructed space with almost no presence inside them, density near void, urban form without urban life. And there is no fauna whatsoever—no microscopic crawl, no winged thing, no hidden mammal in the moss. The lushness exists for no consumer.

Yet it is strange—stranger than the absence of animals or the frozen time. The strangeness is in the scale: you can stand on one sharp ridge and understand that beneath you stretches an entire continent of this conditions, all of it operating under the same impossible equilibrium, all of it loud and empty and wet and cold, all of it young yet unaging. The installations are islands of sound and steel in an ocean of silent plant and mist. Between them, nothing moves. Between them, nothing lives except the still-growing, never-decaying green.

You can walk for days through the lush, silent moss, hearing the distant industrial roar grow no nearer, no farther, because distance here behaves like everything else: present, measured, and entirely unchanged. The rock underfoot is fresh, sharp, unworn. The water beads and falls. The air is cold. The light is flat. And the world continues, not in progress, but in perfect, loud, hollow stasis.
