Here is a place that refuses to be one thing.

Start with the ground: you are high—very high—on a plateau that seems to have forgotten mountains. The elevation is not dramatic in the sense of peaks and valleys; it is dramatic in its flatness, a broad shoulder of the world lifted into thin, hot air. The temperature is relentless, but it does not come from a sun alone. It comes up from below, bleeding through the crust in slow, constant exhalation. There is no water to carry it away—no rivers, no mist, no ice locked in shadow. The dryness is absolute, older than memory. What little atmosphere exists is thin, scorched, carrying nothing but heat and the taste of oxidized metal.

The terrain is black. Not soil—there is no soil. The surface is a compressed sheet of volcanic glass and mineral salt, worn by ages of hot, dry wind into forms so smooth they seem polished by intention. This is the erosion speaking: not violent, not recent. Settled-form weathering, the patient sanding of millennia, has reduced every sharpness to a curve. Shallow bowls hold pools of dark shadow that never fill. Ridges roll like the backs of buried beasts, though the beasts are elsewhere, moving.

The scale is open—regional, valley-in-spirit if not in wall—extending past any immediate edge. You stand in one place and understand that the same black glass continues for distances that swallow walking. The density is hollow. Vast spaces between presences, a near-void. You can travel hours across the plate and encounter nothing but the curvature of the ground and the low, pervasive drone.

That drone is the sound: loud, but not chaotic. It is industrial, deep in the chest, coming from everywhere and nowhere specific. There are no factories you can see—no buildings, no constructed artifacts, no ruins of architecture. The technology is embedded, non-architectural, fused into the geology itself. Vast thermal siphons, heat-engines integrated with the bedrock, turn with geological patience. Pipes of blackened alloy emerge from the glass like fossilized roots, venting superheated vapor into air that cannot hold it. Turbines spin so slowly that a single rotation might take a week, yet they never stop. This is scaled production without walls—energy extracted, converted, released back into the crust in a cycle that has no visible beginning.

Because there is no vegetation—none, almost none, perhaps only traces of silicon-based crusts that grow like frost and are not recognized as plant life—the landscape feels pre- or post-biological. Yet the fauna is overwhelming.

Not in number. The emptiness forbids crowds. Instead, there are the Slow Kings—massive entities, sparse across the plateau, separated by kilometers of black glass. They are overwhelming in presence alone. Some are as large as hills, armored in plates of heat-radiant mineral and something like muscle tissue that contracts over days rather than seconds. They move so slowly that the eye cannot perceive motion without leaving and returning. They crawl between the embedded engines, feeding on waste heat, on vibrations, on the fractures in reality that the machinery produces. To encounter one is to understand scale differently: you are not near an animal. You are near a weather pattern that breathes. There may be only seven visible in the entire region, but each dominates its sector absolutely. The fauna is overwhelming not because it fills space, but because it empties everything around it.

The weirdness is high—nearly breaking—yet revealed in normal light. There is no darkness to hide the wrongness. The sky is pale, the illumination steady, and therefore you see clearly that the horizon folds slightly upward in the east, that distant engines appear stacked like transparent cards, that shadows fall in directions that do not match their sources. Reality here does not shatter; it bends slowly, patiently, in the same way the ground was worn smooth. The industrial processes generate not only heat and sound but subtle fissures in causality—time near a turbine moves with the turbine’s pace, stretching minutes into hours. Nothing is hidden, nothing is mysterious in shadow. Everything is visible, and visible wrong.

The dynamics are slow—glacial, patient. The engines turn. The Slow Kings migrate on paths that take centuries to complete. The erosion continues, rounding, balancing. Change happens, but across durations that make urgency meaningless. A crack in the glass opens over decades. A king dies and its shell becomes part of the black bed over millennia.

You stand on this high, hot, empty plateau. The sound roars through your bones. The air is dry and thin. There are no plants, no water, no buildings—only the black glass, the embedded industrial heart of the world, and the overwhelming, patient presence of things too large and too slow to notice you quickly. The light is ordinary, which makes the wrongness worse. You understand that this place has been in its own state for longer than understanding, and will remain so, turning slowly, in balance, forever.
