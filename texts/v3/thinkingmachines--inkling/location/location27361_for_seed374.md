The flat does not end; it only repeats. At elevation barely above the water table—low, unbroken, regional in its monotony—the ground is not soil but crust. It came into being so recently that erosion has not touched it. There are no rounded pebbles, no carved valleys, no worn paths. Only smooth, warm, black basalt sheeted like fresh skin over a deeper heat, its surface weeping constantly: water forced up through hairline fractures in a thousand places, pooling in shallow basins that never drain and never fill, kept in perfect, unchanging balance. The rock is still releasing the warmth of its birth—temperature holds steady at a humid, oppressive warmth that never cools—and the atmosphere condenses this into a permanent, low-hanging mist. Light is dim, not because the sky is absent, but because the air itself is saturated; illumination comes as a dull, amber diffusion through steam, casting no sharp shadows, erasing distance.

There is nothing green. Vegetation is absent entirely—not dead, but never invited. The mineral world does not support it; surfaces are slick with mineral slime and salt crust, black and gray and rust-brown, sometimes coated in thin, fungal-like films that are not plants but simpler—just chemical patterns eating the warmth. This is a constructed place, settled-scale and dense, layered with human or human-adjacent presence. The settlement—call it a town, though it extends for miles in every direction—sits directly upon the newborn rock. Buildings are urban in organization: streets laid out in grids, walls packed shoulder-to-shoulder, structures layered atop one another in a density that feels near-touching. Yet the technology is primitive. Construction is hand-tooled. Blocks are split with simple metal wedges and wooden levers from the fresh bedrock itself; mortar is mixed mud and crushed shell; roofs are thatch of woven reed-substitute (there being no true reeds) or stacked flat stone. Everything is manual-craft, pre-industrial, settlement-scale but without mechanization.

Because the crust is flat and the water is near the surface everywhere, there is no foundation dug deep. Buildings rest on broad, low plinths of unshaped stone, raised just enough to stay above the constant seep. Streets are boardwalks or packed mud-ridges that never erode—because nothing erodes here—holding their shape in perpetual equilibrium. The architecture is dense and low, never rising high; with no trees and no elevation change, there is no reference for height, and the buildings spread horizontally instead, a thick, horizontal city.

The fauna is abundant, filling every available volume. In the warm pools between buildings swim dense schools of small, hard-shelled creatures—crustaceans or analogues—while larger, slow-moving amphibians crawl the mud-ridges and cling to walls. The air, thick with moisture, vibrates with their presence: insects or simpler organisms swarm in unmoving clouds, their bodies clicking, scraping, breathing out a collective noise. They do not migrate; they do not grow or diminish in number. The population is locked, like everything else, in a state of perfect stasis—born and dying at identical rates, maintaining an unchanging abundance.

And the sound. It is deafening, but not chaotic. It is the sound of equilibrium made audible. The weeping rock produces a constant, low thunder—a thousand streams of warm water hissing through cracks, falling into pools with an endless, identical splash. Beneath that, the fauna generates a dense, continuous roar: not individual cries but a saturated drone of movement, of shells grinding, of breath being drawn in unison. There are no voices shouting, no machinery clanging—technology is too primitive for that—but there is the constant, manual sound of maintenance: hands mixing mud, stones being set, tools striking rock at the same rhythm, hour after hour, never accelerating, never ceasing. The sound does not vary. It has no crescendo, no rest. It is the acoustic form of a world that never changes.

This is normal. There is no strangeness to it, no magic, no anomaly except the absolute perfection of its stasis. The people—if they are people—move through the dense, warm mist in practical rhythms. They harvest the shell-creatures from the pools with woven nets. They repair the same walls they repaired yesterday and will repair tomorrow. They have no written language of change, no concept of progress, because there is none. The settlement is not growing; it is not decaying. The raw geology beneath them is not weathering into soil; it will remain smooth and weeping forever. The water does not rise or fall. The temperature does not shift. Even the dim light holds at its constant, steamy amber.

Walking through it—if one could walk—would be an experience of saturation. Every direction reveals more of the same: dense, hand-built urbanity layered over newborn stone, warm pools reflecting dull light, the absence of green made up for by an overwhelming presence of mineral, water, creature, and structure. The scale is open; you could travel for hours and the flat, the weeping, the dense settlement, and the deafening, unchanging roar would continue, unchanged, emerging always but never finished, a world caught in the first breath of its own existence, holding that breath forever.
