You descend—though there is no stair, only a gradual tilt—into a basin that feels less like a cave and more like the negative space of a buried town. The elevation is wrong here: you are below the surface, yet the scale is open, regional, a valley-sized hollow under a ceiling of black stone that arches high enough to forget its height. Light comes not from any flame or device—there is no technology here, none at all—but from hairline fractures in the vault far above, letting in cool, diluted starlight. It is dim, always dim, a grey-green twilight that never shifts to day.

The air is dry. There is no water—no rivers, no pools, no rain, not even dampness clinging to the walls. And yet the vegetation is lush, stubborn, wrong in a quiet way. It does not drink. It feeds on mineral breath, on the trace salts exhaling from the rock, on something older than thirst. Thick mats of silver-grey lichen drape over the eroded architecture like slow-moving cloth. Fungal spires, broad as tables and low as stools, cluster in scattered gardens across the floor—dense where they grow, but separated by long, bare stretches of polished stone. The density is sparse: much space between presences, a town where the buildings and the life have agreed to leave room for silence, or for sound.

Because the sound is not silent. It is deafening.

The built environment is urban in footprint—this was once a settlement of layered construction, streets that are now shallow troughs, walls that have settled into rounded ridges, terraces of compressed earth and carved mineral that have known no repair for an era so long it approaches the primordial. Erosion has done its patient work for millennia; everything is near-flattened, settled into final form, ancient-stable. There are no sharp edges. Even the tallest remnant, a circular amphitheater of bone-white stone, has been worn by slow wind into the shape of a half-buried dish. No tools made this now; no hands tend it. It is pure geology and absence, a pre-technical city returned to mineral patience.

But the fauna is abundant.

They are everywhere and nowhere in particular—small, dark, countless—spread across the open floor and nesting in the hollows of the old structures. Insects with membranous wings too large for their bodies, and others that crawl in silent, glistening lines along the lichen beds. They do not move quickly; the dynamic here is slow, gradual, patient across long durations. Yet together they generate the noise: a continuous, roaring drone that fills the vault like water might fill a sea, echoing off the ancient stone until the air itself seems to vibrate. It is deafening not because it is violent, but because it never stops. A biological thunder, uniform, slow-changing, the collective voice of life that has outlasted the builders.

The temperature is cool—stable, underground-cool—so the creatures move in languid cycles, feeding, breeding, dying into the lichen in rhythms measured by centuries rather than seasons. There is no water to evaporate, no heat to drive swift change. Everything here has reached agreement with its own ending.

You can walk for miles across the flat, eroded avenues. The structures appear as low, scattered clusters—an old market arc now a ring of stumps, a tower collapsed into a mound—organized once, now barely distinguishable from the geology except for their too-regular spacing. Between them, the lush vegetation grows in islands: fungal groves, lichen forests, fibrous moss that hangs like hair from the broken edges of the vault. The fauna moves through them slowly, abundantly, invisibly loud.

This is a place without technology, without water, without urgency. A dry, cool, dim city beneath the world, loudly alive in a way that does not require speed, spread sparse across its own vastness, built by forgotten hands and then erased by time until only the shape of intention remains. The rules are simple: nothing is made, only worn; nothing drinks, only takes; nothing changes quickly, yet everything, eventually, becomes part of the floor.
