The valley is still being born.

Not metaphorically—geologically, literally unfinished. You stand in the Unshod Basin, a low dish of fresh crust that rose from some deeper pressure only centuries, perhaps decades, ago. Erosion has not yet earned the right to exist here; there are no worn valleys, no rounded hills, no dust born of long abrasion. The rock underfoot is sharp, black-green, glassy, like the inside of a geode exposed to air for the first time. Rain—when it comes—does not run. It beads, slides, pools in shallow black depressions that have no outlets, no rivers carved to carry them away. The moisture is intermittent, atmospheric, a cold damp that lives in the air more than the ground: condensation beading on every vertical surface, fog that settles between structures and never quite lifts.

Yet it is lush. Impossible lush. Without soil in the traditional sense—only a thin, dark slurry of decomposed pioneer moss and mineral grit—vegetation has seized every available anchor. Pale ferns the height of doors curl over railings. Thick-leaved liverworts carpet the rooftops in soft, wet velvet. Fungal stalks grow in the seams between paving stones, not parasitically but partner-like, their white networks visible beneath translucent caps. The cold suits them. The diffuse, normal light—never harsh, never shadowed by high peaks because the elevation stays low—feeds them slowly, steadily. The basin stretches wide and open, regional in scale, a valley-town that extends past immediate sight, layered with settlement that has not waited for the land to settle.

And settle it has not.

This is the turbulence: silent, structural, relentless. The ground breathes. Not violently—there are no earthquakes that shake, no thunder. Rather, a constant, sub-audible rearrangement. Streets tilt by degrees overnight and are corrected by machinery before dawn. New depressions open where none existed, filled instantly with black water and then bridged. The crust is embryonic, pre-formed, and the city built upon it is equally unfinished, equally responsive.

Because it is a city. Or something that occupies the density of one.

The Saturate—no single center, just a continuous weave—packs every inch. Buildings do not stand apart; they lean, interlock, grow into one another like the vegetation itself. Industrial infrastructure is not hidden but integrated: silent engines, enormous and slow-turning, housed in open frames that double as thoroughfares. Pipes run overhead and through walls, carrying warmth—because despite the lush green, the temperature is cold, bone-cold, and the machinery generates the heat that keeps both human and fungal life active. The technology is scaled, mechanized, electrified, but it makes almost no sound. The dynamics are chaotic—creation and dissolution happen constantly—yet the soundscape registers barely a 0.8: a muted hum, the soft rush of condensation, the occasional drip of water onto metal. The chaos is visual and structural, not auditory. You see a factory wall being disassembled and rebuilt in new configuration as you watch, guided by engines that never sleep, but you hear only your own breath in the damp air.

The fauna is abundant, strange, accommodated. Insects with glassy, cold-resistant wings cluster in the warm exhaust ports. Small mammals—pale, large-eyed—navigate the lower pipeworks and the moss-covered alley floors with equal comfort. Birds nest inside smokestacks that never smoke, feeding on the fungal blooms that thrive in the constant humidity. Nothing here is wild in opposition to the built; everything is woven into the saturation. There is no “outside” to retreat to. The density is 2.8—packed, saturated, fully occupied—so ecology and urbanity are the same field.

The weirdness lives in the synchronization. The buildings adjust to the ground’s silent shifts with preemptive grace, as if the architecture anticipates the geology. The vegetation grows not randomly but in patterns that mirror the pipe networks below. The inhabitants—few visible in the open, though presence is felt—move through the shifting alleys with unhurried certainty, carrying tools for adjustment rather than conquest. This is not a place being built upon raw land; it is a place where land and settlement are co-emerging, both pre-erosion, both turbulent, both quiet.

Normal light filters through overcast skies, illuminating the green-black surfaces, the wet stone, the silent turning gears. The scale is open—regional, town-sized, extending into mist where new structures are already half-visible, rising or sinking according to the ground’s quiet whim. Water remains in pockets, occasional and contained, never dominating but always present: a dampness that makes everything glisten, that feeds the lushness, that ensures nothing ever dries out completely.

Here, erosion is not a force of decay but of absence. It has not arrived. Everything is new, packed, cold, and alive with silent motion. The valley breathes, the machinery breathes with it, the moss breathes, and beneath it all the fresh crust continues its unfinished becoming—while the city, equally unfinished, adjusts and holds.
