The place is a pocket—no larger than the hollow of a cupped palm, though within it a world insists. It sits low, almost flush with the ground, embedded in the fresh wound of something larger that broke open not long ago. The edges are sharp: metal sheared with the cleanliness of a recent tear, ceramic cracked like an egg that hasn’t yet been stepped on, its fractures holding a glassy, unworn glint. This is young damage. There hasn’t been time for rust to soften the cuts or for water to round the lips. Everything is recent, exposed, raw.

Inside, the air is warm—not the heat of fire, but the steady warmth of a held breath, of machinery that hasn’t fully forgotten how to run. Dampness lives here in patches, not rivers. Condensation beads on the inner curve of the shell like sweat on skin, occasional and uncertain. Sometimes a single drop falls, delayed, fat with warmth, into the dense occupation below.

The vegetation is almost absent—just faint, hair-thin threads of something gray-green threading through the cracks where metal and stone have parted. They don’t grow; they merely persist, sparse witnesses.

But the density—there is no empty space. Every surface, every angle between scattered artifacts, is saturated. The pocket is fully occupied. Small structures and partial remains are scattered throughout: a segment of mesh tube bent at an unnatural angle, a dial face cracked but still readable, a valve stem protruding like a bone. These are the faint marks of industry, mechanized remnants embedded in the pocket’s base. They do not dominate; they provide architecture for what lives among them.

The fauna is abundant and strange. They are not insects exactly, though they share an instinct for crowding. Translucent, segmented bodies—some no larger than a grain of rice, others stretching like wet ribbons—fill the gaps between the metal fragments. They cling to the sharp edges without cutting themselves, their forms soft where the environment is hard. Some pulse slowly in a dull, unremarkable light that seems to come from everywhere and nowhere—normal, unspectacular illumination that makes every surface visible without drama. They move constantly: feeding on condensation, on each other, on the mineral film left by the broken machinery. Predator and prey are indistinguishable in the press, a silent cycle of consumption and replacement.

The sound is mostly quiet. Not silent—the pocket breathes. There is the faint, wet click of bodies shifting over metal, the occasional drip of water finding its way through the packed masses, a low hum that might be residual electricity in the scattered artifacts or might be the collective vibration of the fauna. Nothing loud. The machinery is dead, but the life is active, moving in processes that don’t need noise.

The dynamics are constant: moisture collecting and falling, bodies migrating from one cracked surface to another, temperature holding steady in the warm pocket, the young sharp edges slowly—almost imperceptibly—beginning their long erosion, altered now by what clings to them. It is a hidden place, contained, intimate, embedded deep within a larger industrial corpse or geological fresh-scar. From outside, one might never know it exists. But inside, every millimeter is claimed, every process is in motion, and the strangeness is absolute—not fantastical with spectacle, but deeply, quietly wrong in the way life adapts to wounds, filling them completely, leaving no room for emptiness.
