What lives here begins with the moss—not as decoration, but as foundation. It is the first and most complete inhabitant, a kingdom of green sponge that has swallowed the stone. Several hand-lengths deep, it carpets every rounded surface in continuous, breathing mat, holding the perpetual rain like a lung holds air, releasing it slowly into the roots that have punched through to the bedrock below. There are no bare patches because the moss allows none; it has made the plateau its body, and everything else lives inside or upon it.

From this green substrate, the vegetation rises in dense, interlocking tiers. Ferns erupt from every fissure where water has worn a temporary channel—channels that shift hourly, yet the ferns remain, their fronds weaving together with aquatic grasses that treat the flat rock as riverbed. These grasses do not merely grow; they colonize, spreading in thick, wet blades that ripple like underwater meadows whenever the turbulent wind tears across them. Above them, the trees have adapted to stunted permanence: willows and dwarf alders, twisted by constant pressure into dense-wooded contortions, their branches interwoven into low canopies that catch the falling ocean and hold it. They create pools at elevation—secondary waters suspended in wood—so that even the height of a branch is wet, dripping, alive with captured storm.

But the occupation is not only green. The air near the ground belongs to insects: tiny, dark shapes that move with mechanical precision through the vertical rain, navigating without error, swarming in visible clouds that hover just above the moss like smoke made of life. They are constant, uncountable, part of the atmospheric weight.

On the spongy surface, amphibians have become the plateau’s walking pulse. Frogs and salamanders move in endless, slow migration across the moss—glistening, relentless—feeding, hiding, breeding, dying, all in the same cool, soaked moment. Their skin mirrors the grey-green light; they do not hurry, because there is nowhere dry to reach. They are the lower layer’s perpetual motion, a constant redistribution of bodies through the green.

Higher, in both the stunted trees and the rusted bones of old machinery, the birds claim territory. They are thick-feathered, loud-voiced creatures built for wet permanence—cool-weather forms that nest in the interwoven branches and, just as readily, inside the hollow frames of dead engines. A rusted wind turbine spine serves not as monument but as roost; a cracked pump house, filled with green growth, becomes shelter. The birds do not distinguish between the organic and the abandoned; to them, the bent metal and the twisted alder are the same architecture—protection from wind that never stops tearing.

And indeed, the old industrial remnants have been fully adopted. Concrete footings are now islands of moss; pipes run nowhere but are filled with fern; engines sit frozen in open air, their housings cracked and inhabited, serving as nesting boxes, as shade structures, as bones gradually being digested by constant moisture and growth. They are no longer machinery; they are habitat.

Every volume is filled. The ground is sponge and migration. The middle air is swarm and frond. The low canopy is pool and nest. Even the rusted steel participates, providing hollow spaces where the loud-voiced birds shout against the roar of rain. Nothing here is rare. Presence is the default condition—abundant, dense, near-touching, high-saturation with being. The life is not delicate; it is relentless, adapted to turbulence and coolness, thriving in conditions that never stabilize. You are surrounded not by individual creatures but by a total ecological occupation, brilliant and noisy, living in every layer from moss-floor to rusted-roof, making this submerged, eroded table its permanent, wet, loud home.
