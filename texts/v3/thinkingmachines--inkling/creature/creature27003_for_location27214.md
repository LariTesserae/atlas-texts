What lives here is mostly absence, but absence with a temperature—warm, damp, and humming.

Begin at the pool. The water is dark and still, bath-warm, holding a heat that comes not from the sky but from below. If you look long enough—longer than comfort allows, past the point where you expect something to move—you might notice the inhabitant. Not in the water, exactly, but at its margin: a single, immobile crust, grey-white with mineral salt, clinging to a submerged ledge of rounded basalt. It is not plant, not animal, not fungus in any recognizable lineage. It is a membrane, barely qualified as being, formed of silicate and dissolved metal that have learned, by slowest accident, to hold a boundary. It does not feed; it absorbs. It does not grow in any visible span; it maintains. The pool is its entire world, and the pool is maintained for reasons that have nothing to do with it—warm, chemically stable, fed by vapor rising through fractures in the stone. The crust is the rare biology, hidden at the extreme edge of possibility, existing not because the place welcomes life but because the machinery of the place creates conditions so constant that chemistry, given enough time, finds one still point where it can pretend to be alive. There may be only one per pool. There may be pools with none. They do not reproduce with urgency; they persist, or they fail, and either way the pool continues its dark, unmoving reflection.

But these are the guests. The true residents are beneath your feet.

Spread across the shallow dish, flush with the polished ground in vast, flat circular formations—dozens of them, miles apart yet working as one distributed body—lie the engines. They were never built. They accreted: crystalline lattices growing like bone over geologic ages, metal-infused plates settling into shapes that happen to turn, happen to compress, happen to release. They are the ones who live here in the meaningful sense. Each is a body without organs as biology understands them, a circular presence embedded in the dark rock, its surface flush with the floor you walk on, its interior a maze of mineral turbines and pressure valves formed by heat and time alone. They do not move visibly; their existence is absolute stasis, an equilibrium so perfect it looks like death to the impatient eye. Yet they turn. Imperceptibly. At constant speed, driven by geothermal heat rising from depths that do not cool. They have no purpose but continuation. They take in heat, turn it into motion too slow to see, exhale warmed vapor through microscopic fractures, and in doing so they generate the drone—the loud, unwavering industrial breath that fills the air without source.

These engines are synchronized without touching. Separated by miles of empty, warm stone, they maintain the same rhythm, releasing vapor that keeps the pools warm and the air humid, maintaining the damp film and the salt traces in the lower depressions. They are the factory, the climate, the architecture, and the inhabitant all at once. The ground hums because they are present, not because they are broken or overworked, but because they are large, constant, and fully themselves.

So the population of this place is layered and sparse. At the surface: nothing. Between the pools: only the rounded stone, the faint white salt, the occasional mist. In the water, rarely and hidden: the crust, the accidental membrane, barely being, sustained by conditions it did not make. And everywhere, beneath and within the rock: the crystalline engines, the true citizens—formed, not made; turning, not striving; producing only the conditions of their own endless present.

There are no others. No predators, no competitors, no society visible to the eye. The crust does not know the engine; the engine does not know the crust. They cohabit through pure chemistry and geography, held together by warmth and the absence of change. You could walk for hours across their shared world—past the dark pools holding their single silent tenants, over the vibrating circular bodies hidden in plain sight beneath the polished basalt, through air thick with metallic moisture and the unceasing hum—and feel that you are passing through the interior of something that lives, not with intention, but with permanence. The place is not waiting. It is not developing. It has reached its form, and in that form, the crust persists in its pool, and the engines turn beneath the stone, and the sound continues without variation, the breathing of a world that has become its own only inhabitant.
