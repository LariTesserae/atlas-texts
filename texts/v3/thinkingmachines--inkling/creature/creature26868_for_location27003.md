What lives here is not a single species but a congregation—an unbroken society of membranous bodies that have made themselves indistinguishable from the vault’s own tissue.

At first they appear to be growths: draped, hanging things, like wet parchment left too long in a cave. They cover the curved ceiling in layered curtains, their bodies folded into one another so thoroughly that the stone ribs between them disappear. Each one is small enough to cup in two hands, though they rarely present themselves alone. Their skin is not skin exactly—more a translucent vellum, stretched over frames that refuse standard anatomy. Some possess limbs that branch and re-branch, too many joints bending in directions that suggest patience rather than purpose; others have collapsed their limbs entirely, becoming only eyes and mouths anchored to the rock by delicate, root-like filaments. A few carry a single, large orb—unblinking, wet-shiny, reflecting nothing—set high on a neck that twists with the slowness of deep water.

They do not fly. Flight belongs to open air, and these creatures have forgotten openness. They crawl, but crawl as geology crawls: over centuries, over hours, in shifts so gradual they seem to be the same motion continued. Colonies slide like tides of muted color—stone-gray, the ochre of old bone, the dull silver of condensation—moving from the eastern ledge to the western pillar not in minutes but in the span of a long breath held across an afternoon. When they move, the air does not stir; they are too light, too yielding, passing over one another with the softness of silk dragged through silk.

The sound they make is the chamber’s true voice. Individually, each emits a faint vibration, a click like wet stone tapping wet stone, barely there. But there are thousands—tens of thousands—layered in the dark, and together they produce a drone that is not heard so much as inhabited. It fills the enclosed space, resonant, bouncing from the smooth arched ceiling in waves that never quite finish. To be inside this vault is to be inside the body of something immense and sleeping, its breath made of countless small, patient exhalations. The echo does not confuse; it deepens, layering upon itself until the drone feels like the mountain’s own heart, slowed to match their rhythm.

They feed without urgency. Delicate mouthparts—almost invisible, like frayed threads—scrape at the mineral sweat that bleeds through fissures in the floor. They are not predators. There is no violence here, no chase, no consumption of each other. They draw sustenance from the stone’s slow weeping, and in turn they contribute to the chamber’s preservation: their shed membranes, their fine organic dust, settle into the pools of condensation, mixing with mineral bloom to form a kind of living sediment that never rots, only accumulates, layer upon patient layer.

In the darkness, they become the architecture. You might reach out—if you were there—and your hand would close not on animal but on warmth, on the slight resistance of folded vellum, on a body that yields and then holds, heavy with the stillness of deep congregation. They have no tools, leave no marks of intent or craft. There is no technology, no built thing, no sign that they understand themselves as separate from the vault. They are primitive not in the sense of lacking, but in the sense of being first—original, untouched, existing exactly as the place requires them to exist.

Some hang so long in one position that they seem to have fused with the rock, only their occasional, microscopic adjustment—a joint unfolding by degrees—betraying that they remain alive. Others cluster near the pools, their lower edges darkening with moisture, drinking not with mouths alone but with their entire surface, absorbing the damp through membranes that never dry completely.

And always, the eyes—those singular, reflectionless orbs—watch without watching. They do not track. They do not need light to exist. They are present, like the droplets overhead, like the warmth seeping upward, like the drone that never ceases: part of the chamber’s eternal, slow transition.

Nothing decays quickly here. The warmth, the damp, their own presence—all of it works to keep everything in a state of gradual, unending now. They have reached their form and hold it, dense, loud in their collective voice, silent in their individual motion, warm as the breath of the stone itself, suspended high in the world where life has chosen not to compete but to accumulate, to hang, to endure.
