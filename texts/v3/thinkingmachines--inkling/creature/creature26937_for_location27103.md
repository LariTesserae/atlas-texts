First, there is the green—not as scenery, but as the primary inhabitant, a single, breathing body broken into a thousand overlapping forms.

The moss is the oldest resident, though “old” here is measured in months, not years. It does not grow in patches; it congregates. Emerald armor thick as a palm, it covers the floor in uninterrupted carpet, swallowing the shallow pools at their edges, creeping up the basalt walls in vertical drifts, and climbing over the rusted pump housing as though the iron were merely another stone that had forgotten to be hard. Where it touches the still-live cable, the moss darkens to a deeper, almost black-green, feeding on the faint warmth and the corrosion; near the geothermal fissures, it brightens to a near-luminous chartreuse, thick with moisture, exhaling its own slow breath of spores into the mist. You do not walk through it—you wade, and it parts reluctantly, springing back behind each step with the patience of something that knows it has all the time in the world.

Within and among this carpet, the ferns establish their own architecture. Their fronds are enormous—outstretched arms of deep green ribbed with lighter veins—growing not in orderly rows but in dense congress, shoulder pressing against shoulder. They lean toward the mist, toward the warm pools, toward one another. In the constant, gentle updraft from the vents, they sway in unison, a slow choreography of leaves that creates pockets and corridors, small rooms within the chamber’s larger hollow. Some have begun to thread their roots into cracks in the basalt, not to anchor firmly—the rock is too young, too brittle—but to drink directly from the weeping condensate. Others have claimed the collapsed scaffolding entirely, using the steel lattice as a spine, growing through its hollow bones until the metal is no longer visible except as dark veins beneath the green.

The vines descend from ledges high above, thinner, more ambitious. They do not merely hang; they seek. Following the humidity gradients downward like threads pulled by water, they wrap around vent openings, weave through the pump’s remaining pipes, and in several places have entered the hollow steel entirely, using the rust-eaten channels as protected root-passages. Where they emerge at the other end—sometimes into a pool, sometimes near that humming cable—they unfurl leaves small and round, like coins of darker green, catching the diffused daylight from above and holding it.

Together, these plants do not compete in any visible way. They overlap, interpenetrate, share moisture and shadow in a saturation so complete that every niche is filled, every surface either growing or being prepared for growth. They are the true population: not individuals, but a collective presence that has replaced the idea of “empty space” with the idea of “full green.”

Then there are the pools—scattered like wet eyes across the mossy floor. They are not deep; perhaps a hand’s span at most, dark with dissolved minerals, their surfaces constantly disturbed by rising warmth. Each pool has its own character of skin: some shimmer with a thin, iridescent film of bacteria or extremophile algae, colors shifting between copper and pale blue-green in the diffused light; others are clearer, revealing black basalt bottoms where small, pale crustaceans—no larger than a fingernail—move with extreme slowness, feeding on the mineral film, barely disturbing the water. Near the warmest vents, where the hiss is loudest, the pools steam gently, and the air just above them is thickest with the metallic sweetness that the moss seems to favor. These waters do not rush; they accumulate, they breathe, they exchange heat with the rock in cycles that take days to complete.

The industrial remnants are not dead. They are inhabited differently.

The pump—rust-eaten, half-buried in fern—still functions, or rather, it still moves. Every few hours, an irregular thrum travels through its housing, a deep, patient vibration that sends ripples through the nearest pool and causes the moss above it to tremble slightly. It moves water through pipes that now serve as root-tunnels for the vines, pulling warm mineral water up from deeper fractures and releasing it in slow, oxidized droplets that bead on its iron surface before falling into the pool below. It has become part of the circulation system—not of industry now, but of the chamber itself. The vines draw from its pipes; the moss feeds on the moisture it weeps; the pale insects hover near its warmth.

The collapsed scaffolding serves as a bridge, a trellis, a skeleton clothed in life. Where it still stands, ferns grow horizontally along its beams, using them as elevated platforms to reach closer to the light falling through the ceiling breach. Where it has fallen, it lies half-submerged in moss and pool, its steel slowly being incorporated into the mineral buildup—layers of rust and calcification and root-tissue merging until it is difficult to say where the manufactured ends and the geological begins.

And then there is the cable—green with corrosion and moss, extending from some forgotten point near the wall, still carrying low voltage. It hums with a frequency so low it is felt more in the teeth than heard, a sustained note that seems to resonate with the geothermal hiss rather than against it. Near its base, the moss glows—subtly, not with the sharpness of artificial light, but with a subdued phosphorescence, an amber-green shimmer that deepens in the mist. Whether the current feeds this, or whether the cable has become a conductor for some chemical process in the moss itself, is unclear. It simply is: a singing wire, maintained by nothing visible, serving no purpose except to exist within the green, to provide a faint, constant energy that the life here has adopted as its own.

What moves through this, other than water and plant?

Rarely, very rarely, there are insects—pale, almost translucent, with long, delicate legs, hovering near the warmest pools in small clouds, no more than a dozen at a time. They do not bite; they do not seem to eat the plants. They drift, they settle on moss, they leave tiny tracks of disturbed spores. Once, perhaps twice a season by some unseen calendar, a high-altitude bird passes through the opening far above—a brief shadow, a single call that echoes once against the curved walls, and then absence.

But the true movement is slower: the millimeter crawl of moss over steel; the imperceptible widening of vent cracks; the gradual brightening of a pool’s mineral skin; the cable’s constant, patient hum. Nothing here rushes. The dynamics are weak because everything is already in balance, already saturated, already touching.

What lives here, then, is not a list of species. It is a condition: warmth shared between rock and machine; moisture that belongs simultaneously to geology and growth; electricity that has outlived its purpose and now feeds a moss that glows in response. The chamber itself is inhabited—by the green that has made it its body, by the waters that serve as its slow-circulating blood, by the broken machines that have become its bones, and by the constant, layered sound that is its breath.

If you sit long enough in the mist, near the warmest pool, near the humming wire, you begin to feel that you are not observing a place. You are being observed—not by eyes, but by presence. The moss holds the heat you bring; the ferns adjust their sway to the disturbance of your sitting; the pool’s surface pauses for a moment before continuing its slow exchange with the air. You are tolerated, perhaps incorporated temporarily into the system—another warm body, another source of moisture, another brief shadow against the soft, wet green-gold light.

This is who lives here: not people, not animals in any conventional sense, but the intertwined life of young stone and older iron, of patient plants and singing wires, of warm black pools and the pale, drifting things that visit them. A single, enclosed breath, suspended near the crown of the world, changing so slowly that to live here must mean to live across centuries in the space of an afternoon, growing, weeping, humming, and waiting.
