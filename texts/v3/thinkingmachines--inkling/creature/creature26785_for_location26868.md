What lives here does not arrive; it accumulates, like the dust that has settled into frost over the floor, like the chord that has never broken. They are few—perhaps not more than four dozen bodies spread across all the hanging tiers, though counting is difficult because they are often still, and because the vegetation has begun to claim them as it claims everything else. You do not see them first. You feel the ropes shift in a pattern that is not wind, and then, in the warm shadow beyond the pillar of light, a shape separates from the wall.

They are dry. This is the first and last thing about them. There is no moisture in their bodies, no pulse that sloshes or drips; whatever once flowed through them has been replaced long ago by something thicker, slower—resin, perhaps, or mineralized lymph, dark as the bruise-purple vines that curtain the ceilings. Their skin has the texture and color of the architecture: aged honey where it is exposed, rust where it is worn, ash-grey where the filaments have woven themselves into pores that never close. They are not dead. They are preserved in the same chemistry that preserves this place—arid, sealed, warm.

Their bodies are compressed vertically, adapted over generations to the low, rounded hollow. They stand—when they stand—no taller than the platforms require, with torsos long and flexible, limbs jointed for climbing ropes and bridging narrow gaps. Their heads are narrow, faces reduced to slits and ridges, ears either absent or grown over with the same spongy, rootless growth that feeds on the updraft. They do not need eyes for the center; they live in the shadow. But in the reflected glow that rises from the white dust, they navigate with a sensitivity that seems closer to hearing—though hearing, in the usual sense, is impossible here. They feel the chord. Their bones, perhaps hollow like the ceramic pipes, resonate with it. They know when a pipe is out of tune not by listening but by the wrong vibration in their ribs.

They do not speak. Speech requires air shaped for meaning, and the air here is already shaped—held in a single, centuries-long tone that fills every hollow. They have surrendered shouting, as the prompt of the space demands. Instead, they communicate through adjustments: a pipe turned a fraction, a rope tightened, a filament knotted into a particular tension. Their language is maintenance. Their conversation is the slow retuning of the chord as temperature shifts or vines narrow a throat. They are the caretakers of the sound, and the sound is their memory.

There is no hierarchy visible, no throne in this hive of stacked spaces—only sectors. Each inhabitant tends a cluster of platforms, a stretch of wall, a family of pipes. They move alone, slowly, wrapped in layers of woven mineral fiber that have hardened over time into a kind of armor or shell, decorated—if decoration is the word—with hooks and shards of soft metal that they shape by hand and patience. These are not ornaments but tools: for scraping mineral crust, for guiding filaments, for repairing the mortar-like patches where vegetation hardens into wall. They are primitive in technology but infinite in repetition. They have built this city not in generations but in accretions, each hand-carved block placed by someone who will never live to see the block weathered smooth.

And they are not alone in their bodies. The vegetation does not merely grow near them; it integrates. Filaments emerge from collars of dried vine around necks that may no longer have distinct necks. Some have become walking gardens, slow-moving pillars of ochre and dry gold, so overgrown that separating the builder from the built environment is impossible. They feed as the plants feed—not by drinking, for there is no water, but by absorption. They stand in the warm updraft, in the reflected light, and draw the mineral aerosols through porous surfaces. They do not eat in any sense you would recognize. They are part of the exchange: light for growth, wind for voice, dust for foundation, and in return, they maintain the pipes. They are the lungs and the ears of the hollow, though they have neither.

The small hard-shelled things—the thumb-sized scuttlers across the ropes—are not their pets, nor their prey. They are co-inhabitants, perhaps the only other mobile life, feeding on the dry papery rustle of falling vine-dust. The builders do not interact with them. They are too slow, too absorbed in the chord.

What is strange—what makes them weird rather than merely adapted—is the possibility that they are not born here, or if they are, they are born changed. There are no children visible, no small versions moving among the ropes. The population is static, replacement occurring not through reproduction but through transformation: when one stops moving, when the filaments fully claim the body, that body becomes part of the wall, part of the pipe-system, perhaps even contributing a bone to become a flute embedded in the new layer. The dead do not leave. They are tuned into the architecture. The chord includes them.

If you wait long enough in the shadow, you might see one approach the pillar of light—not entering the blinding column, but reaching toward its edge, extending a long, jointed arm wrapped in fiber and vine, to adjust a ceramic pipe near the floor. The movement is not hurried. There is no urgency in a space where time is measured in erosion and growth. They work, they absorb, they resonate. Then they retreat back into the hanging tiers, into the fused symbiosis of brick and plant, and you are left unsure whether you saw a being or a function—whether the builder is the one who tends the pipes, or the pipes themselves have grown a body to tend them.

When you leave, back through the throat of eroded stone that closes behind you, you carry the sound with you, and for a long time you wonder: were they ever separate from it? The inhabitants of this place are not citizens of a city. They are the city’s patience made mobile, the dry, warm, vibrating answer to a world that has no water, no sun that moves, and almost no people—only this impossible, thriving, singing exchange between light, wind, mineral, and what was once, perhaps, human, now preserved and singing in the dark.
