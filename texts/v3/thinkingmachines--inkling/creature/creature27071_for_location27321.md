They live everywhere, and nowhere in particular.

Start with the surfaces: they are not scattered so much as laminated. Each creature—if the word still applies—is smaller than the last joint of a thumb, articulated like an insect that forgot to grow, built from braided wire, plates of chitin that have taken on the color of oxidized iron, and a brittle enamel the shade of old rust. They do not crawl. They have arrived. They cling to the vertical iron in perfect densities, shoulder to shoulder, joint to joint, forming a second skin over the factory-cathedral. Where two pipes meet in a rib-cage arc, they cluster in the hollow, not nesting, not breeding—simply occupying the coordinate they have always occupied.

But they are not only on things. They hold the air.

Between the gear-houses, in those minutes-wide silences where the drone thickens and never resolves, they hang in dense, unmoving clouds. They hold shape like smoke that has made a decision and kept it—columns, slow curls, flat discs suspended between beams—each body locked into its place within the cloud. Thousands upon thousands. You can walk through them, or rather through the spaces they have politely left empty, and feel no disturbance. They do not scatter. They do not react. Each one occupies its precise point in three dimensions, drinking the condensation that beads near its mouth-parts without lowering the droplet, without breaking the surface tension, without altering the cool equilibrium that sustains them.

Look closer—though there is no closer, only more of the same. Their bodies generate a light that is barely light: grey-green, flat, without warmth or shadow-casting. It fills the vault evenly, so that nothing is illuminated and nothing is dark; the glow is an ambient fact, like the damp. By it you see that they are not dead. They are not dormant. They are in a state that has replaced biology with geometry. Some hang upside down from the cloudy glass, antennae or wire-limbs extended into the hanging droplets, static as clock hands at midnight. Others rest on the slate floor, not resting exactly, but being present upon it, adding their small weight to the erosion that finished its work long before they arrived, or perhaps long after.

There is no swarm behavior. No hive mind that moves them, no signal that passes through the cloud. They are overwhelming in number precisely because they never gather for purpose. They are the purpose, distributed evenly, covering every available plane without crowding, without absence. In the gaps between machinery—the vast, sparse emptiness that should feel deserted—they fill the void not with movement but with presence. They are the fauna of a place that has replaced life with equilibrium.

And they are merged.

Not symbolically. Physically. Where wire bodies meet iron pipe, the rust-colored enamel seems to have grown from the metal itself, or the metal has grown tired and become them. They drink not water but the state of being wet; they hang not in air but in the specific coolness just above freezing, just below comfort. The constant drone of the motors—low, layered, never changing—seems to pass through them or emerge from them, as fixed as their posture. They do not make sound. They do not need to. The architecture hums, and they are its resonance made visible, made jointed, made small.

You might ask what they want, but the question has no purchase here. They do not want. They do not reproduce, do not feed in any cycle you could recognize, do not die. They are the settled form of this compressed hamlet, the living crust of its perpetual motion. If you left and returned after an age, you would find the same cloud in the same shape, the same creature with its wire-limbs extended toward the same unfalling droplet, the same grey-green glow without source or shadow.

They live, then, in the only way this place allows: as inhabitants of an equilibrium so complete that being alive and being present have become the same thing. Small, rust-colored, luminous without warmth, they cover the vaulted room like a second atmosphere—industrial, motionless, overwhelming—keeping watch over the machines that turn for nothing, in a damp that never pools, beneath a ceiling that has forgotten how high it wants to be.
