They live here not as intruders but as inheritors—hundreds, perhaps thousands, of small translucent bodies suspended in the warm black like living glass beads threaded on invisible currents. At first they seem only light: brief, pale blue threads that drift and fade, the faintest metabolic glimmer of creatures who have never known sun. But stay long enough, and the embers resolve into forms.

The smallest are no larger than the joints of your fingers—rounded, gelatinous, almost spherical—drifting in loose, patient congregations near the dome where the water feels thickest. Others are elongated, slim as dropped needles, moving with barely perceptible undulations of bodies that seem folded from translucent membrane. And then there are the stranger ones: not rounded, not streamlined, but arranged in unfamiliar symmetries—too many delicate limbs radiating from a central axis, each limb jointed with microscopic precision, bending at angles that suggest geometry more than biology. They hang in the middle column, barely moving, letting the water carry them in slow arcs; their bodies are so clear you can see faint, dark lines running through them—not blood, not veins, but something like woven fiber or crystallized thread, organizing their softness from within.

They feed without hurry. Near the walls, where the pale mineral crust thickens into a faint, non-living bloom, the grazers settle—if settling is the right word for bodies that never truly rest. They extend mouth-parts too small to see clearly: perhaps porous surfaces, perhaps delicate rasping fringes, consuming the architecture’s long digestion. Filter-feeders drift with mouths open to the water itself, catching particles of dead tissue, of sediment, of whatever falls slowly from above—though nothing falls here, so perhaps they catch only each other’s shed membranes, the invisible snow of their own slow metabolism. Scavengers weave among them, slender and quick only by comparison, breaking down the dead into finer particles that return to the crust, to the water, to the next generation of grazers.

There is no aggression in their crowding. The chamber is small—chapel-sized, intimate, saturated—but they coexist in a density that should choke them, yet they do not swarm. They brush one another with soft, silent contacts: a limb grazing a membrane, a rounded body bumping an elongated one and drifting apart with the same languid momentum. They do not school. They do not flee. They move through one another’s presence with the indifference of things that have never needed to compete for light, for air, for territory—only for the constant, patient warmth.

That warmth seems to guide them. They gather more thickly near certain curves of the dome, near sections of wall where the chisel marks—worn now into blurred ridges—retain a faint, directed heat, as if the stone still remembers the hands that shaped it. The creatures respond to this without consciousness, clustering where the temperature is most precisely maintained, their bioluminescence flickering slightly brighter—though still dim, still blue, still brief—when they pass through these warmer pockets. They are thermally bound, metabolically tuned to this single, enclosed warmth. Outside this chamber, in colder water or in air, they would not survive; they are native not just to darkness but to this specific, directed darkness.

Sound, when it comes, is theirs: the delicate click of a shell—perhaps a protective casing grown around one of the smaller bodies—brushing against worn tile fused smooth by ages. Or the slow pulse of displacement as one of the elongated ones contracts its too-many limbs and shifts position over hours rather than seconds. Otherwise, they are silent. They do not call to one another. The blue flashes are not signals in any language you could learn; they are byproducts, exhaust, the faint burning of bodies that live very slowly and die very slowly.

And they do die. Not often, not visibly—death here is as glacial as life. A body stops its minimal flux, sinks to the gently undulating floor, and is gradually unmade by the others, by the mineral crust, by the water itself. Nothing rots quickly in this warmth. Nothing decays with urgency. The dead become part of the architecture’s digestion, feeding the pale film that feeds the living, in a cycle so slow it appears eternal.

Yet there is something else living here, something not animal. Not the mineral crust—that is dead, architecture digesting itself. But the chamber’s own slow breath, the directed warmth, the sense that the rounded dome and the fitted blocks are not merely containers but an organism that has not finished growing. The creatures are its inhabitants, yes, but they may also be its expression—bred or summoned or evolved into being by the same ancient craft that built the walls, adapted to maintain the equilibrium of this submerged summit. They move with the chamber’s metabolism, not against it. They are the life that fills the lung of this forgotten spire.

Here, then, is the answer to who lives: not a single species, not a colony with a queen or a hierarchy, but a collective presence—translucent, multi-limbed, patient—occupying every vertical inch of warm water in densities that should be impossible. They are filter and grazer and scavenger and light-source, arranged in unfamiliar symmetries, glowing faintly, clicking softly against worn tile, never growing green, never rusting, never shouting. Only drifting, feeding, touching, and slowly, slowly being remade into the same warm darkness from which they came.
