Here, existence is a solitary verb.

The gliders come first—not as a flock, but as one, then another, separated by days of silence and miles of mist. They hang in the warm, thick air between columns with wings like stretched membrane, yard-wide sails of translucent tissue veined with slow-moving sap. They do not beat; they adjust, tilting fractionally to catch the imperceptible currents that rise from the root-bed’s metabolic heat. A glider might pass within reach of your bridge, and you would feel only a shift in humidity, a faint greenish luminescence tracking across its underside, and then it is gone into the hollow of a distant trunk, not arriving so much as being gradually received by the architecture itself. They migrate alone, always alone, following moisture-rhythms older than memory, and when two meet—if they meet—the encounter unfolds without sound: a circling, a touching of wing-tips, a shared drift downward into a marsh-node where they rest separately, on opposite sides of the black pool, never touching, never calling.

Inside the columns, the root-mazes are inhabited differently.

There are things that creep—not with legs, but with contractile roots, with tendrils that have learned the texture of fiber and rot. They navigate the hollow interiors where gravity pulls sideways, where walking means clinging to vertical root-floors. Some are small, no larger than a hand, clusters of mobile tissue with eyes like dark beads embedded in vine-skin; they hunt not by pursuit but by waiting inside knots, becoming indistinguishable from the living wall until a passing glider’s shadow triggers them, and then they unfold—slow, chemical, inevitable. Others are larger, distributed across yards of interior surface, not one creature but a consensus of creeping parts that share sensation through sap-contact, deciding together whether to tighten a root-bridge or release a drip of warm water onto a traveler far below.

But the most profound inhabitants are those that have forgotten the distinction.

In the marsh-nodes, where black water lies still beneath floating mats of root-hair, there grow clusters—half-plant, half-purpose. They are tissue with awareness: mobile knots of vine that have developed hunting, or perhaps the plants themselves have begun to move with intention. They remember. The weave remembers every cut made by bone chisels, every knot tied in twisted vine, and some of that memory has taken form: shapes that drift at the edges of the pools, not swimming but growing toward prey with the patience of roots seeking water. They have eyes scattered across their surface like dew, and when they move, they do not break the silence; they are part of the silence, part of the fibrous absorption that swallows sound. A hunter in a sap-pool might wait for days—not sleeping, not resting, but existing in a state of suspended chemical anticipation—before it strikes, and the strike itself is quiet, a closing of tissue, a drawing-in that takes hours, the prey not killed so much as incorporated, becoming part of the cluster’s slow metabolism.

The predators, too, are solitary. There is no pack, no chorus of hunting cries. Each waits in its own warm pool, in its own hollow node, following rhythms of moisture and decay that match its particular temperature. They do not compete; the immensity allows for this. A predator might live and die in one chamber, never knowing another of its kind existed miles away in a different column, and its death is not an end but a redistribution—its tissue loosening, its awareness diffusing back into the surrounding root-mat, its memory of bone tools and ancient knots added to the collective storage.

For the plants themselves are aware, in a manner too slow and distributed to be called mind.

They are the substrate and the architecture and, in places, the inhabitants. The hollow columns breathe; they expand slowly, creaking in frequencies below hearing, and in that expansion there is decision—where to thicken a wall, where to allow a root-bridge to sag and collect more water, where to grow a new hollow for a marsh-node that has not yet been needed. They remember the builders, those primitive hands with stone axes worn smooth, and some of this memory has become active: vines that arrange themselves into shapes resembling the old tools, root-knots that form patterns like the twisted cords once used to bind structures together, as if the weave is replaying its own construction, honoring or mourning the ones who shaped it.

And the builders—if they are not entirely dissolved—are present as absence.

Perhaps they became the gliders, learning flight to escape the gravity that pulls toward columns. Perhaps they became the clusters in the pools, giving up mobility for permanence. Or perhaps they are still walking, somewhere in the root-mazes, changed by centuries of slow integration, their bones become part of the fiber, their breath part of the warm rise from deep decay. They left no monuments, only the shape of patience, and now the patience has outgrown them.

Time here does not allow for community in the old sense. Life is spread across the immensity like moisture across a vast root—present everywhere, touching nowhere directly. The fauna moves in long, silent migrations between nodes, following the pulse of weeping and drying, and in these migrations they are briefly together—two gliders passing in the mist, a creeping thing and a cluster of vine-hunters encountering each other in a hollow chamber, recognizing through chemical scent that they are both of the weave, both solitary, both waiting. Then they continue, alone, into the silence.

To live here is to become part of the engineered absence.

You are on the bridge, and something is in the hollow to your left—not watching, exactly, but aware of your presence through the vibration of your feet on the living floor, through the warmth you disturb in the air. It might be a glider, resting with folded membranes. It might be a cluster of tissue with scattered eyes, growing slowly in your direction. It might be the column itself, extending a root-tendril to taste the air you exhale. The mist thickens. A drop of sap falls, very slowly, from an arch miles above, and though you wait, there is no sound—only the fact of its falling, and the knowledge that somewhere, in the warm black pool below, something silent prepares to receive it.

The Weave does not need you. It has its own inhabitants, spread like breath across an architecture of total vegetation, moving in rhythms of decay and growth, predation and patience, each one alone, each one part of the greater living hollow that remembers everything and makes no noise.
