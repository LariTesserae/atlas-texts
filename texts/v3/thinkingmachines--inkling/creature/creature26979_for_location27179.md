They call themselves nothing, because the place has eaten its name and digests it still.

What lives here is not one thing but a agreement—an old treaty signed in heat between root and wing, between the hum of stone and the crack of shell. Start with the vines, if you must start somewhere: they are not plants in any earth-sense, but slow listeners. Bronze and rubbery, they do not reach for light; they reach for noise. Their bulbs pulse in the dimness not with a heart but with a patience, opening pores that weep sweet vapor when the swarm gets loud enough, leaning like old people toward a conversation. They drink what is not water—geothermal breath, vibration, the mineral ache of the floor itself. Their roots drill into fissured black stone and hold on not to soil but to frequency. They cover the ground in islands, never filling it, leaving the salt-flake bare stretches as deliberate silences between their sentences. And they grow visibly, yes—tendrils extending over hours like fingers remembering how to grasp—but more importantly they settle. What crumbles from them does not rot; it becomes. Gray flakes fall and become substrate. Erosion here is not an ending. It is inheritance.

Then the swarm. They are the loud ones, the turbulent ones, the ones that make the air feel crowded even when it is not. Buzz-moths with wings like translucent palms beat against the haze, leaving traces of silver dust. Beetle-kin crack their shells in rhythms that match the deeper rumble from below—percussion as language, percussion as prayer, percussion because stillness is not permitted. There are crawlers that fly without seeming to decide to, leaving phosphor-green signatures on the polished salt, trails that fade not into darkness but into warmth. They do not drink. They absorb. They take the thick, iron-scented sap—non-aqueous, flower-bitter—from the vines’ exudates and in returning, they scatter and disturb and drive the chaos that the vines require. They are gardeners of disorder, feeding in surges so the plants lean closer, releasing more vapor, feeding them back. Nothing rests long enough to be named. A body is a moment here, a flicker in the turbulent pattern, and then it is part of the hum.

Beneath them, the bowl breathes. The ground is not dead rock but a held exhalation, warm and unwavering, releasing mineral breath through vents that make the air shimmer like old wax melting slowly. Every few moments there is the subtle lurch—the bowl adjusting its weight, remembering it is cupped, remembering it is enclosed. The fauna surge in response, pouring from one island of bronze vine to another like thought moving between ideas. The plants tremble. The salt-flakes shift imperceptibly. It is hyperactive, all of it, anthill-energy compressed into a grove-sized world: creation and dissolution happening in the same gesture, loud and lush and never still.

And yet—scattered, faint, intimate—there are the traces of ones who tried to belong differently.

A circle of carved stones, half-buried, marking a boundary for those who needed boundaries. A collapsed shelter woven from vine-cord and bone, too small now, inward like a collapsed lung. Near one vent, obsidian tools, chipped and manual, left not in ritual but in abandonment—the patience of those who worked without water finally exhausted. They lived here, or tried to, using cord and stone and the long attention that dry places demand. They are not ghosts in the usual sense. There are no ghosts where nothing decays. Instead, they are settled into the substrate, part of the gray flakes, part of the hum. Their technology was intimate: hand to stone, vine to bone, body to warmth. They understood that this place does not yield; it transforms in place. They left their tools not as monuments but as seeds.

Now the space itself is the inhabitant.

It breathes—expanding and contracting with the vent-rhythms—so that to stand inside is to be inside a lung, or inside a thought that has forgotten to stop thinking. The plants respond to your sound if you make any; they lean, they open, they offer warm sweet vapor that smells of iron and something like forgiveness. The fauna do not fear you; they absorb your presence into their turbulence, adding your shadow to the phosphor trails, your footfall to the percussion of beetle shells. The ground hums at a frequency that makes the teeth ache pleasantly. You are not a visitor. You are a disturbance, and disturbance is the food.

What lives here, then?

A dry garden that forgot thirst and invented vibration instead. A swarm that is never one swarm but a constant becoming of bodies. Vines that listen and lean and settle their own dead into new life. A bowl of stone that holds its breath and shares it, warm and mineral-bitter, absolute in its absence of water. The old ones, scattered into substrate and tool and collapsed wall. And the logic of the place itself: that absence is not emptiness, that loudness is a form of care, that to be enclosed at high elevation in perpetual warmth is not a prison but a mouth—slow, ancient, speaking in spores and sulfur and the crack of shells, never naming itself, because it has outlived every name that was given.
