What lives here does not gather; it isolates.

The first inhabitants are the towers themselves—root-cathedrals, trunk-pillars, vine-bridges—vegetation not as cover but as continent, total and architectural (2.7). They are not trees in any familiar sense, but something closer to living geology: fibrous, bark-armored, growing with such violent speed that their expansion sounds like ice cracking in slow motion. Each tower stands alone, separated from its nearest neighbor by gulfs of air so wide that light fractures between them. They do not form a forest; they form an archipelago of biomass suspended over nothing. In this near-emptiness (0.5), they are both landscape and population, reproducing not through seeds alone but through collapse—shedding weight, rotting into substrate, and regenerating in cycles so rapid that a structure standing today may be a different organism tomorrow. They glow with cold, frost-bright bioluminescence (2.9), their tissues holding moisture like blood in a body that never stops moving (0.9, 2.6). They breathe the turbulent air, feeding on condensation and the oxidation of young, sharp rock (1.1). They are the place’s dominant life, and its only permanence is the turbulence of their own becoming and undoing.

Between them, in the hollow spaces that bend sound to strange geometries (1.0, 1.2), drift the sparse, solitary creatures—fauna rare enough (1.4) that encountering one feels like witnessing an accident. They are small, likely translucent or frost-coated, adapted to the bitter cold (0.2) and to navigation without ground. Some may crawl along the vine-bridges, feeding on the constant decay of fallen growth; others might ride the violent updrafts (2.6), carrying spores or seeds through the void like living pollen in a storm that never quite lands. They do not swarm. They do not call out—sound is swallowed here, and a whisper travels farther and stranger than a shout. Perhaps they see not in the glaring white-blue illumination that makes the emptiness feel larger, but in the subtle shifts of temperature, in the weeping of condensation (0.9) that beads on bark like slow tears. They leave marks: not language, but wear—paths worn into a single bridge, niches carved by repeated feeding, perhaps the bone hooks and simple metal pins (0.9, 1.1) found driven into bark, lashed with fiber, holding platforms that have long since been crushed by growth.

These artifacts suggest another presence, faint and scattered. Not a civilization—built presence is too slight (0.9), technology too primitive (1.1)—but something that passed through, or perhaps still does, rarely. Small shelters half-overgrown, hand-tools left in dry niches where moisture (0.9) has not yet claimed them, platforms tied to tower sides like temporary nests. Whether these were made by the small bridge-creatures, or by something else that moved through this recently torn hollow (0.2, 1.1) and left only footprints before the plants swallowed them back, is unclear. What remains is not a culture but a residue: single structures, occasional, as if life here is always provisional, always passing.

The moisture itself is inhabited in smaller ways—microscopic, perhaps, living in the frost that accumulates in hollows of fallen growth, in the oxidation-smelling dust of fresh fracture. Seeds and spores drift in chaotic storms, not merely reproductive material but a form of presence, filling the air with potential that may never root, given the isolation and the scale (2.8).

So to ask what lives here is to ask about a system of islands in a cold, bright void. The answer is layered: the towering plants, turbulent and self-consuming; the scattered, silent creatures that navigate between them; the drifting life of spores and decay; and the ghost-trace of primitive hands—whether creature or traveler—that tied fiber to bark and left hooks behind, then vanished into the turbulence. Nothing dominates entirely. Even the vegetation, for all its totality, exists as separate, lonely monuments. Life here is not communal; it is parallel, separated by miles of empty, luminous air, sustained by damp tissue and violent growth, adapted to a world that was born sharp and has not yet decided whether to stay still.
