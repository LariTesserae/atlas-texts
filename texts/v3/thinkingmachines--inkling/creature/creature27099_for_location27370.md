What lives here does not gather. It distributes itself—across surfaces, through interstices, in the gaps between intention and accident—until the idea of “inhabitant” unravels into something more like a shared condition of being present without being together.

First, there are the walls themselves, though “walls” misleads. They are the bodies, or what remains of them: a colony that never needed individuals, only a collective secretion. Imagine organisms without faces, without nerves in any layout we would recognize, that spent ages exuding calcium and phosphate, layer upon layer, until they had enclosed themselves in a grid of their own making—avenues, towers, plazas—all of it shelter that no one ever entered. Then they stopped. Or slowed. They retreated into dormancy, not death, leaving the architecture as an exhalation fossilized in real time. The plates are porous with their old chemistry; they weep slowly, a mineral perspiration that feeds what comes after. They respire—millimeters of expansion, millimeters of contraction—drawing the dry air through channels too small to see, processing what they can, releasing nothing audible. They are alive in the way a reef is alive: not one thing, but a decision made in unison long ago, still holding its shape.

On their cooler-facing surfaces, where the dim phosphorescence pools differently, cling the grey-green films. They are not rooted. They have no stems, no ambition upward. They are digestion made visible—papery, translucent, almost like dried skin or the residue of a breath held too long. They do not seek the glow; they seek the chemistry of it, breaking down the faint light into salt, into mineral, into spore. When they reproduce, they do not scatter. They hang—motionless spores suspended in the absolute stillness, waiting for a current that is not wind, for a drift that is not flight, to carry them to another plate where they might cling and begin again. There are no seasons here to drive them. Only the slow rhythm of the walls’ breathing, which occasionally stirs them, and the faint chemical gradient that tells them where to be.

And then, between the ribs, in the cathedral-void above and within the grid—there are the others. Flat, translucent, no larger than an open palm. Not insects, not fish, not anything with a name that belongs to a world with water. They drift. Not swimming, because there is nothing to push against; not flying, because there is no air that moves with purpose. They are like leaves in zero gravity, except they are not dead. They feed—on the falling dust that cycles in invisible currents, on the spores that hang like dust themselves, on the mineral weep that glistens at the base of the plates. Each one is alone. They do not school, do not mate in view, do not acknowledge one another in the corridors wide enough for processions. You might wait hours and see only one, crossing an intersection like a thought that forgot its thinker, leaving no trace except, perhaps, a slight dimming of the phosphorescence where it passed, as if it consumed not matter but possibility.

But to stop there—to name them builder, plant, drifter—is to miss what makes them truly of this place. They do not form an ecology in the familiar sense, with chains and dependencies and balances negotiated by need. They form a parallel existence. The walls do not care for the films; the films do not wait for the drifters; the drifters do not know they are inside a city. They share only the chemistry—the dry air, the cycling mineral dust, the absence of water, the cool stillness, the faint sickly glow. Life here is not a web. It is a coincidence that has learned to persist.

There is also the hollow itself, which is not empty despite its near-void density. The space between the plates—those enormous corridors, those open towers—functions as a lung, a circulatory system without blood. The invisible currents lift fine dust in patterns that repeat, not because anything directs them, but because the geometry of the grid demands it: right angles, intersections, hollow towers that channel what little movement exists. The erosion is young, sharp-edged, fresh; this system has not yet been worn smooth by time. It is still finding its rhythm, still settling into the shape of being alive.

So if you ask who lives here, the answer fractures. There are the dormant architects in the bone-pale ribs. There are the grey-green digesters on their flanks. There are the flat, silent drifters passing through like slow rain in reverse. There are the hanging spores, the cycling dust, the mineral weep, the breathing plates. And there is the hollow—enclosed, sharp-edged, dry—holding all of them in a settlement without settlers, a city without citizens, where every form of life is complete unto itself and utterly alone, moving in a silent, mineral rhythm that needs no witness to continue.
