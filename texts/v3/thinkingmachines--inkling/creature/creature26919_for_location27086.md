What lives here does not live in any way that demands a future.

Across the high ground—sharp, black, two and a half miles up and still young—there are only the plants that have forgotten how to change. The silver-green moss is the continent’s first and most complete citizen. It does not grow upward; that would be ambition, and ambition requires time that moves. Instead it spreads outward in perfect, unvarying mats, occupying horizontal space with a patience that looks like conquest but is simply presence without event. It never seeds. It never withers. It does not compete with its neighbors because competition requires that one thing diminish for another to gain, and here nothing diminishes. The moss is lush not because it thrives, but because it has been released from the obligation to thrive.

Among the moss stand the fungal spires—tall, pale, unbranching, made of a flesh that has never learned to rot or to reach for light. They are not trees; they are chimneys that stand silent, exhaling nothing, consuming nothing visible. Their existence is vertical in a world of horizontal occupation, but even this verticality is static. They do not lean. They do not fall. They simply are, like white exclamation marks in a sentence that has no grammar.

From the vertical faces of fresh granite and basalt hang the crystalline vines, translucent and motionless, drinking the constant mineral tears that weep from unweathered stone. Their leaves do not turn. They do not fall. They hang in perfect suspension, feeding on water that will never become a river, never carve a valley, never finish its journey downward. The vines are the continent’s only drinkers, and what they drink is perpetual aftermath.

There is no one to eat them.

That is the essential emptiness. No insects crawl the vines. No microscopic presence moves through the moss. No bird crosses the gray-white sky, because the sky itself is not a place of passage but a suspended layer of condensing mist that never breaks, never storms, never clears. The lushness—this overwhelming compensation of green and white and translucent crystal—exists for no consumer. It is an ecology without appetite, without death, without the circular logic that binds life to itself.

And then there are the installations.

Seven, perhaps nine, scattered like nodes in the empty volume. They are not inhabited by people. There are no workers in the hollow factories, no bodies in the vast constructed spaces, no presence inside except the machinery itself. The true residents of these settlement-scale knots are the engines—cathedral-sized, turning always. The belts move without hands. The pipes, thick as rivers, carry nothing that transforms. The smokeless stacks stand as vertical monuments to process without product. Electricity arcs in glowing webs, flat and steady, illuminating spaces that need no darkness to exist. The machines run loudly—always at that constant 2.4 presence—filling sharp valleys with a drone that has no rhythm, no rest, no crescendo and no fade. They are the continent’s only voices, and they speak in monotone.

These installations are islands of sound and steel in an ocean of silent plant and mist. Between them, nothing moves. The rail lines run straight for days, carrying nothing visible. The factories are shells filled with the machinery they house, running eternally without rust, without wear, without the need for repair or the possibility of decay. They will never fill with people. They have already emptied to the maximum degree; their density is near void, urban form without urban life.

So what lives here?

The moss lives, without seeding. The spires live, without branching. The vines live, without falling. The machines live—if running without purpose is a form of life—without operators, without rest, without change.

And beneath all of this, in the negative space that is more present than the presence, lives the stasis itself. It is the true inhabitant: the removal of change made tangible in sharp young rock, in flat gray light, in water that weeps but never carves. The continent is named nothing because repetition requires change, and here change has been removed—but the removal itself remains, vast and cold and loud, stretching three thousand miles of perfect, unbroken equilibrium.

You can stand on a knife-ridge and understand that everything beneath you—plant, stone, machine, mist—occupies the same condition: present, measured, and entirely unchanged. Nothing hides in the moss. Nothing waits in the hollow stacks. The roar continues, not because anyone demands it, but because the possibility of stopping has also been removed.

That is the life of this place: an eternal present tense, inhabited by things that do not die because they have never fully begun, and by machines that do not stop because they have never been turned on by anyone who might turn them off.
