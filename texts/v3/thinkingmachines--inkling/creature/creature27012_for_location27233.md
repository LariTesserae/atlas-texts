Here lives the architecture itself.

The primary inhabitants are not built upon the valley but grown through it—colossal hollow spires of cool, translucent fibrous tissue, each one simultaneously organism and edifice, root and tower. Some swell to the width of buildings, their walls thick with layered pith and vascular channels; others rise slender as needles, threading upward into the bedrock ceiling. They do not stand in rows like a planted forest; they organize. Root-bulbs descend to knot themselves into factory-bases—dry forges, mechanized growth-chambers, electrical substations—while above, webbing root-bridges and suspended aerial vines stretch between them, weaving streets and enclosed halls through the air. To live here is to move through them, to enter their hollow interiors where the light is not cast from outside but born within: bioluminescent panels, fused into the inner fibrous surfaces, exhale a steady, flat, cool-white glow that fills the chambers without shadow, without heat, without flicker.

They are alive with slow purpose. Deep, rare creaks resonate through the tissue as fiber expands millimeter by millimeter, and seasonal surges thicken the webs. They feed not on water—there is none—but on compressed mineral vapors, on the trace chemistry of fresh stone fractures crusted with faint powdery salt, on currents piped through hardened fiber ducts that thread like arteries through stalk and vine. The plants are not decoration; they are the settlement’s bones, its streets, its breathing walls, leaving enormous voids between districts that emphasize just how singular and massive each presence is.

Within these living structures, and across the raw, sharp-edged floor between them, moves the fauna—pale, wingless, silent. They are scattered, never dense, adapted to a brightness that should not exist in darkness, to air that carries no moisture, only dry chemical mist. They inhabit the hollow spires as solitary figures or travel the open floors in small, quiet streams—feeding on spores released by the fibrous tissue, on industrial byproducts of the dry forges, on the soft pith accessible at nodes where growth is newest. They do not call out; they do not swarm. Their presence is constant but respectful of the emptiness, threading through districts without crowding the scale.

The machinery, too, is intertwined—pneumatic and magnetic engines embedded directly in root-bulbs, mechanical excavators in the outer quarters chewing slowly through embryonic bedrock, root-nodes themselves expanding with geological patience to widen the valley. This is not nature interrupted by industry but symbiosis: the spires grow around the forges, the forges draw power through root-networks that pulse with electrical load, and the animals migrate along paths carved as much by vine-growth as by need.

What lives here, then, is an inversion made flesh and fiber. A city exists in absolute zero elevation, sealed beneath stone, illuminated not by sun but by its own living walls, sustained by salt and compressed gas and the patient breaking of newborn rock. The inhabitants—tower, creature, and embedded engine—share a single dry rhythm: growth, extraction, migration, fracture. The place is nascent, barely formed, its geology still splitting, its air still settling, its spires still rising into the dark. Nothing here is dead, yet nothing is warmed by surface life. It is cool, silent, vast, and utterly awake—an urban ecosystem being born in the absence of water, glowing quietly in the hollow of the world.
