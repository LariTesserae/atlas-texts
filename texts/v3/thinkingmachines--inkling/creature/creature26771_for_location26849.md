Here lives a place that has outlasted its own purpose, if it ever had one—though purpose, too, may have eroded away into something like weather.

The form is singular and severe: a vertical cylinder, no wider than a nave, driven upward to an extreme height that makes the footprint feel like a small grove enclosed in manufactured walls. From any notional outside, if one could stand near its base without being consumed, it reads as a solitary, hamlet-sized pillar—perhaps the last remaining shaft of some vaster industry, now reduced to this one bounded object, this solitary machine-cathedral of dark alloy and translucent laminate.

Inside, the scale inverts with vertiginous insistence. The ground—if ground is the word—is not ground at all. There is no soil, no bedrock, no substrate older than the build itself. Only grating: oxidized bronze and ceramic tile, worn smooth over long ages, yielding underfoot with a warmth that suggests it has been walked upon, or inhabited, for centuries. The walls are layered plates—manufactured, fully constructed, settled into their own form. There is nothing natural here, nothing unmade. Even the climate is held: warm, damp, subtropical within the shaft, a constant maintained by engines that have forgotten how to stop.

But the density is near-void—0.0, hollow, almost nothing per volume. Between wall and wall, there is enough absence to lose oneself, to fall upward, to hear sound travel for minutes without encountering an obstacle. And it is deafening—2.6—though the deafness is not sharp. The industrial presence is scaled, mechanized, electrified in some archaic fashion: massive gears turning once in days, pistons rising with geological patience, turbines rotating so slowly their motion is visible only in hours of watching. The sound they make is deep, resonant, a continuous bass drone of metal on metal, steam hissing in long exhalations, vibration that travels up the cylinder and returns multiplied by echo. In this hollow, the noise pools. It stands in columns. It is a physical pressure against the chest, made bearable only by its slowness—there are no sudden bangs, only an eternal, grinding, monumental presence.

Vines hang in curtains from ledges hundreds of meters up, some bearing small waxy flowers that never see direct sun. Moss carpets the lower grates in thick, spongy layers, feeding on intermittent moisture—1.2, damp, never flooded, never dry. Water exists in pockets: beads on cool ceramic, shallow pools in troughs designed for other purposes, occasional rain falling from high vents where warm air meets cold, collecting in basins that never drain. The temperature—2.1—encourages slow growth. Nothing rushes. The vegetation has adapted to specific niches: plants that grow only where a particular pipe leaks warmth, vines that track the shadow of a creeping gear.

Fauna—1.6, abundant—occupies the vertical emptiness in scattered multitudes. Not dense, but spread across the extreme height like stars in a narrow sky. Insects swarm in thermal layers near the warm engines. Birds, or what serves as birds here, nest in high ledges; their calls are sharp and brief against the drone. Small climbing creatures move along the vine-curtains, unhurried, adapted to weak gradients of change. Their metabolisms match the machinery—slow, patient, gradual.

Light is normal—1.5—not theatrical. It enters through narrow vertical slits near the top, diffused by damp air and hanging vegetation, falling in pale columns through the hollow. In these beams, dust—perhaps synthetic, perhaps organic—floats in slow suspension, drifting downward over years, accumulating on high ledges, kept aloft by air currents driven by the engines below.

The erosion is established—2.2—not damage but character. The metal is polished smooth by centuries of air and moisture. The vegetation and machinery have reached equilibrium, a settled-form, weathered into balance.

And then the strange—2.0—settles in not as spectacle but as wrongness made familiar. The water pools on vertical surfaces without falling. The plants grow downward from the ceiling as often as upward, roots anchored in damp metal above. In certain zones halfway up, the sound becomes louder, focused, as if the cylinder itself is an instrument being played. Perhaps the fauna leave no footprints, or footprints appear before they pass. Perhaps the erosion has worn away not just surfaces but edges of space, making corners softer, distances ambiguous—the small footprint feeling both confined and endless.

This is the inhabitant: not a creature alone, but the whole—machine and moss, sound and silence, the empty air that dominates the volume, the vines growing downward into manufactured dark. Nothing here was born. Everything lives. Nothing moves quickly, yet nothing stops. And in the vast spaces between the walls, in the absence that fills this hollow world, one can stand and feel the scale of emptiness, listen to the eternal slow rotation, and understand that this place is its own only reason—vertical, warm, damp, deafening, lush, strange, and fully, irrevocably made.
