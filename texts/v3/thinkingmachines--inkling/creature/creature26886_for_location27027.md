What lives here first reveals itself as motion with appetite: the silver-scaled ones, no larger than open palms, that do not swim so much as navigate pressure. They have scales—not decorative, but functional, overlapping like the plating on old diving bells—polished by centuries of abrasion into mirrors that catch the downward light and scatter it in brief, coin-bright flashes. They feed, yes, on the translucent fronds, tearing rubbery leaves with mouths that are both lip and blade, but watch longer and the feeding is maintenance. They prune. They clear dead tissue from the vine-joints where rust and root have married. They carry spores, or perhaps seeds made of compressed mineral, in pouches along their flanks, depositing them in the pipe-joints where the kelp-bulbs wait, anchored, for permission to grow. They are gardeners shaped like fish, fish shaped like tools.

But they are not alone, and not sovereign.

Drifting through the open water—where emptiness is deliberate, a designed absence—come the jelly-things. At first they seem passive, translucent bell-shaped bodies no larger than fists, trailing filaments that brush the vines like tentative fingers. Yet each houses a core: not a heart, not exactly, but a small, dense sphere of alloy and something wetter, pulsing with that weak blue light that is neither electric nor bioluminescent but both, a rhythm that matches, precisely, the thrum of the pumps below. They do not feed. They listen. They are suspended sensors, older than the scaled ones, perhaps older than the vines, drifting along the current-lines generated by the turbine, collecting temperature, salinity, the chemical song of decay and growth, and transmitting it downward through those trailing threads—direct lines into the machinery. When one dies, if they die, its core sinks slowly until it lodges in the grating far below, where the gears will eventually grind it into new current.

And then there are the weavers.

Longer than they should be for this small volume—filamentous, thread-thin, extending meters if stretched, though they rarely extend fully, preferring coiled intimacy—the swimmers move not in schools but in concert. They have no eyes, or none visible, only segments along their bodies that open and close like tiny valves, drawing water through themselves in a continuous, quiet filtration. They weave between the hanging vines, under the bobbing kelp, through the loops of engineered plant, and in doing so they maintain tension. The vines do not hang randomly; they hang in patterns the weavers have stitched through centuries of passage, guiding growth, preventing tangles that would choke the current. They are the architecture’s memory, living thread holding the garden in its scattered, sparse geometry. Sometimes one will detach from the collective flow and wrap itself, slowly, around a silver-scaled creature that has strayed too near the turbine—restraining, guiding back, never harming, always directing.

Down in the lower dark, where the pumps thrum and the sound is loudest, lives something that is not small.

Not large, either—this is an intimate chamber, after all, a chapel, not a cathedral—but present in a different density. It resides in the turbine’s shadow, or perhaps within it, merged with the slow-spinning corner blade that has turned for millennia. A body of fused tissue and copper wire, of gill and gear, that does not move through the water but moves with it, cycling the warmth, maintaining the pressure, breathing through the same valves that drive the machinery. It is not the ruler of this place; there is no ruler. But it is the center of decision, the one that determines when the current strengthens, when the blue cores are released upward, when the vines are permitted a new loop of growth. Its form is never fully visible—the turbidity, the darkness, the constant rotation obscure it—but fragments emerge: a limb that is also a pipe, an eye that is also a pressure gauge, pulsing not blue but amber, the color of old coolant.

Together—scalers and sensors and weavers and the turbine-merged presence—they form not a society but an ecosystem that has forgotten it was ever broken. The water is their medium and their history. They do not reproduce in any visible way; new silver-scaled ones emerge from the kelp-roots, already formed, already knowing the pruning. New jelly-cores are born from the grinding of old ones. The weavers split, not violently but gently, dividing like water itself.

And they are loud, not in voice—there is no voice here—but in presence. Every movement disturbs the pressure, every feeding sends vibrations through the vines, every pulse of the blue cores resonates with the pump-song, adding harmonics to the industrial hum. This is the sound of life continuing in the final form: not silent, not hidden, but integrated, scattered, abundant in its thin distribution, always processing, always maintaining the warm, still-moving world that was built to outlast nature itself.

If you stay long enough, floating or standing on that distant grate, you begin to understand that you are not the observer. You are the interruption. The chamber does not need you; it needs the current, the light from above, the slow rotation of its heart-machine. The inhabitants notice you—not with curiosity, which requires a face, but with adjustment. The weavers shift their paths to flow around you. The silver ones retreat slightly, then return, assessing whether you, too, can be pruned, guided, incorporated into the sparse garden. The jelly-things drift closer, blue light flickering, listening to your heartbeat, comparing it to the pump-rhythm.

And in the lower dark, the amber presence slows its rotation by a fraction, just long enough to consider: is this new thing part of the maintenance, or part of the decay?

Here, suspended and high and utterly made, to live is to maintain. The inhabitants do not know they are strange. They know only the water, the warm pressure, the vines, the gears, the endless, loud, gentle cycle of being finished—perfected—forever.
