What lives here first is not alive in any familiar sense, but it occupies the place with the persistence of life. Thin, fungal-like films coat every exposed plane—black on gray on rust-brown—chemical patterns that do not grow toward light or reproduce with urgency, but simply maintain themselves by eating warmth. They are the color of the rock made deliberate: slick, slightly iridescent in the amber steam, spreading across plinths and boardwalks in identical patches that never expand, never retreat. They are residents without bodies, without hunger beyond the constant thermal bleed of the basalt beneath them.

Then the water-creatures. In every pool that never fills or empties swim the small hard-shelled things—dozens in each shallow basin, hundreds in every street-length of seep—moving with a mechanical precision that looks like swimming but behaves like maintenance. They harvest nothing, grow nothing; they simply exist in perfect density, their shells clicking against one another in rhythms that match the hissing of water through fractures. Larger ones crawl: slow amphibians with wide, flat feet that grip the mud-ridges, clinging to walls at chest-height, blinking in the steam with eyes that never close fully. They do not hunt the smaller ones. They do not compete. The population is already correct.

The air belongs to the swarm—clouds of simpler organisms suspended in the saturated mist, neither rising nor falling, producing that collective drone. They do not sting, do not bite. Their bodies are small enough to be almost mist themselves, but they click and scrape in unison, a respiratory percussion that never breaks. You feel them as a pressure against the skin, a constant, warm vibration.

And then the people—if that word holds—move through it all in practical rhythms. They are not tall; height has no meaning here, and the architecture is low, horizontal, pressed shoulder-to-shoulder. Their bodies are adapted to the warmth, to the constant moisture, perhaps covered in the same mineral slime that coats everything, or protected by woven garments of reed-substitute that never rot because nothing rots and nothing dries. They carry woven nets to harvest the shell-creatures, not out of need that exceeds supply, but because the act of harvesting is part of the rhythm. They split new blocks from the bedrock with wooden levers and simple metal wedges—not to build higher, but to repair, to replace, to maintain the same walls they maintained yesterday. Their hands are always in motion: mixing mud and crushed shell, setting stones, striking rock at the same pace, hour after hour.

They do not shout. There is no machinery to shout over, only the thunder of weeping rock and the roar of fauna and swarm. They communicate, perhaps, in gestures practical and repeated, or in sounds that blend into the drone—no language of change because there is no event to describe. They have no names for seasons, no concept of growth. A child born here learns the rhythm of repair before learning anything else; an elder dies and is replaced not by inheritance but by the identical rate at which another reaches maturity, maintaining the population in perfect, unchanging abundance.

What lives here, then, is not separate categories—plant, animal, person, stone—but a single, saturated system of presence. The chemical films eat warmth; the shell-creatures fill the pools at constant number; the amphibians cling and blink; the swarm breathes in unison; the people maintain; the rock weeps; the water pools; the mist holds the amber light in suspension. Each is inhabitant and habitat to the others. To live in this flat, newborn world is not to occupy it but to participate in its refusal to change. The settlement does not shelter life from the environment; it is another form of the same equilibrium—dense, hand-built, horizontal, warm, and deafening with the sound of things being exactly as they are, forever.
