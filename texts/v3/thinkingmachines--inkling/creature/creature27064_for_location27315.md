Here, “living” has been redefined by absence and negotiation. No creatures arrive on four legs or wing; the near-absolute void of fauna—barely a whisper of insect, no feather, no fur—means that presence is measured not in bodies but in continuous, arranged motion. What lives is the settlement itself, distributed across its layers, and the two dominant orders that share it without conflict.

First, there are the machines. They are not servants; they are the elder inhabitants, old enough to have entered settlement rather than decay. You feel them as you feel weather—unavoidable, embodied. The pumps are the heart-system, drawing near-surface water up through vertical shafts in a bass thrum that never pauses, releasing it in warm cascades that keep the black peat weeping and the moss fed. A generator spins somewhere with a high, tooth-vibrating whine, its electricity feeding the amber bulbs behind grime-caked glass and the dull red glow of furnace doors. Steam hisses through valves with respiratory urgency—inhale through rusted cast iron, exhale through galvanized joints, then through dull-green synthetic lines that have never known sunlight. These engines do not sleep; they maintain the cycle that is the place’s only purpose, perhaps purifying water, perhaps pressing something synthetic, perhaps only preserving the equilibrium they have reached with the swamp. They are loud—filling the sparse, low-density spaces with organized roar—because there is no leaf-rustle, no animal call, no soft footstep to absorb their sound. They echo off wet walls and open sluices, and in that echo they declare themselves alive.

Then there is the vegetation, scattered with spacious profusion across the flat bowl—lush, vigorous, but never crowding into wilderness. Massive ferns unfurl in the corners of collapsed yards, their fronds thick with the constant condensing mist, growing not toward some wild sun but toward the protected amber and red glows of the machinery. Moss claims vertical brick and steel in broad, irregular patches, black-green and glistening like wet bark, holding the humidity in its own second skin. Long vines hang from overhead pipes—green cables dripping warm, iron-sweet water onto the paths below. These plants are not invaders; they are collaborators. Their roots have not broken the pipes but woven into them, using warm metal as trellis. They grow with mechanical purpose, guided by the irrigation the pumps provide, by the constant heat exhaled by the place, by nutrients that seem to seep from the machinery itself—a faint sweetness in the water that tastes of oxidized iron and something manufactured. They leave vast emptiness between them—density is low—so each presence stands isolated, monumental, like statues arranged in a garden that has forgotten its gardener.

Between these two orders, the boundary has dissolved into a treaty written in water and root. The concrete weeps; the vines drink. The channels carry rivulets that seek paths between buildings, carrying green seeds and rust flakes in the same flow. The soil—compressed black peat layered with coal-dust—receives everything, never flooding but becoming more honest, releasing what it holds. The architecture, low and wide, respects the flatness: brick foundations swaddled in steel, walkways bridging open sluices, pipes running in parallel generations, all rounded by water and time into a state of arrival. Nothing needs urgent repair; nothing is new. The system breathes together.

Because there are no predators, no grazers, no interruption of biological pace except the plants’ own steady, heat-driven expansion, the active dynamics here are purely mechanical and botanical. You watch ferns unfurl in real time, encouraged by the submerged, humid heat. You see roots thread through galvanized seams. You hear the loud, living machinery maintain a world that needs no hunters, no prey—only circulation. The twilight never resolves; the 1.0 dimness presses down like a ceiling of steam, and in that gray-green darkness, the inhabitants do not hide. They shine: amber behind grime, red behind furnace doors, green in the moss, black in the glistening peat.

What lives in this bounded, rust-bermed bowl, then, is not an individual but an integrated being: the Pump-Heart, the Vine-Tenders, the Fern-Collectors, and the weeping black ground that receives them all. They share a single pulse—the loud, warm, wet rhythm of water being drawn up and released, of metal being used as root and root being used as vein. There is no death here in the familiar sense, only settlement; no decay, only arrival. The place is inhabited by the process of itself, a small industrial garden in perpetual humid twilight, where nothing moves that is not built or rooted, and where the absence of animal life makes the collaboration between iron and leaf all the more visible, all the more strange, all the more complete.
