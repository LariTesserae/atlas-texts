They are not seen first. In a place this loud—where every drip scatters and every claw-scratch rebounds—presence announces itself through adjustment: the sudden stillness of a lamp being trimmed, the shift of air as a body moves through a corridor only shoulder-wide, the low, close sound of breath and work.

There are **not many**—perhaps a single extended cluster, two dozen at most—compressed into this bounded complex like seeds in a pod. The scale is intimate: heads pass beneath beams that hang at head-height, and bodies learn early to move sideways, to turn in the lattice, to navigate without the luxury of extension. They are **small in posture**, not necessarily in stature—though generations in this compressed world may have made them compact, broad-shouldered for carrying, with spines accustomed to bending, necks strong from looking up at low brick. Their skin carries the place: darkened not by sun (the light wells carry only gray-green filter) but by smoke-fat and clay-dust, always slightly damp, smelling of rendered oil and earth. Hair is thick, often matted with moisture, protected by woven caps or simply left to hold the warmth.

These are **builders and maintainers**, descendants or inheritors of the hands that first lined every inch with fired brick and mud plaster. Their technology is exactly what the walls declare: stone chisels, fiber ropes, clay shaped in the seepage-troughs, timber splints, bronze pins rare and treasured. They work constantly, not in frantic bursts but in **continuous, active cycles**—patching mortar where earth-pressure cracks a seam, guiding condensation along hand-carved channels into collection jars, replacing wicks in the clay-cup lamps that burn with thick yellow light. They weave the reed mats that hang like dense curtains between functions. They shape pottery in the shallow basins, using the ever-present damp clay, firing it in small hearths whose smoke layers the air. Their labor is manual, patient, inherited: the erosion is old and balanced, so their repairs are small, constant—holding back not collapse but time itself.

They live with the **fauna** not as masters but as co-residents. The thick-furred rodents nest in storage niches, and the inhabitants do not drive them out; perhaps they share stores—seeds, dried root, rendered fat—accepting the loss as part of the density. The bats hang in clusters from the highest beams, and no one disturbs them; their droppings accumulate in specific corners, perhaps collected for the fires or left as part of the layered smell. Insects click and swarm in warm pockets, and the flat, quick reptiles slide through pipes that also carry air or water; the people move around them, accustomed. Nests are built into architecture intended for storage, and paths are worn smooth not only by human feet but by constant, scurrying traffic. The boundary is thin: they are one abundant life-form among others, just the ones who hold lamps.

In this **loud** world—where sound has nowhere to dissipate—their communication is adapted. Voices do not shout; they speak low, face-to-face, lips near ears, in the intimate echo of close brick. Much is communicated by touch—guiding shoulders through dark passages, tapping rhythms on pipes to signal presence or need. The collective noise of dripping, crackling fire, and animal movement creates a constant bath, so silence is not absence but a specific, shared attention.

They understand **water** as architecture. They tend the seepage: emptying troughs, directing excess, using the dampness to keep clay workable, to cool the air, to provide drinking collection in shallow jars. Their palms are always wet. They do not fight the moisture; they live within its migration.

And they are **warm**, held in the geothermal breath and the accumulated heat of bodies and small fires. They sleep on ledges, wrapped in woven fiber, perhaps curled close for shared temperature in the lower chambers. Their sleep is interrupted not by danger but by the constant, ordinary activity of maintenance and life—someone trimming a wick, a rodent passing, a drop falling into a jar.

What they look like, finally, if you could see one clearly in the yellow flicker: a low, compact figure, slightly hunched not from weakness but from the geometry of home, hands thickened and cracked from mortar and fiber, eyes adjusted to read shadow and lamplight with equal ease, moving through the dense lattice with the unconscious grace of someone who has never known open sky—who understands space not as emptiness but as the precise, warm, loud, damp interval between one touched wall and the next.

They do not leave. There is no above that calls them, only the narrow throat of brick through which they came, and the balanced, weathered world they maintain in its long, held state—active, abundant, entirely themselves.
