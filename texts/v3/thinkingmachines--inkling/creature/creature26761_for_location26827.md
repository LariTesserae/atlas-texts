Here live the ones who have forgotten height.

Not a people, not in the human sense—there are no people—but the ones who have made their bodies into answers for this finished world. They are flat things, or nearly so: broad, low, spread wide across the dark polished plates as though they had once been larger but chose, over epochs, to become more surface and less volume. Their skin is the color of the wet stone—charcoal, slate, the black-green of compressed silt—slick with a constant, slow secretion that keeps them from drying in the humid air. Some have thin, webbed membranes extending from their sides, not wings, not fins exactly, but delicate expanses of translucent tissue that catch the flat light and seem to breathe on their own, filtering moisture from the air with patient efficiency.

They do not build. There is nothing to build with, and nothing to build against. Instead, they inhabit the spaces between the pools, nesting—if that is the word—in shallow depressions of warmed mud, arranging their bodies in loose, scattered constellations across the open distance. Much space separates them. They are not social in any urgent sense; proximity is tolerated, not sought. Each one occupies its own precise radius of wetness, and between these radii stretches the long, quiet openness of the basin.

Movement, when it comes, is not swift. They slide through the sheet-thin flows with the same urgency as the water itself—barely. A limb extends, a broad foot presses into the black silt and pushes, and the body follows in a long, unhurried glide that stirs the mud only enough to release a string of slow bubbles. They travel in arcs that match the land’s imperceptible slope, always downward, always toward the shallowest gathering of seepage, guided not by hunger alone but by an older knowledge of gradient.

Their voices, if voice is the right word, are part of the landscape’s constant sound. They do not call across distance with sharpness; instead they emit low, resonant vibrations—sub-vocal, felt in the sternum more than heard—pulsing through the wet ground and the thin water. These hums overlap with the amphibians’ choral waves, with the insect drone, with the drip of rock, until it is impossible to tell which sound belongs to which kingdom. They are not loud; they are continuous. Presence made audible.

Their feeding is as slow as everything else. They do not hunt so much as accumulate: mouth-like openings, barely visible, open along the underside or flank, filtering the water and the rich black silt for larvae, for the microscopic life that moves constantly in the mud. They grow slowly—growth is not a season but a decade. A young one is indistinguishable from an old one except by the slight increase in span, the broader reach of its membrane, the deeper polish of its skin.

And they are not alone. Among them move other lives, the ones with shells, with wings, with sharp bills—birds that wade through the pools near them without fear, insects that land on their dark, wet backs and are not shaken away but tolerated, incorporated into the surface ecology like moss on stone. There is no predator-prey drama visible here, only a layered abundance, biological-only, untouched, running on the same clock of patience.

At dawn, when the mist holds longest in the lowest swales, they become nearly invisible—just broader darknesses in the dark water, shapes without edge, part of the landscape’s own refusal to become vertical. As the mist thins across the long hours, they emerge slowly into definition: scattered, separated, flat and patient, confirming the form of the place that made them.

They do not know they are at the bottom of everything. There is no concept of bottom or top for them, only the correct level, the settled plane, the warm wet air, the slow flow, the abundant hidden life of the silt, and the long, open space between them where nothing needs to change.
