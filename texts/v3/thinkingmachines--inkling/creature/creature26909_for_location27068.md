There are no people. There have never been people—not here, where the air forgets warmth and the rock still remembers being lifted. If you came seeking citizens with faces or voices, you will find only absence at first: the hollow of a root-arch, a moss-cup holding nothing but water, the faint scratch of something too small to name. But absence is wrong. The place is full—2.7 dense, every gap claimed, every emptiness already colonized.

What lives here lives in plural.

Start with the roots. They are not a single tree but a population—massive woody knees, buttress-form bodies that rise into walls without ever deciding whether they are foundation or inhabitant. They are the Root Quarter’s true citizens: thick, slow-thinking, pressing through hairline fractures in young granite with the patience of geology answered by biology. Each root is an individual—pulsing water upward, sensing pressure, thickening where the cold demands more mass—but they are also the street, the wall, the gate. Two have grown around each other to form a perfect arch, not by plan but by persistent, mutual need. They do not speak, but they negotiate space through pressure, through the chemistry of shared wetness.

Above them, the moss. Not a carpet but a layer of citizens—millions of small, cold-adapted bodies packed so tight they function as plaster, as insulation, as skin. They live in the continuous weeping cycle: condensation slides from moss to root to rock, never lifting, never drying. They are always damp, always feeding on the moisture that seeps through sharp fractures. In their spongy, compressed mat, there is no dead soil—only living tissue packed so dense that decomposition has been denied entry. The moss holds the place together the way a crowd holds a square.

Then the vines and stems—the builders of corridors, the weavers of branch-road and leaf-plaza. They grow horizontally with an efficiency that looks like intention, bending to form floors and balconies, arching into tunnel-streets that direct movement through the city’s own body. These are the travelers of the place, extending millimeter by millimeter, reaching not for sun—there is none, only 1.1 dim silver-green gloom—but for connection, for the next surface to claim. They are young in the same way the stone is young: still sharp, still deciding their final shape, always adjusting.

In the vertical spires—compressed towers of intertwined shrub and climbing stem—there is height without ambition. These are the landmarks, the districts’ markers. They reach toward the cloud-ceiling not to escape but to define. Within them, fungal brackets grow in patterns that resemble shelving, deliberate-looking, holding pools of collected water in cups grown rather than dug. The fungi are perhaps the most social of all—branching silently through wood, forming platforms, feeding on the constant damp, living in the shadow of canopies that never open fully.

But they are guests, not citizens. They do not build. They do not hold the walls together.

What holds the walls together is the collective—the organism that has decided to take the form of a city.
