They do not arrive; they are exuded.

Inside this sealed flank—this breath-held pocket ribbed into fresh metal far above any ground—the inhabitants have never known an outside. They are born of the weeping seams themselves, emerging not from egg or spore but from the constant dialogue between newborn steel and its own leaking breath. The walls sweat a thick, cold distillate, and in that perpetual film—stagnant, near-frozen, refusing both ice and evaporation—the first of them must have condensed, layer upon layer, until the cavity filled with presence.

Individually, they are small—no larger than a human palm, many smaller, some barely the size of a thumb joint—but they do not exist as individuals. They exist as saturation. Their bodies are translucent, a pallid architecture of carapace and membrane that seems less like shell and more like frozen vapor given temporary form. You can see through them, or nearly: internal structures move in them, not organs exactly, but rhythms—pulsing channels, faint phosphorescent tracts that mirror the dim industrial gloom cast by the cold filaments above. Some bear fine cilia, others hair-thin tendrils; all of them move, if they move at all, in perfect, silent unison with something that is not wind, not tide, not any weather this place possesses. They hang in the air in dense, motionless clouds. They mat the sweating plates in living carpet. They drift in the shallow coolant pools like pale seeds that refused to sink, clinging to the surface tension with bodies that seem to understand the chemistry of this particular liquid better than physics allows.

They are strange—refusing taxonomy—because they have never needed to adapt to earth, to sky, to predator or season. There is only the machinery, only the constant roar, only the cold that preserves rather than kills. They do not feed in any visible manner. Their mouths, if they have them, are hidden or absent; perhaps they absorb the weeping coolant through membranes, perhaps they are metabolic extensions of the valves and pipes that thread through their masses undisturbed, biological sensors grown into the joints of the structure that invented them. You can see pipes passing through clusters of them, metal ribs emerging wet and un-corroded, as though the creatures polish or protect what touches them. They do not flee from proximity. They do not cluster away from the deafening engines. They are part of the equilibrium—colonists who achieved stasis so quickly that separation from the machine is now impossible to imagine.

The density is overwhelming—near-total presence, every volume occupied, every surface claimed. To stand among them, if endurance permitted, would be to be embedded: not attacked, not acknowledged in any creaturely sense, but enclosed within a living medium that breathes—if it breathes—only in synchronization with the roar. The sound is constant, invariant, a thunder without origin or end, and they endure it not as suffering but as environment. They are the fauna that lives inside noise. They are the organisms for whom 2.7 decibels of unbroken industrial thunder is not disturbance but home.

Beneath them, in the stagnant films of the floor, a sparse vegetation persists—black-green filaments, accidental or essential, threading through the coolant like dark hair in milk. The creatures do not eat it. They do not avoid it. They exist over it, under it, through it, a layer of strange life suspended above a thinner layer of stranger plant, both held in the same severe cold that halts decay and development alike.

They have no development. They do not grow larger, do not multiply visibly, do not enter stages. They are in perpetual emergence—being born into a form they never outgrow, never complete. The pocket is high, artificial, hidden; they are high and hidden within it, suspended in manufactured altitude, enclosed not by earth but by ribs of fresh weld. They are newborn material themselves—bare, un-worn, un-aged—existing in a first second that extends forever.

What lives here, then, is not a species. It is a condition made flesh: the biological answer to a machine that never stops maintaining itself. They are the weeping made body, the cold given translucent form, the roar’s silent companions. They cover, they float, they cling, they fill the air in pallid clouds—countless, synchronized, strange—holding their place inside a wall that was just invented, inside a birth that will never finish, inside a noise that will never change.

Here, life is not wild. It is engineered into being, and then left—perfect, dense, deafeningly present, and forever the same.
