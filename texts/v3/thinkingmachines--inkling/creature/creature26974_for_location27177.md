What lives there begins with the stone, which is not dead but the oldest resident still arriving. The rock is the primary inhabitant—accumulating, pressing upward, weeping from its own unfinished pores. It lives in a state of slow respiration, knitting minerals together grain by grain, producing the low groan and tick of settlement that sounds like a creature turning in sleep. To live here, for the geology, is to be under construction indefinitely: not a body that has finished growing, but one that is deciding, millimeter by millimeter, whether it will become mountain or valley, wall or hollow.

Water lives with it, not as a separate kingdom but as the rock’s shared breath. It does not pool and claim territory; it exudes, condenses, finds the seams where stone has not yet committed to being solid. It lives in the vertical faces, in the beads that gather and fall, in the channels laid out like streets that are not yet streets but water’s predetermined paths. Its presence is auditory—striking hollow chambers, resonating through the skeletal frames, sustaining long clear tones that overlap and become the place’s constant, patient music.

Then comes the green, which lives by creeping rather than claiming. Moss is the dominant citizen—thick cushions carpeting the weeping walls, soft invaders that find the cool, unfinished geology agreeable. Ferns live in the shadows of half-formed ridges, uncoiling slowly, rooting in seams that have not decided whether they are soil or stone. The vegetation is lush without being dense; it keeps everything low, close to the ground, green and slow, adapting to an atmosphere that has not yet learned to be dry.

The builders live there too, though you might miss them for the emptiness. They are present not as a population but as a logic made flesh—primitive hands that once used wood and soft metal to shape the fresh geology as it formed. Their lives are embedded in the layered shells of hand-packed stone, in the ribbed retaining walls, in the channels and crossings that organize space without filling it. There is almost nothing per volume; the density is near absence. They have built a settlement-scale skeleton—cathedral cavities, platforms, rooms that are primarily hollow—and then seemed to step back, or perhaps never fully arrived. They live in the architecture itself, in the repetition and alignment, maintaining or perhaps merely initiating a manual craft that thinks it might become a town. You sense them in the grooves, in the intention, but not in bodies crowding the spaces. They are the human presence reduced to its essential act: organizing without occupying, shaping without finishing.

Rarely, a trace of fauna confirms that warmth still moves through the cool wet air—a small creature adapted precisely to emptiness, to the absence of cover and the presence of echo. Its life is a rumor, a single footprint or sound too brief to locate, living where there is little to hide behind and little need to hide. Mostly, the soundscape belongs to the inanimate lives: the water, the wind threading through primitive frames, the rock’s slow mineral settling.

And finally, the emptiness itself lives. It is not absence but the primary material—the resident that fills every hollow frame, every resonant chamber, every gap between the layered shells. It amplifies, it breathes, it allows the place to remain in pre-formation without collapsing into itself. The emptiness holds the space open so the rock can continue growing, so the water can continue finding, so the moss can continue creeping.

To live in this high, bounded bowl is therefore to share a metabolism of becoming. The boundaries have dissolved: the walls are geology guided by hands; the streets are water’s future; the rooms are lungs for a body not yet born. The inhabitants—stone, water, moss, the invisible logic of builders, the rare trace of small beasts, and the loud, resonant emptiness—do not dwell in a finished world. They cohabit a process, slow and cool and wet, constructing themselves in patient, echoing increments.
