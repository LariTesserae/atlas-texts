What lives here is not separate from the space; it is the space, made hungry and vocal.

Begin with the walls themselves—the ochre architecture that breathes without lungs. These are not plants in any garden sense, but dry, fibrous colonizers that have replaced geology with tissue. They grow in ropes of bronze wood that twist like muscle under tension, in brittle crystal formations that shatter like burnt coral, in spongy mats that drink heat instead of rain. They hang downward in thick, swinging curtains of root and stem, filling every cubic breath with near-touching saturation. Some extend tendrils that recoil with animal quickness, sensing the pressure of bodies moving through them. Others unfold dry, tooth-lined pods—slow mouths that wait for the smaller fauna to brush too close, then close with a papery crunch. They do not merely grow; they perform growth, extending shoots in real-time toward the centers of noise, metabolizing so rapidly that they wither and are replaced within hours, feeding their own collapse back into the root-substrate. They are the floor you spring upon, the ceiling that sags, the corridor walls you brush through—living substrate that is also predator, also witness, also graveyard for those crushed beneath falling stalks.

Within this woven body moves the fauna—not scattered, but saturated, a storm of presence made biological.

There are the drummers: insectoid shapes with too many legs—perhaps eight, perhaps twelve, the counting impossible as they blur into motion—who clamber through the hollow stems and strike the fibrous walls in relentless rhythm. Their bodies are armored in harvested plant-fiber, woven not by craft but by instinct, dry husks layered over chitin that glistens like polished bone in the mineral warmth. They do not drink. They feed on vibration, on the acoustic energy of the swarm itself, and on each other when the density becomes too great. Their clicking layers upon clicking until it becomes thunder.

Then the burrowers—larger, unclassifiable forms that move through the spongy floor like living plows. They have no faces that align with recognition: part root, part mouth, bodies coiled in thick bronze tissue that seems to grow directly into the substrate they consume. They push through decaying fiber and fresh mineral bloom alike, leaving trails of crushed husk and scattered seed-vaults behind them. They emit low, vibrating calls that resonate through the dry air, drawing others toward them in chaotic surges—not for cooperation, but for collision, for feeding, for the turbulent exchange that defines life here.

Above and between them navigate the winged shapes—creatures of dry membrane and brittle frame who thread the open spaces beneath hanging fronds. Their wings rasp continuously, not with the softness of feather but with the sound of burned paper being torn slowly, endlessly. They do not rest on branches; they hang from them by hooked limbs, feeding on mineral dust and the heat that rises from rapid plant metabolism. When they swarm, they create their own climate—gusts of arid air pushed downward, pockets of turbulence that cause the vegetation to twist and extend toward them, as if the plants themselves are reaching for the noise.

But the boundary dissolves. Some creatures nest so deeply inside hollow seed-vaults that they become part of the plant’s reproductive architecture, their bodies merged with the dry tissue, emerging only when the vault splits open to feed on whatever has gathered outside. Others wear armor grown from fiber they have not made—living in symbiosis so complete that removing one kills the other. Many appear to feed not on substance but on sound itself, clustering in the loudest zones, vibrating in response to the drumming, growing larger or splitting in two as the acoustic pressure peaks.

There are also the hybrid ones—part root, part mouth—that cling motionless to the vegetation for hours, then suddenly recoil or snap forward with plant-speed quickness. They emit the lowest vibrations, the bass notes beneath the thunder of wings and legs, and they seem to direct the chaos—not with intelligence, but with presence, drawing the swarms into patterns that immediately collapse and reform.

Together, they form not a community but an engine—loud, unstable, Solaris-like in its refusal to settle. Waves of migration surge through the dense growth, leaving crushed fiber behind them like snow. Creatures are born in the midst of feeding, die in the midst of mating, are crushed by falling stalks, are consumed by tooth-lined pods, are replaced before their husks cool. The plants twist toward them, die for them, become them, and the creatures, in turn, become substrate when they fall—feeding the mineral warmth, feeding the noise, feeding the next rapid cycle of dry, strange life.

This is who lives here: not inhabitants of a place, but the place made animate—dry, fibrous, drumming, swarming, always becoming louder, always collapsing upward into the next form of itself, in the warm, waterless dark.
