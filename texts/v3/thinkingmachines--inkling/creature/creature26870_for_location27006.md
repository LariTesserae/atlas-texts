What lives there is not a collection of things but a single, continuous occupation—living tissue that has replaced the idea of ground.

Begin with the surface, though “surface” is misleading, since there is no underside to this world. The moss and sedge and dwarf willow do not grow *on* the land; they have become its outer skin. They form one mat, root-laced and spongy, thousands of miles without interruption, breathing through every pore. To call them plants is technically true but insufficient—they behave like a shared organ, exchanging moisture and chemistry through root-networks that have long since decided to cooperate rather than compete. They are cold-green, always damp, never sleeping, pressing against each other in such density that individual leaves lose their meaning. They are the first inhabitants, and they are everywhere.

Beneath and within them, woven through the limestone and basalt like silver thread through dark cloth, live the engines. These are not built; they are cultivated by time into precision. Massive fungal colonies—pale, patient, spreading in geometric patterns across the rock—form matrices that draw water through stone, filter dissolved minerals, and release purified streams in rhythmic pulses. Bacterial films coat every weeping surface with chemical purpose, operating without heat or sound, extracting energy from cool gradients. The root-networks themselves have become pumps and valves. Together they form a biological machinery that covers continent-spanning grids, silent and relentless, processing the plateau’s constant weeping with the logic of factories but the body of organisms. They are inhabitants too—ancient, symbiotic, embedded—making industry out of growth, turning erosion into function.

The animals move through this machinery as if they are part of its design. Flat beetles, slow as drifting sediment, graze on the fungal membranes, their bodies cool and dark, scraping at the silver threads. Translucent insects—wingless, almost invisible—drift through air thick with spores, navigating not by sight but by the chemical signatures of the mat. In the root-layer, where sky is only a rumor, amphibians with skin like wet leather live entirely within the interwoven roots, never surfacing, breathing through the constant moisture that surrounds them. They are small, numerous, cold-blooded, and utterly quiet. There are no calls. There is only movement—predator and prey, filter and flow—proceeding in such silence that it feels like the machinery itself is thinking slowly.

Even the water lives here, not as visitor but as resident—seeping, gathering, moving through channels carved to the exact angles it prefers. And the stone participates: porous limestone and dark basalt weep continuously, feeding the system, laying down mineral deposits in thin geometric layers that glow faintly with captured cool-light.

Nothing here is separate. The vegetation is the floor and the wall. The fungal colonies are the factories and the plumbing. The beetles and amphibians are the gears and the breath. The plateau itself is not a place that contains life; it is a life that has achieved the scale and cool logic of a continent—packed, saturated, dynamic, functioning with relentless purpose, and holding in its silent, wet machinery a strangeness that comes not from alienness, but from perfect, unbroken integration. No human made this. Nothing made it. It simply became itself, over millennia, and now it lives—completely, quietly, entirely.
