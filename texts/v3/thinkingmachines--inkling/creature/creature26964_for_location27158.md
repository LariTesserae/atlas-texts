Here, nothing walks that was not grown.

The absence of animals is not emptiness but a complete reassignment of presence. Every role—builder, traveler, voice, witness—has been assumed by the matter itself. What lives in this warm, freshly-scarred basin is a single, distributed organism expressing itself through municipal form: a valley-wide consciousness of root and stalk that has dissolved the boundary between citizen and architecture.

Consider the stalk-dwellers. They are not buildings with occupants; they are the occupants made hollow and vertical. Segmented spires—some thick as columns, others needle-thin—rise in organized clusters, their interiors not rooms but continuous vascular channels where sap moves like blood through an open body. They lean, correct, lean again within hours, responding to pressures invisible to animal senses. Their walls are living tissue, translucent in places, and through these membranes one does not see “outside” but elsewhere within the same being—another district, another version of the valley in parallel growth. They do not shelter life; they are life sheltering itself.

Between them stretch the vine-weavers: not roads but muscular, contracting avenues of woven fiber that tighten and relax in slow, breathing rhythms. They carry nutrients and information—if there is a difference—connecting tower to tower, root-floor to sky-canopy, forming a nervous net across the region. Where they cross, they knot into plazas of membranous bridge, yielding under weight with slow muscular contractions, as if the ground itself remembers how to step.

Beneath everything, the substrate-breath: a dense woven mat of compressed root-fiber and fresh humus, young and unsettled, tearing and reforming in sharp fractures. This is the foundation and also the mind of the place—a root-net that bends geometry, that allows a path to lead forward and deposit the traveler in a distant district because the tissue grew between locations faster than walking allows. It is not soil; it is the basal tissue of the entity, alive and yielding.

Then there are the resonators: the drum-like fungus caps arranged in open spaces, vibrating with internal hydraulic pressure, sending pulses through the root-net. They are not instruments played by hands; they are the voice-boxes of the collective, producing the low droning chords and percussive pops of opening flowers, the crackle of cell division, the rush of sap through hollow highways. The sound is deafening not because it is loud, but because it is total—every surface, every corridor, every spire participates in the roaring turbulence of growth. The plant-city amplifies its own metabolism into storm-state.

And there are the ephemeral ones: explosive white flowers that bloom as districts, then wither into brittle scaffolding within minutes, only to be instantly digested and overgrown by crimson vines that feed on the dead tissue. Growth and decay are not cycles separated by time but simultaneous motions, visible in the same breath. A street of woven ferns might constrict into wall, then relax into arcade—no decision made, just the continuous turbulent flow of a single body reorganizing its own shape.

In the cool pockets, cup-shaped leaves line the pathways like gutters, catching heavy droplets, feeding root-bowls that serve as cisterns. Water is not gathered by need; it is held in the body’s own cupped palms, precise and intermittent, sustaining the metabolic density that keeps the temperature warm and steady.

What moves here? Not individuals. There are no separate beings to count. The spires do not house residents; the vines do not connect citizens. The entire valley—this low, young, barely-elevated depression between sharper ridges—is one inhabitant, layered vertically so that sky is only another canopy and ground is only another root-floor. It builds with urban precision because it is arranging its own limbs. It sounds with storm intensity because it is speaking with every cell. It shifts with chaotic instability because it is alive in hyperactive flow, never settled, never finished, constantly tearing through old sheaths and bursting into new forms.

In this place filled absolutely, without gap, there is only this: pure plant presence, dense and layered and near-touching at every point, having taken every role except the animal, and having forgotten why any other form was ever necessary.
