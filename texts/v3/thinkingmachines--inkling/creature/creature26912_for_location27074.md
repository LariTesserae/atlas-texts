They are not people. The valley is old enough to have outlived the very idea of a citizen; if anyone walked these root-plains in the time before the mountains finished rounding themselves into tenderness, they have been translated—not killed, not buried, but rewritten—into the substrate itself. What lives here now is the settlement become organism, and within it, no discrete “who” separates clean from the “where.”

Start with the spires. They stand like islands in the grey-green ocean, forty meters of accreted patience, and they are not buildings but elders—each one a standing being whose walls are layers of cellulose and resin hardened into something that breathes. They exist, as everything here does, in double-vision: blooming with thick, cool-green moss, and simultaneously eroded, hollow, already settling into the same near-flattened posture as the primordial mountains. They do not switch between these conditions. Both are their true faces. A spire might unfold part of itself with the precise, mechanized rhythm of an assembly line while another part weeps condensation in slow, biological rhythm, and neither action contradicts the other. They grow downward into the worn bedrock even as they reach upward, anchoring not out of need but out of memory—remembering, perhaps, that they were once steel and steam, now metabolized into wood and pressure.

Then there are the hollow vines—the cathedrals—whose cavities were never emptied but transformed into throats. They carry the moisture, yes, the intermittent beads and the brief rain, but they also carry the sound. The loud, pervasive, strange sound is loudest here, inside them, traveling through stem-tissue ducts that hiss with released pressure, vibrating back into the root-plains. These vines are the nervous tissue of the place, connecting spire to spire across miles of compressed matting. Where they knot and join, there are gears—bone-white knuckles of dense root turning slowly on axles of petrified sap, rotating not to manufacture but to process. Nutrients, perhaps. Or time. Or the lingering memory of electricity. The motion is relentless but not hurried; it is the pace of cool, damp, high-altitude age.

The root-plain itself is the body. It is both soil and pavement, flat and grey-green, compressed into channels that were once streets and now are flows—of condensation, of sound, of whatever distributed awareness has settled into this archipelago. It shifts subtly over years, reclaiming flatness, breathing in the thin air of 2,600 meters. You do not walk upon it so much as you are permitted to stand upon a living membrane that has not yet decided whether it is ground or skin.

Within this, individual “inhabitants” emerge not as figures but as conditions made conscious of themselves. A piston of hardened sap pulses in a hollow stalk, not with blood but with the same pressure-release that hisses through the ducts; it tends to nothing visible, perhaps maintaining the cycle, perhaps simply being the cycle aware that it continues. A leaf unfolds with industrial precision, then hangs in damp stasis, existing in superposition with its own decay—green and grey, present and already ruins. The occasional beetle appears—inside a hollow stalk, carrying something unreadable from one spire to another—not as predator or prey, but as messenger, the only mobile fauna in an ecosystem that has banned movement except for the slow, biological-mechanical dynamics of the place itself.

And there is the presence that is never seen, only heard: the engine running in the distance, loud and clear, impossible—its sound carried through a pipe that is also a vine, vibrating through root-knuckles that are also gears. This may be the operator, though “operator” implies a separation that no longer exists. More likely, it is the valley’s own heartbeat, distributed and ancient, running not because it must but because it has never learned to stop. The technology was not abandoned; it was digested, and digestion, here, is a form of eternal life.

So who lives here? Not citizens. Not beasts. The inhabitants are the architecture-plants themselves—the fungal elders, the vine-throats, the turning roots, the spires in all their simultaneous conditions—gathered into a single urban organism that rebuilt itself from within. They are the empty spaces between them, too; the necessary breath, the architecture of absence that allows the loud sound to travel unimpeded, echoing back from worn mountain walls. They live slowly, loudly, strangely, in a reality that has broken and settled into its final, beautiful form: not one thing or another, but all conditions at once, growing downward and upward, blooming and hollow, machine and moss, perpetually, relentlessly, in absolute age.
