They do not live here so much as function here—each form a specialized limb of the hollow’s single, continuous metabolism, distributed across bodies too small to dominate the void, too numerous to ever be absent.

In the black pools, the crustaceans move first. They are translucent as the water they claim, bodies built of jointed glass and pale cartilage, no larger than a fingernail but assembled with clockwork exactness. You do not see them individually; you see the pools in motion, a granular shimmer, as thousands filter and click in synchronized gears. Their sound is not chirping but calibration—tiny mandibles and jointed legs striking against quartz edges and each other in rhythms that match the water’s pulse through the fractures. They do not rest on the bottom; they maintain the bottom, scraping bacterial films, breaking fungal mats into particulate, turning organic fall into suspension with industrial patience. Their transparency is functional: they are the hollow’s filtration system given mobility, living membranes through which the black water is continuously renewed.

Above them, in the gray-green haze, the insects claim the emptiness. They gather in columns—vertical, rotating, silent except for a high, glassy vibration—wings so clear they disappear against the mist except for the moment they catch the wrong light and flash like breaking ice. They swarm not randomly but in assembly lines of flight, feeding on the condensation that beads on every rock face, excreting back into the pools with chemical precision. Their wings are not for escape; there is nowhere to escape to in this sealed pocket. They are ventilation and transport, carrying spores from fungal islands to bare stone, carrying dissolved oxygen downward in the turbulence of their bodies. To stand among them is to feel the air worked upon, turned over like a sheet on a factory line, never still, never allowing the mist to settle into true fog.

Then the amphibians—the only ones that generate their own heat, and therefore the only ones required to never stop. They are dark, slick, with eyes like wet obsidian that reflect the green-gray light without absorbing it. They do not sleep; they cycle through the islands of moss and fern in perpetual rotation, feeding, being torn, regenerating, feeding again. Their skin weeps a thin mucus that glistens like oil, leaving trails across the sharp basalt that slow the cutting edges only by millimeters per decade. They are the hollow’s combustion—small, cold-blooded engines forced into constant motion to maintain the temperature differential that drives the air’s circulation. Where they cluster, the moss grows darker, fed by their waste; where they are absent, the fungal mats spread thinner, waiting. They do not hunt with stealth but with mechanical urgency, swallowing the crustaceans that drift too high, torn in turn by the insects that land on their backs to feed.

The vegetation, too, lives as machinery. The moss does not spread softly but clamps in emerald clots, each clot a self-contained processing unit breaking down the ice that falls from the weeping walls, absorbing minerals from the quartz scars. The ferns stand rigid, black-veined, not photosynthesizing in any leisurely sense but pulling energy from the filtered light with the efficiency of solar panels arranged in darkness, their roots penetrating the near-surface water membrane like pipes tapping a reservoir. Between these islands, the fungal mats spread with the geometry of spilled coolant—oil-black, iridescent, digesting dead amphibian tissue, dead insect wing, dead crustacean shell before it can accumulate. There is no decay here in the usual sense, no slow rot; there is only rapid disassembly and reincorporation, the organic matter never allowed to become soil because soil has not yet been invented in this young wound.

Even the ice belongs. It forms on the rock faces in crystalline patterns so regular they seem stamped, growing in thin, sharp plates that accumulate weight with mathematical patience until they crack away with a sound like a closing latch. They fall not randomly but along the channels where water weeps most steadily, plunging into the pools to dissolve with a hiss—coolant introduced into the system, regulating the temperature of the crustacean machinery, preventing the water from warming enough to still them.

What lives here, then, is not a collection of species but a single, distributed body with no center and no brain. The crustaceans are its kidneys. The insects are its lungs and its bloodstream. The amphibians are its heart, beating not in one place but everywhere at once. The moss and fungi are its digestive tract, spread across islands like factory floors separated by corridors of active void. The water is its circulation; the ice, its temperature regulation; the sharp rock, its skeleton, still being worn but not yet worn.

You stand in the center and feel it: not that you are being watched, but that you are being processed. The clicking of the crustaceans does not stop when you enter; it adjusts, integrating your presence—your heat, your exhaled breath, the disturbance of your steps—into its continuous accounting. The insects do not flee but flow around you in columns, measuring your outline, incorporating your shadow into their flight patterns. The amphibians do not hide but continue, sliding past your boots with eyes that do not recognize you as foreign, only as a temporary obstruction in the line.

There are no rulers. No predators sit above this chain. The predator and prey are the same machinery seen from different angles. To live in this hollow is to be part of its relentless, young industry—to move, click, filter, burn, dissolve, and reform in the wet dark, while the water pumps and the ice breaks and the mist never clears, and the small, sharp room continues its production without end, untouched by hands but entirely possessed by its own emerging, mechanized life.
