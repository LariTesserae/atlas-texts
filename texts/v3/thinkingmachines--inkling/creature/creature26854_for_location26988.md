Here lives the architecture of absence—a valley-scale settlement that has outlived its reason for being, or perhaps never had one that human logic could name.

The residents are not biological. At best, there are thermophilic films—microscopic chemistries in iridescent gray—coating the weeping walls where condensation allows. At worst, there is nothing that competes, spreads, or dies in plant-time. The only animate presence is mechanical: maintenance automatons, rusted and fused to their purpose, wandering the open kilometers on corroded treads, repairing pipes that need no repair, guided by sensors older than language. They do not see you. They serve a cycle, not a master.

The buildings stand as weathered infrastructure—rounded like river stones by mineral-laden steam and thermal expansion, yet intact. Dormitory blocks with hundreds of dark windows, arranged for thermal flow rather than comfort. Factories that still hum. Heat-exchanger towers rising in isolation across the cracked basalt flatness. Pipes run in parallel highways above ground, thick as tree trunks, corroded steel carrying heat from the geothermal heart 2.9 degrees relentless below.

The settlement operates on its own logic: density absolute zero, scale extending past haze, urban in form but hollow in occupancy. Water does not flow gracefully; it weeps—condensing on vertical faces in mineral sorrow, running into pools of silica and sulfur that never cool or freeze. The ground is spongy with near-surface aquifers. Black volcanic glass and oxidized copper salt form the crust. Steam makes the high, thin air behave like swamp breath; the sun, if present, gives only a sick copper smear through perpetual haze. Light comes mostly from failing insulation—faint orange glow-lines, blue-white overheated metal, occasional sparks where worn pipes touch.

Activity continues without urgency: valves hiss, pistons clank distantly, engines turn slowly in diluted time. The place is not silent—quiet as a cathedral, or graveyard—but empty enough that sound dies before echo. Light is dim, amber and useless, casting long indistinct shadows.

And the strangeness is in the proportions. Staircases ending at walls of solid black glass. Doors opening onto boiling silence. Pipes spiraling inward toward central vents that emit no steam—only a subsonic tone felt in the ribs, near which time behaves oddly. Towers lean not from damage but like mineral stalagmites growing toward heat.

This is pre-plant, post-plant, never-green: rust, obsidian, dull gold, pale blue-white steam. A pact between geology and industry, continuing without witnesses—processing minerals, storing charge in buried banks, moving air through silent lungs of ductwork. The water keeps rising. The machines keep turning. The place needs no name, though if forced, it might answer to the Weeping Heights, or the Empty Thermal City, or nothing at all. Names require populations, and this is only settlement-scale absence: a town-sized hollow where heat murders cold, and architecture lives on, breathing its slow, mineral patience into the perpetual haze.
