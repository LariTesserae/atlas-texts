What lives here does not announce itself.

Begin with absence: fauna is 0.5, which means the flatness holds no grazers, no flight, no burrowers—only the negative space where an animal’s shadow should fall. The silence, too, is 0.5; so the inhabitants make no call, no footfall, no rustle. They exist below the threshold of intention. Density is 0.8, sparse—presence scattered like salt across the warm plates—so you do not find them in communities but in isolations, each life-form separated from the next by miles of bare, constructed ground.

The visible inhabitants are the dark mats: vegetation at 1.1, barely present, clinging only where water at 1.3 lingers longest. They are neither fully grass nor fully lichen, but something embryonic, a crust that refuses verticality. They spread flat, mirroring the continent’s refusal of height—no stems, no leaves reaching upward, only a thin, near-black membrane sealing itself to the mineral seams. They absorb the relentless 2.7 heat not as damage but as sustenance, darkening further where the sun is most direct, never burning, only deepening their color.

Their roots do not dig into soil—there is none, only more fitted plate. Instead they adhere, drinking the intermittent dampness that pools in shallow depressions and the faint mineral vapor that rises through scattered vents. They grow not by reaching but by tightening, expanding millimeters across the warm surface, settling into the seams like a second skin. They are dynamic at 2.3—highly active—but the activity is invisible, internal: slow thermal negotiation, cellular adjustments to contraction, the quiet processing of condensation as it arrives and evaporates. When distant flat thunder rolls across the open scale, they do not shiver; they absorb the vibration, holding it in their flat bodies.

Because erosion is 0.3—emerging, undeveloped—these mats have not been carved, worn, or diversified by time. They are uniform, pristine, repeating the same low form everywhere they appear. They have not learned complexity because the ground has not yet taught it through fracture and wear. They are life in its first grammar, flat and repeated.

Yet absence breeds replacement. Where fauna should be—0.5, nearly absent—there moves instead a distributed, microscopic presence within the mats themselves: tiny, plate-colored entities, never seen individually, living inside the dark fibers. They do not travel with intent; they circulate, processing heat and mineral trace in a constant, slow metabolism. They are the animal function dissolved into the plant form—no eyes, no movement toward or away, only perpetual, quiet transformation. Together, the mat and its hidden inhabitants form a single being, flat as the continent, silent as the air.

Water shapes their geography. At 1.3—intermittent, damp—they cluster only in pockets: shallow basins where overnight condensation holds until noon, or where thin, un-eroded channels deliver moisture from one depression to another. Along these channels, the dark mats form faint living veins, not cutting the ground but accompanying the water, holding it against evaporation with their own flat bodies. They steam gently at midday, releasing what they have taken, leaving pale mineral rings at their edges—ghosts of moisture, not soil.

In the far distance, near a vent releasing warm vapor, density shifts slightly—0.8, still sparse, but here a darker cluster gathers, a small continent of life upon the larger plane. They are closer together, not touching, separated by finger-widths of bare plate, yet present enough to form a shadow where otherwise there is none. They are the only shadow-casters on a world without ridges.

So the place lives not through abundance but through persistence. The inhabitants are the mats, the hidden circulators within them, the moisture they hold, and the profound absence that defines them—no fauna, no sound, no height, no erosion-carved niche. They construct themselves layer upon layer like the ground itself: slow, flat, fully constructed, and utterly, quietly alive with the motion of becoming.
