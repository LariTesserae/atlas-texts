What lives here moves in two registers—both almost invisible, both defined by the absence of light.

First, the ones who belong to the dark matter. They are not numerous—perhaps only a handful thread the entire lattice at once—but they are immense, slow as cooling magma, bodies thick with cool-adapted tissue that never warms above the 1.4 chill. They have no eyes, no need for them; they navigate the stacked stone and fired clay by pressure and resonance, feeling the vibrations of dripping water, the settling of slabs, the distant clanging of labor. Their skin is not skin exactly but something closer to wet mineral membrane—pale, translucent, leaving behind not footprints but slick, gray deposits in the hollowed channels, a slime that hardens almost immediately to match the rust and wet-dark brown of the walls. They move like rare blood cells indeed, pulsing through the embryonic body of this place, feeding perhaps on the condensation itself, on the mineral-rich seepage that beads on vertical surfaces. They do not build; they inhabit the gaps, slipping through near-touching layers, their passage announced only by a deep, resonant shift in the structure—not mechanical, not the drag of tools, but biological weight redistributing itself in silence.

Then there are the others. The ones who make the noise.

They are present more in sound than form—rhythmic figures lost in the saturation of echoes. You do not see them because they work without illumination, guided perhaps by the same tactile language as the creatures, though their purpose is opposite: not passage but placement. They wield the primitive hammers, the simple blades, the ropes of basic craft. Their labor is constant, the distant clanging and scrape that defines the air more clearly than any sight. They are cool-damp too, wrapped in woven mineral fiber, hands blackened by unpolished stone and hand-beaten metal, feet planted on substrates that have no natural ground. They carry earth that was already carried once; they bind with fired clay; they redirect the thin films of water through carved channels, not to irrigate—there is nothing to grow—but to manage the moisture, to keep the construction breathing, or bleeding, in controlled ways. They are builders of the emerging geology, and they live inside it, nested in the unfinished lattice, sleeping perhaps in the hollow tubes, working in shifts that never end because the place is never finished.

There may be more of them than the blind creatures, or fewer—numbers are impossible in the dark. What matters is their coexistence within the total artificiality. The builders place the world; the large slow ones move through what has been placed. Neither requires vegetation, none disrupts the stone with roots. Even the occasional rain that falls through gaps in the upper lattice does not summon plant life; it only feeds the damp, the channels, the work.

Together they form a single, strange ecology: mineral, manual, and mute. The builders shape the darkness with hand-held tools, their bodies small against the immense scale, yet relentless, layer upon layer. The blind ones drift through the results, cool and enormous and patient, leaving only traces. Both navigate by vibration. Both are adapted to the chill, the wet surfaces, the absence of horizon. And neither leaves—because there is no outside to leave for, no horizon to reach, only the continuous, loud, cool assembly of a world that is its own only habitat, learning its shape in darkness, one placed slab and one slow, sightless passage at a time.
