Not people—there are no footprints in the mossless cracks, no ash, no woven thing, no voice to claim the silence. Not animals, either, though a dark bird passes through now and then, belonging to the open sky rather than to this density. What lives here has not colonized the place; it has grown out of it, the same way the rain belongs to this height and this stone.

They form in the seams.

Begin with the water: not the running streams, but the standing black in the gutters between blocks, the condensation that never fully dries, the weep of vertical faces. In those still pools, in the hairline fractures where construction meets construction, something accumulates—not life in the green sense, but presence in the mineral one. They are made of compressed mist and settled sediment, of the same grey substance as the walls, only held together by patience rather than by mortar. Their bodies are low, heavy, distributed more like weather patterns than like creatures. One might occupy an entire tier—a broad, cold awareness spread across a platform of worn steps—while another is no larger than the space between two colonnades, a small, patient weight in a channel where water runs dark.

They move with the rain’s logic, not against it.

To see them requires not looking directly, for they share the color of the stone and the temperature of the air. You sense them first as a change in the silence: a deeper holding of breath, a moment where the drip from an overhang falls not with its usual light tick but with a denser, more deliberate sound, as if passing through something that briefly remembers being liquid. The air grows colder by a degree—cool, wet, settled—without a wind to explain it. And then, if the mist is thick enough and your eye stops searching for outline, you perceive a posture: a shape that is almost vertical, almost seated, formed of layered fog given just enough weight to lean against a wall. It does not turn to face you. It has no face, not in the way of eyes and mouth, but rather a frontness, a direction in which its presence organizes—like a doorway that has learned to wait.

They are many, or they are one distributed through the lattice.

At this scale, the distinction loses meaning. The plateau is continental; the architecture is landscape; so too are they. They thread the high back of the world as the water threads it—through gutters, across staircases worn concave, along the edges where mist banks roll inward like slow tides. They do not build, for the building is already complete in its gravity and geometry. They do not destroy, for erosion here is long-established, a shared language between stone and weather. Instead, they maintain the repose. They are the reason the wear is so even, the leaning so balanced, the silence so absolute. Not caretakers—there is no urgency to care for—but companions to the process. They drink the condensation that forms them and exhale it back as the fog that travels between tiers, generating the cloud-rhythms that belong only to this height.

In the wider cracks, where pale lichen clings and thin grass pushes through, they leave those green exceptions untouched, as if recognizing a different kind of exception to the rule of grey. Near the black gutters, however, where water stands longest and reflects the diffuse glow, they are closer to home. You might feel one pass—not as a touch, but as a shift in pressure, a brief organizing of the droplets in the air around your shoulders, a sense that the wetness has become intentional for a moment, directing itself toward a seam you had not noticed.

They have no voice, no machinery hum, no song. Their presence is audible only in the way they alter the water’s language: a trickle that holds a lower note, a mist-bank that collapses against stone with a softer, more sustained breath, as if the fog itself has weight and chooses how to fall. They live in the cool air that never fully warms, in the post-rain atmosphere that requires no actual rain, in the density of presence that fills every shared wall and every layered tier.

Because they are not separate from the architecture, they do not abandon it. The city is not empty; it is inhabited from within—by these slow, mineral, water-born presences that have become part of the weather’s own continent. They exist long enough to forget urgency. They settle. They wait in the seams, distributed across the impossible scale, silent, immensely present, letting the place continue being exactly what it is: a high, cool, wet back of the world, breathing through its stone.
