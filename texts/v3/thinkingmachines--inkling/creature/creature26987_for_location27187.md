They are not one thing, but they are not many things either—not in the way a forest is many trees. They are a continuity that has agreed to be discrete.

Look closer, and the multitudes resolve into bodies that refuse the sharp distinction between animal and architecture. Each is soft, vaguely cylindrical or lobed, perhaps the size of a forearm or smaller, though size here is a suggestion rather than a rule; some have grown into the walls themselves and can no longer be said to have boundaries. Their surfaces are matte, never glossy—colors of old bone, of dried apricot, of the pale interior of a seashell left too long in warmth. They do not have faces. They do not need them. Instead, from the anterior—or what seems like one—extend feelers: not antennae in any insect sense, but longer, more deliberate appendages, perhaps four or six per body, sheathed in a translucent, leathery cuticle that pulses with slow chemistry. These feelers do not twitch. They extend, hold, withdraw over minutes, over hours, tasting the air for gradients of heat, for the faint mineral exhalation of the vents below, for the presence of others.

Their bodies are built to secrete. From pores that open like slow mouths—never hurried, never urgent—they release the materials of the city. A honey-colored shell, calcium-rich but not brittle, layered like the growth rings of some impossible tree. A fibrous membrane, leathery and warm, that binds towers together like ligaments. They do not construct with intent, not in any human register. They excrete as trees exude resin, as bodies exude heat, and the result, accumulated across centuries, across thousands of these slow lives working in parallel, has become the hollow’s vertical settlement. They are the city and they live in it, nesting in the spiraled towers they have made, curling into the low domes, draping themselves across the hanging gardens of swollen tissue like moss that remembers it was once flesh.

They move, if movement is the word, by growing into new positions. A body that needs to shift from one level of the settlement to another does not crawl. It extends, thickens, secretes a temporary bridge of shell and membrane behind it, and allows its mass to redistribute over days, over weeks. Some never move at all. They become pillars, their soft bodies calcifying at the core, their feelers still extending, still testing, still maintaining the slow exchange of chemicals that keeps the hollow warm and balanced. They think—if thought is the word—in decades. A decision to expand a tower, to thicken a wall, to redirect a flow of warm vapor, is not made and executed but settled upon, like weather deciding to become a season.

There are distinctions among them, subtle, readable only in the texture of their labor. Some are builders primarily—bodies rounder, more prolific in secretion, nesting in clusters high in the spiraling towers where the heat accumulates and the shell grows fastest. Others are weavers, thinner, more elongated, moving—growing—through the fibrous membranes that connect structures, binding, repairing, maintaining the tension that keeps the architecture from collapsing into itself. Still others are the keepers of the mats, the copper-green velvet that covers every plane; they are smaller, more numerous, embedded in the velvet like seeds in pulp, absorbing not moisture—there is none—but heat and trace gas and the faint sweet breath of the deep earth, processing it into the syrupy fluid that fills the bladder-ferns, feeding the translucent canopies that filter the wrong-angled light.

They communicate without sound, without light, without any signal that travels faster than they do. They communicate by proximity, by chemistry, by the slow diffusion of warmth and secretion through shared tissue. When thousands press near—to the point of touching, of overlapping, of becoming indistinguishable from one another and from the walls—there is no chaos because there is no urgency. No one needs to flee. No one needs to feed in any violent sense. They absorb, they process, they build, they wait. The crowding is not conflict but collaboration, a density of presence that replaces the need for individual will. They are silent not because they suppress noise, but because noise serves no purpose in a world where everything happens slowly enough to be felt before it needs to be heard.

And yet they are not ignorant of pattern. The spirals of their towers are too regular, the levels too evenly stacked, the ventilation of warm air through porous shell too precisely arranged. They do not understand mathematics. They do not understand architecture. But they understand—if understanding is not too sharp a word—the feel of balance, the taste of equilibrium, the slow geometry of heat seeking its level. They build like coral builds reefs, like mycelium builds networks: not by plan, but by the accumulated wisdom of bodies responding to conditions with perfect, patient consistency.

In the center—if there is one, though the layout is circular, recursive—you might find the oldest of them, or not old in years but in accumulation. A mass that has grown for centuries, no longer distinguishable as one body or many, draped in layers of old shell and fresh membrane, its feelers extending not outward but inward, into itself, maintaining some internal chemistry that sustains the warmth of the hollow. Around it, thousands of others nest, build, maintain, exist in the near-perfect silence, broken only by the occasional creak of hardening shell, the whisper of syrup, the almost imperceptible shift of mass as another body decides—over the course of a season—to grow somewhere new.

They are not waiting for anything. They have already arrived at the only future they need: to be here, to build, to absorb the geothermal breath, to fill this small enclosed world with the dense, silent, lush persistence of life that has forgotten water and replaced it with warmth, with secretion, with the slow and endless architecture of being together, quietly, forever growing.
