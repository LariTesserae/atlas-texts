What lives here does not breathe air, because air is a fiction in this place. The residents are not visitors but fixtures, anchored in a world that forgot surfaces. They are the stalk-organisms—towering, translucent, vascular presences that have grown so slowly through ages that sharpness has dissolved even in them. Each one rises from a crack in the smoothed basalt of the drowned plateau like a spine left standing after everything else softened. They are not trees, though they borrow the patience of trees; they are hundreds of feet of slow fiber, thick as old trunks, branching at their heights into delicate, lush filaments that do not seek sun—there is none—but instead catch and cradle faint chemical glows within their translucent tissues. Their illumination is not a beacon but a warmth, a dim bioluminescence felt against whatever surface comes near, more pulse than light, turning the absolute dark of the liquid medium into something navigable by sensation rather than sight.

They stand alone. The density is sparse—much space, miles of rushing black water between each presence—so every stalk is an isolated cathedral in an open, regional emptiness. One here, another beyond the thunder of the current, another lost in the rolling silt of a valley floor. They do not crowd; they do not compete for territory in any visible way. They anchor not with roots but with woven nets of fibrous thread bored into the weathered stone, drinking the mineral-saturated thickness directly, feeding on pressure and chemistry in cycles so slow they seem like geological events rather than metabolism. Yet they move. Always. The perpetual storm of current—deep, continuous thunder—pushes them into a slow, hollow sway. As they bend and recover, they resonate. Deep tones, not melodic but structural, join the roar of the rushing liquid so that the loudness of this high, drowned place is never chaos but a choral pressure, a single vibrating body made of water and plant and stone.

Within their translucent walls, they pulse—contracting, expanding—suggesting a breath without lungs, a feeding without mouths visible to the observer. They are lush, thriving, intricate in their branching, yet utterly without urgency. There are no predators here, no prey, no animals at all—an absolute absence of fauna that makes their quiet existence feel not lonely but complete. They do not need to defend. They simply persist, glowing faintly, resonating, drinking.

And there are others who lived—who built. Not present now, but their intention remains scattered across the ridges: circular platforms of stacked dark basalt, worn smooth as river stones; low walls; single standing stones aligned with a care that implies hands, simple tools, pre-metal craft, long-established settlement now emptied of bodies. The structures sit half-buried, aligned in patterns that echo the placement of the stalk-organisms, as if the builders recognized the towers not as scenery but as companions, or as markers in a landscape they too navigated by sound and pressure. No bones remain. No trace of the builders’ bodies. Only the stones, settled and long-existed, inhabited now solely by absence and the rolling silt.

The place itself—this open-scale plateau extending past any single view—seems to breathe as one. The water is not background but body: thick, cold near absence, carrying sound like bone carries vibration. Dark clouds of sediment roll in waves across valley floors. From deeper fissures, occasional plumes of warmer, darker liquid rise, briefly disrupting the eternal roar before it settles back. The stalks sway with these shifts; the stones do not move, but they are part of the motion, rounded and settled into it.

So who lives here? The translucent towers, separated by miles of rushing black, glowing with internal warmth, anchored to ancient rock, singing hollow tones into the thunder. The worn basalt circles and alignments, holding the shape of primitive hands that are long gone. And the coherence itself—the weird, intense activity of a world without animals, where water, plant, stone, and current move together as if the drowned crown of this old world were a single slow organism, cold and loud and entirely awake. You do not observe them from outside. You are inside the liquid, feeling their resonance through whatever you are, moving through the spaces they leave between them, aware that in this high, forgotten place, life has taken a form that needs no sky, only depth, pressure, and time.
