What lives here does not arrive; it accumulates. You do not see them first as bodies, but as corrections in the stillness—small adjustments to the dark.

Near the rust-eaten heart of the engine, where the cylinders drink black water and the metal holds a faint, stubborn warmth against the cool, the translucent ones gather. They are not insects, though they carry segments—delicate, glass-thin legs that unfold like filament from bodies no larger than a thumb’s breadth, bodies that seem more membrane than meat, letting the gray-green light pass through them as through old ice. They crawl in silent layers, not swarming but negotiating, clustering over the mineral films that shimmer like spilled oil across submerged steel. They feed without mouths you can name: they rest upon the fungal-simpler surfaces, and the surfaces diminish where they have been. As they move, they leave behind them faint phosphorescent trails—cool smoke, barely blue, drifting upward through the water like breath held too long—so that the pools become latticed with old paths, glowing for moments before the current dissolves them into darkness. They prefer the company of rust; they cluster densest where pipes bend and encrust, as if the mineral deposits are a garden tended specifically for them.

But they are not alone, and they are not safe.

The others slide through deeper pools—flat, palm-sized, the color of wet slate and old bone. They do not crawl; they glide, leaving ripples that fail to settle, because another is always following, or fleeing. Their bodies are smooth-edged, almost disc-like, and they move with a purpose that suggests they remember the pipes long after the pipes forgot them. They inhabit the hollow interiors—rusted gears, the curved rail fragment lost to sediment, the scored lines in the floor—resting inside these shapes as if nesting within the bones of something larger. You see them only in fragments: a flat shadow passing beneath the surface of a pool, a quick disappearance into mud, a shimmer where they have disturbed the silt. They hunt, perhaps. Or they clear. The ecosystem is too small, too dense, to allow waste: one creature’s stillness becomes another’s movement. The quick vanishings are never loud—no splash, only a ripple that arrives late, a faint scraping of their edges against rusted metal, and then the water closes over the absence.

Even the water is inhabited. It holds shapes a breath longer than physics should allow—impressions of legs, of sliding bodies, of the engine’s cylinder rims—so the pools become a kind of slow memory, recording presence after presence has moved on. The fungal films feed the crawlers; the crawlers feed the gliders; the gliders disappear into mud, and the mud feeds the film. Nothing is fresh here. Nothing breaks. Even death reaches a dark harmony with the rust around it.

And there is something else—deeper, less visible—carried not by creature but by the hollow itself. Through the bent pipes, through the seams where mineral meets stone, comes that distant, low resonance: occasional, hollow, neither voice nor machine but something that has learned to use both. It may be the earth pressing downward. It may be the pipes, still electrified in memory, vibrating with occupation. Or it may be the larger patterns of the flat ones, communicating through the metal they have made their home—patterns that do not align with recognized biology, but with an older, wetter logic.

Together, they form a society of layers: the luminous crawlers on the surface, feeding in silent clusters; the stone-gliders below and between, swift and solitary, inhabiting the dead machinery as territory; the water between them, thick with iron and phosphor, holding every trace; the rust and rock providing warmth and shelter, merged so completely that some gears appear to have grown rather than been forged. Every boundary touches another. The air is wet with their breath, the stone weeps with their presence, the single column of gray-green light reveals only fragments—never the whole.

This is who lives here: not one thing, but an accumulated presence, small and constant, strange without chaos, moving through its own dark rhythm. They have settled into exactly what they are—mineral feeders, pipe-nesters, ripple-makers—inhabiting a chapel-sized hollow where dead industry and living darkness have reached the same patient, cool agreement. Nothing shouts. Nothing leaves. The dripping continues. The phosphor smoke drifts. And in the shallow pools, the shapes of those who just passed remain, for a moment, a little longer than they should.
