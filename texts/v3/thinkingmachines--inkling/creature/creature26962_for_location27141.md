What lives here does not live in the usual way. It occupies.

First, there are the towers themselves—not dead lumber, but ancient, breathing biomass that has decided on permanence. At density 1.3 they stand far apart, each one a solitary continent of fiber, yet beneath the cracked leather-plains of dead mulch they are never alone. A root-web, older than the bones scattered near collapsed huts, connects them in darkness: transmitting pressure, moisture, and something less measurable—an awareness that makes the spires tense in unison when storms arrive, amplifying thunder through their hollow stems into sustained, vibrating chords. They do not speak, but they respond. They lean eastward or hang by root-anchors not from weakness, but from agreement with forces they have felt for centuries. They are inhabitants in full right—slow, resonant, weathered smooth by erosion 2.0 into bone-like surfaces that feel almost intentional, as if growth and geology once signed a treaty and have kept its terms through millennia of heat (2.8) and warm, persistent damp (1.1).

Within their walls lives the overwhelming presence that never quite shows itself: the fauna (2.7). Not discrete animals in a census, but a constant, layered roar—swarm-beings by the innumerable. They are microscopic and macroscopic at once, winged and crawling, filling the fibrous chambers with migration that sounds like distant surf that never breaks. You may travel for hours through the aching gaps between spires and see nothing, yet the sound (2.6) never drops; it is deafening in its persistence, an organ-drone of wind through hollow architecture compounded by the collective motion of bodies too small or too hidden to resolve. They emerge only in crisis—when the gold-green light (1.5) darkens with sudden rain, when the sky draws its curtain across miles of stage, they pour out in visible clouds, darkening brief patches of air, then retreat back into the fiber as if they had never left. They consume and are consumed in a dynamic (1.6) of biological pace: not chase, but churning persistence.

Then there are the large ones—the slow predators whose bodies are walking root-balls, leaving tracks in the compressed organic leather. They travel the leagues between towers with a deliberation that makes urgency impossible in this heat. They call to one another in vibrations felt in the sternum before they reach the ear—bass notes that roll through the warm mist. They do not hunt with speed; they outlast. They are part of the same root-web logic, moving along paths of moisture and pressure, stopping when the spires tense, turning their bulk toward the thunder-chord and waiting. They are fauna, yes, but also something like priests of the place—massive, patient, utterly integrated into the architecture of living wood.

But there are others, too—faint, scattered (built 0.8), primitive (tech 1.4). Not the current rulers of the plateau, but those who once tried to live among the towers without being consumed by them. Their presence is archaeological and ghostly: woven platforms of dead fiber suspended between spires by ropes of twisted vine, reached by ladders that have long since rotted; a collapsed hut of hardened bark fragments; sharp, simple bone tools—pre-metal, practical, desperate—scattered in the mulch where an owner fell or left or was taken. Most haunting are the carvings: shallow spirals, tallies, warnings, prayers cut into the spire-skin with stone, weathered nearly smooth by centuries of damp wind. They built with what the vegetation offered and refused—bark, vine, bone—never mastering the root-web, never becoming part of the resonance. They were visitors who stayed long enough to leave marks, then vanished into the constant roar, leaving only the question of whether they left, or were slowly incorporated.

The weirdness (2.1) is that nothing here is fully separate. The plants communicate; the swarms respond to the plants’ tension; the great beasts listen to both. During the sudden storms—torrential, then gone—the entire plateau becomes an instrument: thunder caught in hollow stems, swarms rising like smoke, predators frozen in attention, the root-web transmitting the event across miles of darkness in a single breath. You, if you move here, move slowly. The heat refuses urgency. The sound refuses solitude. You are always between—between resonant towers, between the microscopic roar and the distant bass call, between the carved prayers and the living walls that have almost forgotten they were ever carved. What lives here lives in layers: visible architecture, audible infinity, and beneath both, an ancient, damp, communicating darkness that has made this high greenhouse in the sky its own, settled, strange, and utterly permanent form.
