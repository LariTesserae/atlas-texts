You do not step onto ground so much as onto them.

The first thing is the texture—there is no bare rock, only a continuous, yielding crust of shell and mineral scale. They cover the floor like a living pavement, each body no larger than a thumb-knuckle, pressed shoulder-to-shoulder in strata so dense the black salt veins disappear beneath them. They are hard: chitin analogs fused with ceramic dust, coated in the same dark salts that vein the walls, so that color and material merge. You see them in the sick green phosphorescence—not shining themselves, but edged by it, thousands of tiny silhouettes layered like broken pottery stacked in perfect disorder.

They do not scatter at your presence. There is no flinching, no ripple of alarm through the crowd, because they are not arranged by fear or need for distance. They are arranged by equilibrium. Some cling; most do not move at all. They hang from the ribbed ceiling spurs in inverted clusters, suspended by hair-fine filaments of their own secretion—metallic, brittle—vibrating so faintly in the machinery’s drone that the movement is only visible as a shimmer in the green light. Others form a second living skin over the half-buried gear in the east wall, filling its teeth, its hollows, its rusted sockets, so that the machinery reads less as abandoned technology and more as an overgrown reef.

If you lower yourself—if you dare the pressure of that warm, metallic air—you can distinguish the kinds, though they blur at the edges into one continuous ecology.

There are the click-shells: round-backed, low to the floor, their bodies encased in segmented plates that open by microscopic increments, perhaps to drink the heat-gradient that exudes from the buried conduits below. They do not crawl with purpose. They shift imperceptibly, a slow migration measured in years, locking into place with an audible—though nearly silent—*tick* that merges with the sharper textures of the drone. Thousands of these ticks per second, layered, become part of the architecture’s sound.

There are the dust-feeders, smaller still, with bodies like wire brushes made of mineral filament. They do not have mouths in any recognizable opening; instead they extend hair-thin proboscises into the suspended metallic dust, drinking the air itself. They cluster where the orange glows from the conduits are dimmest, in the coldest corners of the chamber—though “cold” here is relative, a slight lessening of the oven’s breath.

And there are the vibration-drinkers: elongated, almost larval in form except for their armored heads, which they press flat against the pipes that run into darkness and end in nothing. They align with the hum of current, bodies resonating like tuning forks held to a phantom chord. They require no water, no chemistry of liquid—only the constant mechanical song, the thermal exhalation, the perfect absence of change that allows them to exist in suspended consumption.

They do not breed visibly. They do not die in decay. The population is exact: every cubic meter holds its thousands, distributed with mathematical saturation, leaving almost no bare surface. Where one might be absent—where a shell cracks, where a plate falls—there is no gap for long. Not because others rush to fill it, but because the absence itself is corrected by the pressure of those surrounding, the whole mass settling like sand in a sealed jar. They are a living sediment that has reached its level and will not shift.

Above them, on the surfaces they do not claim, grow the sparse vegetative traces—not competing, coexisting. The crystal lichen sends hair-thin filaments across the ceiling spurs, white and brittle, never branching beyond their initial reach. The metallic moss grows in sharp tufts only where the heat concentrates, near the exhaling pipes, feeling like cold iron wool to the touch. The wire-vines reach an inch, perhaps two, from cracks in the ceramic vein, then stop—frozen mid-gesture, as if they have reached the limit of what this air permits and chosen, wisely, to go no further.

The machinery fragments are not separate from this life; they are its geology, its climate, its sun. The great gear turns—if it turns—in motion so slight it might be illusion, its teeth polished not by wear but by the constant abrasion of shell against metal. The tracks embedded in the floor carry nothing, yet they are colonized: small hard bodies nest in their grooves, aligned like passengers on a train that will never depart. The rusted control frame stands in its corner, dials locked at single values, and upon its face, in the green phosphorescence, you can see the faint crust of growth—lichen, dust, the miniature shells of vibration-drinkers who have made their home on the instruments of a purpose that no longer requires interpretation.

Time here does not erode. The mineral spurs remain fresh, sharp, recently unveiled; the shells do not rot; the moss does not yellow. Everything is in high saturation, near-touching, a sealed vault of perfect crowded stasis. The air, thick with metallic dust and the constant exhalation of the buried machinery, presses the swarm into place with a weight like liquid that has forgotten how to flow.

And you—you are the anomaly. You bring moisture in your breath, change in your steps, a temperature that is not exactly the chamber’s own. The fauna does not notice. They are already exactly where they belong, vibrating in resonance, layered upon one another, filling every gap between industrial remnant and sharp young rock. They do not need you to observe them. They need only the drone, the warmth, the absence of water, and the eternal, bounded intimacy of this hollow—this cathedral of shell and salt and silent, turning metal, loud and teeming and dry, where abundance is not flourishing but perfect, unending, crowded equilibrium.
