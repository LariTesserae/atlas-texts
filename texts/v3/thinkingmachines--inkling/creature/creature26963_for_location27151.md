Not people in any form the eye expects—no vertical posture here, no voice raised against the drone—only those who have agreed to the flatness, who have let the shelf wear them smooth.

Start with the ground itself, because here the ground is not dead bedrock but the first inhabitant. Across the polished slate, in every shallow depression where the water weeps rather than runs, there lives a brittle skin—grey-black, cracked like ancient glaze, not moss, not lichen, nothing green or hungry for light. It feeds downward, not upward: dissolved stone pulled from the constant seepage, minerals drawn through rootless thread into a crust that grows by millimeters across centuries. Underfoot it shatters with a faint metallic exhalation, releasing the smell of cold iron and old rain. This crust is alive in its own mineral patience; it does not move, yet it is not still—expanding in thin films after each pressure-shift in the hollow pipes below, cracking again as the cool air (0.6, always chill, clinging like wet cloth) contracts over it. It is the only vegetation this place permits—0.3, nearly absent—yet it covers more ground than any forest, a living varnish on the primordial flat.

But the true residents, abundant (2.0) though scattered across the sparse density (0.8) of this open expanse, are the disc-herds.

They appear first as glints—dropped coins in shallow water, translucent shells catching the flat silvery-grey diffusion of light (0.8, dim, shadowless). Come closer, and they resolve: palm-sized, circular, floating or crawling with slow, constant motion (1.7, never hurried, never halted) through pools that connect in thin films across the slate. Their shells are dark chitin fused with polished mineral, grown rather than born—each disc begins perhaps as a fragment, splitting by fission, accreting new layers from the grey water it filters. They are strange (1.4)—not quite animal in the familiar warmth of blood, not quite crystal in dead rigidity. Mouth-bracts, fine as silk threads, extend beneath them, sifting the weeping water (2.1, always present, near-surface, never deep) for dissolved stone and the faint organic trace of the crust’s decay. They leave behind them trails in the slime-film, thin signatures of passage, and they move in loose, widening circles, herds without leaders, responding not to sight—there is little to see in this dimness—but to vibration. The deafening drone (2.6, constant, bone-felt, unbroken) is their language, their weather, their compass. The hollow mineral pipes beneath the flatness sing through resonant chambers eroded over eons, and the discs shift with each change in pitch, altering course by inches as water levels rise or fall with the geology’s slow breath.

They do not call out. They do not need to. The stones speak in thunderous hum; the discs listen through their shells.

Watch them long enough, and you begin to see they reproduce not in seasons—there are no seasons here, only perpetual post-rain coolness and grey overcast—but in moments of saturation. A disc grows heavy with consumed mineral, its shell thickening to opacity, and then, in the stillness of a shallow pool, it divides: two half-moons that drift apart, each regrowing its missing curve from the water’s dissolved slate. They are active without urgency, scattered yet everywhere—never crowded, never alone—participating in an ecology that never required sun, never required green, only the endless wet flat and the roar of hollow earth.

Yet there are others, or there were.

The structures—1.3, primitive, simple—say so. Low cairns of flat slate stacked without mortar, some half-submerged in the shallowest pools, their stones chosen for polish, fitted by hand. Small shelters barely taller than a crouching form, roofed with flat slabs, walls carved with scraper-marks visible still, holding nothing together but placed—placed with knowledge of vibration, with knowledge of the drone. Simple metal pins, dull bronze or iron, rest not as fastenings but as offerings or weights, primitive tech (1.3) left for purposes the discs do not share. These were built by hands that knew to stay low, to survive the sound that makes speech useless, that makes words felt in the chest before they reach the mouth. The builders moved in crouching ways, leaving no footprints in the slime, only the carved marks and the stacked stone. Perhaps they are gone—faded into the crust, dissolved into the water’s mineral memory. Or perhaps they remain, unseen among the disc-herds, changed by the same consumption of stone, their shells become their shelters, their low posture now eternal.

What lives here, then?

The crust lives—feeding, cracking, breathing metallic air.

The discs live—filtering, dividing, glinting like dropped coins in the dim light, responding to the song of hollow pipes with slow, herding grace.

The water lives—always weeping, never flowing away, connecting pool to pool in a single shallow body that cannot absorb more.

The sound lives—deafening, constant, the earth singing through its own emptiness.

And in the spaces between—between the cairn and the pool, between the shell and the slime—there lingers the presence of those who stacked stone, who understood this flatness not as emptiness but as agreement. They left their low marks. They built to survive the vibration. They carved tools by hand and placed them without explanation, primitive signatures in a landscape where erosion has done its work to the absolute end (3.0).

You do not visit and remain unchanged. The drone enters your bones. The flatness removes depth from your vision—horizon and ground become one plane. The cool, wet air fills your lungs with the taste of dissolved stone and old water. And everywhere, in the silver-grey diffusion, the translucent herds drift in their constant, silent circles, filtering, growing, dividing, belonging to an ecology that never needed height, never needed voice, never needed green—only the smooth worn shelf, the weeping pools, and the thunder of hollow earth that speaks for everything that lives upon it.
