What lives here does not arrive; it accretes.

There are no faces, no footprints in the moss that stay long enough to read. Fauna is nearly absent, yes—rare as a miscalculation—but absence is not emptiness. The inhabitants are the ones that never needed to enter, because they grew from the wound itself.

Start with the vines. Not invaders, but citizens. They have learned the language of the pipes so thoroughly that they have become fluent in both directions: metal barked over with cambium, living tissue pulsing not with steam alone but with sap, with a slow, green circulation that mirrors the industrial flow beneath. They hang in curtains not as decoration but as partition walls, as nervous tissue, dividing the space into neighborhoods of damp and darker damp. Some bear the faint phosphorescence—not eyes, but a kind of awareness, enough to reveal the curve of a root, the turn of a valve, the shape of a presence passing slowly through. They grow visibly over days, extending tendrils toward the warmest conduits with the patience of beings who measure time in centuries.

Then the machinery—not dead tools, but slow-metabolism organisms. The gears turn once an hour, and in that turning is a kind of breathing. They have been running so long and so quietly that they have become topography, yes, but also personality: patient, unhurried, producing heat and pressure and purified water according to cycles that predate memory. No visible hand tends them; they tend themselves, or each other, in a network of rust-streaked catwalks and bellows that function like a circulatory system without a heart, or with a heart distributed everywhere. The pumps exhale. The furnaces pulse with dull red light like slow hearts. They are not haunted; they are inhabited by their own purpose, grown so dense and continuous that purpose has become being.

The rock, too, is resident—not backdrop, but body. The black basalt and glassy obsidian are young, sharp, still weeping mineral sweat, still learning softness. They sweat and drip and form shallow black pools that steam gently, not as scenery but as mouth and gland. The hollow is the body; the pools are its breath made liquid.

And in the interstices, where pipe and vine have fused into something neither plant nor engine, there is a hybrid life—structures that seem to have been built and then forgotten, left to be colonized, yet they continue to function, producing according to rhythms that belong to no clock. They are the true natives: the pipe-vines, the moss-carpeted gears, the roots that have become valves and the valves that have become roots.

There may be others—unseen, implied in the feeling that the place is held together by rules not quite natural. Not ghosts, exactly. More like a dormancy that is also a watchfulness. The air tastes of copper and sweetness. Gravity feels slightly delayed, thickened by depth, as if the hollow has its own conception of weight and consent. If you stand still long enough, you sense that you are not alone, but you are also not with anyone. You are inside something that is fully occupied by the marriage of sharp rock, hot damp, dim light, and lush, unhurried life—a settlement with no citizens, only co-inhabitants; an urban grove where every surface is both built and grown, and the distinction has long since dissolved into a single, breathing, very slow presence.

The hollow is not dead. It is dormant in its own vast way, and its residents—the vines that think in steam, the gears that grow rust like bark, the sweating stone, the phosphorescent shadows that move without feet—are not waiting for you. They are simply continuing.
