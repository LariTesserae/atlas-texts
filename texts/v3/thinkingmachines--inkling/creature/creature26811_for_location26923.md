What lives here does not live singly. It lives in accumulation, in the removal of boundary between one body and the next, so that to ask who inhabits the flats is already to misunderstand—the answer is always plural, layered, and moving at speeds that do not match human patience.

Begin with the ground, because the ground is never bare. It is carpeted—not metaphorically, but actually, physically overlaid—by the small hard ones. They are crabs with shells the color of wet rust, beetles with carapaces dark as the silt they move through, other armored things without common names that crawl in silent sheets across the black concrete. You do not see them arrive. They are simply present in densities that make the surface seem to breathe, a low, continuous ripple of bodies feeding on whatever the heat and water have rendered edible: the slime films, the decayed mineral salts, each other. They leave glistening trails in the black coating, not slime exactly but a secretion of their own passing, so that the open lots become written upon, mapped in faint silver arcs that vanish only when the next rain-of-condensation washes them back into the dark. They never sleep. The climate does not ask for rest, and they have learned not to offer it. If you stand still long enough, the vibration reaches your feet—not sound, but pressure, the knowledge that thousands of jointed legs are working in concert beneath the illusion of emptiness.

Then the water, which is not empty either, though it pretends to be still. The canals hold black water that moves barely, choked with sediment and the deeper dark of accumulated chemistry, but beneath that skin is constant motion. Eels roll there, long bodies breaking the surface only in slow, silent ripples, then vanishing back into the black. They are not alone. There are things in the canals that do not need air, that navigate the warm, rust-heavy water by feel and chemical trace, leaving no wake, no trace except a slight thickening of the darkness in certain pockets where they cluster to feed on what settles out from the dead industry above. The water is their highway, their shelter, and their digestion—everything in this place eventually returns to the black flow, and they are the ones who process that return.

Above, the air is thick with life that has given up on noise. Tiny wings form hazy clouds—moths with wings dulled by humidity to the color of ash, flies with bodies reduced to essentials, and smaller things, barely visible, that move in swarms so dense they further dim the already muted light. They do not buzz. The wet heat has dampened them, or they have dampened themselves, adapting to a world where vibration is danger and stillness is survival. They feed on the condensation itself, on the spores and particles that hang in the permanent mist, on the bodies of the dead that fall from the clouds into the pools below. To stand in the open lot is to be inside their breath, to feel the slight resistance of countless bodies against your face, your hair, the backs of your hands—never biting, never stinging, only present, claiming the air as thoroughly as the crawlers claim the ground.

But there is also the life that is not animal, not quite, the life of pure chemistry made visible. Every surface—steel, concrete, rust—is coated in thin dark films that thrive in wet heat. They are neither plant nor true fungus, just the chemistry of abandonment given form: black, glistening, slowly spreading across the ribcages of warehouses, running in mineral tears down cooling towers, filling the cracks with a substance that is alive without being alive in any recognizable way. It does not photosynthesize; it feeds on oxidation, on the slow release of minerals from dead metal, on the heat rising from the ground table. It is the skin of the place, and it is growing still, patient as geology, turning the urban skeleton into something organic without ever becoming green.

Together, these are not separate inhabitants. They are one system wearing different shapes. The crawlers break the slime into pieces; the slime feeds the water; the water feeds the eels; the eels, when they die, feed the crawlers again. The winged things move between, carrying spores or eggs or nothing at all, connecting the canals to the warehouses in a network of presence that requires no sound. They have adapted to the hollow grid, using the collapsed factories as shelter, the rail beds as paths, the open lots as feeding grounds where the black silt offers constant, low-grade nourishment.

And then there is the absence that completes them. You—the one who moves through, who feels the low vibration, who notices that you are the emptiest thing present. You are not part of the equilibrium. You disturb it not by violence but by your isolation, your singularity, your need for the climate to change. They do not flee from you; they do not acknowledge you. They continue their silent sheets, their rolls in black water, their dampened flight through hazy air, maintaining the established rhythm of heat and water and labor turned inward upon itself.

What lives here, then, is not a creature but a condition made flesh: the flatness, the wetness, the relentless warmth given bodies to wear it, to move through it, to digest its rust and exhale it back as silence. They are the delta remembering what it was—water organizing itself—long after the geometry of human purpose has been forgotten. They do not need the place to recover. They do not need it to decay further. They have reached terms with it, and in reaching terms, they have become it: a living, silent, overwhelming presence that fills every empty lot not with noise, but with the quiet pressure of being exactly, permanently, home.
