They do not enter; they accrete.

Start with the smallest, because here scale collapses inward and the intimate is the only true size. In the silver films of the pools—those still depressions that mirror the root-lattice above without ripple—there are bodies like droplets made solid. Not fish, not larvae exactly, but cold things with translucent membranes stretched over frames of dark bone or chitin, suspended in the water as if the pool itself had decided to hold a shape and keep it. They do not swim. They occupy. Their wings—if they are wings—are folded so flat against their sides that they look like seams in stone. They rest on the moss not as animals rest, but as moss rests: as an extension of the surface, indistinguishable until you notice the slight pulse of darkness, the way a leaf might be darker if it held its own shadow inside.

Then the ones that crawl, or rather settle, across the woven roofs. Small, low, closer to the ground than to any sky—because sky is a forgotten word here, and height is only a matter of layers. They have bodies of packed earth and fiber, covered in the same deep green that carpets everything, so that moving means only a shift in texture, a brief interruption in the moss. Their tools are still with them, not dropped but grown into their grasp: chipped stone that has been held so long the edges have rounded to match the hands, bindings of root that continue from their fingers into the walls they tend. They do not tend actively. They are in equilibrium with the tending. A wall is repaired not by their action but by their continued presence against it, the slow pressure of their bodies encouraging stone to round into root, root to thicken into wall.

Above, in the lattice of ice and root-fiber, hang the others. Not falling, not flying, not sleeping in any sense that expects an awakening. They are suspended by their own weight, or by threads that grow from their mouths, their feet, their backs—threads that weave into the ceiling so that it is impossible to say where the creature ends and the architecture begins. They are larger, though large here means no wider than a spread hand. Their eyes—if they have eyes—are open, unblinking, reflecting the pale constant light without absorbing it. They do not watch. Watching implies a future event, and future has been smoothed out like the erosive walls. They are present in the way the light is present: constant, distributed, without focus.

But there is more, because density is absolute and nothing is left out.

The air itself, heavy with bedrock and condensation, seems inhabited—not by breath, but by occupation. You feel them in the gaps that do not exist, in the spaces between the ferns where a shoulder might fit if shoulders still moved independently of the wall they lean against. They are not a people in the sense of separation. They are a settlement in the biological sense: organisms settled into one another, into the moss, into the bone platforms, into the pools. Some are seated—if sitting can occur without posture—on the low stone ledges, wrapped in vines that do not constrict because they have no reason to constrict or release. Their skin, where it shows, carries the gray-green of the stone, the waxy dark of the fern, the silver of the condensation. They wear nothing because they have become the clothing of the place. Their hair, if hair remains, hangs like the vines: thick, unmoving, part of the garden that has forgotten to be garden.

And sound—there is none, but if there were, it would come from them, or rather, its absence is their most active presence. They do not breathe loudly. They do not call out to divide space, to claim, to warn. The air absorbs vibration because they have taught it how, through long, silent occupation. Even their hearts—if hearts persist—must beat at the pace of the moss growing, which is to say imperceptibly, in a rhythm that matches the freezing temperature rather than defying it.

What lives here is not dead. That is the wrong category, the wrong expectation brought in from outside the pocket. They are completed. The primitive tools, the chipped stone, the woven reeds—these are not signs of backwardness but of arrival. They reached a technology that was sufficient, then they allowed themselves to be rounded by it, to settle into the same equilibrium as the walls. They built the tiers, the platforms of packed earth and bone, and then they inhabited them so thoroughly that the distinction between builder and built dissolved. A platform is not where they sit; it is where they have become flat and supportive, a living extension of the ground.

In the pools, in the moss, in the hanging lattice, in the narrow gaps between walls where ferns crowd—everywhere there is presence, and nowhere is there absence. They are the reason the water does not ripple: their bodies have taught the surface tension how to hold. They are the reason the moss does not overgrow its boundaries: they are the boundary, their presence defining where green stops and gray-green stone begins, though the line is only visible if you look for separation, and separation does not exist here.

Let them become more specific, more themselves. One, near a pool, has fingers that have elongated into root-hair, touching the water not to drink—nothing here drinks in urgency—but to maintain the silver film, to keep it continuous. Another, in the lattice, has become so integrated with the woven root-fiber that its ribs show through like the ribs of a leaf, translucent, holding the cold light inside its chest like a lantern that never needs fuel. A cluster near a wall of packed earth and bone sit—if that is the word—in a circle that is not a circle because circles imply space between, and there is no space. They are pressed together, shoulder to shoulder, vine to vine, and in their center is a small depression, a pool within the pool, where condensation gathers not despite them but because of them, drawn by their collective coldness.

They are not waiting. Waiting is a dynamic, and dynamic is frozen. They are holding. The pocket has forgotten the sky, and they have forgotten the need for sky, for flight, for sound, for change. What lives here lives in the perfection of suspension: lush, dense, damp, silent, complete. They are the inhabitants, and they are the inhabited place, and there is no longer any useful way to tell the two apart.
