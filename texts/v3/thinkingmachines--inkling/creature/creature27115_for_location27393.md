What lives here is not separate from what it lives in; they are the same occupation continuing in different densities.

First, the weave itself lives—though “plant” is too lonely a word for it. It is a continuous tissue, silvery-grey and fibrous, growing not in individuals but in uninterrupted saturation. There is no seed that falls away, no stalk that stands alone. Every surface is carpeted, every interval filled by stalk and mat-weave of tissue that drinks not water but chemistry and mineral exhalation from the still-cooling rock below. It smells of warm copper, of crushed seed-case without rot, because decay has not been invented here; there is only conversion, one form of grey tissue becoming another. The vegetation is lush in the way that total presence is lush—no gaps, no bare soil, no invitation for emptiness to enter. It forms the ground, the walls, the canopies, the streets. Where it thickens, it becomes architecture without ever having intended to be.

Within and through this weave move the others: silent, numerous, industrious beings of soft body and many limbs, colored in the same palette as their world—bone-white and grey, lacking contrast, lacking noise. They are not builders in the manner of creatures who shape dead matter with tools; they have no tools, no fire, no wheel, no metal. Instead, they shape by pressure, by procession, by the accumulated chemistry of their passing and their waste. Their bodies are gentle, unarmored, moving in constant quiet migration through fiber-avenues they have compressed over generations of instinct. They are the urban force—not through design, but through density. Where they gather and press, the plant-matter hardens into root-cement and shell-secretion; pathways become streets, clusters become nesting cores, rising spires and low domes grow not upward by will but by the accumulated weight and secretion of bodies living in close negotiation.

So what lives here lives in layers that refuse to separate. There is the geological warmth, still exhaling, still contracting—the ground itself alive with cooling birth-heat, feeding the mineral breath that the weave draws upward. There is the weave, saturating every plane of the flat continent, forming corridors of sight and walls of total occupation. And there are the soft-bodied multitudes, grey and white, moving through it like slow blood through tissue, never striking, never calling, never disturbing the silence except for sounds so quiet they feel like pressure: the tick of expanding fiber, the abrasion of limbs through narrow channels, the distant low breath of rock settling.

Together they have made a city without emptiness—hundreds of miles of grid-like clearings, arched bridges of living rope, compressed towers, all grown rather than built, all occupied. To live here is to be part of the density. The creatures do not inhabit the city; they are its continuing motion. The plants do not shelter them; they are the medium through which the creatures move and the material from which the creatures form walls. Even the flat, dark basalt beneath participates, feeding warmth upward into a world where nothing is finished deciding what it will be, except that it will be full, and warm, and utterly, quietly alive.
