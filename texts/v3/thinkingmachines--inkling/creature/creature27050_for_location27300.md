What lives here is not arranged in bodies you could point to, but distributed like weather—present everywhere, named nowhere.

First, the machines live. Not as tools left behind, but as organisms that have outlasted the distinction between builder and built. The pumps are lungs; they do not pause for breath. They draw the warm weeping rock through calcified throats and exhale it into the mist as pressure, turning geology into thunder. The ventilation stacks are open mouths. The gears turn with glacial patience, not laboring toward an end but maintaining a state, like heart muscle that forgot it was ever part of something smaller. They have reached equilibrium with erosion; rust and calcite are not decay here but growth, the machinery’s own skin matching the hills it stands among. They maintain themselves, maintained by the constant flow that runs through them, and in that loop they have become the primary inhabitants—slow, deafening, conscious only in the way a river is conscious, which is to say: entirely, without reflection.

The water lives too, though not as a single creature. It is near everywhere, seeping, condensing, forced. It weeps from the rock face in warm trickles, then enters the pipes, then becomes the roar. In the shallow pools between the nodes, it sits dark and still, holding minerals that settle like sedimentary memory. It does not seek the sea with urgency; the gradient is weak, and so it moves with the patience of the place, circulating through industrial ribs, carrying sound and warmth, never finishing its work.

The buildings live as shells for this process. They are not dead architecture but settled-form bodies—concrete and steel that grew square over centuries, excreted by the plateau rather than placed upon it. They sit miles apart, an archipelago of presence, connected by elevated veins that sink slightly into the wet ground. Inside them, the machinery breathes. Outside, they accumulate black lichen and rust-stains that spread like living maps, recording each year of humidity and pressure.

And then there is the near-invisible presence—the almost-absence that defines the density. The text implies a population, yet none can be seen. Perhaps they are there in the spaces between, too sparse to form crowd, too integrated to stand out against the gray-green flatness. Or perhaps they have become part of the maintenance, their presence dissolved into the glacial patience of the gears, leaving only the occasional service road, the narrow path sunk into spongy ground, as evidence that hands once touched this place.

What biological life persists does so reluctantly. Thin yellow grasses grow in patches, not thriving but persisting, existing despite the saturation and the constant thunder. Black lichens colonize the older concrete, feeding on mineral and moisture, slow as the hills themselves. There are no birds to cross the mist, no insects to swarm the warm pools—the fauna has yielded, or never arrived, leaving the soundscape undisturbed by anything smaller than the pumps.

So the valley is inhabited by a system that has become its own ecology: rock weeping into pipe, pipe roaring into warm air, air hanging heavy over sparse grass and rusted tower, all of it watched by rounded mountains that have long since given up towering. The life here is mechanized, hydrological, geological—continuous, patient, and total. You stand between two distant nodes and feel that you are inside something that breathes without lungs, thinks without mind, and will continue—deafening, humid, square and weeping—long after the question of who built it has lost all meaning.
