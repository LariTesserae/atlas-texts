They do not announce themselves, and that is the first thing you learn: in a place so complete in its stillness, presence must be earned slowly, observed in increments longer than breath.

Start with the ones that are almost visible—the translucent shrimp, yes, but look closer: it is not merely passing through. Its shell has become something like the bulbous plants that thread the bolt-holes—translucent, yes, but veined with the same dim amber-green that sleeps in the glass conduits. It moves along a corroded brass railing not by swimming but by holding, its limbs adjusting by imperceptible fractions, matching the thermal expansion of the metal it clings to. It does not hunt. It does not flee. Once every long season, when the smokestack releases its single slow bubble of trapped air, the shrimp tilts—barely—toward it, as if receiving a message delivered in a language of pressure and dissolved mineral. Then it resumes its position, a living filament in the machinery’s sleep.

The pale, eyeless fish are more numerous, though still scarce—perhaps seven, perhaps twelve, scattered like thoughts through the rounded rooms. They do not swim so much as maintain. Each rests in the crook of a collapsed stair, or beneath the curtain of kelp that hangs from an overhead pipe, and they hold their places not by effort but by agreement with the water’s patient drift. Their bodies are the color of the concrete before it was rounded, of the ceramic tiles softened to velvet—pale, soft-edged, without urgency. They sense the distant groan of expanding copper not through ears or eyes but through the very mineral density of the bath they inhabit, feeling the settlement’s slow heartbeat as a change in warmth across their flanks. They are not watching over the place. They are distributed within it, sensory nodes of a body that has forgotten it was ever separate.

But these are only the ones that still wear the shapes of creatures.

The true inhabitants are harder to locate because they have achieved the same final form as the architecture itself. They live in the joints—where aquatic moss carpets a gear-house roof and the roots have threaded so deeply through rusted bolt-holes that plant and steel are indistinguishable. There, in the shared tissue, something like awareness has settled, not in one mind but spread like the bioluminescence in the tubes: faint, patient, collective. You might call them the settled ones, or the merged. They have no bodies you could point to, only territories of influence: a curtain of kelp that responds, over months, to the opening of a distant valve; a cluster of translucent bulbs that pulse—once every few years—in rhythm with the half-turned copper cog below them, as if the gear’s motion is being remembered, or completed, by the growth above it.

One of them—if it can be said to be one—exists primarily in the narrow vertical street that has become a canyon of layered construction. Its presence is felt in the way the water moves there, not in current but in intention: a slight, almost imperceptible gathering near a particular rounded concrete block, where the temperature is a fraction warmer, where the amber-green light pools more thickly in the glass tubes. It does not think in images. It thinks in erosion and growth, in the slow turn of gears and the extension of tendrils. It knows the settlement as a body knows its own shape—without reflection, complete.

There may be others like it, distributed, isolated in their own chambers within the forty-pace shell. Or perhaps they are all fragments of a single being that has spread itself through every available volume, using the shrimp as mobile extensions, the fish as resting sensors, the plants as its constant, visible form, and the dormant machinery as its bones—locked half-turned, holding positions not of labor but of posture.

What lives here, then, is not abundant. It is occasional, tentative, as you observed—yet not absent. It is the harmony made conscious: industry and vegetation and warm water held in a balance so long established that it has become a kind of being, suspended, patient, complete. They breathe the bubbles from the smokestacks not for air but for news. They drink the minerals not for thirst but for continuity. They move, when they move at all, at the speed of corrosion, of root-growth, of a cog completing its turn across centuries.

And in the silence—beneath the faint, sleeping glow of the conduits, among the rounded edges and the velvet moss—they wait, not for change, but for the continuation of what they have already become. Together. Utterly still. Utterly present.
