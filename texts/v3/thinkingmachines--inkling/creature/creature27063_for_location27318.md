Start with the air itself—because here, air is not empty medium but occupied territory. Between the hollow spires, in the miles of open carpet, moves the overwhelming presence: not a swarm in the singular, but a permanent weather system made of bodies. They are small—most no larger than your palm—winged on four translucent membranes that catch the high light like wet silk or the inside of a shell. Their bodies are dark, compressed, almost seed-like at the core, dense with the grey-green spores they harvest from the spire-crowns. They do not fly so much as drift with intention, riding the same wind that sings through the hollow tubes, and they gather in clouds so thick they cast moving shadows on the mat below. You do not hear them individually; you hear their aggregation, a constant, rustling thunder, the sound of a million soft brushes against fiber and air. They feed, they mate, they fall—when they die, they drop into the spongy substrate and are absorbed without ceremony, becoming part of the decomposing warmth that feeds the floor. They are the constant. They are the noise made visible.

But the noise is layered, and beneath the wing-clouds, hidden inside the living carpet, are the processors—those who never see the open air. The burrowers are pale, eyeless, soft as unripe fruit, segmented bodies no thicker than a finger, with mouths that work in slow, grinding rhythm. They live entirely within the root-hair weave, eating the decayed fiber, exhaling the heat that keeps the plateau warm despite its height. You only know them by the occasional ripple in the mat’s surface, a slow bulge traveling like a swallowed breath, or by the tiny, perfect holes they leave near the water-pools—ventilation, perhaps, or access. They do not climb; they do not need to. The substrate is their world, and it is vast.

Then there are the spire-dwellers—climbers, slender and long-limbed, with feet that secrete a faint adhesive and fingers that hook into the woven cellulose like needles in cloth. They scale both the inner and outer walls of the hollow towers, navigating the smooth, river-worn curves with patient, vertical grace. Their skin is the color of old moss, grey-green and slightly damp, absorbing the condensation that beads on the spire walls each morning. They drink from the woven basins at the feet, yes, but they also tend—if that is the word—clearing the rivulets of debris, perhaps guiding the flow with small, arranged pebbles or with their own bodies. They are more sparse than the wing-clouds, solitary or in pairs, and they move with the slowness of things that have never needed to hurry.

But you asked for whoever lives here, and the answer is not only them—because scattered across the open scale, separated by miles of silent carpet, are the ones who leave marks.

They are rare. You might walk for hours between sightings. They are not large—perhaps waist-high to the spires’ colossal height—but they stand upright, though with a permanent stoop, adapted to the spongy give of the mat and the low, resonant hum that vibrates through everything. Their bodies are strange: not exactly skin, but layered like dried spire-skin, fibrous and papery, grey-brown and weather-resistant, absorbing moisture rather than repelling it. Some have faces—wide, flat, with eyes like dark pools of still water, reflective and calm—while others seem featureless, wrapped in woven strips of fallen tower-fiber that hang like dried vines. They do not wear clothing so much as carry it, anchored with pegs of sharpened bone or simple metal—tools not of construction but of shaping, cutting fiber, binding shelter.

They build nothing permanent, because permanence is not theirs to claim. At the bases of some spires, near the shallow green pools where morning condensation gathers, you find their temporary architecture: small shelters woven from fallen spire-skin, half-collapsed, anchored with bone stakes, already being eaten back into the mat. They come, they stay a season, they leave. The structures remain as husks, slowly softening. Near these sites, they arrange stones in circles—not walls, not boundaries, but patterns, precise and deliberate, sitting at the water’s edge like silent questions. They press paths into the substrate by repeated footfall—thin lines through the moss that grow over within weeks, leaving only the faintest depression, a memory of walking.

They carry tools of primitive making: blades of simple metal, likely found or traded rather than smelted here, edges kept sharp against fiber; sharpened stalks used to cut and weave. They do not compete with the hollow towers. They borrow from them—skin for shelter, fiber for cord, the hollow echo for something like communication. Some have been seen—not clearly, never clearly—standing inside the spire bases at dawn, heads tilted back, mouths open not in speech but in tone, matching the wind-chord, letting the resonance travel through their hollow bones or hollow chests, becoming part of the instrument.

And here is the deeper strangeness, the borrowed logic: they do not seem entirely separate from the place. When they die—if they die here—the mat accepts them quickly, but sometimes, in the rare silence between wind-gusts, you feel that slow pulse traveling through the substrate, and it does not come only from the root-weave. It comes from them, or what remains of them, integrated, resonating with the spire-song, part of the ancient metabolism. They are not masters of this high warm basin; they are its occasional, thoughtful guests, shaped by its patience, leaving only circles of stone, paths growing over, and shelters that become part of the spore and the song.

So this is who lives here: the constant wing-cloud in the air, the hidden burrowers in the floor, the patient climbers on the walls, and—scattered, silent, resonant—the woven ones who come and go, leaving marks of hand and stone in a world that remembers nothing permanently except the hollow, singing, warm breath of total vegetation.
