The thunder is inhabited first—not by a body, but by a refusal to be empty. What lives in the roar is the chamber’s own unfinished will: a deafening, breathing argument between the water and the stone that creates it. You do not hear the sound so much as enter its lungs, suspended inside a hyperactive birth that has no mother and no child, only this pocket of churning coolness near a summit that has not yet decided to be a mountain.

Along the one ledge where the white light strikes longest, the moss lives—not green, but a translucent, thread-thin pallor, barely committed to the idea of chlorophyll. These are not plants in any settled sense; they are filaments of tentative agreement, clinging to fresh crystal that weeps minerals into the mist. They feed on what the walls exhale, on the mineral breath of geology still in shock from its own emergence. There are so few of them—perhaps seven, perhaps fewer—and they do not grow so much as hesitate, reaching into the spray with a fragility that suggests they know they are premature. In the silver columns of falling light, they appear almost like nerves exposed on the inner surface of something that is still learning to feel.

In the water itself, drifting without school or shadow, is the ghost of a single larva—pale, elongated, half-formed, its shell either torn away or never completed. It is not dead; death requires a finished form, and this creature has not arrived there. It moves in the argument of the current not by swimming but by being argued, tossed in patterns that seem less like navigation and more like the chamber testing whether such a thing can be tolerated. Sometimes it vanishes into the churning for long moments, then reappears near the surface, trembling, a solitary exception to the emptiness.

But the most unsettling inhabitant is the one who is already gone.

On the warm lip of newer stone, the cairn remains: small stones stacked by a hand that was manual, tentative, unmechanized—chipped with harder rock or bone, arranged without symmetry, as if the builder was counting not to remember but to understand whether counting was possible here. The tool-marks nearby score the wall in simple, testing lines—not writing, not art, but inquiry made physical. This being, or these beings, should not have reached this height, this hidden embryonic pocket, this place submerged in its own first waters. They were not settlers; they were interruptions. Primitive, early craft—perhaps not human as we would name it, but possessing bones to chip with, possessing the urge to stack, to mark, to leave proof of passage before the turbulence reclaimed everything. They built in a space that was still constructing itself, left traces that are not ruins because nothing here has had time to fall into ruin, and then they vanished—torn away like the larva, or perhaps they never left at all but dissolved into the feedback loop, becoming part of the harmonic breathing of the noise.

What binds them all—the moss, the ghost larva, the absent maker, the weeping walls—is their shared state of being pre-formed. The chamber itself lives most actively of all. Its dynamics are its metabolism: micro-shifts in the fissure above alter the angle of the piercing light; cracks open in the crystalline face like mouths and seal within moments; fragments of raw mineral detach, float, and are swallowed back into the churning. The water shapes the stone, and the stone, still soft with becoming, reshapes the flow in a continuous negotiation. The deafening thunder carries within it this rhythm—almost a pulse—suggesting that the space is not containing these lives but laboring to produce them, or to produce the conditions where life might eventually stop being tentative.

There is no food web here, no ecology. There is only adjacency in solitude. The moss feeds on mist; the larva is torn by motion; the maker left only stones that have been smoothed by water into anonymity. Everything occupies the hollow density alone—the vast emptiness between each presence making every inhabitant feel solitary, unfinished, caught in the cool, sharp temperature of a world that has not yet finished deciding what lives and what is merely the violence of becoming.

If you stretch your arms in this pocket, you can almost touch both walls, yet the space between feels infinite—not because it is large, but because it is so thoroughly occupied by the work of birth. Nothing here is stable enough to be called resident. They are all guests of the thunder, temporary shapes in a chamber that is building itself through noise, leaving only these trembling traces: translucent moss, a pale drifting ghost, a small cairn of tested stones, and the constant, deafening argument of water and newborn rock.
