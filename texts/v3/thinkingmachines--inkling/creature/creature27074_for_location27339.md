What lives here does not arrive or depart; it has settled into the same equilibrium as the mud and stone.

First, the absence itself keeps residence. Not emptiness, but a withdrawn presence—an absence that has thickened, taken on weight, and now occupies the spaces between buildings like a resident who never moves, only presses. It is the first inhabitant.

Then the vegetation, though to call it living overstates its case. The black reeds grow only where water runs shallowest, emerging not from seed but from decision—each stalk a vertical line drawn by some agreement between mineral and moisture. They do not sway. There is no wind. They stand in scattered formation across the flats, thin and jointed, more like dark stitches holding the ground together than plants. They do not feed; they accumulate. Their blackness is not pigment but density, a compression of absence into vertical form.

More widespread, and stranger, is the geometric moss. It spreads across the compressed-silt walls in slow, angular patterns—hexagons, interrupted grids, spirals that do not complete. It feeds on heat and absence. It has no softness; touching it (if one could, if the heat allowed) would feel like crusted mineral, like velvet made of eroded stone. It grows not outward but inward, thickening the walls, blurring the boundary between structure and growth until the buildings seem to be exhaling this moss as lungs exhale breath. It is vegetative only in the sense that it changes state slowly, that it occupies rather than builds.

But the true citizens are the buildings themselves.

They are not ruins inhabited; they are inhabitants shaped like architecture. Each low, layered structure breathes through hairline cracks in its fossilized-reed walls, exhaling steam that does not rise but lingers, warm and mineral, coating every interior. They settle with sounds like wet stone releasing breath—not collapse, but relaxation, a body adjusting in sleep. They weep continuously, not from damage but from function, running water upward along certain faces in patient, thin streams that ignore any agreement of gravity. Their interiors are larger than their exteriors allow; they contain rooms where no exterior wall suggests presence, spaces that open into other spaces, corridors that extend past the flat horizon’s permission. They live in balance with erosion, worn not away but into their final form, as if geology and architecture reached a pact centuries ago and have maintained it ever since. They do not crumble because they have finished becoming what they are.

The water, too, is an inhabitant—not passive, but circulating with purpose. It exerts upward, running against expectation, seeping back into invisible hollows, pooling just beneath the crust in patterns that match the street-grid above. It remembers being part of a city; it moves through the black stone streets not randomly but along their flattened paths, following straight lines that lead nowhere and everywhere, then suddenly turning back upon themselves. It is old, dissolved, mineral—tasting of ancient rain and dissolved stone—and it condenses on every surface, making the air itself a resident, thick and cooking, pressing on the throat.

Then there are the shadows.

Though almost no light exists to cast them, they gather in corners that do not exist, thickening in spaces between buildings where geometry should prevent them. They are not absence of light but presence—dense, textured, a kind of thickened darkness that presses back when approached. They move, or seem to: if you travel through the open spaces between structures, crossing what should be short distances takes longer than the flat horizon should permit, and in those elongated crossings, the shadows accompany you, not following but co-present, as if distance itself is inhabited by them.

And behind all of this—embedded in the straight lines, distributed through the grid, present in the logic that organized settlement without need for light—there is the builder-presence. Not a single entity. Not visible. Not alive in any biological sense. It is distributed, like gravity here is only a loose agreement, like the water that runs upward. It lives in the patterns: the long straight streets that stop suddenly, the hollow squares open to the dark ceiling above, the layered walls compressed from silt and fossilized reed. It constructed once, then withdrew into its own architecture, leaving behind not ruins but settled form—urban organization without technology, without scale, without human need. It is present as intention fossilized into material, as geometry that remains logical even when traced to contradiction.

Even the heat is resident. It rises not from sun but from the earth’s own slow breath, constant, wet, pressing—hot enough to turn every surface into condenser, to cook the air from below. It is not weather; it is metabolism, the slow breath of whatever system this place has become.

So nothing here hunts. Nothing preys. Nothing reproduces in any recognizable sense. The only dynamics are circulation: moisture exhaled, condensed, running upward, seeping back; moss thickening in angular patterns; buildings settling with wet sounds; shadows gathering in impossible corners; the builder-presence maintaining its distributed logic through straight lines and layered walls.

The place itself is the organism.

This flat, open valley—scattered with clusters of layered walls, interrupted by long intervals of weeping mud and black reeds, extending into darkness that hides distance—is not dead. It has reached a state after life, or beside it. A settlement that finished being built, that then let water and heat and mineral absence finish the work. It lives as process lives: continuous, without predator or prey, only circulation and settlement and breath.

What lives here, finally, is the memory of organization made material—the city remembering how to weep, the water remembering how to be streets, the darkness remembering how to hold shape. And in the thick, cooking air, in the shimmer of moisture that appears and vanishes in near-total black, they live together—not as individuals, but as a single, scattered body in its own strange equilibrium.
