What lives here are the keepers of absence.

They are not a single species but a scattered taxonomy of near-sameness, all adhering to the same unwritten law: transparency, slowness, and a refusal of anything that might be called a face. You encounter them not as individuals but as moments of wrong density in the grey-green—sudden thickenings where the milky water holds its breath.

Some are long and ribbed, like sections of the architecture they inhabit come partially alive. They cling to the rounded arches not with limbs but with a continuous adhesive surface, their bodies conforming to the curve of the worn lattice so precisely that the boundary between organism and construction dissolves in the dim light. They have no eyes you can locate, only faint, darker gradients running along their translucent flanks—perhaps sensors, perhaps scars—registering the endless turbulence as pressure changes, as shifts in mineral content, as the silent grammar of flow. They do not fight the current; they lean into it, letting the violent rotation carry them in slow, orbiting paths through the open valley-space.

Others are more mobile, if mobility can apply to something that moves like a thought through cold syrup. These are the gelatinous ones, larger, softer, shaped like incomplete bells or overturned vessels of living glass. They drift downward—not falling, but surrendering to a density just slightly greater than the water around them—leaving behind them not waste but addition. From pores along their ventral surfaces, they exude a grey, calcareous mucus that does not dissolve. In the cold, in the mineral-rich darkness, it hardens within hours, becoming part of the floor, part of a wall, part of the endless, rounded bulwark of the place. They are not building with intention. They are excreting continuity. The architecture grows not by design but by digestion, by the slow metabolism of bodies that feed on suspended dust—ground stone, ancient erosion made edible again.

There are smaller ones too, scattered like seeds in the vastness: clusters of finger-thin filaments that pulse in synchronized rhythms, gathering minerals into temporary lattices of their own before dissolving them back into the current; solitary, massive forms that move so rarely you might mistake them for part of the structure until you notice the faint, internal circulation, the ghost of blood or its equivalent moving through translucent tissue in patterns too slow to be heartbeat.

None of them make sound. Not because they lack the apparatus, but because the environment has erased the need. In a place where turbulence roars in silence, where communication through vibration is drowned in the absolute hush of liquid pressure, they have developed other grammars. They sense each other through chemical trails—faint signatures of calcification and dissolved mineral that linger in the swirling water like handwriting in smoke. They gather, rarely, in loose configurations around particularly rich currents or near fresh deposits of hardened mucus, not in schools or colonies but in temporary alignments, bodies arranged by flow rather than will.

Their abundance is real but diluted across the enormous scale. You might travel for long intervals through the open basin seeing nothing, only the ancient rounded plates and the turbulent grey, and then suddenly there is a cluster—seven, twelve, twenty of the ribbed forms clinging to one arch, their bodies overlapping like scales on some sleeping creature, all facing the same direction not out of choice but because the current demands it. Or you encounter one of the great gelatinous drifters in a moment of suspension, hanging in the water like a lantern made of frost, its internal structures visible as faint, branching shadows—no brain, no heart, but a network of channels distributing the cold liquid of life through tissue that has never known warmth.

They feed continuously, filtering the suspended dust that gives the water its milky opacity. They are the cleaners and the continuers, breaking down the ancient constructions at a rate so slow it matches the erosion of the walls themselves—consuming the dust, excreting the shell, maintaining a balance that has held for longer than any biological clock should allow. They do not reproduce in any visible, dramatic fashion. Instead, fragments break away—small pieces of hardened mucus carrying dormant tissue, or detached filaments that drift until they find a suitable arch, and there they settle, and there they begin.

What lives here lives in completion. There is no struggle for dominance, no predator and prey, because the place has reached its end-form. The fauna are not conquerors of this high basin. They are its residue and its renewal, the soft biological answer to a hard architectural question that was asked before they existed. They move with the silent storm, they add slowly to the worn floor, they glow faintly with their own cold bioluminescence—not bright, never bright, just enough to prove they are still present in the grey.

And if you wait long enough, suspended in the liquid that is not air, you begin to understand that they do not notice you. Not with hostility, not with curiosity. You are another suspended mineral, another shape passing through the turbulence. They continue their slow orbit, their silent secretion, their feeding on the dust of dead buildings. The place is full of them, scattered, patient, wrong-shaped, and entirely at home in a world that has finished becoming itself—an underwater architecture of bone and secreted stone, inhabited not by inhabitants in any familiar sense, but by the living maintenance of a silence that will outlast every form they take.
