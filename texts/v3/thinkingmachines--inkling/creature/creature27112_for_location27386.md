They begin in the seams.

Where the thumb-marked limestone mortar has cracked—not broken, never broken, only split with the patience of something that was never meant to yield—the vines take root. They are not climbing; they have no direction. Broad leaves, dark as wet slate, unfold in clusters that do not spread. One cluster ends, and miles of black hexagon begin, and then another cluster, identical in density if not in exact posture, holding the same lush saturation, the same arrested abundance. The ferns gather in corners where staircases of carved limestone ascend three steps and stop, facing nothing, and there they become fountains without flow, green geometry echoing the sharp edges of the plates beneath them. They do not grow beyond their islands. They do not wither. The roots wind through artificial channels drilled into the substrate, drinking from the capillary weeping that never stops and never runs.

Among them, the animals rest in absolute stasis.

The frogs—if they are frogs—sit at the edges of pools that reflect sky like polished metal. They have too many eyes, arranged in patterns that suggest design rather than evolution, and they do not blink. They do not breathe visibly. Their skin carries the same dark saturation as the leaves, as if they have emerged not from egg but from the compressed clay itself, taking on the color of the tray that holds them. Some perch on the woven reed-and-clay arches, those primitive gate-structures held together with copper wire, and there they sit on the same crack in the left pillar, at the same angle, whether the arch stands near or far. Distance does not change them. Repetition is their nature.

Insects with translucent shells drift or cling—there is no wind to carry them—among the green clusters, their bodies letting the bright, unfiltered light pass through in static prisms. They do not crawl in sequence; they are positioned, fixed in the equilibrium like the droplets that cling to vertical walls and never fall.

And the birds—if flight is still a concept here—perch with feathers like woven grass, textured and still, some floating on the unmoving water, others settled on pillar-gates that stand with no wall attached, facing the open flatness. They do not call. There is no sound but the low, single-frequency hum rising from the black clay, a resonance of compression and heat that barely registers, never varying.

They do not hunt. They do not flee. The frogs do not flick tongues; the insects do not crawl toward light; the birds do not preen. They are present at an abundance of 2.2—scattered, visible, undeniable—yet they exist outside process. Life here is not lived; it is maintained, engineered through the same hand-drilled hydrology and compressed substrate that shapes the valley itself. Even their biology feels constructed: the extra eyes, the translucent shells, the woven feathers, all suggesting materials—reed, wire, clay, limestone—reconfigured into beings that hold stillness as their primary function.

They are part of the strange. The archway miles away is not similar to this one; it is the same arch, the same vine growing at the same angle in its crack, the same frog perched with the same impossible eyes. The animals confirm this repetition. They are the biological inlay in a manufactured world, lush in their pockets, scattered across the open regional scale, existing in sharp-edged youth, never aging, never moving, part of a breath held forever in a hot, bright, silent tray.
