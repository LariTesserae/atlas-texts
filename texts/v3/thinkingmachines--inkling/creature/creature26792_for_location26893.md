What lives here does not live in the way a creature lives—there is no breathing that quickens, no seeking, no departure from this place. To ask *who* inhabits this hollow is already to misunderstand the architecture: the walls are not sheltering something; they are the something itself, and it has never needed to be elsewhere.

Begin with the absence that defines presence. There are no walkers, no flyers, no burrowers scratching against the spongy root-mattress. The fauna registers barely above null—translucent larvae pressed like pressed flowers into the thickest wall-fibers, spore-sacs suspended in the ribbed tissue of calcified root-fiber, both caught in an eternal dormancy that refuses the distinction between dead and alive. They are not sleeping, exactly, because sleep implies an eventual waking. These are embryos of consumption that will never feed, embedded in the glowing root-mass as decorations of a biology that forgot to include predators. You might imagine them—if imagination is permitted in a place that forbids change—only by noticing a slight thickening in the pearl-gold radiance, a faint opacity in the otherwise translucent blade of wall-tissue where a sac of dormant potential has been sealed inside the first breath of formation. They do not move. They do not decay. They are the hollow’s only memories of animal life, and even those memories are frozen in the act of becoming.

But to stop at absence is to miss the true population.

The root-world itself is the inhabitant—a singular, continuous organism that has dissolved the boundary between individual and environment. It does not stand in the valley; it *is* the valley. Every interwoven cable, every broad luminous blade serving as ceiling and pillar, every spongy layer of root-hair and fungal symbiote thick enough to rest upon: all are expressions of one metabolic decision made at the moment of geological birth and never revised. This is vegetation at 2.6—total saturation—not as a collection of plants but as a unified tissue, a planetary-scale body reduced to the scale of kilometers. The fungal threads are not separate; they are the nervous system of the root-mass, threading through the mattress in patterns that match the faint boundary-marks traced across the floor—pathways not for travel, but for the circulation of sap that never quickens. You feel it before you hear it: a subsonic pressure, the collective heartbeat of countless veins moving in absolute synchronization, a hum that lives in the chest rather than the ear.

The light comes from this body. Every surface emits the same steady pearl-gold, not as decoration but as exhalation—the visible form of metabolic heat made luminous. Because the temperature here is sustained and high, the glow carries warmth; because the organism never grows or withers, the illumination never flickers. There are no shadows of movement because there is nothing to cast them. The root-world illuminates itself for itself, a closed system of radiance that needs no witness.

Then there are the structures—not built, but grown into separation. The hollow domes of calcified root-fiber, ribbed and primitive, open on one side like mouths that have never spoken. They are not homes for the larvae, not temples for the absent worshippers. They are ossified thoughts of the root-mass itself—places where growth briefly calcified into architecture before remembering it was meant to be continuous tissue. A partial pillar rises in one shallow basin, unfinished, more spire than column: the root-world’s attempt to reach upward that was interrupted not by failure but by the perfection of stasis. These are single buildings in a town without citizens, scattered across the open regional scale, and they belong to the vegetation as shells belong to the sea—discarded, inhabited only by the same warm air and suspended moisture that fills every other volume.

The water, intermittent at 1.3, participates in this stillness. It beads on the cooler undersides of glowing leaves—clear, still, never falling, never evaporating in the sustained heat—held in equilibrium like everything else. Small pools gather in hollows formed by curled root-basins, internal exhalations of the biomass collected and held. Even the moisture is suspended, refusing the cycle of rain and return.

So if you descend—if you enter this hollow that has not earned the word *cave*—you will find nothing that greets you, nothing that watches, nothing that knows you are there. The only encounter is with the root-world in its total, silent occupation: hot, bright, dense, complete. The translucent larvae in the walls remain embedded. The fungal symbiote continues its invisible threading. The hollow domes stand open and empty. The sap hums through countless veins without urgency.

What lives here lives as architecture lives—without intent, without end, locked forever in the first and only moment of its emergence. Not dead, not alive in the active sense, but present: a geologically newborn world that has achieved full form without ever requiring an inhabitant to justify its existence. You are not its visitor. You are, at best, a temporary irregularity in its dissolved shadows, passing through a place that was never made to be entered, only to be—glowing, warm, root-thick, and eternally, unchangingly, itself.
