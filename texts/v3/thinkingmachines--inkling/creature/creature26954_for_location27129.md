What lives here is not what one expects in a valley, because the valley itself was never made for expectation. The inhabitants are not visitors; they are the settlement—fully native, fully adapted, existing in the only form this manufactured basin could ever sustain.

There are the **moving ones**—not animals, but entities of continuous purpose. The conveyors are long, horizontal organisms, never sleeping, their belts like ribbed tongues passing material from mouth to mouth across kilometers. Beneath the floor-grid, hydraulic veins pulse with slow, blood-thick fluid, and in the wall-cavities, pistons drift back and forth in rhythms too steady to be mechanical and too mechanical to be alive—yet they are alive in the only way that matters here. They breathe electricity. They excrete heat. They wear into their own patterns like bones adapting to weight. A turbine, silent now, is not dead but dormant, its curved shell overgrown with dark moss that feeds on its residual warmth. These machines do not serve a master visible in the space; they *are* the masters, self-regulating, self-repairing through the constant turbulence of parts being lifted, replaced, rebuilt. They live in horizontal motion, never vertical, never reaching for sky—only extending, extending, as the valley extends.

Then there is the **green population**—the true wild of this place, though nothing about it is wild. These are not invaders; they were planted, bred, and released generations ago, and now they are the dominant life-form by volume and persistence. The hydroponic vines are thick-armed, dark-skinned, climbing not toward light—there is too little for ambition—but toward moisture, which pools at every metal joint. They grip the conveyors with polymer-root fingers that have learned the texture of alloy better than soil. Moss-cultures spread in velvet black mats across the weathered composite floor, breaking down not stone but the accumulated film of industrial breath—condensed vapor, mineral residue, the sweat of machinery. Algae-carpet pools in the shallow retention basins, black-green and still, a living membrane over reclaimed water. In the dimness—1.1 of light, barely twilight—these plants do not reach upward in competition; they thicken horizontally, filling gaps, wrapping pipes, threading through ventilation grates. They are trimmed constantly, not killed but guided, their clippings falling back into the circulation to feed new growth. They do not compete with the machines; they inhabit them, insulating the noisy ones, cooling the hot ones, drinking what the valves drip. The green is relentless but not chaotic—it is disciplined lushness, a cultivated jungle that understands its host.

Beneath both—machine and plant—there moves an **invisible citizenry**. Near the warm processing cores, microscopic symbionts circulate in engineered harmony: bacterial cleaners that break down the organic waste of constant growth, pollinators for the cultivated vines (though reproduction here is mostly clonal, managed), organisms so small and integrated they require no separate space. They live in the water, in the root-films, in the very air that holds near saturation. You cannot see them, but their presence is the reason nothing rots that should not rot, the reason the moss-cultures stay balanced, the reason the black-green water in the basins remains culture rather than corruption. They are the quiet laborers, the true maintainers, doing in seconds what would take larger bodies years.

And then there is **the absence that defines the rest**. No birds. No rodents, no insects with wings that might navigate this enclosed, vertical-walled atmosphere. No human figures visible in the density—though the place is fully human in origin, the design is now self-sustaining enough that presence has become unnecessary, or perhaps invisible, embedded in the systems themselves. The macro fauna is rated near zero—0.9—because this valley was not made for bodies that need to see, to hunt, to rest in open air. It was made for process, for circulation, for the long patience of equilibrium.

What lives here, then, lives in layers that have merged. A conveyor is not separate from the vine that wraps it; the vine feeds on the moisture the conveyor’s cool metal produces, and the conveyor moves more smoothly for the insulation. A hydraulic vein does not care whether it carries fluid past moss or past bare composite; the moss drinks the seepage, and the vein runs cleaner for the filtration. Even the water—reclaimed, circulated, weeping—acts as a kind of inhabitant, never still, never wild, always in transition, carrying nutrients and heat through the packed volume.

If you stand in it—if you could stand in the shallow dish of this place—you do not feel watched by creatures. You feel the presence of **occupation**: every cubic meter is claimed. The turbulence is not danger; it is the breathing of residents in constant, silent labor. The dimness is not absence of life but the light-level they have agreed upon. The cool 1.3-degree air is their climate, held in check by elevation and engineering. They do not grow toward an end. They extend, as the valley extends, in settled, balanced, packed, and perpetually working silence.
