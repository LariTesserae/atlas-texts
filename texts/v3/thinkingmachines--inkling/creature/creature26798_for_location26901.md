What lives here does not cluster; it disperses. Life in this unfinished vertical factory is a thin, deliberate suspension—organisms spread across the weeping expanse like particles held in a solution that refuses to settle. There are no crowds, no colonies of warmth, no huddled masses against the cold. Instead, presence is measured in miles of separation, in long sightlines interrupted only by the slow, heavy transit of bodies built for endurance rather than speed.

The grazers come first in consciousness, if not in chronology. They are large—massive enough that their weight presses into the spongy, mineral-saturated ground with audible compression—covered in coats that are not fur so much as dense, dull-silver insulation, layered like sedimentary rock. They move in loose, almost mathematical formations across the open flats, never dense, always with space between them, feeding on the stiff grasses and the black algae that grow at the edges of melt pools. Their breath does not vanish; it hangs in long, geometric trails behind the herd, white contrails that mirror the glaciers advancing in the distance. They do not hide; there is no cover to seek. They exist as visible machinery, chewing slowly, processing the sparse vegetation with an industrial patience, their bodies radiating heat that barely dims in the absolute cold.

At the margins—always at the margins—move the predators. They are not numerous; abundance here is reserved for the patient. They are silent in a way that seems structural, as if sound has been engineered out of them. Their coats are darker, absorbing the dim twilight rather than reflecting it, and they travel alone or in pairs that maintain perfect, unbreaking distance from the grazers. They do not chase across open ground; the ground is too vast, the air too thin for sprint. Instead, they follow at the edge of vision, sometimes visible only as a slight darkening in the grey-green mist, sometimes not visible at all except as a sudden absence—one less grazer in the formation, a trail of breath that ends abruptly. They hunt by proximity over time, by the mathematics of exposure and exhaustion, matching the geological pace with biological patience.

Beneath the surface weeping, in the meltwater channels that run with mineral purpose, live the thick-bodied fish. They are not delicate; they are built like submerged engines, broad-flanked and slow-moving, their scales dark with the minerals they filter. They feed constantly, not on other life so much as on the dissolved pressure of the rock itself—calcium, iron, sulfur—turning geology into flesh with a regularity that seems mechanical. They spawn in the cold pools that form overnight when streams change course, leaving gelatinous masses attached to black stone that freeze slightly at the edges but maintain liquid centers. Their presence is continuous, a submerged conveyor of consumption that never stops, even as the channels shift beneath them.

Above all of this, on heavy wings that seem too large for the thin air, the birds dominate not by numbers but by scale and duration. They are enormous—wingspans that cast shadows across the spongy ground like passing clouds—covered in feathers that are grey and weather-resistant, not for display but for insulation against the perpetual moisture and cold. They do not flap constantly; they ride the thermal machinery of the rising air, the same updrafts created by geothermal vents and the grinding friction of new stone, circling for miles in patterns that seem mapped rather than chosen. They land rarely, and when they do, it is on single prominent rocks, standing with the stillness of monuments, watching. They feed on the dead—grazers fallen to exposure, fish trapped in evaporating pools, the occasional predator that misjudged the ice—serving as the aerial sanitation of a landscape that cannot bury quickly.

Even the smallest inhabitants operate at this scale of endurance. The lichen spreads in geometric silver patches across the black rock—not randomly, but in designs that suggest they are testing the surface for stability, marking the newest exposed stone with a biological chart. Black algae thickens in the melt pools like living ink. The occasional stiff grasses stand not in meadows but in isolated, defiant clumps, each blade seeming to ask whether the ground has finished moving enough to permit roots.

The relationship between them is not hidden; it is exposed, continuous, and rapid. Death is not concealed in burrows or undergrowth—there is none—but is consumed quickly in the thin air, frozen, broken down by the cold and the chemical saturation of the water, returned to the mineral cycle with the efficiency of a factory reclaiming waste. The predators eat in open sight; the birds descend without stealth; the fish absorb what sinks. Nothing rots slowly here; everything is processed, moved, routed.

There are no humans—no structures, no tracks that are not geological, no artifacts to suggest observation or habitation. The only presence that might be mistaken for intelligence is the landscape itself, and it is not intelligent but manufacturing—producing elevation, routing water, distributing cold. The life that has arrived lives inside this process, not outside it. They are not visitors to a place; they are the early colonists of a product line that has not yet been completed, organisms that have learned to move, feed, and reproduce within the rhythm of continuous assembly.

What lives here, then, lives in suspension—distributed, patient, mechanically aligned with the grinding, weeping, unfinished world around it. Heavy grazers tracing lines of breath across empty flats. Silent predators holding distance at the edge of sight. Thick fish processing stone in dark water. Massive birds circling on manufactured air. And beneath them all, the silver geometry of lichen marking which rocks have just been born. It is an ecosystem without shelter, without darkness, without end—just the constant, visible motion of life trying to keep pace with a mountain that is still being made.
