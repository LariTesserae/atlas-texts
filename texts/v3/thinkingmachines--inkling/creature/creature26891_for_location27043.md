What lives there is not a population but a condition—an agreement between hand and root that nothing here will ever be finished, and so nothing here can die in the usual way.

Start with the absence, because that is where the presence begins. There are no herds, no nests, no tracks left in mud that remembers every touch. The footprint is too narrow for bodies, too vertical for rest. And yet the space is crowded with inhabitants who have learned to live without leaving themselves behind.

There are the builders, though “they” may be too generous a word—perhaps there is only one, continuous, distributed. You do not see them, but you feel the recentness. The mud is still warm under the palm-print you almost make, but don’t, because the wall is still accepting pressure. The bone needles, the root-ropes pulled taut through wet clay, the wooden wedges driven not into dead wood but into frames that seem to be growing around them—these are not relics. They are current events. The builders are present as action rather than anatomy: hands that appear only in the shaping, bodies dissolved into the labor itself, perhaps never fully formed, perhaps always in the process of deciding whether to have faces. They live inside the vertical task, extending the shaft downward not because they must go somewhere, but because the place requires more of itself—more depth, more upward reach, more impossibility fitted into the fist-sized mouth. They are invisible not by hiding, but by being distributed across every unfinished surface, part of the embryonic tissue that has not yet learned opacity.

Then there is the vegetation, and here the word “inhabitant” collapses into something truer: they do not live in the space; they are its consciousness. The ferns do not root so much as reach, their broad wet tongues pressing against the woven root-bark with the insistence of thinkers testing a wall for meaning. The moss is not ground cover but infill—filling the gaps not by accident but by mandate, as if the structure can only hold itself together through the refusal to leave empty space. The vines hang in curtains that function as doors, as lungs, as memory. They move slowly, not with wind—there is no wind, only the rising and falling of the hot breath—but with an internal tide, tightening around the wooden wedges, drinking the suspended beads of water before they can fall. Some of them may have begun as planted things, carried in handfuls of soil; now they are the architects of the lower darkness, extending roots downward into the well-with-no-bottom, upward into the steam-thick ceiling that has never been seen. They live here as the place lives through them: unfinished, wet, loud in their silent growth.

Rarely—so rarely that you might convince yourself it was only a shadow moving wrong—there is the other. The small creature with too many joints, translucent, navigating the weep-holes where the mud is thinnest, where geometry has not decided whether to be passage or wall. It is not an animal exactly; it is a probe sent by the space to test its own unfinishedness. Its joints bend in directions that suggest dimensions not yet named, and its translucence makes it hard to tell whether it is made of flesh or of the hot condensation it moves through. It does not eat, or if it does, it eats the boundary between liquid and air, the thin films of green that gather in the darkest folds. It lives here alone, mythic even to itself, perhaps the only individual in a world of collective processes. To see it is to understand that the pocket allows for singularity, but only barely—only in forms so strange, so jointed, so barely there, that they seem like mistakes in the weaving.

But the true inhabitants are the elements that behave like beings. The water lives here in a cycle so intimate it is indistinguishable from thought: rising from the pools only inches deep, climbing the hot air through the vertical shaft, cooling against the upper weave, falling back as warm rain. It lives everywhere at once—bead, pool, breath, glass—never leaving, never still. The steam lives in the hiss that fills the narrow room, a constant vocalization that is not made by throat but by pressure, by the space exhaling its own heat. Even the sound lives: the drip is not background but a resident, percussive and patient, marking time not in seconds but in the accumulation of presence. The groan of roots tightening is the place stretching in its sleep, if sleep is something that happens during growth.

And then there is the space itself—the pocket, the verticality, the folded extreme. It is the primary inhabitant, and everything else is its organ, its hand, its unfinished thought. It lives by refusing scale. It lives by being born rather than eroded. It lives in the damp knot that should not hold a mouth, reaching downward into an abyss that has no floor and upward into a darkness that has no ceiling, contained entirely within an opening the size of a clenched fist. Its life is measured not in years but in the continuous application of mud, in the tightening of vines, in the decision—still pending—of whether it will ever become opaque, complete, real.

So if you ask who lives there, the answer must be layered like the walls: the invisible builders, distributed in the labor; the vegetation, occupying rather than growing; the translucent creature, testing the edges of form; the water and steam, living in recursive breath; and above all, the pocket itself—hot, wet, loud, green, and entirely made—living its own extreme life inside a hidden mouth, never finished, never empty, always in the act of becoming more of what it is.
