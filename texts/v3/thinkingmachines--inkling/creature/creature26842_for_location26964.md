What lives here does not live in the usual sense—there is no hunting, no fleeing, no urgency of birth or decay—because the place itself has finished with urgency. Life here is not an event but a resonance, distributed so evenly across the emptiness that you must stand still long enough for your eyes to stop searching for motion and start recognizing presence.

The primary inhabitants are the shell-beings. They are conical, yes, but that is only their silhouette against the relentless glare; seen up close—if you can approach without your own shadow disrupting the balance—they are translucent vessels, thin as blown glass and tinted the faintest gray-blue, like ice formed in a sky that never storms. Each is perhaps the length of your finger, tapered to a soft point at the top and open at the base, where it seals itself to the hollow stone of the spires with a membrane so fine it seems to be made of the same damp air. They do not crawl. They do not cluster in the way animals cluster for warmth or defense. Rather, they cover the resonant tubes in colonies that are mathematically patient: arranged in spirals that follow the grain of the eroded rock, dense enough that in some places the spire beneath becomes invisible, yet never overlapping, never competing for space. Millions of them. Millions spread across miles of flat stone, separated by stretches of bare rock that look dead until you realize the distance between colonies is simply the breath the system takes—to walk those empty miles is to walk through a held note, and the shells at either end are still listening to each other through the vibration in the ground.

Inside them, through the translucent walls, you see the geometry. Not organs—no heart, no gut, no frantic red machinery of biology—but chambers. Simple, repeating, hollow spaces arranged like the spires themselves, miniature echo-chambers. They are filter and instrument both. The perpetual, fine condensation in the air moves through them, not consumed in any greedy way but drawn slowly across their internal surfaces, leaving behind whatever trace minerals the dampness carries, while the beings themselves contribute back: each shell hums a microscopic harmonic, barely perceptible alone, but multiplied across millions, they do not merely accompany the drone of the spires—they complete it. The deafening chord you hear is partly them. They are the living part of the instrument, and the instrument is the world they inhabit.

They do not reproduce in cycles. They do not age visibly. Some shells are smaller, perhaps younger, perhaps older—there is no way to tell, because none are discolored, none are scarred, none are broken. When one is finished, it does not rot; it seems to become more translucent, more aligned with the light, until it is indistinguishable from the air around it. The colony maintains its number with a patience that looks like permanence. They are in balance with the erosion, with the light, with the sound.

In the mirror-still pools that gather between formations, there are others: minute blue-green forms, not shell but drift, suspended in perfect calm despite the thunderous air vibrating everything else. They are smaller still, visible only as a faint coloration in the water, like ink diluted by a hand that never moves. They do not swim. They hang at various depths—some near the surface, catching the blinding white-blue glare and refracting it downward, others settled closer to the polished stone bottom—forming a slow, vertical architecture of presence. Perhaps they are the seeds, the unformed, the ones waiting for conditions that will never change enough to require them. Or perhaps they are the memory of the shells, dissolved and holding its shape in liquid. The water never ripples because they do not move; they exist in a state of such profound equilibrium that disturbance itself is foreign to them.

Then there are the lichens, older even than the colonies. They cling to the darker, northern (if north means anything here) sides of the spires, silver-gray and dry against the perpetual damp, forming crusts that look like frost that forgot to melt. They do not spread aggressively; they have found their margin in the shadows where the light, for all its brutality, cannot quite reach, and they stay. They are the only ones who seem to possess a kind of history, growing in layers so thin that each might be a century, recording nothing but their own persistence.

The emptiness between these presences is not absence. The dust hangs suspended—fine, mineral, never falling—caught in the perpetual vibration, and within that haze, if you watch long enough, you see that even the air is inhabited. Not by insects, not by birds (there are none, and the sky is black-blue and empty), but by the distribution itself. The life here is a field. To be a shell-being is not to be an individual with a boundary; it is to be one node in a network that spans the plateau. The clusters are not separate communities but organs of a single, dispersed being that breathes through the spires and listens through every translucent wall.

And then there are the traces of those who came from elsewhere and understood, immediately, that this was not a place to colonize. The flaked obsidian blade, half-buried not in soil—there is no soil, only polished stone and suspended dust—but in a shallow depression where it was placed, not dropped. The bone needle, worn smooth not by use in the usual sense but by the same constant vibration that wears the ridges round. The cairn of three stacked stones, standing alone in one of the flat stretches with no path leading to or from it. These are not ruins. They are acknowledgments. Someone arrived—perhaps drawn by the drone, perhaps lost in the open distance—made the simplest possible tools, arranged the simplest possible marker, and left. Or perhaps they stayed, and became part of the field, their own bodies now translucent, internal geometry visible in the right angle of relentless light, added to the colonies not as conquest but as translation.

Because that is what lives here: translation. The place translates vibration into being. The shells translate air into harmony. The pools translate absence into shape. The lichens translate shadow into silver-gray persistence.

If you stand long enough—high, already, before you realize it, held by the cool precise air and the deafening constant chord—you begin to understand that you, too, are being filtered. Not consumed. Not welcomed in any warm, biological way. But registered. Your breath, your warmth, your temporary disturbance of the suspended dust are drawn through the system, added as a microscopic harmonic, and then you are part of the equilibrium too. Not dead, not alive in opposition to them, but distributed. The elevation holds you closer to a sky that offers nothing back. The erosion has finished its work. The sound never changes. And in the vast spaces between the clusters of translucent shells, you realize that the emptiness was never empty—only patient, holding its breath, singing through everything that has learned, here, to become still enough to hear it.
