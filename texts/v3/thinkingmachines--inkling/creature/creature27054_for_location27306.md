Here is who lives there—though “who” fractures quickly, and “where” bleeds into the living itself.

At the ground, in the silver-green velvet, live the rustlers: burrowing creatures no larger than a knuckle, translucent-boned, moving with such constant urgency that their presence is not seen but heard—a continuous tearing of dry paper, never resolving into individual bodies. They are prey and predator in the same breath, negotiating death not as separate actors but as one restless texture in the moss. They do not sleep; they shift. The moss breathes with them—cushion-plants puffed like dark lungs, stunted grasses with translucent roots that grip the porous basalt not as anchors but as fingers holding air. This green-black lining is not backdrop; it is participant, exhaling the wet fog that clings to every surface, feeding the pools that freeze by morning and thaw by midday.

Above them, the air holds a single distributed body. The translucent-winged insects are not a swarm but a decision made visible—thousands of wings beating not in chaos but in unison, rising and falling as if pulled by one hidden lung. They gather over the shallow pools, disperse toward the eastern wall, gather again. There is no leader; there is only the choreography. And the birds—high-altitude, sharp-crying—move with them in arcs that echo the insects’ rises, diving not randomly but in patterns that seem rehearsed, their piercing calls cutting through the wind’s continuous roar with the precision of signals. Predator and prey are not opposed here; they are phrases in the same sentence.

Then there are the marks of brief, primitive interruption: flat basalt stones stacked into a broken arc near the northern pool, held without mortar by patient hands; chipped obsidian tools half-swallowed by moss; and at the center of the shallowest pool, a single weathered pole of dark wood wrapped in faded fiber. These speak of occupants—early, tool-using, manual—who entered, shaped shelter, placed an object of unknown purpose into the still water, and withdrew. Or did not withdraw. Perhaps they are part of the rustling now, dissolved into the choreography, their brief occupation leaving only these manual traces as bones leave shells.

But the true inhabitant is older and stranger. The central pool, open to sky and wind, holds perfectly still—reflecting not clouds but the black rock below, as if it is not water but an eye fixed downward. The fauna’s synchronized movement, the moss’s breathing, the constant drip-echo against stone—all of it behaves as if coordinated by a presence distributed across the hollow. The water weeps more heavily from the rock when no observer stands near; it slows when watched, not out of shyness but out of recognition. The crescent arch of basalt frames empty air and casts no shadow, suggesting that whatever lives here does not obstruct light—it is the hollow’s own awareness given form.

So: there are the small burrowers tearing through moss; there are the insects and birds, one body in many parts; there is the living carpet of silver-green and dark cushion; there is the trace of human hands who came and left only tools and a pole; and beneath and through them all, there is the bowl itself—a cool, wet, loud consciousness that expresses itself through synchronized flight, still water, and the rhythm of freezing and thawing. The pole in the pool may be its spine. The broken stone arc may be its half-remembered boundary. Whoever lives here is not inside the place; the place is inside them, and they have never left. They are the echo that does not fade, the choreography that needs no conductor, the wet breath that turns every exhalation into visible proof that something here is still thinking, still watching, still holding its small high hollow together against the vertical emptiness beyond the walls.
