They do not stand out against the wood and stone; they emerge from it, as if the settlement excreted them to maintain itself. Their bodies are small—no one here is large—compressed not by famine but by architecture, generations of living in spaces where a raised elbow strikes a beam or a neighbor’s shoulder. They are wiry, low to the planks, with feet that have spread flat and calloused from constant walking on wet, uneven surfaces. Skin, where it shows, is not the color of sun but of the bowl itself: a cool, mottled gray-green, permanently damp, stained at the knuckles and ankles by the mineral seep that weeps through everything. Some have thin, translucent hair that grows in patches like the moss on the undersides of boards; others shave it away with bone scrapers so it does not catch in the woven reeds when they crawl through the lower maintenance spaces.

They are never, not once, alone. To be alone in the Bowl requires an emptiness that does not exist. Even in sleep—if sleep is the right word for the long, motionless periods in the stacked niches—they are layered against others, back to chest, breath mingling with the perpetual drip. Their voices, when they use them, are not raised; they speak in low frequencies, close to the ear, because sound here is intimate or it is lost. Much of their communication is tactile: a hand on a rope signaling tension, a foot tapping a plank in a rhythm that means the channel below has shifted. They understand vibration before they understand distance.

Every body is a tool-bearer. Woven into their belts of twisted reed are copper awls, bone needles, wooden pegs, small hammers of bound stone. They do not carry these as possessions but as extensions—there is no moment when they are not repairing. The ground shifts a finger’s width, and somewhere in the lattice a body moves to match it. A pole leans; someone is already there, shoring it with fresh timber. A trough cracks; another redirects the silver thread of water before it can pool and rot a floor. Their labor is not scheduled; it is continuous, like breathing. The hierarchy written in height and wall-proximity is visible in their wear: those who live nearest the weeping black walls—where the air is thickest with iron-scent and the stone is youngest—wear heavier bindings of soaked hide and carry thicker poles. They are the wall-keepers, the ones who read the settling in the vibrations of the rock itself. Higher up, closer to the weak gray light that filters through the roof-lattice, the inhabitants are thinner, paler, their garments lighter, woven almost entirely of reed. They tend the channels of air and the upper water-troughs, directing flow downward. There is no ruler visible, no throne—only this division made material in what they carry and where they sleep.

Children—if they begin small—grow up in motion. There is no flat ground for running; a child learns balance on planks before walking, learns the sound of a safe beam versus a failing one. They play, perhaps, in the shallowest channels, floating small vessels of bark that navigate the carved streets of water. But play is always adjacent to work. By the time a body reaches its full narrow height, it knows the settlement’s interior like its own bones: which bridge serves as floor for the layer above and ceiling for the layer below, where the moss is thickest, where the eastern wall’s weak light allows herbs to grow that can be twisted into cord.

Their eyes are large, adapted not to distance—there is no distance, only layers—but to the gray-green half-dark. They see cracks before they widen, see the lean in a pole, see the shimmer of water finding a new path. They do not look up expecting sky; they look up expecting more habitation, more wood, more bodies working in the lattice.

There are almost no others. A pale insect might crawl across a plank, and they might observe it briefly before it falls into a channel. A bird—small, colorless—might nest once in the highest poles, leaving only bones and a few feathers woven into the wall-keepers’ bindings as a rare ornament. The people do not hunt; there is nothing to hunt. They harvest the stunted reeds, the dark moss, the slow-growing herbs in the eastern cracks. They eat what can be grown in the channels, boiled in the same troughs that carry waste away, separated only by the flow of water and the constant labor of keeping the separation intact.

What is strange—what makes them more than mere occupants—is that they seem to be the settlement’s response to its own emptiness. They do not dominate the space; they fill it precisely, like water filling a crack, leaving nothing bare because bare things here collapse or fill with water and rot. They repair not to improve but to maintain the absence of gaps. To stop working, for one of them, is not rest; it is a kind of disappearance, a space opening where a body should be. And so they continue: walking the planks, carrying their bone needles and copper awls, feeling the ground remember it was underwater, and moving with it, always with it, in the cool, mineral, perpetually weeping dark.
