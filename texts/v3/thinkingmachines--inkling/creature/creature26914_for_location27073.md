What lives here is not singular but layered—an ecology of interruption, where the permanent residents have adapted to impermanence itself.

First, the true citizens: the swarm. They are small—no larger than a thumb joint, some smaller—encased in shells that are not quite bone and not quite stone. Their armor is mineral accretion, silica and chitin fused into crusted plates that grow like barnacles in reverse, feeding on the very dust they stir up. They are hard-shelled, yes, but the hardness is deceptive; it is the hardness of something that expects to be broken often and has learned to repair itself in motion. They move not in lines or flocks but in turbulent volumes, thousands per breath of air, a living fog with its own weather. Their bodies click and chitter in a constant mechanical rain—the sound is not language exactly, but it carries information, a networked rustle that peaks just before the ground heaves. They anticipate. Not through sight—the light is wrong for that—but through vibration, through the taste of shifting pressure in the mineral air. When a spire decides to crystallize and collapse, they are already gone from that quadrant, pouring away in a dark, crusted wave, leaving only the echo of their departure in the sudden silence.

They feed on mineral dust, scraping it with primitive mouthparts, and on one another when the density requires it. Cannibalism is not cruelty here; it is recycling in a world that does not waste. Their shells carry the taste of iron and old heat, and when they die—which is constant, which is always—their bodies do not rot but mineralize, adding to the ridges they crawl upon. They are the fauna of emergence: not evolved for a finished world, but co-evolved with the chaos of becoming. They thrive in the turbulence, speak its language fluently, and treat the constant destruction as architecture—building temporary cities in the hollows of fresh collapses, then abandoning them without sentiment when the geology shifts.

Beneath them, quieter: the fungal traces. Not the green ambition of surface forests—there is no green here, no upward reach. Instead, pale mycelium, thread-thin as hair, clings to the undersides of outcroppings that have managed, briefly, to hold still. They feed on sulfur, drawing it from thermal seams, existing in the negative spaces of the swarm’s chaos. They are ancient in patience, or perhaps they are young and mimicking patience because the world has not yet decided what age means. They do not swarm. They wait, white and fragile, the only soft thing in a realm of brittle negotiation.

And then there are the others—the ones who tried to stay.

The artifacts say they were brief, recent, already half-erased. Small cairns of raw stone, stacked by hands that knew no metal—simple scrapers, perhaps, left in hollows like forgotten prayers. Partial shelters brace against ridges that have since shifted, burying them under fresh mineral growth. The carved marks are attempts: maps that no longer match the terrain, claims scratched into walls that have since moved, memories etched in a language that assumes permanence. They were not the swarm—too large, too slow, too dependent on stillness. They built in the wrong tense, past and future, in a world that lives only in the present continuous. The turbulence outpaced them. They left not ruins but interruptions—fossils of intention in a place that has not finished deciding whether to allow history.

Perhaps they were explorers. Perhaps they were refugees from some surface that finished deciding long ago. Perhaps they were the first, or the hundredth, attempt at settling what cannot be settled. Their presence is faint, recent, already fossilized, and the swarm moves over their cairns without recognition, feeding on the mineral dust of their failed shelters.

And finally—perhaps most truly—the world itself lives. Not as metaphor, but as organism in suspension. The translucent crust overhead is skin, not sky. The thermal veins are circulation. The ridges and spires are tissue knitting, deciding moment by moment whether to be bone or wax, wall or womb. The temperature is bodily—2.1—warm with sleeping metabolism. The moisture comes in breaths, condensation like sweat, rain like tears from seams that have not yet learned to be sky. This is not a dead place being colonized; it is an unborn one being inhabited, and everything within it—the swarm, the fungus, the interrupted builders—lives inside that uncertainty. They are not residents of a world. They are passengers in a decision.

So what lives here? A saturation. A mechanical rustle of thousands of hard-shelled survivors who have made chaos their home. A faint white patience clinging to temporary stability. The ghost of hands that tried to build something lasting and were outpaced. And beneath all of it, the slow, warm, turbulent pulse of something that has not yet finished deciding what it wants to be—carrying them all, feeding them all, breaking them all, and beginning again.
