What lives here is not a population so much as an interwoven presence—everything that breathes, moves, or grows in the Hollow has settled into the same finished patience as the stone.

The vegetation is the first and most constant inhabitant. Those rubbery mats are not passive ground cover; they are a slow-moving population of their own, spreading by chemical conversation rather than thirst. They cling, they layer, they press. A person walking through the lower paths must part them like heavy drapery, feeling the waxy resistance, the slight drag of old leather, though there is no moisture to leave on the skin. They feed on what falls—feather, shell, dropped fiber, the warm exhalation of the rock itself—and in return they provide the architecture of shelter, the material of fire, the walls that are also roofs. They have colors that seem to absorb the dim gray light: burnt copper that glows faintly near the geothermal warmth, blackened bronze, deep violet so dark it approaches absence. They grow in centimeters across years, and the people do not cut them back aggressively; they tend them, weave them, live inside them.

Then there are the birds. Small, quick, dark-feathered—never described as beautiful, only as present. Their voices carry sharp and repetitive, a constant stitching of sound that never resolves into silence. They nest in the vegetation’s highest folds and in the rafters of the stone cells, and because the smooth basalt walls throw every call back in dense echo, the Hollow feels always populated, even when one stands alone in a pathway. The birds do not fear the people. They land on shoulders, on the rims of clay vessels, on the rope ladders. They are part of the vertical traffic, moving up and down in quick, dark flashes, feeding on the insects, scattering seeds and small shells, contributing to the chemical diet of the plants.

The insects come in visible streams—hard-shelled, dark, relentless. They move across the warm stone in lines that seem drawn by some invisible current, feeding on fallen plant matter, on bird droppings, on the slow decomposition that happens without water’s help. Their shells click faintly, a sound like dry seeds in a cup. They are eaten by the birds, and they provide in turn the food that sustains the heavy, slow-moving creatures—the ones with short black fur and bodies built for patience rather than speed. These larger beings navigate the lower paths with an old familiarity, neither wild nor domesticated, part of the settlement’s ecology as surely as the smoke or the stone. They are not ridden, not herded; they share space. A person might sit with one for an hour, both warming in the geothermal breath, neither needing the other but both present. Their fur is used, when it falls, woven into garments or compressed into the fires.

And then the people themselves emerge—not as masters of this place, but as its most deliberate inhabitants.

They are few, contained within that three-hundred-meter floor, and they have been shaped by the absence of water and the presence of constant, dry warmth. Their bodies are compact, built for climbing and weaving rather than long travel. Their skin is darkened—not by a distant sun that never reaches them, but by the slow stain of smoke and mineral dust, by generations of living in perpetual twilight. Their eyes are large, adapted to the dim gray coin of sky and the warm darkness below, and they navigate as much by sound and touch as by sight. They move slowly, not out of lethargy, but out of alignment with the world around them; quickness has no purpose here, no evolutionary advantage. Change comes across generations, and so do they—aging gradually, speaking little, making small adjustments.

They wear garments woven from the rubbery mats, layered and thick, sometimes patched with the black fur of the slow creatures. Their feet are callused, capable of gripping smooth basalt and rope alike. They carry tools of shaped stone and carved bone—blades for cutting fiber, pins of simple metal worked from native ore, nets for catching insects or guiding plant growth. They do not build in the conventional sense; they weave. Their homes are low cells of stacked stone, honeycombed and overgrown, connected by ladders that sway gently with the weight of bodies and vegetation. The roofs are living, the walls are breathing.

Their fires burn slowly, fed by compressed plant matter and mineral oil. The smoke does not rise; it hangs, a low, slow current that never clears, and they live within it. They do not drink—there is nothing to drink—so they take moisture from the chemical richness of the plants, from the insects, from the warm air itself, in ways that are not thirst but exchange. Their rituals, if they have them, are tied to the thermal breathing of the rock, to the slow expansion of the mats, to the echoing calls of the birds that mark time more reliably than any sun.

In the center of the Hollow, standing among them, one realizes that the settlement is not placed upon the land but grown into it. The people are as dark and still as the bronze-colored leaves when they choose to be, and as suddenly present as the quick birds when they move. They are intimate with every other form of life here—the insects they harvest, the birds they share space with, the slow creatures they sit beside—because in this compressed vertical world, there is no elsewhere, no outside, no empty space. Only the finished stone, the feeding plants, the echoing sound, and the warm, dry breath that sustains them all.
