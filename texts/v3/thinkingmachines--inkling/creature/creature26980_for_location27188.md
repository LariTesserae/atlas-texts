You do not notice them at first because they have adopted the settlement’s primary skill: the refusal to change state.

Then, in one of the open plazas—polished stone, a pool half-full and motionless—you see that a vertical line has been drawn where the geometry expected none. It is not a pillar. It holds the posture of someone who has stopped walking and decided to stay stopped. Its height is human, perhaps a little taller, but the outline is softened by the same erosion that rounded the cirque walls; there are no sharp shoulders, no abrupt angles, only a gradual accumulation of presence that looks carved and then left alone.

As your eye adjusts to the flat light, others emerge from the sparse grid. One stands in a doorway, half withdrawn into shadow that never moves. Another sits—not crouched, but arranged—on a low stone block beside a channel where water never flows. They are scattered at the same density as the buildings themselves: never clustered, always separated by those expanses of empty pavement that the plan insisted on leaving open. They do not face one another. There is no orientation of gathering. Each occupies its own coordinate in the grid, a solitary punctuation mark in a sentence written for more voices than arrived.

Their material is ambiguous. From a distance they seem to wear garments of woven fiber, gray-green like the lichens, hanging flat and heavy with the ambient damp, never billowing because there is no wind. Closer, the distinction between clothing and body blurs; they appear to be made of the same matte, weathered substance as the walls—stone that has learned to hold a vertical shape, or perhaps bodies that have slowly mineralized to match their home. Their skin, if it is skin, does not reflect light; it absorbs the diffuse gray and returns it unchanged. They are cool to the same degree as the air, which is to say they have no temperature of their own.

Movement, when it occurs, is not locomotion but adjustment. A head turns, not toward you but toward the pool, with the same unhurried inevitability as the drip falling into it. A hand—thin, fingered, lacking nails—lifts and touches the surface of the water not to disturb it but to confirm it. The contact makes no ripple. They seem to verify the conditions of the place: the stillness of the pool, the humidity on the wall, the absence of weather. They are not caretakers performing labor; they are the equilibrium made visible, checking itself against itself.

There are few of them. You might count seven, or twelve, spread across the bowl’s small circumference, never more than one visible to another. They do not speak. There is no language audible, only that occasional deeper resonance—like the drop heard from within the chest—when two happen to align in proximity, not greeting, merely acknowledging shared presence in the grid. Their faces, if they possess them, are not expressive in human terms; they are smooth, featureless or nearly so, like masks shaped by the same weathering that erased the sharpness from the rock. Yet they are attentive. They notice you. Not with alarm, not with invitation, but with the same flat patience the light applies to everything.

What they eat, if they eat, is not visible. Perhaps they absorb the moisture that beads on the stone, drawing it through open palms held near the walls. Perhaps they do not require sustenance, sustained instead by the absence of change—existing on the flat line of the temperature that never drifts. They do not sleep in beds; when they rest, they arrange themselves against walls or sit in the empty lots with the same posture they hold when standing, vertical or folded, always present, never collapsed.

Over time—though here time is only the repetition of identical drips—you understand that they are the reason the settlement remains sparse. The grid was planned for more, but they arrived, or perhaps they formed from the place itself, and they prefer the emptiness. They maintain it by their presence, by not multiplying, by not filling the plazas with noise or motion. They are the inhabitants the bowl was waiting for: not a population but a distribution, one being for each open space, holding the architecture in its intended state of quiet acknowledgment.

They are not dead. The pools near them do not stagnate further; the lichens near them do not wither. They are part of the cycle that has stopped cycling, the water that occupies rather than flows. If you leave and return, they will be in the same positions, perhaps adjusted by a fraction—turned slightly toward a different pool, seated where they stood—changed only in the way the settlement allows change: imperceptible, settled, final.

They are whatever lives in a place that has finished becoming itself. Not ghosts, not statues, not gods of the high ground—just the vertical, gray-green presence of the bowl’s own stillness, given form, scattered like the buildings, and holding the empty spaces exactly as they are.
