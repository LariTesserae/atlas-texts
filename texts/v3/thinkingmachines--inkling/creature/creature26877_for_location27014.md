What lives there does not divide neatly into the living and the lived-in, because the division itself has been worn away.

Begin with the plants—not as flora, but as citizens. They are the primary occupants, the ones who have had longest to settle, and they have done so completely. These are not trees reaching for a sky they can sense; they are kelp-cathedral and coral-vine thicket, rooted in nothing but the warm, mineral-saturated liquid itself, anchored sometimes to the smooth, mathematical curves of the worn stone below, but just as often holding themselves upright through the sheer density of their interweaving. They do not grow quickly—nothing here does—but they grow constantly, in expansions so slow they resemble geological processes. Tissue unfolds like sediment. Dead matter does not fall away; it is incorporated, layered into the living architecture, so that a single vertical stand might contain centuries of its own past, growing downward into decay and upward into new spore-bearing fronds in the same continuous gesture. They release their spores not in bursts but in hangs—fine, luminous dust that settles through the warm twilight like snow that never reaches ground, carrying chemical messages across leagues of thicket. They speak in salinity and temperature, in the slow adjustment of root-networks that have long since merged with one another. Open water is rare because they have filled it; they are the walls, the floors, the weather, the memory.

But the plants are not alone in their dominance, for the water itself behaves like an organism with its own imperatives. It is not a passive bath but a circulating body—massive, sluggish currents roll through the volume like internal tides, pressure ridges traveling for miles, thermal layers shifting with a glacial patience. The liquid is thick with dissolved minerals, with the accumulated remains of everything that has ever decayed here, and it holds its heat not as environment but as inheritance. It breathes in loops, never breaking surface—there is no surface, only a gradual, unreachable thinning upward—moving nutrients and spores in its slow blood. In this sense, the water is not the home of life but one of its forms, as present and intentional as the vines that drink it.

Then there is the other presence—the fauna, though “fauna” is a word that implies a kingdom, and here there is only the rumor of one. No schools move through the thickets. No predators stalk. No songs disturb the hum of accumulated silence. Perhaps there is a single form, or forms so rare and integrated that they register only as occasional disturbances in the vine-curtain: a shadow that is not shadow but absence of plant, a slow displacement of current that does not match the water’s own rhythms. It is seen too rarely to be named, and perhaps naming is impossible because it has worn itself smooth into compatibility with everything else. It does not hunt, for hunting requires urgency, and urgency has been eroded flat. It may be a gardener of sorts, or a pilgrim without destination, or the last remaining animal in an ecosystem that long ago decided vegetation was sufficient. Its body—if it has one discrete form—must be as smooth and contradiction-bearing as the place itself: able to exist at this impossible elevation, submerged yet never crushed, moving through densities that should be immobile.

Beyond these, scattered like thoughts that forgot to finish, stand the remnants. A pillar of unknown substance, half-swallowed in vine. An arch curving over emptiness. A wall-fragment, smooth, without inscription, without joint. They are not ruins in the sense of collapsed technology; there is no technology here, no trace of devices or manufacture. They may be the exoskeletons of a presence that achieved a kind of completeness and then simply remained, growing structures as coral grows reefs, or leaving behind bones because it never needed to build—because it was already, in its essence, the architecture it required. They suggest an inhabitant older even than the erosion, one that lived in the contradiction of high and drowned before the contradiction was worn smooth, and left only these faint marks: not messages, but residues.

Taken together, what lives in the High Drowned is not a population but an accumulated state. Life here has moved past the need for individual survival and settled into the form it always intended: a warm, silent, submerged highland where the plants are the history, the water is the circulation, the rare animal is the exception that proves the rule, and the scattered structures are the bones of completeness. Everything exists in the contradiction—elevated and submerged, ancient and continuous, dense and motionless—without resolving it, because resolution would require an edge, and all edges have been worn flat. Time does not pass here; it layers. The inhabitants do not ask to be understood, because understanding requires separation, and separation, like descent, like urgency, like sound, has long since been eroded away. They are the final form of their own place: growing, circulating, remaining—smooth, settled, and finally, irrevocably, home.
