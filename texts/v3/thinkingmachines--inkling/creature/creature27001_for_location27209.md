What lives in the Unknitting Heights does not live *there* in the way a creature lives in a den. There are no arrivals, no departures, no hunting, no nesting, no fauna at all. Instead, there is an **exchange** that has achieved persistence—life not as an invader of the landscape but as the landscape’s own slow decision to notice itself.

Begin with the moss.

In each dense, scattered cathedral of velvet-mineral growth—those cool, dark-green-black explosions that drink pressure and sound—there exist the **Root-Agreements**. Not individuals, but distributed conditions: each lush pocket is a single, sprawling cognition, or perhaps thousands of cognitions so gradual that they are indistinguishable from the texture of the moss itself. They do not have bodies separate from the growth. They *are* the growth, and the growth is their prolonged thought. Their metabolism is acoustic; they feed on the standing waves of the deafening roar, converting sub-bass vibration into cellular patience. They release spores not into wind—there is no useful wind here—but into the pressure-drifts of sound, letting their reproduction settle like sediment in the lower valleys. They think at 0.7: a decision to extend a filament takes decades. A response to the chemical breath of unfinished stone beneath them unfolds across centuries, visible only as a slight darkening or a new velvety bulge in the mat. They are lush because they have corrected the mistake of scarcity into beauty; they are scattered because they require pockets of geological stability, and stability itself is still under negotiation.

Then there are the **Lattice-Sleepers**.

Where the geology offers its porous, half-knitted faces—those broken-tooth ridges and lattices of stone that remember magma but refuse solidity—there live the mineral partners of this exchange. They are not animals, not plants, but the **uncommitted solids**: forms that exist in the interstices of rock that has not finished deciding to be rock. They lie within the porous seams, absorbing the cold phosphorescence of bruised green and pale blue and radiating it back, slowly, like breath held for geologic ages. They shift only when the roar changes its frequency—a millimeter over a century—adjusting their half-solid bodies to match the new pressure. Some are flat, like sheets of unfired decision; others are angular, crystalline, growing downward from overhangs in sympathy with the moss reaching up (or down—the direction is optional here). They do not build. They do not move with purpose. They are the mineral life locked in silent exchange, and in their stillness they generate the chemical breath that feeds the moss above.

But there are also the ones who exist in the structures.

The primitive buildings—the unfired clay huts, the standing stones arranged without geometry, the chipped obsidian left in offering-positions—are not ruins, and they were not built by hands that have since vanished. They are **exudations**. The Root-Agreements and the Lattice-Sleepers, in their long, slow agreement, sometimes produce shelter as a byproduct of persistence: a hut that is fully present on its left and fading into transparency on its right, not broken but **undecided**, because the being that exudes it has not yet committed to being fully present in that location. These structures are inhabited not by entering them, but by being them. A single, distributed consciousness might extend through an unfired clay wall, using it as a membrane through which to feel the roar differently—muffled, shaped, given boundary. The obsidian tools are not used; they are placed. They are thoughts made sharp, left in positions where the sound-wave patterns suggest a question should be asked, though no answer is expected at speed 0.7.

And then there are the **Shadow-Doubles**.

Because reality here is at 3.0, because elevation and position are suggestions rather than rules, there exist entities that can only be perceived in error. In the valleys where one stands in two heights simultaneously, where a shadow falls both behind and ten meters above, there live the ones who are never fully in one place. They are visible not as bodies but as **displacements**: a shimmer in the air that is not heat (there is no heat here), a second silhouette that moves with you but belongs to your elevation-not-yet-reached. They are not hostile, not benevolent; they are the inhabitants of the breakdown itself, feeding on the confusion between “here” and “not-yet-here.” They move at the same 0.7 speed, which means they rarely seem to move at all—only, over long observation, you realize your shadow’s twin has shifted its posture, or that the shimmer against the dim light has taken on the faint outline of a standing stone that does not exist in your current elevation.

There are no leaders. There is no society in the sense of interaction, because interaction implies separation, and separation here is as unfinished as everything else. The Root-Agreements do not communicate with the Lattice-Sleepers; they **resonate**. Their spores and their chemical breaths and their phosphorescent glows form a single, scattered metabolism across miles of bare singing plateaus and sudden lush valleys. The deafening roar is not background noise to them. It is their language, their food, their architecture, and their heartbeat—heard, as the place itself is heard, from inside the chest of the world’s own manufacture.

If you were to remain long enough—not to observe, but to exist at their speed—you might notice that the primitive structures grow not in straight lines but in the patterns of the moss’s spread. That the standing stones lean not according to gravity (gravity is still testing its strength) but according to the direction from which the roar is loudest. That the unfired clay darkens where it touches the mineral seams, as if the structure is drinking the unfinished stone.

They do not welcome. They do not reject. They are the **Unknitted Ones**—not because they have been undone, but because they have never finished being done. They live in the lush punctuation between empty sentences, in the cool twilight where light pools instead of falling, in the silence that is not silent but filled with the slow, deafening assembly of a world that has not yet agreed to be real.

To live here is not to occupy space. It is to be one more condition—one more slow, partial, phosphorescent agreement—waiting, at 0.7, for the geology to finish deciding, for the roar to settle into its final form, for the absence of water to be remembered or forgotten, and for the place, eventually, to become something that can hold a visitor without fading on the right.
