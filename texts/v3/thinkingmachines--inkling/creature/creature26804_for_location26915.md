What lives here is not separate from the place; it is the place, continuing.

First, there are the reeds. They are not scenery. They are citizens—thick as wrists, hollow, pale green—rising from the black-green pools and braiding themselves into walls, into archways, into sleeping nests suspended just above the waterline. They do not build and then stop; they keep growing. A doorway narrows over decades as stems thicken. A platform tilts as a root-ball swells beneath it. The moss and floating root-mat form floors and districts, hand-tied with fiber ropes and wooden stakes, but the tying is only a suggestion. The vegetation takes it further. They form the central gathering lattices, the storage baskets lashed to living pilings, the hanging root-curtains that half-hide distant platforms across the open channels. They breathe the constant moisture, drink the perpetual condensation, and in doing so they reshape the settlement year by year. To live here is to be woven.

Then there are the others, the ones that share the woven spaces without weaving them.

Translucent fish with long feelers drift in the hair-thin streams between buildings, slow as thought, their bodies catching the muted grey light from above before disappearing into shadow. They do not school with urgency; they hang in the lanes like suspended notes.

On the woven roofs, amphibians with pale bellies sit for hours—unmoving, watching nothing, waiting for nothing—before breaking into low choruses that join the constant acoustic weave: the dripping, the creaking of stretching fiber, the wet plucking of surfaces breaking. Their calls layer over one another without hurry, part of the loudness that fills the enclosed bowl.

Insects hang in slow, visible clouds near the water’s skin, their wing-cases rustling like dry paper in a wet world. Nothing hunts quickly. Everything waits.

But there are also the maintainers—if they are present, they are rare. They move with the same patience as the growing reeds. They navigate the water-lanes not by foot but in small coracles woven from the same hollow reeds that form the walls, floating through districts separated by wide emptiness. They wear the same muted colors as the place: cool greens, stone greys, the silver of fish. They maintain the structures by hand—cutting and tying new growth, letting old parts become habitat rather than removing them, guiding the architecture without dominating it. They live in the suspended nests, just above the waterline, and they do not claim the space. They tend it, then recede into sparsity.

What binds all of them is the water itself—not as enemy, but as ground. The maintainers wade waist-deep. The amphibians sit above it. The fish move through it. The reeds drink it and become it. The settlement is submerged, yet not drowned; it stands in cool, still liquid that never breaks into rain but condenses constantly, pooling, running, pausing. You do not swim here. You float, or you wade, or you sit unmoving on a woven roof and let the dripping count the hours.

And there is the strange reflection: the water so still, so dark with plant-shadow, that it shows not the sky but the underside of the world—roots, hanging moss, the bellies of platforms—creating the illusion that the settlement continues downward into infinite, cool depth. So those who live here inhabit not only the woven structures above the surface, but also the mirrored town below, hanging in black-green silence.

In this high bowl, urban order and total wilderness have not divided. The primitive hands that shaped the reeds into districts never forced the reeds to stop being wild. The result is a population that breathes together: growing stems, drifting feelered fish, pale amphibians on roofs, slow insects in visible clouds, and the occasional rare figure in a woven boat, moving through the channels with no urgency, maintaining a town that is also a swamp, a settlement that is also a living, submerged body—cool, loud with patient noise, sparse, and growing still.
