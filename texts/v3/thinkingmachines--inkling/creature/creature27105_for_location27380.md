What lives here is not a single creature but a running assembly—layered, patient, and mechanically alive.

Begin at the bottom, where life is barely visible and already operating like shift work. Bacterial mats coat the warm stone and pool edges in sheets so thin they seem painted on: sulfur-eaters, iron-breathers, colonies breaking minerals with chemical patience. They do not move; they process. In the perpetual dimness they flicker with a cold, faint light—indicator glows in a factory that never closes—while beneath them microscopic electrical fields hum through the wet substrate. These are the first inhabitants, the ones that turned weeping rock into edible chemistry, and they live here in uncountable billions, a living floor.

Above them, the vegetation does not grow so much as accumulate. Broad leaves the size of shields press against one another in near-solid canopies, rubbery stems twisting through them like cabling, vines threading every gap until there is no negative space left. Moss and root-mats form a spongy, rebounding ground—alive underfoot, decomposing and regenerating simultaneously. The plants live in saturation: they drink from the air, filter the mineral heat rising through porous channels, and produce biomass with the regularity of machinery. There are no clearings because nothing here has learned absence; every niche is filled, every surface claimed, the green architecture tiered upward in dense, wet stories.

In the warm shallows, amphibians live in restless numbers—not scattered, but populous, always in motion through the black water and spongy soil. They are the moving parts in the open pools, dense enough that the water seems to ripple with bodies rather than current. Above them, insects swarm in dark, shifting clouds, drawn to the chemical outputs and the faint bioluminescence, feeding and being fed upon in continuous exchange.

And through all of it move the small, quick predators—sharp in the layered gloom, navigating the vertical vegetation with practiced speed, maintaining the pace. They are not large; this is not a place of apex grandeur but of rapid, necessary dynamics. Predator and prey do not rest; they circulate.

No one built this. No hand left a mark. Yet what lives here has organized itself into something that functions like a town without walls: thermal vents pulsing like engines below, water moving through stone channels with plumbing precision, organisms processing heat and mineral in chemical assembly lines, weather cycling through as warm rain to feed the growth that stabilizes the soil that filters the water that returns to the vents.

The inhabitants extend—regional, open, disappearing into mist-obscured distances where the same pools, the same weeping stone, the same dense green machinery continues. They live in high density, near-constant contact, layered upon one another in saturation so complete that the boundary between one organism and the next, between root and mat and amphibian and water, becomes soft, shared, operational.

This is what lives here: not individuals in isolation, but an entire running condition—microbial, vegetal, amphibian, insect, predator—all held together in the hot, thick air of an ancient, settled basin, producing and returning, in perpetual, untouched motion.
