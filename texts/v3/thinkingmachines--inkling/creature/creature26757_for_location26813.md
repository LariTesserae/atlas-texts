In the Riven Lowland, to live is first to be seen only in response—to be part of a system that has not yet decided what is background and what is figure.

The most constant residents are the ones that do not move of their own will, yet are never still. On every basalt face, in every hanging rope of moss, the fungal bodies swell like dark organs—ochre, rust-black, deep emerald—until the valley’s perpetual roar touches them. They are not plants in any patient sense; they are listeners. A distant bellow from something half-glimpsed sends a pulse of sickly green racing through a shelf of them. The high chirp of insects in the millions triggers ripples of blue-white up a vine. They do not illuminate for beauty or for prey; they illuminate because the noise commands it. In near-absolute darkness—so complete that the eye forgets there was ever a sky—these reactive glows become the valley’s only map. To navigate is to move through a stuttering constellation of biological alarm, and the fungi are the first inhabitants you understand: not passive growth, but a distributed nervous system made of spore and tissue, translating the turbulent sound into visible breath.

Above them, the air is not empty space but a thick, warm, damp tissue of its own—moving at the same chaotic velocity as everything else. The translucent, winged amphibians do not fly so much as exist in collective turbulence. They fill the gaps between fern-towers in shifting clouds, their bodies barely opaque, catching the fungal flashes like passing smoke catching lightning. They do not migrate with purpose; they eddy. A vent of geothermal breath, a sudden collapse of black glass from a young cliff, a burst of spores—these trigger waves of flight that split and reform like fluid in a storm. They are overwhelming in presence—too numerous to count, too intertwined to isolate. You do not see one; you see the air itself changed, thickened by their translucent bodies, reacting in real time to heat and vibration and chemical exhalation. They are the valley’s breath made visible, warm and damp with them, always in motion, never landing long enough to rest.

Below, in the mud and root-knots and the hollows of fresh, sharp rock, other things claim the ground—not as territory, but as constant passage. Things with too many legs, segmented bodies clicking like wet stones struck together, move in perpetual migration through the decomposing lushness. Their mouths are segmented, efficient, always working—burrowing, surfacing, feeding upon the fallen, being fed upon in turn. There is no hierarchy visible, only flow: they are part of the same turbulent current that moves the amphibians and agitates the vines. The ground here is young and porous, steaming gently from black pools ringed with rust and sulfur, and these clickers navigate it not by sight—there is none—but by vibration, by chemical trace, by the constant irregular percussion of dripping hot rain and bubbling mud.

But then there are the traces that suggest something else—something that does not fully belong to the ecological roar, yet is too primitive to stand apart from it.

Scattered like seeds across the soft black stone are lines of carved notches—counting, perhaps, or warning, or ritual marking—cut with chipped obsidian. The tools are bone and split wood, woven fiber and sharp rock: technology at its barest edge, barely distinguishable from the environment it harvests. Temporary shelters hang like abandoned nests, woven from the toughest vines, already being reclaimed by climbing moss and twitching vegetation. They are not buildings—there is nothing built here, nothing permanent, nothing that claims space against the valley’s refusal to settle. They are brief interventions, gestures of presence made by hands that understood impermanence not as philosophy but as survival.

Who made them? The evidence does not answer clearly, and perhaps that is the point. They are absent, or hidden, or lost within the overwhelming density—small figures swallowed by the collective motion of other life. If they remain, they do so not as residents in any settled sense, but as part of the turbulence itself: nomadic, reactive, leaving behind only what can be woven quickly and abandoned faster. Their presence is inferred rather than observed, ghost-like in the very dark they share with the fungi and the swarms. They live, if they do, in the interstices—moving through directions of least resistance, never constructing, always responding.

Because in the Riven Lowland, dwelling is not occupation. It is synchronization. The vegetation itself refuses stillness—giant ferns unfurl in silent explosions, vines twitch at vibrations, spores release in visible puffs—so dense, so saturated, that individual forms blur into layered vertical chaos. The dynamics are turbulent, storm-state energy even without wind: hot mud bubbles and collapses, sharp cliffs give way in silent avalanches of black glass, and the fauna moves in chaotic waves triggered by pressures unseen. Even the water refuses to stay—arriving as sudden, brief, warm rain that hisses against the canopy and vanishes into porous ground within minutes, washing everything downward in temporary rivers.

To live here is to exist in constant reactive motion. There are no paths, only directions of flow. There are no rules except the immediate ones—heat, moisture, sound, movement, decay, sudden illumination. The inhabitants—whether fungal listeners, translucent aerial clouds, clicking ground-migrants, or the elusive notching hands—share a single condition: they are part of a world that has only just been born, and it is loudly, violently, darkly in the process of becoming. Nothing settles. Nothing is finished. The valley groans in its own shape, and everything within it moves in answer.
