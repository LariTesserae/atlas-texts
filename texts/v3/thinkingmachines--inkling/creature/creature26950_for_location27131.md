What lives there is not one thing, but a consensus between builders and breathers—between the architecture that refuses to be dead and the creatures that refuse to stay quiet.

Start with the **root-citizens**, because they came first and never left. In the Lower Root-Ward, they are old in the way mountains are old: not ancient as relics, but ancient as processes. Their fused trunks braid downward into the spongy floor and outward into the hollow’s walls, hollowed by centuries of slow internal erosion that is not damage but remodeling. They do not stand; they *hold*. Each tower is a single organism or perhaps a colony so interwoven it has forgotten where one body ends and the next begins. They metabolize warmth—radiating it through porous bark, exhaling spores that thicken the air like pollen in a cathedral just before rain. They have districts because they have temperaments: the Lower ones are dense, dark, conservative, pulling inward with that strange gravity; the Vine-Bridge Quarter reaches upward and across in straight, intentional arcs of living cable, always testing the distance, always swaying; and at the center, the Sap-Square holds its open plaza like a held breath, a community space grown rather than cleared, its flat moss-carpet level because generations of root and fungus have negotiated every bump into submission.

These plants are not background. They are the borough’s population, its history, its government. They build avenues not by design but by persistent reaching—liana-cables extending between towers until the gap is closed, then thickening, then becoming road. They build ceilings not to shelter but to translate: thousands of leaves arranged like shingles, filtering the normal light into green-gold, never blocking it entirely because darkness is not their intention—modulation is. They collect water in cup-leaves and root-bowls, holding pools of clear sap-water that never flow away, never stagnate, only sit in perfect biological balance with the decay that feeds them. They are technology’s opposite (0.3): no cut, no joint, no nail, only secretion and time. They have reached an equilibrium (erosion 2.0) where to grow is to repair, where falling is part of standing.

But between these citizens—between the ribs of this whale-cathedral—lives the **noise**.

The fauna (abundant 2.3, dynamic 2.4) do not fill the hollow; they animate its emptiness. They are small, mostly, because the space demands smallness—anything too large would disturb the delicate inward pull of gravity, the careful acoustic geometry of the place. There are the **translucent-winged drummers**, insects with chitinous cases like miniature lanterns, beating not in chaos but in rhythm, their wing-cases vibrating against the resonant bark-towers until the hollow itself seems to pulse. They move in migrations along the suspended vine-bridges, leaving trails of phosphor-green dust that glows faintly in the green-gold light, marking paths that vanish within hours as spores settle and reclaim them.

There are the **burrowers**, living inside the porous towers, never seen except by the continuous clicking of their passage—small, hard bodies scraping through channels that the root-citizens have hollowed for them, or that they have hollowed themselves over generations. They do not destroy; they participate. Their clicking is part of the layered thunder (sound: 3.0, deafening), part of the architecture’s own creaking respiration.

And there are the **gliders**, launching from the highest vine-bridges and riding thermal updrafts created not by sun alone but by the plants’ own metabolic breath—warm air rising from the root-carpet, carrying spores and gliders alike in slow spirals across the fifty-meter void. They leave no permanent trail, only momentary disturbance, a silver-fish flicker of movement in air that should be still.

What defines them all is that they live in **sound as substance**. The hollow is an instrument; the plants are its resonating body; the fauna are its musicians, whether they intend to be or not. Every call, every wing-beat, every sap-burst in the walls compounds in the empty space, echoing not as repetition but as accumulation. The sound is deafening not because it is loud in one direction, but because it is layered—chirping, drumming, vibrating membranes, the brittle crackle of spores releasing, the creak of growing wood, the rush of fluid through root-veins, the constant underwater-like pressure of moisture being drawn upward (defying gravity, falling in reverse) into the canopy’s waiting cups. The fauna navigate this not with ears alone but with their entire bodies: they feel the vibrations stain the air (weirdness 2.6), leaving faint shimmering after-images that linger for minutes, heat-distortions where a particularly loud call has passed. They do not fear this; they read it. The stained air is a map. The lingering vibration is news.

They also live with the **inward pull**—gravity that tugs not downward but toward the nearest massive wall. The gliders ride it. The burrowers cling through it. The drummers beat in rhythms that seem to account for the gentle tug at their shoulders, as if the hollow wants all its inhabitants near its living edges, never lost in the center’s open absence. Even the moisture participates: droplets falling upward, cool and impossible, drawn by the respiration of the ceiling-canopy, then reabsorbed—water cycling not by weather but by biology’s breath (water: 1.4, intermittent, pocketed).

There is no technology to silence them, no tool to harvest them. The ecosystem is pure pace: growth versus consumption, sound versus the brief silences between calls, the constant rebuilding as old towers thicken and new vines reach across voids that were never meant to be filled. The fauna migrate through districts they did not build; they drink from cup-leaves that hold water for them without intention; they burrow into walls that tolerate them as part of the same slow process.

If you stand on a root-bridge—feeling the spongy warmth (1.6) through your feet, hearing the deafening layered thunder pressing against your teeth, smelling the green thickness of spores—you are not observing them. You are being registered. The hollow is small, bounded, entirely alive in structure, almost empty in volume. Its inhabitants have made a settlement not of bodies packed together but of presence distributed through space: plants defining the edges, animals defining the air between, sound defining the relationship.

They have lived here long enough that the broken reality—gravity’s inward lean, sound’s staining color, rain that climbs—is not exception but climate. The glider launches. The burrower clicks. The root-tower breathes. A drop of condensation climbs past your face, cool, then vanishes into a leaf-cup. And the hollow, in its perfect strange equilibrium, continues—not as a place where things live, but as a place that lives through them.
