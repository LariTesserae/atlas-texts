At first there is only vacancy—the kind that makes you check over your shoulder not from fear but from the pressure of being the only quick thing in a world that has forgotten speed. You walk through the circular lanes, past the worn plazas, and the emptiness feels absolute. No footprints disturb the dust except your own, and even those seem already half-erased by the hot, abrasive wind that moves in long, echoing currents through the hollow. You tell yourself the settlement is dead, that the hand-shaped bricks and compressed earth walls are merely bones left behind by people who understood this pocket well enough to leave it, who followed some older migration upward through the crack in the world’s skin.

But the absence is too organized to be true absence.

You begin to notice that the vegetation does not grow randomly. The thick, ribbed fans of black-green spread in groves that mirror the layout of the dwellings—arching over fallen walls not like invasive overgrowth but like canopies raised over sleeping figures. The crimson mats cluster thickest near the circular doorways, as if drawn there, feeding on something that still emanates from within. And the stalks—those thick, outward-weaving stalks that form tangled archways—do not bend toward heat blindly. They bend *around* certain spaces, framing them, leaving dark, cold hollows untouched, untouched not by neglect but by intention. The plants are not wild. They are cultivated, or they are the cultivated.

Then you see one.

It is seated in the center of a circular dwelling, against a wall of fired clay worn smooth as river stone though there has never been water here. It is not hidden; it is simply still—so still that your eye had passed over it as architecture. Its form is humanoid, but its material is wrong. The skin is not skin but compressed earth and hardened fiber, the same substance as the buildings, fused now with root-systems that have entered through the base of its spine and woven upward through the ribcage. Thick stalks of the crimson vegetation grow through its legs, not piercing but integrating, the dark leather-fan leaves sprouting from its shoulders like slow wings. Its head is turned slightly toward a thermal vent in the floor, and from that vent comes the low rumble—the bass note that has filled the hollow since you arrived. The figure breathes with it, or it does not breathe at all, but its chest—if it is a chest—expands in the same rhythm as the vent’s release, a cycle measured not in seconds but in the long exhalation of geological time.

You realize there are others.

Not many—the density remains near-void, the city still breathes in its spaces—but they are distributed like seeds, one here, three there, separated by the long, bare corridors of cracked clay. They sit in open plazas, in storage pits lined with smooth stone, on terraces where flat mineral slabs have been worn round not only by wind but by the slow abrasion of bodies that have rested there for ages without shifting. Some lie half-buried in the lush mats, only a face—eroded smooth, featureless except for a shallow hollow where a mouth might once have spoken—emerging above the crimson growth. Others stand in narrow lanes, bent at the waist, fused with the plant-cord ropes that hang like ceremonial bindings from their arms. They do not decay; they have become part of the erosion and the settlement, weathered into the same rounded softness as the stone, neither dead nor alive in any quick sense, but present in the way that mountains are present.

Their technology is not separate from them. The woven fiber ropes, hardened by heat, still hang in loops from their hands. Tools of fired clay and smooth black stone rest in their laps, not dropped but held, fingers—if they are fingers—wrapped around handles that have grown into the grip through long symbiosis with root and mineral. They were the builders, clearly—the ones who arranged the circular dwellings to catch or avoid the thermal currents, who paved the plazas, who dug the storage pits. And they never left. They stayed, and the heat and the vegetation transformed them into inhabitants that match the ecosystem: feeding not on food but on geothermal seep, absorbing steam that never condenses, their bodies becoming channels for the mineral-rich warmth.

There is no quick intelligence visible—no eyes watching, no sudden turn of a head. If they perceive, they perceive at the pace of the plants and the vents. You stand near one for long minutes, long enough for your own breath to grow shallow in the desiccated air, and it does not acknowledge you. But the vegetation responds. A nearby stalk cracks—dry, paper-burning slow—and the sound seems to travel through the mat that connects to the seated figure, a vibration in its fused root-system. The bioluminescent edges of the black-green leaves flicker, dull and heat-adapted, in a rhythm that matches the vent’s rumble. They are in conversation, if conversation is the right word for an exchange that takes hours, conducted through chemistry and vibration and the slow expansion of fiber in extreme heat.

The cold spots—those darker hollows that the vegetation avoids—are the only places where these beings are not. You understand, slowly, that those are the absences. Perhaps they are graves, though there is no burial; perhaps they are wounds in the thermal field, places where something went wrong, where the balance broke. The buildings are arranged around them not by accident but by a logic of avoidance older than ruin. The inhabitants sit in the warmth, in the glow, in the loud resonance, and they leave the cold empty.

As you move deeper into the pocket, you understand that this is the population: not a crowd, not a society in motion, but a scattered congregation of the transformed, integrated with the lush, waterless growth that thrives on heat. They are the urban fabric made flesh—compressed earth given root, fired clay given posture, woven fiber given permanence. The city breathes through them. The lanes are their paths, worn round not just by feet but by the slow shifting of their weight over centuries. The storage pits hold not provisions but compressed mineral salts, perhaps offerings or perhaps the residue of their own chemistry. They live here because they built here, and they stayed to become part of what they built, achieving the settled equilibrium that the prompt spoke of—not dead, not gone, but settled into a state of being that requires no water, no sun, no quick animal energy.

You are surrounded now. Not threatened—there is no aggression in this pace—but witnessed by a presence that does not hurry to witness. The loud, deep machinery of the world continues: the vent rumbles, a mature frond snaps and falls onto clay with a sound like dry thunder, the wind whistles through empty doorways that are not empty at all but framed by seated figures you had mistaken for stones. And you realize that in this hidden bowl, low and enclosed, the inhabitants are not missing. They are everywhere, spread like scattered seeds across the plate, separated by vast emptiness not because they are few but because they are immense in their stillness, each one a town unto itself, each one holding the space between buildings with a patience that has outlasted the need for speed.
