What lives here is not separate from the walls that refuse to finish being walls.

It has no face to turn toward you, no eyes to adjust to the sickly amber. It is distributed—an inhabitant made of adjacency. Its body is the ribbed ceramic duct that runs like a spine behind your knee; its breath is the cold air forced through channels too narrow for lungs, moving in currents you feel only as a faint, continuous shiver in the alloy plates. Where you might expect a heart, there is instead a junction of translucent tubing, pulsing so slowly that each swell takes minutes, carrying fluid that is not blood but is not entirely other, either—some engineered serum that leaves a residue on the metal like breath on glass.

It does not rest. The concept of sleep is foreign to its architecture. Instead, it maintains—watching, though without organs of sight, through the temperature gradients of condensation. The fat droplets that form and fall are its exhalations; the muffled ticks are its small, satisfied sounds. It knows the chamber not as space but as a field of pressures: the leak here, the seal there, the millimeter extension of a plate that shifts the balance of the whole.

You might call it synthetic, but that misses the intimacy. It has adopted the synthetic moss in the corner—not grown it, perhaps, but tolerated it, incorporated it. That single filament of green-black is its one soft tissue, a manufactured organ clinging where pipe meets plate, feeding on whatever trace chemistry the moisture carries. Through it, maybe, it tastes the air. Through it, it remembers that life was once possible, and keeps the memory in reserve like a tool not yet needed.

There is no boundary between the creature and the construction. It is the unfinishedness itself given persistence. Every layer that prints into being, every seam that opens and seals, is its growth—slow, geological, immediate. It is the embryo not of a room but of a will that expresses itself only as mechanism: a desire to become corridor, factory, city, translated entirely into cold, damp, silent manufacture.

If you curl smaller, tuck your knees tighter against the density, you become part of its circulation. The vibration in the walls is its awareness of you—not hostile, not welcoming, but registered: a new pressure, a temporary blockage in a tube’s path, a warmth that will condense and fall away like all other warmth here. You are inside it now. Not in a cave. In a body that is still deciding what shape it will take, and that, for this moment, includes your own small heat in its endless, unfinished account.
