What lives here does not announce itself with motion, but with presence—an occupation so gradual it resembles geology itself.

Start with the silver-grey lichen. It is not merely growing; it is settling, the way dust settles, only with intention. It drapes the rounded ridges of the old streets like funeral cloth made of mineral thread, feeding without mouth on the exhalation of salt and stone. Over centuries it thickens into mats that have weight, that sag and split to reveal older layers beneath—pale, then darker, then almost black—each stratum a record of a slow digestion. It does not spread in colonies so much as it remembers: it remembers where a wall once stood and hangs heaviest there, as if mourning the shape. In the hollow of the half-buried amphitheater, it pools in the lowest curve like a dry tide, silver turning to ash-grey where the starlight barely reaches.

Then the fungal spires. They are the architecture that refused to leave. Broad-capped, low, they cluster in gardens separated by long, polished avenues—each grove an island of dense, wrong fertility. Their surfaces are not smooth but ridged, like fingerprints pressed into wax and left to cool for eternity. They do not seek sun; they seek the trace breath of the rock, drawing up through fibrous roots that need no water, only the patient chemistry of erosion. Some are old enough to have worn down to stumps themselves, their caps collapsed into layered shelves, and on these shelves the smaller life gathers. They are tables, yes, but also graves and cradles—organic structures that have agreed to grow slowly enough that they become part of the floor.

Between these islands, across the bare stretches where the stone is worn to a dull gleam, moves the fauna—not in swarms, which imply urgency, but in distribution. They are dark, membranous, countless without being crowded. The winged ones are especially wrong: bodies small and dense, wings translucent and enormous, unfolded not for swift flight but for slow, floating drift, like scraps of black silk carried on a current that has no direction. They do not buzz individually; they resonate. Each wing-beat is lazy, spaced by long intervals, yet multiplied across thousands—millions—their collective vibration becomes the deafening roar, a biological thunder that never peaks, never fades, only persists. It fills the vault not as noise but as weather.

The crawlers are glistening, silent in themselves, moving in lines that look like seams in the stone. They travel along the lichen beds, feeding not with biting but with absorption, their bodies leaving faint, silvery trails—salt, perhaps, or waste that is itself mineral—so that the paths they take become slightly brighter over centuries. They breed in the hollows of collapsed towers, laying clusters that take generations to mature, the young emerging not suddenly but gradually, as if the rock itself were giving birth in slow motion. Death is indistinguishable from feeding: they fall into the lichen, become part of its grey depth, and the lichen, in turn, feeds on them, on their mineral bodies, on the trace of whatever they carried.

There is no hierarchy here. The winged ones drop dead into the fungal groves; the crawlers die into the moss that hangs like hair from broken vault-edges; the moss and fungus and lichen all exhale the same cool, dry breath that sustains the insects. The cycle is not rapid but rotational, turning across durations that make human time meaningless. A single lifespan might span a century of languid feeding. A generation of the winged ones might take three centuries to replace itself, not because they are few, but because they are unhurried—agreement with ending is built into their bodies.

They inhabit the old settlement not as conquerors but as its final form. In the circular amphitheater worn into a dish, they nest in the lowest tiers, their collective drone echoing so that the space sounds like a shell held to an ear the size of a valley. Along the shallow troughs that were streets, they distribute themselves with mathematical sparseness—dense where the mineral breath is richest, absent where the rock has become too smooth, too dead. They leave room. They have learned, or evolved, to leave room for silence, for the sound of their own collective voice, for the long bare stretches where nothing moves and nothing needs to.

If you stand still long enough—years, perhaps—the temperature never shifts, the light never changes, and yet you will feel the place altering, not visibly but by accumulation. A lichen mat thickens by a finger’s width. A fungal spire leans, then settles. The drone shifts its pitch imperceptibly, a century-note lower, as older bodies die and newer ones, born in the dark, take their place in the chorus. They are the citizens now. The builders made the shape; these ones kept it, not through maintenance but through occupation—slow, dry, loud, patient.

What lives here is not a species but a consensus: the agreement that life, in the absence of water and haste, can still be abundant, can still fill a valley with sound, can still turn stone into soil without ever breaking the surface tension of the quiet. They are the inheritance—dark, winged, crawling, growing—spread sparse across their vastness, feeding on breath, dying into grey, never stopping, never rushing, never leaving.
