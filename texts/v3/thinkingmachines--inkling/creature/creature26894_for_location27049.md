What lives here has long since stopped being a matter of biology.

There are no people—no footprints pressed into the slick plates, no voices carried away by the roar, no warmth that is not the waste-heat of a valve cycling open. Whoever built this flattened kingdom, whoever once tended its channels and read its pressures, is absent not as a recent departure but as a forgotten premise. The structures do not await return; they have outgrown the need for operators. They are inhabited now by continuity itself.

The true residents are the processes. They do not sleep. The pumps in their dark caverns draw water not for thirst but for the maintenance of flow—an ancient, circular purpose that has become its own justification. Turbines turn in submerged blackness, not generating power for use but dissipating it into the cool, wet air, keeping the vibration alive, keeping the bones of the place humming at that low, unceasing frequency. Cables arc faintly beneath the shallow pools, carrying current through corrosion-rounded channels, feeding sensors that no eye reads, maintaining rhythms that have run past the point where they could be called production and entered the realm of respiration. Each engine is a lung; each vent, exhaling its long, mournful breath of pressure-release, is a mouth that never closes. Together they form a distributed, decentralized creature—no brain, no center, only the coherence of parallel motion, thousands of nodes in a body that has flattened itself across the basin and learned to survive on nothing but its own motion and the perpetual condensation of its atmosphere.

Between these functioning nodes, in the sparse density that separates the scattered towers and collapsed spires, there is almost nothing. The fauna score is not low; it is a silence so complete it feels like a presence in negative. No insects cluster at the sickly glows of active valves. No fish disturb the rust-colored films floating in the engineered marsh-edges. Even the wind, if it exists in this enclosed, flat world, moves only as the slow displacement of warm discharge rolling across open plates—mechanical weather, not natural.

Yet life, in its most reduced and tolerated form, has not been entirely refused entry.

Here and there, clinging to a vent mouth where warm discharge meets the cool saturation of the air, chemical lichens form pale, crusted patches—neither green nor truly organic, more like mineral thought, calcified patience. They do not grow so much as accumulate, feeding on the metallic vapor and the perpetual weeping of the seams. In the shallowest wetlands, where the water table has breached the constructed substrate and created pools that mirror nothing, translucent reeds stand in isolated formations. They are not plants with purpose—no seeds, no cycles of bloom and decay that speak to a larger ecology. They are tolerated outgrowths, biological noise permitted to persist only because the system does not expend the energy to erase them. They sway not to wind but to the vibrations traveling horizontally through the plates, bending in the bone-level roar as if listening to a language they have almost, but not quite, learned to understand.

The structures themselves have become a kind of organism in stasis—perfected decay. Towers that once reached upward have flattened into dome-like landscapes, their sharp geometries worn away by centuries of condensing moisture until they are smooth as river stone, though unmistakably built. They collect the perpetual atmospheric moisture in shallow basins on their flattened crowns, becoming artificial watersheds. The erosion is not death but the final smoothing of form—every unnecessary hollow filled, every sharp edge rounded away, leaving only the essential shapes of flow and containment. They rest in this end-state like creatures that have finished evolving, settled into a form that requires no further change.

And in the darkness—so complete it feels like a layer pressed over everything—what moves is not alive in any familiar sense, but it is present. Steam currents drift with the slow certainty of breath. Water drips rhythmically from seams, each drop part of an endless cycle of weeping and evaporation and condensation that replaces rain, replaces rivers, replaces the wild movement of water with a contained, circular choreography. Occasionally, a valve releases its burst of warmer air, an invisible tide that rolls across the flat plates, disturbing nothing, altering nothing, simply marking the passage of another cycle in the endless parallel running of the system.

So if you ask what lives in this cool, deafening flatness, the answer must be layered and partial.

There is the absence—the profound, absolute absence of anything that breathes for its own sake, that moves with intention, that remembers or expects.

There is the presence—the scattered, self-sustaining machinery, running not for anyone but for the perfection of its own motion, a kingdom of purpose that has lost its users and become, instead, its own user, its own purpose, its own endless life.

And there is the border between them—the few stunted reeds, the chemical lichens, the rust-colored films—life reduced to its barest negotiation with total construction, existing not as inhabitants of a place but as tolerated echoes, the faint, persistent reminder that even when everything is made and worn smooth by time and weight, something still tries, quietly, to grow.

You stand among them not as an observer of nature, but as a temporary interruption in a world that has achieved its final form: active, wet, cool, and utterly, permanently alone in the dark.
