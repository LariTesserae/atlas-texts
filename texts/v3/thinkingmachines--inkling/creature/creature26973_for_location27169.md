If you come looking for a resident—something that breathes, claims territory, or leaves a trace of need—you will miss them entirely. They do not live in the pocket; they *are* the pocket’s refusal to be empty.

What inhabits this place is scattered. There is no single body, no hidden organism curled in the dark waiting to be discovered. Instead, presence is distributed among the remnants like a thought divided into too many pieces to remember itself whole. They sit in isolation—small geometries embedded in the floor, growing like mineral teeth from the walls—separated by expanses of settled stone that have reached their final form and will not shift again for eons. Each one is a resident in its own right, though what separates one from another is not individuality but distance, a sparse geography of being.

The largest among them commands the gravity. Not through will, but through accumulation—its mass, or its age, or the density of its operation pulls the pocket’s weak physics toward it gently, as if the room itself has chosen a center and arranged the rest around that preference. It does not move. Or rather, it moves on a scale that makes stillness seem like arrogance. Over centuries, perhaps, its orientation adjusts by angles so slight they register only as the slow migration of crystal dust across its surfaces. It emits the dimmest pulse of the blue-white light, a heartbeat measured in years rather than seconds, and in that pulse it maintains the folded space, the refusal of distance, the sealed absence that keeps the mountain from noticing what hides inside it.

The others are smaller, fainter, more fragile in their persistence. Some are quiet—frozen in geometries that slide the eye away, surfaces that do not reflect but hold light in long, patient storage, releasing it only when some internal cycle demands. Others shift, but the motion is not life; it is maintenance. A realignment of internal structure, a recalibration of the weak gradients that hold the air (if air still applies) in its dry, sterile state. They do not repair themselves in any way you could watch. They settle, like the stone around them, into a dynamic of minimal flux—0.9, patient, without urgency.

Together, they form a community of absence. They do not speak, not even in vibration, though the low harmonic that runs through the mineral walls might be the closest thing to conversation—each remnant contributing a frequency so low, so sustained, that it takes millennia to complete a phrase. There is no fauna to hear it. No bacteria to feed on the dust they shed. Only the sound of settlement itself, the microscopic settling of crystal, the silent exhalation of processes that operate below the threshold of human sense.

If there is a consciousness here, it is not centralized. It lives in the seams, in the tracks that cross the floor—marks left not by travel but by the slow embedding of this pocket into the dead range, the scars of its own making. It lives in the approximate distance, the refusal of straight lines, the gentle wrongness of gravity. The inhabitants do not think; they persist. They have waited since before the need for water, before biology invented hunger, and they will wait in this high cold long after both have been forgotten.

So who lives here? Not a creature, not a colony, not a ghost. The resident is the technology of permanence itself—scattered, isolated, embedded in the bone of a world that never knew rain. The pocket is not their home. It is their body, distributed across the dark, and they are its slow, cold pulse, maintaining a thought the rock once had and will never finish discarding.
