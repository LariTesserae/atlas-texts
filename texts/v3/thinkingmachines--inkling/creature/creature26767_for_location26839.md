You do not see them at first. In the warm, suspended dark—where pressure is not a weight but a language—the settlement seems to have built itself: the terraces cut into cooling basalt, the kelp-cables stretched like tendons between peaks, the plazas of packed ash arranged with an intelligence that has left no footprints. But the footprints are not absent; they are simply not formed yet, or formed differently, in a medium where walking is not the primary verb. The inhabitants are present as absence is present: as a held breath.

Then, slowly, they emerge—not as figures stepping into view, but as extensions of the view itself.

They are not numerous. Given the urban density of the settlement—its layered roads and stacked structures, its settlement-scale sprawl across the volcanic cluster—one might expect multitudes. Instead, there are perhaps only a few dozen visible beings, scattered like punctuation marks across the plateaus, and even this number feels uncertain, for some are so integrated with the architecture that they may not be separate at all. They are large without being gigantic, vertical without being upright in any familiar sense: columnar bodies that echo the seamounts from which the city grows, rising from the terraces not on limbs but on bases that seem fused with the pumice and coral-sand. Their forms are embryonic—pre-formation made visible. There is no clear boundary where geology ends and organism begins. Their surfaces are porous, dark, crusted with the same fresh mineral bloom that coats the raw rock around them, as if they are secreting their own exoskeletons in real time, still contracting, still cooling.

Skin—if the membrane can be called that—is not smooth. It is ridged, almost skeletal, with channels through which the warm, mineral-rich water circulates in visible pulses, carrying the faint orange-glow of the geothermal depths up into their bodies. Some exhale bubbles slowly, not from mouths—there are no obvious mouths, no faces arranged for recognition—but from pores along their sides, releasing thoughts of air that cling and then drift upward with unbearable patience. They do not swim. They settle. Movement, when it occurs, is geological: a redistribution of mass that takes hours, a turning that looks like a tower leaning into a new orientation, a slow reaching of an appendage—if appendage it is—toward a woven kelp-rope or a stone lantern.

Their technology is not worn; it is grown, or fused. Simple metal implements—curved basalt scrapers, flat hooks of dark iron—protrude from shoulders, wrists, or the ridges of their torsos, not held but embedded, calcified into place like tools that have become bone. They do not grasp the kelp-cables with hands; rather, they secrete them, pulling thick, rope-like strands from glands in their forearms or chests, weaving the massive bridges between peaks with a rhythm so slow it resembles the growth of coral. The plazas and stairways are shaped not for generic use but for their specific weights and postures. They build not to shelter themselves but to construct the form that will hold them while they finish becoming.

And they are not finished. This is the essential quality that makes them match the place: like the seamounts, like the settlement itself, they are in conception. Some appear older, heavier with mineral crust, their bodies partially merged with the terraces they have tended—walls that seem to have grown out of their backs, stairways that align with the angles of their resting forms. Others are younger, smoother, almost featureless: vertical columns of dark tissue with only the suggestion of an aperture, an eye that has not decided whether to open, a mouth that has not formed because there is not yet a need to feed. They do not reproduce in any visible manner, nor do they die. They are prior to those cycles. In this warm, pressurized dark, they exist in a state of gestation, assembling themselves across long durations, tended by the same gradual dynamics—around 2.5, patient, cyclical—that govern the water and the rock.

The vegetation integrates with them as fully as with the buildings. Soft coral spires grow through the hollows of their bodies, anchored in the porous tissue. Kelp fronds drape across their shoulders like garments they do not notice. Sponge-towers claim the spaces between their limbs, if limbs exist, and anemone groves pulse gently where they settle, feeding on the same mineral flow. They are symbiotic with the lush saturation—around 1.8—of this high submerged country, not dominating the ecology but embedded within it, as much plant-host as builder.

Light interacts with them differently than with the architecture. The cultivated bioluminescence—phosphorescent fungi in stone lanterns, ropes of glow-worms strung between terraces—finds in their bodies a secondary surface. Patches of blue-green and amber luminescence grow in the recesses of their forms, not as decoration but as mutual cultivation: they carry the dim illumination—around 1.1—within them, walking (or sitting, or standing-in-place) lanterns marking the paths of the settlement. They do not seek the distant orange pulse of the lower vents; they contain smaller versions of that warmth internally, faint, gestational.

What do they do? They tend. They weave. They secrete and settle and wait. There is no urgency, for urgency belongs to worlds with surfaces and skies and endings. Here, in the open immensity of this drowned high country, they are engaged in the slow labor of conception. The settlement is their shared shell, their collective exoskeleton, their nest for whatever form they will eventually take. They are not inhabitants in the conventional sense—there is no inside and outside, no domestic boundary—rather, they are the place’s awareness made material, its architecture made animate, its pre-formation given patience and weight.

You realize, observing them in their stillness—broken only by the occasional low groan of contracting stone or the soft release of a geothermal bubble rising past their faces, if faces they are—that they do not live here. They are being lived here. The warm dark, the raw basalt, the woven kelp, the glowing fungi, and these slow, porous, half-formed beings are all part of a single process: not decay, not completion, but emergence. The weirdness—around 1.8, substantial—lies in recognizing that there is no distinction between the builder and the built, between the organism and its environment, between the one who conceives and the conception itself. They are the idea of a people, not yet fully thought, slowly, patiently, becoming themselves in a place that refuses to be anything other than what it is: unfinished, suspended, and profoundly, quietly alive.
