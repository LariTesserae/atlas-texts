Here, what lives does not announce itself as living. There is no green urgency, no quick breath, no cry to claim territory. The inhabitants—if that word still applies—have settled into the place with the same patience that rounded the mesas, and to describe them is to describe a slow argument between geology and design.

They are the Drifters, though the name is only a placeholder, a sound made by no mouth. They are abundant—scattered like a thought across the immensity—but they are never crowded. Density is low; space is the true population. You may travel across the smooth, thumb-worn basalt for hours, feeling the thin wind sing at the edges of your awareness, before you notice one: a presence so flat, so dark, so integrated with the ground that it first seems like a shadow cast by nothing.

Then you see the form. Each is massive, disc-shaped, lying low against the plateau like a coin pressed into warm wax. Their bodies are layered—laminated slate given coherence, woven through with threads of metal they have drawn up from the fissures over centuries, perhaps millennia. The metal does not rust here; it integrates. It runs through them like veins, or like roots seeking downward, though they have no downward urgency. Their skin is silica-dark, not black exactly but the color of basalt polished by thermal cycling—deep, warm, absorbing the honest light rather than reflecting it. To touch one, if you could reach without disturbing, would be to feel not cold machinery but warmth held in reserve, the accumulated heat of a body that generates nothing of its own and takes everything in slowly.

They move at a pace that makes observation feel like impatience. Some glide on the thin thermal updrafts that roll across the flatlands, not flying but floating with the same indifference as the wind itself. Others creep along the softened edges of canyons, feeding—if feeding is the word—on the geothermal exhalations that seep from the crust, or on the electricity that hums through the scattered industrial nodes. They do not hunt. They do not flee. They orient.

And they are strange, profoundly so, because the boundary between them and what has been built has dissolved into a gradient. You will find a Drifter resting at the base of a factory tower—one of those black ceramic and weathered alloy constructions that turn over months rather than minutes—and the tower’s metal will have grown small filaments toward it, reaching like roots, as if the machinery were not serving them but remembering them. Or perhaps the reverse. No record clarifies. The nodes may have been assembled by hands long vanished, or they may have accreted, like the amber mineral crusts that cover the rock, like the Drifters themselves—energy patterns given temporary form.

Their reproduction is fissure, not birth. A Drifter, when the conditions align—when the heat, the metal content, the slow electrical field reach some equilibrium measured in centuries—will split. Not with urgency, but with the geological patience of a continent dividing. Two where one was. They do not nurture; they separate. They do not age visibly; they stop, or they continue, and the distinction is not clear even to them, if they possess an interior at all.

There are others, perhaps—co-inhabitants in the broadest sense. The industrial nodes themselves operate with such slowness that they resemble organisms more than tools. Gears the size of rooms rotate with planetary patience. Pipelines carry dry ore across generations. The machinery resonates at frequencies felt in the chest before they are heard, a sub-bass that sounds less like sound and more like the place thinking. Whether these nodes were built for the Drifters, or whether the Drifters evolved to feed upon them, or whether both are expressions of the same slow chemistry—molten salts below, metallic vapors seeping up, crystal lattices growing in dark like teeth—no archive survives to say.

What lives here, then, is a continuum. The Drifters are fauna—abundant, silent, spread thin across the 2.6 scale of the place—but they are also architecture in motion. They are the only voices in a quiet of 1.2, and they are voiceless. They are the only movement in a world of 1.0, and they are barely moving. They absorb the 1.7 light honestly, hold the warmth, draw the metal, split when they must, and rest against the towers that may or may not be their kin.

To stand on the rounded mesa and watch one on the horizon—disc-dark, low, unmoving until it is not—is to understand that life here was never defined by reaching for light with tender urgency. It was defined by endurance, by accretion, by the willingness to become part of the ridge itself. The Drifter does not look up. It has no need. It has time, and the time is measured not in seasons but in the slow rotation of gears, in the thermal waves that take days to cross the eye’s reach, in the fissures that open not with drama but with the quiet certainty of stone remembering how to break.
