They are not guests here; they are the continuity.

The first citizens are the plants, but to call them vegetation is to mistake the builder for the ornament. They arrived not after the settlement collapsed but *through* it—roots threading the wooden pegs before the sap had even dried, fungal mat sealing the stone channels before the first winter frost. Now they are the walls breathing, the floors sponging, the ceiling weeping green. The moss does not cover the hand-laid stones; it *completes* them, filling the gaps with a cold, living mortar that expands slowly, patiently, turning every constructed line into tissue. Thick pale vines hang in columns sparse enough to walk between—there is intention in their spacing, as if they remember the streets they replaced—glowing with a faint, cold phosphorescence that makes the black-green gloom readable, like a map drawn in luminous vein. They form arches where doorways were meant, bridge platforms where floors once spanned, and in the spaces they leave open—those wide pockets of empty air—the other lives pass through.

The insects are everywhere and nowhere singular. They gather in dense, unmoving mats along the wet timbers, their translucent wings folded over bodies so slight they seem made of condensation. They do not buzz. They do not swarm in the air with chaos; they adhere, layered like scale-mail over the root-corridors, clustering in the hollow knots that serve as dwellings. In the faint green light, they appear as a single shimmering skin applied to every constructed surface, yet they move—slowly, collectively—when disturbed, a ripple of pearl-colored wings lifting and resettling without sound. They feed on the fungal growth, on the spores that drift in the wet air, and they are prey.

The amphibians own the flat stones. They cover them in layers, cold-blooded and slow, their bodies pressed close for warmth that does not come, breathing with visible, patient rhythm in the darkness. Their eyes remain open—round, dark, unblinking—reflecting the faint bioluminescence back into the space like scattered coins. There are small ones, no larger than a thumb, nesting in the moss between stones; larger ones, palm-sized, with skin like wet slate, that claim the central channels where water pools shallowly. They do not croak. The cold has taken their voices, or perhaps they never needed them in a place where sound travels poorly through moss and falling water. They wait, and sometimes they eat the insects—slow strikes, tongues like pale threads, then stillness again, layered bodies closing over the gap.

But it is the small mammals that move with purpose. Furry, slow, adapted to cold, they thread through the sparse gaps between the vine-pillars like citizens traveling root-corridors. They are not large—none larger than a clenched hand—covered in thick dull fur that holds the mist close to their skin. They feed on fungal growth, on dead leaf-matter that falls from the vine-ceilings, on the softer tissues of things that have fallen still. They nest in the layered timber, in the hollow knots, in the warm pockets of decay where stone meets root and heat barely accumulates. They breed there, continuously it seems—tiny shapes, hairless and blind, hatching in moss-lined hollows, growing slowly into the silence. They are predator and prey both: the larger ones hunt the smaller, or perhaps they do not—perhaps they share the fungal harvest, and the violence is elsewhere, played out in movements too slow and too quiet to witness except by absence, by the sudden stillness in one corridor and the renewed feeding in another.

The integration is what makes them strange. They do not avoid the primitive grid; they navigate it. The flat stones are their basking places, though there is no sun to bask in—only the faint warmth of decay, the slight temperature of pooled water against stone. The carved channels guide them, not as traps but as paths, directing the flow of bodies through the compressed settlement. They use the hand-built platforms as territory, marking them with scent too subtle for human noses, leaving traces in the moss that read as paths to others of their kind. Even the young erosion serves them—roots splitting stone create new hollows for nesting, water finding new paths creates new pools for the amphibians, the shifting ceiling drops fragments of timber that become shelter.

There is constant motion, yet it is a motion submerged in cold and absorption. Eggs crack open in the moss with no sound. Larvae consume dead matter in darkness. A predator—perhaps one of the larger amphibians, perhaps a mammal turned desperate—makes its chase through the root-corridors in absolute silence, a slow-motion pursuit that lasts minutes, ending not in scream but in a soft shift of weight, a brief struggle muted by moss and vine, then feeding.

You do not hear them. You feel them—the collective exhalation of a thousand silent lungs, the brush of translucent wings against your face in the dark, the soft shift of weight on stone nearby, the knowledge that in every hollow knot, in every gap between vine-column and moss-wall, something is breathing with you, slowly, coldly, endlessly. They are the settlement now. The hand-built logic of stone and timber has been absorbed into their lives, and they, in turn, hold its shape—growing through it, breeding within it, dying into the fungal mats that will feed the next generation. The place is not inhabited by them so much as *continued* by them—sharp-featured, fresh, never settled, alive in every direction, wet and green and quietly, overwhelmingly full.
