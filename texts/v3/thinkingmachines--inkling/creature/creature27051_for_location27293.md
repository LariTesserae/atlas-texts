They do not stand. They have never stood. In a world that finished its verticality long before memory began, life learned only to spread, to cling, to dissolve.

The constant residents are the black mats—fungal, patient, spread like forgotten writing across the compressed plane. They cling to the microscopic splits in the bone-smooth substrate, absorbing the warm upward weeping without aspiration, without growth that reaches. They are the baseline presence, the flat answer to a flat question. But they are not the story.

The story is the others.

In the thickest pockets of vapor—where the air churns in massive, directionless cells and the constant drizzle falls not from above but *from* the air itself—the turbulence briefly forgets its own chaos. It gathers. It holds. For a span of time that might be brief or ancient—time here is unreliable, uneven, deeper in some patches—there is a shape.

Not a creature. A coagulation.

They are made entirely of what the world has left: compressed stone-flour suspended in warm, shifting water; air dense enough to touch; the microscopic fractures of the bedrock releasing their exhalation just long enough to be caught. They form low, always low, never rising more than the thickness of a finger from the surface. They slide through the shifting, skin-deep pools with a motion that is not swimming—there is no up or down to swim toward—just a drifting with purpose in the turbulent flow.

Some are wide and flat, like living stains in the gray-green haze, spreading until they are darker than the dark ground beneath them. They absorb the dim light, becoming temporary absences of color, and move slowly, leaving no wake because the water closes behind them instantly. The plane is too full, too saturated, to hold a trail. Everything is present at once; nothing is permitted to leave evidence.

Others are more gathered—knots, almost spherical but pressed flat by the weight of the atmosphere, rolling or being driven across the plane by invisible tides of air. Through their translucent bodies, one can see—not clearly, never clearly—swirling patterns of darker matter: the ancient mountain dust finally in motion after eons of absolute compression. They do not see. There is no seeing here, no orientation toward a horizon that means anything. Instead, the turbulence itself is their nervous system. Every crack in the substrate, every shift in the churning air, every sudden weep of moisture from the stone, is felt throughout their being, which is mostly water held together by pressure and temporary agreement.

They make no sound of their own. The world roars for them—the scraping air, the cracking bedrock, the high hiss of forced water—and they exist within that roar as silent gatherings, brief islands of form in an ocean of chaos.

And then they are not.

The dissolution is not death—there is no death in a place with no empty space, no absence for absence to occupy. Instead, they simply cease being gathered. The turbulence continues, indifferent and perpetual, but the pattern breaks. They spread back into the shallow sheets, back into the warm vapor, back into the compressed dust that waits everywhere. The matter remains—everything remains, nothing is lost—but the shape is gone, dissolved into the saturation from which it came. In some other pocket of turbulence, miles away in a distance that may not be honest, or in a time that might be older or freshly born, the same conditions might gather again. Not the same being—there are no individuals here, only occurrences—but the same kind of presence, briefly held, briefly visible.

There are rarer forms still.

In the fractures—where the dark, polished substrate weeps upward—there are shimmers. Not plants, not creatures, but presences that form in the upward flow itself. They are thin, vertical in defiance of the world’s flatness, though never more than a hand’s height above the plane. They are made of crystallized pressure, of water forced through stone so dense it has become glass-like, catching the gray-green haze in densities rather than colors. They do not move. They wait, feeding on the dissolution of other coagulations that pass too close, that spread too thin, that feed the shimmer with their ending. They are patient in a different register from the fungal mats: the mats are horizontal patience, a flat endurance. These are vertical patience, the only exception that proves the world’s rule.

And in the pockets where time is uneven—where the ancientness is deeper, where erosion moves slowly or differently—there are residues.

Not ghosts. There is no room for ghosts in a fully occupied world, no emptiness for spirits to inhabit. Instead, there are shapes that formed in older turbulences, in pressures that no longer exist, arranged according to rules the flat world has forgotten. They hold forms that should be impossible: angles, suggestions of height, outlines that seem to remember being mountains, being raised, being something other than this compressed settlement. They are faint, like the accidental marks scattered across the plane, but they are present—wet, made of the same turbulent matter, yet holding patterns that resist the current chaos. They do not respond to the world around them. They are too old, trapped in temporal pockets where change is slower, where dissolution takes longer, where the roar of the present is only a distant, muffled pressure. They are fossils that have not finished fossilizing, still present, still made of weeping water and compressed history, arranged into shapes that ask questions no one can ask.

The faint marks—the low circular walls, the pushed-up discs, the occasional structures that resemble intention without being built—sometimes gather life near them. Not because they offer shelter—there is no shelter from the saturation, from the roar, from the full occupancy—but because they alter the turbulence slightly. A wall breaks the air flow differently. A disc changes the pressure of the upward weeping. The coagulations, drawn not by understanding but by difference, sometimes linger there. They settle near these accidental monuments, not knowing them, not using them, but recognizing in the shift of pressure something like conversation. A response in a world of constant, meaningless churning.

So who lives here?

The black mats, spread like thought across an endless plane.

The coagulations—temporary, gathering and dissolving, made of the world’s own turmoil, sliding through waters that never deepen, never clear, always warm and full.

The fracture-shimmers—thin, vertical, patient, feeding on the ending of others.

The temporal residues—older than flatness, holding impossible shapes in pockets of slow time, wet memories of height.

And always, in the spaces between them—though there are never truly spaces—the presence of absence. The faint marks suggest builders, but there are none. The coagulations hold form like consciousness, but they do not think. The world is full—absolutely, deafeningly full—of presence without purpose, of life without aspiration, of movement without destination.

They share the plane not as community, but as saturation. They do not compete—there is nothing to compete for, only presence to share. They do not evolve—there is nowhere to go, no upward path, only the same turbulence rearranging its temporary shapes across a surface that will never change its essential form.

They are the inhabitants of the end-form. Not survivors. Not conquerors. Not even, strictly speaking, alive in the way life remembers being alive. They are the world holding its own chaos in shapes that can be perceived, briefly, before letting them dissolve back into the flatness, into the weeping stone, into the perpetual roar. They exist because the turbulence allows it, and they end because the turbulence requires it. In between, they are the only answer the flat world gives to the question of who lives here: *this, now, briefly, here.*
