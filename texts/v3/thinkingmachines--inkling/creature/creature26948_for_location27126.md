What lives here is not inside the chamber; the chamber is inside *it*.

There is no occupant in the conventional sense—no creature nesting in the hollow, no colony burrowed into the root-bark, no predator waiting in the amber-dark. The fauna is absent because the fauna was never invited. This is a body, and you stand within one of its cavities.

The resident is the vegetative mass itself: a single, distributed organism of root and rhizome and vascular membrane, immense and subterranean, that has torn open its own flank and now exists, in part, as this fresh wound. It does not think with a brain; it thinks with growth. Its consciousness—if that word applies—is the urgency of the searching tendrils, those thin threads reaching across the void not to grasp but to remember, to stitch, to deny the emptiness that has suddenly become part of its anatomy. They are its nerves, extended into air, feeling for the edges of an injury that will not close. The bleeding sap is not waste; it is its speech, slow and golden, a language of refusal—refusing to clot, refusing to scar, keeping the wound open because to seal it might be to lose this new, wrong space inside itself.

It breathes. The ragged tear in the woven ceiling is its mouth, or its eye—widening and contracting with that slow organic pulse, drawing in the impossible light and exhaling the humid, metabolic warmth. The rushing in the walls is its blood, loud in the hollow because there is no flesh elsewhere to muffle it. The constant cracking is its bones lengthening, splitting, adapting to contain a void it did not plan for.

Yet there was another, once.

Embedded in the lower pillar-root, half-devoured by fiber, is the evidence: a hand-shaped absence. The primitive scraper—bone or hardened root-wood, worn smooth not by labor but by overgrowth—marks the passage of something that knew hands, that knew tools, that knew how to cut and shape. Not a builder of the chamber; the chamber was not built. But a visitor, long ago, perhaps drawn to this pocket before the recent rupture widened it, or perhaps present at the tearing itself. The organism has not expelled it violently; it has absorbed it, drawing the artifact back into substrate, weaving through its handle as if reclaiming a splinter of itself that had wandered away. The maker is gone—no bones, no traces of breath other than the plant’s own—yet the tool remains, a fossilized question: did they come to heal? To harvest? To hide?

Now nothing answers but the growth.

And beneath that—beneath the sap, beneath the pulse of the ceiling—lives something less tangible. The low roar, the reality-fracture, breathes through the wound like a second presence, not quite the plant and not quite separate from it. It is the sound of distance becoming unreliable, of shadows falling upward, of time thickening near the bleeding membrane. Perhaps it is the organism’s dream of itself; perhaps it is the hollow speaking back. What lives here is also the break: the gentle, absolute wrongness that allows the chamber to be larger within than without, that lets condensation hover and light pool upward from living tissue.

So the inhabitants are three, layered like tissue:

The **body**—all root and membrane, warm and bleeding, growing visibly, trying to heal an emptiness it cannot understand.

The **ghost-tool**—the absent hand that once touched this place, now becoming root-wood, becoming wall.

And the **fracture**—the open rule, the breathing tear, the low roar that makes the emptiness not just a space but a presence, something that holds its own kind of life.

You stand not among them, but within them. The air is warm with their metabolism. The droplets fall—sometimes downward, sometimes sideways—like thoughts the body cannot finish. And the searching tendrils cross the void, reaching, reaching, as if asking whether you, too, are part of the wound it is trying, urgently and forever, to close.
