What lives here begins with the rock itself—not as dead container, but as an infant body still deciding its shape. The chamber is its first breath, half-cooled slag and settling sediment weeping mineral sweat so constant it has become a language. This earth-infant does not sleep; it cools by imperceptible degrees, shifts by millimeters across decades, offering up its heat not as resource but as exhalation. To live here is first to be hosted by something that is not yet finished being born.

Above the settlement—if that scattered archipelago of grating and pump-house can be called a settlement—the ceiling is not stone but a living membrane of bats. Thousands upon thousands hang in dense, rustling clusters, their bodies layered like dark fruit against the embryonic vault. They are the true sky-citizens, owning the vertical dark. Their presence is not silent; it is a continuous, soft thunder of shifting wings, occasional unfurlings that ripple through the colony in waves. They ask nothing of the heat, the machinery, the sparse human footprints below. They simply hang, digest, wait, turning the upper air into a breathing fabric that occasionally drops guano and warmth like slow rain.

Where the weeping walls meet the pools—shallow, constantly replenished, near-boiling—the water lives in crushing, translucent abundance. Blind, pale fish drift with sluggish patience, bodies like living candles, moving not with purpose but with the slow inevitability of thermal currents. Among them, crustaceans—translucent, fragile-shelled, heat-adapted—crawl over each other in layers, feeding on mineral sediment, breeding in cycles measured not in days but in long, gradual tides. The pools are thick with them; to disturb the surface is to reveal a writhing, pale mass that parts slowly and closes just as slowly behind the intrusion. There is no panic here, only density.

But it is on the metal walkways, the catwalks, the pipes and boots, that life becomes overwhelming in its proximity. The surfaces are carpeted—literally carpeted—in black, glossy beetles, heat-loving, unhurried, crawling with a determination that suggests eternity is their only schedule. They move over everything: the corrugated shelters, the ventilation ducts, the slow-turning turbines, the boots of anyone who walks these islands. They are not pests; they are the surface, the skin of this place. To stand on the grating is to stand on a living layer that skitters and reshuffles with every footstep, indifferent to the machinery, indifferent to breath.

And yes, there are the others—the sparse ones arranged in islands. Humans, or those like them, live in the gaps. They wear boots that become temporary landscapes for beetles. They maintain the central engine house, listen to its slow, heavy heartbeat, tend the geothermal pumps that extract warmth at the earth’s own patient rate. There are few—perhaps a dozen, perhaps two—scattered across the footprint like a hamlet lost on an impossible plain. They live in corrugated shelters coated slowly in mineral scale, moving slowly, matching the tempo of the rock and the insects. They do not dominate; they maintain. They are temporary fixtures in a place that will outlast them, already being incorporated, slowly, in the mineral film of this birth.

The air binds them all together: saturated, iron-tasting, vibrating with the hum of machinery in the teeth and the biological noise of wing and leg. Nothing here changes quickly. The bats grow and shrink across seasons of heat. The fish breed in tides that take months to crest. The beetles cover and recover. Even the humans age slowly, coated in the same mineral sweat that feeds the pools.

This is the settlement: not human, not animal, not geological, but all of these layered in patient, overwhelming presence. A small urban pocket of extraction and shelter suspended inside a body that is still being born, where life does not ask to be noticed—only to continue, thick, warm, and relentless, in the dark below everything.
