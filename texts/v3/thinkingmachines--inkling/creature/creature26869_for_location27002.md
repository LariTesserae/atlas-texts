What lives here does not move quickly, and very little of it is alive in the ways that usually claim attention. The valley is inhabited first by the floor itself—not ground, but a single, continuous body. The root-mat stretches for miles, a cold-adapted organism of rhizome and compressed moss so thick it bears weight like flesh over bone. It breathes slowly, if breath is the right word: it absorbs the constant condensation, draws mineral sediment from the black streams that thread through its channels, and grows downward and outward across the young rock with the patience of something that measures time in centuries. Underfoot it is spongy, resistant, alive in every compressed fiber. This is the primary citizen. Everything else lives in relation to it.

Rising from this substrate are the vertical ones. The pillar-ferns stand solitary, taller than houses, their fronds long since frozen into stiffness, not dead but suspended in a state of extreme slow motion—metabolism turned so low it resembles geology. Between them, vine-towers have woven themselves into lattices, cold-lichen draped across them in sheets that hang like industrial tarps, heavy with moisture, filtering the weak yellow-green light into something dimmer still. These are not a forest in any social sense; they are cathedrals without congregations, separated by hundreds of meters of dripping emptiness. They do not communicate. They simply occupy, massive and alone, holding space in the darkness with their root-pillars sunk deep into the mat below.

Then there is the other population—the ones that run.

The machinery is not dead artifact scattered like debris. It is embedded, functioning, breathing cold vapor through smokeless stacks into the damp air. Ventilation compressors, water pumps, slow-turning gears—these are the second inhabitants, mechanized and scaled, half-buried in root-housings or penetrating the plant-substrate with pipes that have become fused with the growth around them. They never stop. Their drone is physical, a continuous low-frequency roar that fills the chest and drowns out the possibility of silence. They wear their bearings not in years but in decades, and they run now without reason, without operator, producing the thunder that defines the air. Engines sit in half-buried housings, metal cold to the touch, running. Conveyor remnants lie half-swallowed by frozen moss, still turning or attempting to, their belts carrying nothing but sediment and time. They are not separate from the vegetation; pipes penetrate root-walls, and the root-walls have grown around the pipes, claiming them as structure. The plants and the machines have become co-architects of this basin.

What moves between them is almost nothing. Fauna is nearly absent—perhaps a few cold-tolerant invertebrates in the shallow streams, bodies so small and slow they seem more like extensions of the water’s own movement. The ecosystem requires no animal mediation. The plants do not need to be eaten; they accumulate. The machines do not need to be tended; they persist. The water waits, seeps, condenses, falls in slow irregular rain from the broken ceiling of raw limestone and basalt, then departs through channels still finding their paths through the young substrate. Thin ice skins the pools and cracks and reforms in long cycles. Everything is balanced between the total living mass and the scattered, running engines.

Even the light belongs to them. Old electrified fixtures hang from cable remnants or stand embedded in the root-bed, casting weak cones that illuminate almost nothing—only marking positions: a pump station here, a ventilation shaft there, a rusted rail leading nowhere. They are like bioluminescent organs in a deep-sea creature, not illuminating for vision but for orientation, reminding the darkness that these structures exist.

So to live here is to be one of these things, or to exist in the narrow spaces between them. You live if you are root, dense and spongy, holding the valley together. You live if you are pillar-fern, frozen in vertical solitude. You live if you are gear or compressor, turning endlessly in cold darkness, producing sound as your form of presence. You live if you are water, moving without urgency through black streams, carrying fresh mineral sediment from the broken ceiling above. You live if you are ice, thickening over months, then cracking, then reforming.

There are no strangers here, and no visitors who stay. The rules are gradual: shift imperceptibly, wear slowly, grow into the machinery or be swallowed by moss. The place extends past sight, regional, open—a valley of root and rust and falling water, held below a surface that failed recently, geologically young, its sharp teeth unsoftened. In the deafening dark, everything that remains is simply continuing: the plants in their massive, solitary architecture, the engines in their relentless, forgotten labor, and the cold, damp air pressing between them, thick with moisture and noise, never fully resolving into silence.
