What lives there does not live alone.

Begin with the moss—though it is not one moss but a sovereignty of them, dark green and silver velvet swallowing every vertical plane, every shoulder of fresh limestone, every arch of the old shell-cities. They do not grow; they occupy. They seal the fractures in the rock with bodies so dense you could press your palm into a wall and withdraw it wet, carrying spores, carrying the cold. Beneath them, in the perpetual shade they manufacture, ferns unfurl in slow agreement, and the aquatic willows have woven themselves into something like governance: root-lattices thick as rope, interlocking into corridors, into partitions, into the very grammar of streets through which black water moves undisturbed. The reeds stand in breeding masses of their own—not silent, exactly, but holding a silence so complete it functions as voice. They are residents of the highest order, architects of the damp, and there is no surface they have not claimed.

But the valley is older in its bones than its green skin suggests. The true founders are the shell-builders—colonies of freshwater calcifiers, generations compressed into calcium, raising towers and arched bridges across the flat before the willows arrived to colonize them. These are not ruins. They are fused now, overgrown, compressed with peat and root, become the substructure of raised walkways that no foot requests. They secrete still, slowly, in the cold black channels—adding layer to layer in a city that never issues permits, only growth. Fungal shelves protrude like balconies from the rock, supporting gardens of moss and lichen that hang in frozen tapestries, dripping with condensed air. Everything is multi-level, accidental, coherent: plazas of shallow clear pools ringed by reeds; streets of open water; the fallen logs trapped and rotting so gradually they have become beams, supporting new colonization in their decay. The inhabitants include the dead that have not finished becoming structure.

Then there are the ones that move.

Amphibians crowd the shallows in breeding masses—skin slick with the same cold that seeps from the limestone, bodies pressed together in the root-corridors, silent except for the occasional ripple that betrays a shift in weight. They do not call; the cold dampens sound, and they have learned the hush. Above them, in the translucent green ceiling of vegetation, birds nest—small, quick, threading through reed-walls that serve as both wall and avenue. But the water holds its own citizens: small fish in dark schools, sliding through channels like thoughts through a dense mind, and insects—cold-water clouds that drift barely audible, wings like frost in motion. And through the lattices, slender predators—quick, patient—hunt in the root-corridors, bodies made for pushing through absolute density, leaving only the near-inaudible press of movement, the faint disturbance of packed presence.

Even the water is not background but inhabitant. It stands at ankle-deep, yes, but it also seeps, weeps, condenses, moves through root-channels with a faint press of its own intention. It tastes on the teeth of anyone who breathes there. It is the first law and the constant resident, shaping the temperature, carrying the cold, feeding every layer.

There is no emptiness to host a stranger. To enter is to become part of the saturation—to push through a silent, cold, living settlement where moss, reed, shell, amphibian, fish, bird, predator, fungus, and falling log are not separate lives but a single dense occupancy. The sharp white shoulders of the valley remind you it is fresh, recently revealed, perhaps by ice retreat or sudden collapse; there has been no time for softening, only for this complete, relentless colonization. Life rushed in with such totality that the void disappeared, replaced by process, by presence, by a city that breathes through roots and secretes its own walls, operating in perpetual, packed, wet silence.
