They do not crowd the islands; they inhabit them the way moss inhabits stone—present, integrated, never dense enough to dominate the green silence between. You see them rarely, and when you do, they are usually in transit: crossing a suspended lattice between tower-complexes, descending through a downward-opening shaft into the young earth, or standing motionless on a platform edge where the vegetation has been permitted to claim the railing entirely. They are not numerous. The settlement’s scale is regional, but its population is scattered like seeds in a windless room, each one responsible for an enormous radius of quiet space.

Their bodies, when visible, share the valley’s muted luminosity. In the bluish or amber glow of the structures, skin—if it is skin—appears smooth, slightly iridescent, holding moisture the way the leaves do. They are tall, or perhaps elongated by posture and the strange proportions of their garments, which are not worn so much as grown around them: woven membranes of the same self-repairing substance that coats the buildings, draping, tightening, or opening with adjustments so minute they seem like breathing. Some have faces that are almost familiar—two eyes, the architecture of a mouth—yet arranged with a precision that feels calculated for aesthetics other than human comfort. Others are obscured entirely, wrapped in layered textiles that shimmer faintly, moving with a fluidity that suggests bones arranged differently, or none at all in the places where human anatomy expects them.

They move slowly. Not sluggish—there is intention in every step—but with the pace of water finding its level. On the suspended walkways, they do not hurry. They pause often, not from fatigue, but to observe: a fern unfolding, a moisture collector adjusting its geometry, the distant silhouette of another island softening in mist. Their stillness is active. They are listening to something the valley offers that louder worlds have lost.

Most live in the vertical spaces—high in the ring-districts, in chambers that open downward into the fractures, in suspended pods that relieve their own weight and hang with the softness of breath held. These spaces are not decorated; they are configured. A dwelling might be a single curved wall that has grown a seat, a sleeping hollow, and a conduit of glowing channel-water, all from the same substrate as the valley’s young stone. They sleep—if sleep is the word—submerged in warm, damp darkness, wrapped in the bioluminescent moss that they cultivate on interior surfaces. The boundary between inside and outside is porous. Plants thread through their living spaces, and they thread through the plants, pruning not with tools but with touch, guiding growth along channels that feed both root and resident.

There are others who are less visible, perhaps not visible at all except in effect. In the downward extensions, where the architecture sinks into the unstable crust, there are presences that maintain the equilibrium—adjusting the millimetric shifts, guiding the nanoscale smoothing of stone, ensuring that the sharp young fractures do not claim the structures that relieve their own weight. These ones may not have bodies in any conventional sense. They are felt in the sudden correction of a platform’s tilt, in the precise timing of the rain-collection cycle, in the way a path clears itself of fallen vine only to let another grow in a more beautiful arrangement. They are the settlement’s patience made manifest, working in the spaces between dimensions that should not fit within the walls.

The fauna—those rare flickers of too many legs or too smooth hide—are not feared. They are tended. In the canopy tunnels between districts, there are small shrines or stations, geometric and silent, where offerings of moisture or light are arranged for the creatures that avoid the brightest glows. Some of the visible inhabitants wear symbiotic layers: living cloaks of silver moss, or collars of slow-moving invertebrates that clean the air around them. The relationship is collaborative, not possessive. They do not dominate the valley’s sparse animal life; they make space for it, ensuring that even in this strange urban openness, creaturehood survives in its hidden patterns.

Children—if the small ones are children—move differently. They are faster, closer to the plants, often found in the green emptiness between islands rather than on the platforms. They play not with objects but with growth: planting spores in specific fractures, guiding vines to form temporary arches, sitting very still in the fog until the bioluminescence responds and brightens around them. They memorize paths that have no names, navigating by the temperature of the air, by the sound of distant structural breathing, by the taste of moisture in particular hollows.

And then there are the ones who remember, or choose not to. The valley had not existed long enough to earn a name from anyone who still remembered the world before its mouth opened. This implies that some among them do remember. They are the oldest, not in appearance—age does not mark their smooth faces clearly—but in the way they stand at the edges of districts, looking across the meadow of unchecked growth toward another island a mile away. They do not speak often. When they do, their voices are low, matching the distant hum of the structures, and they speak in terms of adjustment rather than history. They do not need names for the valley. They call it by its temperature, its moisture cycle, its particular sharpness of edge. They have settled here not to conquer the young land but to learn its patience, to let its strangeness become their own.

Together, they form not a society in the crowded sense, but an ecology of presence. The settlement breathes with them: expanding by millimeters, collecting moisture, distributing it in glowing channels, repairing its own surfaces in silence. They are part of that repair. They wear the architecture, live within its downward extensions, guide its relationship with the aggressive contentment of the green. They are sparse, yes—scattered across the islands like thoughts in a vast, quiet mind—but they are deeply embedded. They have made the wrongness beautiful not by fixing it, but by becoming strange with it, moving slowly through their active, submerged becoming, leaving the sharp edges untouched, the spaces between filled only with breath, vine, and the soft percussion of rain on something that has learned to hold its shape without breaking.
