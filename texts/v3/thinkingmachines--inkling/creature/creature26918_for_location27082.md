To live here is to be digested by the place, not merely placed upon it.

The most visible are the **shell-beings**—though “shell” is too defensive a word. They grow in concentric plates of translucent mineral fused with something like chitin, each layer recording not years but cycles of crystal growth and shatter. Between the plates, a soft light pulses: blue-white, phosphorescent, matching the rhythm of the grinding stones far below. They do not walk. They are continuously reassembled. A limb detaches, drifts through the damp air, and reconnects elsewhere at a different angle, as if testing which configuration suits the local turbulence. Often they exist in two positions at once—one body pressing its face to a crystal factory, another already threading through the moss—until an unobservable threshold collapses them into one form with a faint crackle of static. Their mouths are not mouths but refined lattices; they feed by pressing against the crystal production, extracting electricity and mineral memory. They are silent not by choice, but because sound has never been necessary; they read the world through pressure in the held-breath air and the electric hum threading through their plates.

Then there are the ones who forgot they were ever separate from the road.

These **conveyor-symbiotes** do not ride the sliding stone belts—they are spread across them like second skins. Flat, membranous, dark as unoxidized basalt, they extend tendrils into both the rock and the moss that claws at the edges. As the plates grind and move, carrying sediment and seed, the symbiote moves too, but parts remain behind—fragments that root into cracks and become new growth—while the central mass continues, distributed across miles of sliding geology. They feed not by eating but by absorbing the friction-heat where rock meets rock, storing it in their membranes and radiating it back into the warm air. To watch them is to see a creature that has become landscape, passenger and vehicle collapsed into one continuous expression.

Between these, filling every thinner layer of presence, are the **abundant small ones**. Some cling to the downward-growing roots, drinking the silver beads of condensation that never quite become rain. Others inhabit the brief gleaming channels, swimming not through water but through the *possibility* of water—existing in superposition with the stream until turbulence forces the channel to vanish or persist, at which point they either dissolve into mist or solidify into brief, bright shapes that flicker away.

There are also the **filament-creatures**, barely distinguishable from the electrical veins themselves. They thread through root and wire alike—thin as excited light, luminous—feeding on the static that crackles hidden in the moss. They have no stable bodies, only paths of energy that occasionally knot into temporary nodes to reproduce or observe. They are the pulse made visible, biological outputs of the same force that splits the ridges.

What unites them—if union applies to beings that share bodies across distance or exist in overlapping states—is their absolute adaptation to instability. They do not startle when reality bends. A shadow may fall upward through one; an afternoon may skip or linger for an hour; and they simply continue—reconfiguring, feeding, distributing. Their biology is as metabolic as the land: they digest crystal, exhale warmth, reproduce by leaving fragments in fresh fractures.

There are no predators here in any familiar sense, only competition—silent, damp—for access to mineral flow, conveyor heat, electrical connection. One being adjusts its shell-plates to block another’s lattice-feeding; a symbiote extends its membrane to claim a sliding plate. It is all reconfiguration, never noise.

If you observe long enough—though observation forces them into single positions—you might realize there is no boundary between the fauna and the machinery. The shell-beings refine what the factories produce. The symbiotes distribute the seeds that become the vegetation that holds the moisture that feeds the drinkers. The filaments are the electricity that drives the grinding. They are not inhabitants of the plateau.

They are the plateau’s metabolism, made visible, silent, and warm, continuously becoming themselves in the dim green twilight.
