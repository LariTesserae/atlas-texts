What lives there is defined first by slowness so complete it resembles growth rather than motion.

There are the amphibians—translucent, their skins like wet glass held up to the gray-gold diffusion, bodies that do not leap or dart but extend gradually into the moss-basin, as if each limb is deciding, over seasons, whether to become branch or root or remaining flesh. They breathe in the thick, warm air that refuses to thin, and where they rest, the mineral-weeping of the stone seems to quicken slightly, as though their presence draws the cliff’s constant drip downward into collaboration rather than fall.

Above and among them, the smaller inhabitants accumulate into sound rather than silhouette: insects and small crawling birds, not flying but clambering through the layered vegetation in continuous, patient presence. They do not call individually; they chorus, a loudness built from thousands of near-silent feedings and movements, the noise of saturation itself made audible. Their bodies are scaled for the dense green—broad-leafed heat-plants overlapping like armor, moss curtains draped in dark folds—so that pushing through requires not breaking but joining, becoming part of the spongy second floor woven by root systems that hold soil and moisture in cooperative refusal of chaos.

The vegetation is not backdrop; it is resident. The moss lives in thick, dark folds over the mesas. The broad-leaved plants grow not in competition but in shared layers, their roots interlacing a hidden architecture beneath the visible world, maintaining the marsh-edges at impossible altitude. They thrive on the geothermal breath of the rock, on the perpetual post-rain that never dried, and in doing so they replace what erosion gently takes—balanced, settled, working at the pace of geology.

And then there are the others: not organic, not dead. A dark pylon stands half-submerged in a moss-basin, its geometry gentle but wrong, non-reflective, silent. It is not abandoned. Scattered like seeds across the open regional expanse are the worn footprints of larger arrangements, integrated into stone, anchors that persist and work—perhaps guiding the slow growth, perhaps maintaining the thermal contradiction that keeps extreme height tropical, perhaps simply existing at the rhythm of centuries. They hum subsonically through root and rock, a vibration felt more than heard, part of the constant percussion of falling water droplets and breathing organisms.

Everything here resolves its contradictions by refusing to hurry. The fauna grows into its movements. The plants hold the wetness in shared layers. The ancient machinery cycles in geologic time, maintaining without command. Even the rock breathes and weeps, not baking but releasing, feeding the shallow marshes that squelch softly under any weight.

This is who lives in the Weeping Highregion: translucent, growing amphibians; chorus-crawling insects and birds; cooperative, layered vegetation that forms a second floor; and silent, working remnants that never rushed and never will—together suspended in perpetual saturation, loud with gentle persistence, and moving, if at all, only in the slowest rhythm of a place that has solved itself into being entirely, patiently, home.
