What lives here does not arrive; it has always been in the process of becoming itself, so slowly that birth and death are indistinguishable from posture.

There are the drifters first—the smallest citizens—though “smallest” is only true in body, not in presence. They are translucent, almost not there, suspended in the warm mist like dust that decided to think. They move not by will but by the breath of the hollow itself, drifting in vast, patient arcs between the polished walls. Each one is a faint point, and together they are never a swarm but a scattered constellation, maintaining the emptiness even as they fill it. When they pulse—lazy, unhurried, separated by minutes that feel like seasons—they leave trails of dim light hanging in the damp air, silver-less and cool, blue-green whispers that fade so gradually the darkness seems to absorb them with reluctance. They feed, perhaps, on the weeping; they drink the condensation that never falls fast enough to be rain. Their abundance is spread across centuries of volume, so that to stand in one place is to share the space with thousands and feel utterly alone.

Then the growth—that lushness which forgot the sun and made its own covenant with warmth. Along the floor and climbing the lower curves of stone, moss spreads in thick bands that glow, not brightly, but with a cool, internal phosphorescence, as if the rock itself had learned to dream in color. It is soft blue, muted green, occasional slow pulses of amber—never enough to illuminate, only enough to remind the dark that something alive is watching back. Between the moss, fungal sheets spread like velvet laid over bones, absorbing the sweat of the walls, turning mineral breath into flesh. And deeper, hidden in cracks where erosion has stopped but not finished, root-structures thread downward—not seeking light, which has no meaning here, but seeking the chemical warmth of the ancient stone, drinking the alloy, the salt, the memory of pressure. They grow without urgency. A root claims an inch in the span of generations.

But the hollow is not only small life. There is the large one—perhaps more than one, though they are solitary, separated by distances of patience—whose presence is felt more in the ripple than in the sight. It moves across the shallow pools with long, soft limbs, not swimming but walking through the stillness, its weight so distributed that the water barely seems to notice until an hour has passed. The ripples it leaves take an hour to flatten, and in that time the dark mirror of the pool holds its shape, a slow record of passage. Its body is large, warm, slow-moving, perhaps covered in the same faint bioluminescence or perhaps absorbing the glow of the moss into its own damp skin. It makes the low, resonant calls—not often, not in panic, but as part of the architecture of sound—vibrations that travel through the wet stone and the suspended mist, joining the constant murmur of weeping walls and the deep, periodic pulse that comes from the artifacts.

Because the artifacts are inhabitants too, in their own silent fashion. The pillar near the center stands like a bone grown through the earth rather than placed upon it—an alloy that has forgotten how to decay, integrated with the rock so thoroughly that removing it would be like removing a spine. Near it, the partial ring is embedded in the wall, half-submerged in moss, altering the air in ways that have no name in modern language: pressure shifts subtly, and the water beads on nearby stone remember gravity differently, hanging in shapes that should fall but do not, or falling upward toward the ring’s influence. The flat disc rests on a pool’s surface without sinking, thinner than a leaf, untouched, functioning on principles that do not announce themselves. They do not hum. They do not glow. But they contribute to the loudness—a bass vibration felt in the chest, like the heart of the mountain itself beating once every few days, deep and patient, setting the tempo for everything else.

The water weeps upward in places, drawn by these silent companions, defying expectation without breaking it, creating paths that trace silverless lines back toward the ceiling that isn’t there but feels closer near the artifacts. The temperature never changes—no drafts, no cold pockets—just the uniform, breathing warmth, 2.1, damp and heavy, carrying the scent of wet stone and something sweeter, like flowers that decay so slowly they have become eternal, never fully rotting, never fully fresh.

So the hollow is full. Not crowded—never crowded, maintaining its cathedral emptiness—but inhabited by layers of patience: the drifting dust of life, the glowing moss and velvet fungi, the root-things seeking chemical warmth, the large solitary walker leaving ripples that outlast memory, and the silent bones of technology that have settled into erosion and become part of the final form. They share the same breath. They move to the same glacial rhythm—pools shifting boundaries across centuries, moss claiming new inches across generations, the drifters pulsing their brief lights in patterns that will not repeat for millennia.

And in the absolute darkness—black not as absence but as presence, thick with mist and sound—they are exactly, finally, where they belong. Not surviving. Not thriving in urgency. Simply being, in a place where abundance and emptiness are the same thing, and the loudness is only the sound of everything, together, not needing anything more.
