What lives here lives in plural and in absence.

First, the columns. They are not trees that have died and become wood; they are organisms that finished arguing with time and became its accounting system. Each one is a rounded, flattened pillar of compressed root-felt and bark turned to leather, but inside—through pores you can sense rather than see—the vascular engines still turn. They are inhabitants of a different order: beings that have replaced metabolism with production. They do not grow upward or outward; they circulate inward. Sap moves through them with the rhythm of assembly lines, not seasons. Hardened pith-valves open and close with a precision that suggests intention, yet there is no mind directing them—only the long discipline of equilibrium. They stand spaced like piers, and in the spaces they leave, the emptiness is deliberate, as if they built the hollow for something that never arrived, or for something that left long ago and left the machinery running. They breathe without lungs, pump without hearts, and their sound—the relentless low drone, the underwater factory hum—is the voice of their living.

Then the small ones. They are numerous, as the description admits: winged things, burrowers, climbers. But abundance here is a kind of loneliness distributed across volume. You do not see them congregate. A flash of movement across a root-beam—something with translucent wings, navigating by the greenish columns of light—then gone into the shadow between columns. Small percussion in the moss: nesting, burrowing, the soft mechanics of bodies too light to disturb the hum. They follow paths worn smooth not by erosion but by repetition across ages, paths that go nowhere new. They inhabit the surfaces and traverse the air, but they do not fill it. Each creature is alone in the cathedral of root-rib and void, spread so thin across the hollow that their presence is statistical rather than felt. They do not compete. There is nothing to compete for; the plants provide substrate, not food in any urgent sense, and the water comes on its own eternal schedule. They exist as annotations—marks that life still moves, still scatters, still briefly interrupts the count of the dripping.

The water, too, lives. Not as a river or pool with personality, but as a rhythm made physical. It beads in cool pearls along the woody columns, holds in black still pockets of the floor, and falls with a counting sound that never hurries, never pauses. It is the place’s only clock, and it tells a time that has already ended.

There are no larger animals. No predators sized to the hollow, no grazing herds, no beings that sit and observe. The scale refuses them. This is a bottom chamber, a low pocket where coolness collects, and whatever once lived larger in the older structure above has either withdrawn upward or dissolved downward into the substrate itself.

And then there is the absence that behaves like a presence. The technology is not hidden; it is dissolved. The industrial sound comes not from machines placed among the plants, but from the plants having become machines. The inhabitants, therefore, include the system itself—the fused biology-mechanism that breathes and counts and refuses change. To live here is to be part of a factory that makes nothing, serving an equilibrium that needs no repair.

You stand among them. The column-inhabitants do not notice you; they have no orientation, only circulation. The small creatures flee or freeze, then resume their solitary traversals. The drip continues its count. Nothing asks you to stay, nothing drives you out. You are simply another temporary body in the hollow, another brief occupation of the empty air between the root-pillars, listening to the machinery of a world that finished becoming itself long before you arrived, and will continue—unchanging, damp, counting—long after the sound of your steps has faded into the moss.
