Here, life is not a visitor. It is the mortar.

Begin with the **Weave-Plants themselves**, for they are the ones who remain, who occupy, who *are* the place. They do not live *in* the Spire-Weave; they have become it. Each pillar is an individual—though “individual” feels wrong for something that has spent millennia deciding exactly how thick its wall should be, exactly how hollow its heart must stay to carry sound. They rise in scattered congregation: bone-white trunks thirty meters through, anchored by root-balls that sprawl like submerged towns. Their surfaces are not bark but calcified fiber, pale as old ivory, translucent at the edges where growth is still—barely, almost imperceptibly—continuing. Along the seams of older tissue runs that faint phosphorescence: not illumination, but memory of light, a greenish-blue pulse like milk-glass remembering the sun. They feed without mouth or leaf, absorbing the eternal chemical currents that thread the dark water, and in doing so they have sculpted themselves into architecture. Hollow galleries. Suspended galleries. Bulbous nodes that hang like lungs in the black. They are cool to the touch—though nothing touches them without being changed—just above freezing, matching the water. They do not sleep, but neither do they wake. They are in a state of solved persistence, having reached the final equation of their form long before anything arrived to observe it.

Then there are those who live *within* the solution.

The **Clingers** come first—small, translucent, almost not present until the faint greenish glow of a root-mat reveals them. They possess elongated limbs, not legs so much as articulated filaments, and they move with the patience of organisms that have never known urgency. They cling to the woven substrate, filter-feeders in the truest sense, drawing the same dissolved minerals that feed the plants but through delicate, pulsing membranes. Their bodies are clear, showing internal chemistry like slow-moving lanterns—no eyes, of course, no need—only sensory threads that read pressure and vibration. They clean. They graze. They deposit, in their passing, the seed-cases that the plants will eventually absorb back into themselves. They are abundant in number yet sparse in presence, scattered like dust across the vast root-mats, never crowding, never competing. They have found the exact density the system allows.

Inside the hollow stems—those resonant bamboo-bones—live the **Burrowers**. You do not see them often; you hear them. They have made the pillars their bodies, threading through the calcified fiber, expanding chambers, smoothing passages with generations of gentle abrasion. From within, they make the architecture sing. The deep groan of plant tissue flexing under eternal pressure is partly them, partly the plants themselves, partly the water pressing through hollow galleries like breath through a throat. The burrowers are smaller, darker, soft—perhaps related to the clingers but transformed by enclosure, their translucency deepened to something like smoked glass. They eat the accumulated detritus that the clingers leave, that the plants exude, that the dead become. Nothing is wasted; they are the digestion of the Weave.

And then, moving between the spires through the vast, empty black—never resting, never altering the landscape they inhabit—are the **Gliders**. They are the largest presence here, though “large” is relative in a place where scale has lost its surface reference. Soft bodies, dark not with pigment but with absence, shaped like submerged petals or old lungs. They propel themselves on silent, pulsing fins, not swimming so much as breathing through the water, creating currents that the clingers feel and the burrowers respond to inside their hollow homes. They glide from root-ball to root-ball, following routes older than the structures themselves—routes perhaps carved not by them but by the chemical currents they have learned to ride. They do not hunt; there is nothing here to hunt that has not already agreed to be part of the cycle. Instead, they clean broader surfaces, they deposit larger seed-cases, they die eventually—always eventually—into the root-weave, where their soft tissue is absorbed without ceremony. While alive, they are the Weave’s wandering consciousness: never altering, never building, only passing through, maintaining the equilibrium by their passage alone.

But do not think of these as separate kingdoms. The boundary between plant and animal is not sharp here; it has been smoothed like the architecture itself. The clingers’ seed-cases become the plants’ new growth. The burrowers’ waste feeds the root-mats. The gliders’ bodies become substrate. Even the sound—loud, constant, dominating—is a collaboration: the plants provide the hollow instruments, the burrowers play them from within, the gliders cause the low sustained drone as they pass through the galleries, and the clingers provide the constant rhythmic clicking of their feeding, a percussion of small lives holding the dark together.

There are others, of course—scattered, unnamed, part of the abundance that fills the spaces between structures. Small, constant lights: not lanterns but organisms that have developed a faint, chemical luminescence of their own, marking edges in the black like stars that have fallen downward instead of up. They move in patterns, returning to the same hollows, following the same slow routes, part of the 0.4 dynamic—frozen not in death but in perfect repetition.

So who lives here?

The **architects**, bone-white and hollow, who built a world and then stopped changing it.

The **clingers**, translucent and patient, holding to the woven walls.

The **burrowers**, hidden inside the pillars, making them resonate.

The **gliders**, soft and dark, passing between monuments on silent fins.

And beneath them all, the constant presence of the water itself—carrying chemistry, carrying sound, carrying the dissolved memory of a world that reached equilibrium and found it good.

They do not wake. They do not sleep. They maintain. In the Spore-Spire Weave, to live is to have become exactly necessary, exactly enough, and to hold that form in the cold, loud, dark water—forever, and without hurry.
