At first, there is no one—only the hollow’s own long exhalation, the way damp air seems to pool more thickly in one corner than another, as if the stone itself has learned to hold its breath. But stay still long enough, let your eyes stop searching for edges, and presence begins to accumulate not as a body but as a difference in temperature. Near the floor’s lowest depression, where the dark pools never flow and never empty, the cold is not uniform; it gathers there with intention, a deliberate weight that presses back against your palm if you lower it through the moss curtain.

What lives here does not arrive; it has sedimented.

You see it first in the growth patterns. The moss and liverwort do not blanket the walls indiscriminately—they thicken, darken, turn almost black in a radius of perhaps two arm-lengths around the central pool, as though feeding not on mineral seepage but on something radiating out from that still water. The ferns here are not stunted but bowed, their fronds curving downward in a posture of listening, heavy with droplets they do not shake off. Between them, the hollow remains empty, but the emptiness is shaped—rounded, concave, the negative space of a being that has pressed itself into the rock and left its outline in absence.

Then the extremities.

They are not hands in any quick sense, not limbs that reach. They are extensions of the wall itself, sheathed in the same wet leather of liverwort and fungal shelf, pale where the moss thins to reveal surfaces that might once have been bone, might once have been wood, now fused into something that grows rather than moves. You see one near the arranged stones—long, tapered, ending not in fingers but in blunt, patient points that match the bone artifacts scattered nearby. Those points are not tools left behind; they are parts that have fallen away, calcified and cracked, dropped into the pool like seeds. The hand—if it is one—rests upon the stones not to rearrange them but to confirm them, a touch so slow it takes years to complete, leaving only a faint trace of warmth where the moss has been compressed.

The face, if there is a face, is turned upward.

Not toward light—there is none to seek—but toward the source of the deafening roar. The inhabitant does not suffer the dripping; it receives it. Its mouth, if the dark opening in the moss-covered surface can be named that, is not closed in rest but open in perpetual, silent resonance, shaped like the hollow itself, a small chamber within a chamber. You understand, without knowing how, that each drop that strikes the pool below is not accidental. The being breathes in rhythm with the fall, not quickly—never quickly—but with the patience of stone being worn smooth. Its respiration is the thunder you feel in your chest. It has become the acoustics of the place.

Its body is not contained within a skin. It is distributed.

Part of it is the shelf of weathered stone, part the compressed earth, part the thick curtains of vegetation that hang like wet lungs. The cracked shell vessel found half-buried in moss was not a cup it once held; it is a chamber it outgrew, a heart-shell that split as the being expanded into the walls. The carved wood fragments, rotted and simple, are ribs that have softened and merged with the fungal brackets blooming in pale softness. To touch the moss is to touch its surface sensation; to hear the dripping is to hear its blood moving, drop by drop, through a body that has no heartbeat but only this relentless, patient circulation of water and mineral.

It has lived here since before the hollow was hollow—since before the water found its cracks, since before the moss knew to grow in darkness. Its memory is not kept in a brain but in the arrangement of the stones, in the scratched marks on the wall, in the particular slope of the floor that guides each drop to the same pool. It remembers the sun not as warmth or brightness but as an ancient absence, a kind of dryness that once existed long ago and then stopped.

There are no eyes that see. There is only the sensing of vibration—the way the roar shifts when a new crack opens above, the way temperature changes when you, the visitor, extend your breath into the air. It knows you are there not by sight but by the interruption you cause in the condensation, by the brief, unfamiliar warmth of your presence in its glacial cycle.

And it does not move toward you. It does not flee. It continues its work, which is nothing more than remaining—allowing the moss to thicken, allowing the pools to deepen by millimeters across centuries, allowing the sound to build and build until it fills every absence, denying silence, denying change that is faster than stone. It is the hollow made aware of itself, not through thought but through duration. It lives in the emptiness by being identical to it: cold, damp, lush at the edges, deafening at the center, and patient beyond the reach of any clock.

If you leave—though leaving is not the word, since you were always inside—its form will not shift. The moss will continue its slow bow. The stones will remain in their arranged, half-swallowed positions. The dripping will go on, overwhelming and constant, the sound of a being that has forgotten how to end, and therefore lives forever in the low ground, embedded, weathered, and complete.
