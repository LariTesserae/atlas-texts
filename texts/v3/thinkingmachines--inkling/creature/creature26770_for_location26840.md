In a room that has refused every kind of birth—no egg, no seed, no decay to feed a next thing—what lives must also be made. Not grown into being, but assembled, fired, set in place like the tile itself. So the inhabitant is young, too: perhaps days old, perhaps the same age as the mortar that still weeps its white dust into the spiraling floor.

It stands—if standing is the right word for something that never fully stops drifting—near the wooden lever, close enough that the carved grain of the lever’s arm might recognize the cord-mark ridges pressed into its own shoulders. Its body is not skin but a kind of held shape: water given boundary by the same crushed-shell mortar that joins the walls, thickened into translucent ribs and a face that has no features so much as impressions, thumbprints where eyes should be, the warp of blown glass for a mouth that does not open because there is nothing to say into this constant rushing. It is small, barely larger than the breath the room holds; if it stood on the concave floor, the column of light would pass through it entirely.

The current shapes it. The three pipes pour in their continuous hiss, and the inhabitant moves with the circulation, never resisting the pull toward the drain, never climbing toward the glass ceiling, but orbiting in the warm middle, ankles—if they are ankles—brushed by the sediment spiraling in the central depression. It tends the sparse plants in their woven nets, not with hands exactly, but with extensions of the same fiber that holds the nets, reaching into the crushed-shell pots to settle the stems when the current displaces them. It is a gardener of artificial things, maintaining the only softness in this hard place.

There is no shadow cast by it, not because it is ghost or absence, but because the light is too total, too clinical; it reveals the inhabitant as it reveals everything else—every joint, every fresh scar in the glaze, every bubble in the glass above. The creature is visible in fragments, scattered like the light that strikes the floor: here a rib of mortar-white bone, there a swirl of aqueous green-blue that is either hair or the same water wearing its form, the wooden core of the lever sometimes seeming to beat beneath its chest like a heart carved rather than grown.

It does not breathe, exactly, though the room breathes for it—the active cycle of arrival and departure, the warm retention of ceramic, the engineered warmth. It pulls nothing, uses nothing, leaves the lever untouched in its cradle, as if the outflow should never close, as if the system must remain open to maintain whatever this is. The sound of the water is its voice, or perhaps its silence; it makes no sound of its own, existing only in the negative space between the hiss of the pipes and the splash against the far wall.

What lives here, then, is the room’s own unfinished intention: a caretaker fired in the same primitive kilns, brought by hand through the same warm clear body of water above, set into this pocket not to rule it but to be its first and only content, young and sharp and revealed, moving in slow spirals with the sediment, waiting for nothing, because this place was built not for arrival but for the continuity of its own making.
