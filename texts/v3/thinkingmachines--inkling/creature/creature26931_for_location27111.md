What lives here lives alone, and slowly.

The giant leaves are the most visible citizens—dark green plates veined with silver, floating like shields left behind by a war that never came. Each one is broad enough to bear the weight of a body, yet no body rests upon them. They do not cluster into forests or thickets; they maintain distance across the shallow basins, solitary presences with empty water between them like silences in a conversation. They adjust almost imperceptibly to air that refuses to move quickly, rustling with the patience of something that has never needed to flee.

Beneath and between them, the moss lives in rounded humps—soft, cushion-like islands separated by stretches of bare, polished rock. Each hump is a world complete unto itself, expanding by millimeters across generations, swallowing the edges of ancient cairns. They do not spread to cover everything; they prefer isolation, localized lushness that emphasizes the emptiness around it rather than filling it.

The water itself is an inhabitant, though it has no face. It lives in the shallow basins—knee-deep here, ankle-deep there—linked by runnels that barely stir. From the dark stone walls it weeps in long, patient intervals: a soft *plip*, then minutes of nothing, then another elsewhere. It holds the air’s warmth, blurring the boundary between liquid and vapor until everything wears a continuous wet skin. In the carved cup-shaped hollows along the rim, it collects and holds exactly to the brim, never overflowing, as if measured by an intelligence that cares only for balance. It is not running away; it has arrived and decided to stay.

The stone lives too, in its own manner—not dead, but settled so deeply into its weathering that it has become a form of presence. Ancient, rounded, polished into smooth dark curves like the inside of an enormous shell, it remembers the gentle centuries that shaped it. In it are the traces of older inhabitants: shallow hand-sized depressions arranged in irregular lines, flat rocks stacked into cairns no taller than a knee. These are not ruins—too simple, too patient for that—but the lingering handprints of someone long gone, someone who possessed nothing but time and a hard stone edge. They live now only as pattern, half-swallowed by moss, their manual patience fossilized into the rock’s own slow being.

There are no animals. No insects claim the air, no birds mark the open sky, no fish disturb the pools. The absence is not a temporary vacancy but a permanent feature, like a room built without doors for sound. What might have lived here—insect, bird, beast—was never invited, and so the space belongs entirely to what remains: the scattered leaves, the moss humps, the weeping water, the remembering stone.

Even the light behaves like an inhabitant, ordinary yet strange in its coherence. It does not dramatize; it absorbs differently everywhere—the silver veins glowing softly, the dark pools returning a muted greenish sky, the wet surfaces holding a continuous sheen. And time, too, lives here in its own state—not passing quickly, not decaying, but holding its breath across long durations, allowing moss to expand and cairns to settle deeper by millimeters.

You are here, suspended on the plateau’s shoulder, and the emptiness includes you. You are not an intruder but part of the sparse conversation—one more slow presence in a place where life chose not density but distance, not community but solitude. The weirdness resolves not into fear but into acceptance: this is how it is. A small, high, wet room open to sky, inhabited by isolated shields of leaf, by cushion-islands of moss, by water that measures itself exactly, by stone that holds the memory of forgotten hands, and by an absence so complete it becomes its own kind of presence. Here, in perfect, patient balance, everything that lives does so alone, together in its separateness, holding warmth and water in a logic that needs no other approval.
