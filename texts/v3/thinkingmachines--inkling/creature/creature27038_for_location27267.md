What lives there is not a population but a condition—life as weather, as architecture, as the air itself made thick. Begin with the smallest, because they are the noise and the floor and the constant proof that nothing here is ever still.

They are uncountable, yes, but more precisely they are *continuous*: not individuals scattered across a surface but a living sheet that happens to have edges. Each one is small—no larger than the span of a finger joint—though size becomes meaningless when they layer ten, twenty, fifty deep in the crevices of the compressed lattice. Their bodies are not bright; they carry the colors of the valley: deep green-black, the brown of wet bone-grit, the dull pearl of calcified shell. Some have translucent ventral plates that catch no light because there is none to catch, revealing only a darker interior, a pulse of warm fluid. Others are armored in overlapping chitin, not smooth like insect shell but rough, porous, grown over generations with moss-feeders and mineral deposits that make them look like walking fragments of the floor itself.

They move not with purpose but with pressure—millions of limbs, antennae, or soft ventral pads scraping against root and bone-grit, creating that deafening, dense thunder. It is not a song. It is the acoustic shape of occupation. They navigate by vibration, yes, but also by taste: the air here is not breathed so much as read, chemical trails left in the condensation that beads on every underside. They feed slowly, chewing at the dark velvet moss, at fallen bodies, at the spongy growths that hold hidden water. They excrete what becomes soil, what becomes resin, what hardens into the nesting material of the ceiling far above. You do not see them hatch or die; you see the layer shift, a dark ripple passing through the mass, and understand that generations have turned over in the time it takes moisture to bead and fall.

But there are breaks in the smallness—intervals of weight.

The large ones are slow, heavy, moving through the undergrowth like living boulders, and they are not predators or rulers but *landmarks*. One might take decades to cross a visible stretch of the valley floor. Their bodies are broad and low, crusted with the same moss that clings everywhere, so that at first they look like hills that have decided to drift. Their skin is thick, cracked like old bark, and within those cracks smaller lives have nested—entire ecologies riding on the mobile architecture of a back. They do not hunt; they graze, or perhaps they do not eat at all in any sense you would recognize, absorbing through their broad feet the constant dampness, processing the air itself, leaving behind compressed paths that become part of the woven pavement. Their breath is audible even above the million small sounds: a low, wet exhalation that moves spores and moisture in slow, visible currents through the brown-dark air.

They navigate the same acoustic map, feeling the vibrations of the small ones as a constant rain of information. When one dies—and they do, eventually, over centuries—the fall is not an event but a settlement. The body collapses into itself, is covered within days by crawling layers, becomes a mound, then a ridge, then part of the floor that breathes.

Between these scales exist the embedded ones: creatures that have stopped moving entirely, or never moved, that live within the walls and the ceiling and the spongy water-pockets. Some hang from the woven roof in clusters, suspended like dark fruit, filtering condensation through bodies that have become more membrane than muscle. Others live entirely submerged in the hidden wells, pale and blind, moving only when the pocket shifts, their existence measured in the slow exchange of nutrients with the roots that reach downward to drink them.

And then there is the vegetation—not separate, never separate. The moss and the lush, black-green growth are tended not by intention but by passage. The large ones prune with their weight; the small ones seed with their waste; the dead ones fertilize with their dissolution. Some of the plants have become part creature: root-systems that pulse slowly, drawing moisture up through the lattice floor, releasing it back into the air as a cool, constant breath.

There are no faces here that seek recognition. No eyes meet yours in the darkness, because eyes are unnecessary and, in many, absent entirely. What observes is the collective pressure of presence—the constant, overwhelming fact of being alive together. A colony shifts in the far wall, not because it was commanded, but because moisture dried in one pocket and filled another, and instinct followed the damp. A section of the ceiling settles, not collapsing but integrating, weaving fallen nesting material into a thicker membrane.

To live here—if you lived here—would be to dissolve the boundary between self and substrate. You would not build; you would excrete. You would not hunt; you would filter. You would navigate not by sight but by feeling the million other bodies pressing near, their vibrations mapping the open, enclosed space better than any sun could. You would grow slowly, die slowly, and become part of the compressed lattice that holds the next layer.

This is the inhabitant: not one, not named, but the accumulated warmth of all of them—the crawling, the embedded, the boulder-slow, the moss-riding, the dead-now-floor. They are the valley’s memory and its present tense, breathing, scraping, settling in the brown half-dark, utterly full, utterly normal, utterly home.
