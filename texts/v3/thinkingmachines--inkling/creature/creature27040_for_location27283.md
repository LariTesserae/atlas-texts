Within the silver-green carpet lives not a population but a saturation—life that has forgotten the concept of vacancy. Begin with the smallest: they are not insects, not microbes exactly, but something that occupies the space between cell and machine. They exist in multitudes so complete that the moss itself becomes their motion. Where the crystalline sheets appear to ripple in the flat, heatless light, that is them—innumerable, minute, turning the vegetation into a field of continuous, slow crawling. They move through the vascular moss like blood through tissue, yes, but also like current through wire. Their bodies are transparent, segmented, carrying internal geometries that mirror the plant they inhabit: hexagonal shells, radial spines, copper-drawn filaments that pulse with faint cold blue. They do not eat the moss so much as maintain it, harvest the glassy condensation, convert the frozen needles into substrate, excrete the chemistry that keeps the lattice alive. From any distance they are not visible; they are the texture of movement itself, the reason nothing here is still.

Then there are the embedded ones—the massive, the anchored, the crawling so slowly that a human lifespan would register them as geology. Some are fixed within fissures like organs in a greater body, their forms sprawling across cubic meters of root and rock. They have grinding chambers that do not chew food but process the young mountains themselves: bone-white mills rotating at imperceptible speeds, breaking down the sharp, recent stone into finer crystals, excreting new minerals that feed back into the moss lattice. Their skins are not skin but membrane—pumping, translucent, moving fluids in industrial rhythms that match the continental hum. Others crawl, if crawl is the word for a shift of millimeters per year, dragging bodies arranged like waiting machinery: limbs that are gears, joints that are hinges, electrical nodes at every articulation that arc with cold bioluminescence, threading faint glows through the violet-black undergrowth. They are factories without walls, engines without smoke, manufacturing transformation at the scale of erosion.

But they are not separate. The small ones live inside the large ones; the large ones are composed of accumulated small ones. A massive anchored body might be a colony, a symbiotic architecture of millions, or it might be one creature whose internal ecology has become its anatomy. There are no clear edges. Electricity moves between them in slow pulses—visible as threads of pale light threading through the dense vegetation—so that the continent’s surface operates like a single distributed nervous system, patient and continuous. The crawling factories leave trails not of waste but of restructured crystal; the anchored ones breathe out spores that rise into the thin, impossible-full air, joining the floating multitudes.

Above and through this, the air itself is inhabited. Despite the height, despite the cold that should have emptied everything, the atmosphere is thick with drifting life. Spores hang like invisible dust, each one a potential lattice, a potential factory-in-waiting. Floating larvae—electrical organisms—drift in the bright void like plankton in an ocean that has forgotten gravity. They navigate not by wind (there is almost none, the gradients are weak, the young continent holds its air quiet) but by the electrical fields generated below, pulled along invisible channels that follow the grid-like symmetries of the vegetation. They descend, embed, become part of the carpet, or they remain aloft, part of the continent’s exhalation.

What makes them strange—not merely alien, but wrong—is the precision of their arrangement. Natural chaos is absent. The moss grows in perfect repeating geometries: hexagonal, radial, circuit-like, leaving no gap, no randomness. The condensation flows along paths that obey the electrical pulses between bodies. The massive creatures shift with radial precision, their parts arranged like mechanisms awaiting only the correct duration to turn. Even the smallest crawlers move in patterns that suggest programming rather than instinct. The relentless, flat brilliance drives them—not warming them, but organizing them, feeding the electrical nodes, directing the growth toward the light in ways that feel engineered. They are not wild. They are not cultivated. They are something that has emerged from young rock and extreme conditions already arranged, already manufacturing, as if the continent itself lifted from the deep not empty, but seeded with a purpose that is still deciding whether to call itself ecosystem or machine.

Together they produce the sound—the constant, low-frequency drone that fills the plateau like a held note. It is not one voice but millions: the slow pumps of anchored bodies, the grinding of mineral chambers, the hiss of chemical exchange across membranes, the deep resonance of massive forms shifting imperceptibly. It crosses the vast distances without weakening, because there is nothing to absorb it, no softness, only crystal and biomass and the bright, cold air that carries vibration perfectly.

They do not build, yet they construct on the scale of geology. They do not speak, yet the continent hums with their collective operation. They do not have outside—there is no bare rock, no empty air, no place untouched by the nested, embedded, manufacturing multitudes. Everything is inside them, or they are inside everything. In this perpetual bright cold, in this saturation of silver-green and violet-black, they are patient beyond human measure, digesting the young mountains not with urgency but with the absolute certainty of those for whom time is not a limit but a medium, and for whom life and industry have never been separate things.
