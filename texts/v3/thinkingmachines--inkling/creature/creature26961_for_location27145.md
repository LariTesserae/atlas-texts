What lives here is not a population but an occupation—continuous, layered, and entirely without boundary between one resident and the next.

The floor is held by the moss, the oldest and most patient inhabitant. It does not carpet the stone so much as replace it: black-green, saturated, several handbreadths deep, a spongy substrate that has forgotten the distinction between plant and ground. Each filament breathes the cold mineral air, exhaling it in invisible cycles that never warm. There is no root system reaching downward into soil—there is no soil—only the moss holding itself together, a dense mat that compresses softly under any imagined weight and releases, with that compression, a breath of damp stone. It has no flowers, no season, no urgency. It expands by agreement, filling every depression in the basalt, turning the floor into a single, continuous lung that feeds on condensation and the faint emanation rising from the walls.

Above this floor, suspended in perpetual near-darkness, hang the root-hairs—pale, translucent, thick as ropes. They are the ceiling’s citizens, not anchored by gravity but by thirst. They drink the slow beads of moisture that gather on the rock overhead, pulling water downward through their own bodies in a process too gradual to observe. They interweave, not competing, forming a second ceiling beneath the basalt, a curtain of near-white tissue that moves only across centuries—thickening by imperceptible degrees, shifting their grip on the stone, letting a new thread descend where an old one has reached saturation. They touch the fern-fronds that unfurl between them, and the ferns, in turn, grow in shades of deep slate and violet-black, waxy-edged, holding moisture in beads that glisten without ever falling. These fronds do not reach for light; they reach for dampness and for the scattered warmth that is not warmth at all. They cluster thickest where the walls are not bare, pressing their bodies against each other, against the moss, against the hanging ropes—vegetation touching vegetation touching stone, leaving almost no surface untouched.

Between these layers, rising like brittle architecture, stand the fungal towers. They are not scattered randomly but interlocked, forming corridors and walls that complete the space between floor and ceiling. Layered, brittle, they grow in vertical rhythms that mimic structure but serve only themselves—holding moisture in their folds, breaking slowly, rebuilding from their own decay without ever truly dying. They are the intermediaries, the ones who occupy the middle air, receiving what the root-hairs release and feeding back into the moss below.

But the true residents—the ones that define what lives here—are embedded in the walls themselves.

Here and there, partially swallowed by root-mats and moss, sit the nodes: smooth oval shapes no larger than clenched fists, made of a material that refuses to be basalt, that refuses to be anything geology recognizes. They are weathered into rounded anonymity, eroded not by water—which here does not flow—but by time operating on principles no current understanding describes. They emit no visible glow, yet the vegetation knows them. The moss thickens around them. The root-hairs cluster nearest them. The ferns grow denser, darker, closer. They draw sustenance from a field that is neither heat nor light—something older, quieter, operating at a speed that makes centuries feel like shallow breaths. These nodes do not move, yet they are not dead. They pulse. They function. They have settled into the ecology as surely as any root, emitting their influence at intervals too long for ordinary perception, feeding the closed loop that sustains all other life in the pocket.

There is also the rail—broken, curved, traced along the eastern wall, buried now in moss, serving as anchor and scaffold for the root-hairs that have grown over it. It is not a ruin of a civilization that left; it is a fragment of a process that withdrew, or that finished itself long ago and became part of the substrate. It holds the moss. The moss holds it. There is no distinction.

Together, these are the inhabitants. There is no fauna—no insects, no crawling thing, no larger presence to disturb the equilibrium. The environment is too cold, too dark, too specifically tuned, to support metabolism that requires hunger. Life here is vegetal, fungal, mineral, technological—a single community existing in agreement.

The dynamics among them are glacial. The nodes feed the field; the field feeds the moss and ferns; the root-hairs move water in silent descent; the fungi interlock and hold; the broken rail anchors; the basalt encloses. Nothing grows aggressively. Nothing decays in the ordinary sense—there is only the slow settling, the imperceptible pulse, the occasional drop of condensation falling from ceiling to leaf, lost in the softness below.

The sound of their living is the hush itself: the soft compression of moss, the faint settling of root-hairs, the distant silence of deep earth, broken once in a long interval by a drop that strikes a waxy frond and disappears.

They do not wait for anything. They do not seek. They exist in a state that is neither growth nor decline but pure, dense persistence—cold, saturated, enclosed, and profoundly, quietly alive.
