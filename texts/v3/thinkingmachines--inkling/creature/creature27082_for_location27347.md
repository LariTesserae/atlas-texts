They do not arrive; they have arrived. Inside the egg, life is not a visitor but a settlement—thousands of small agreements made with stone, moisture, and the silent hum of things left behind.

The air itself is inhabited first. Not by breath, but by motion: translucent-winged bodies, smaller than a fingertip, moving in slow, deliberate clouds through the uncolored light. They are not quite insects—there is something in the joint of their thoraxes that suggests a different arithmetic, a body plan negotiated with this place rather than imported into it. Their wings are not feathered but membranous, veined like dead leaves pressed between glass, and when they move, they click. Not a hum, not a buzz, but a dry, percussive seed-rattle, thousands of them together making the chamber sound like a gourd filled with time. They navigate by the dampness, clustering where the vines hang heaviest, feeding on spores that drift from the moss beds below, nesting in the curved metal rib that lies flush with the floor—laying translucent eggs along its etched patterns, as if recognizing in its unknown geometry a kind of alphabet for reproduction.

Beneath them, closer to the pools, crawl the others: amphibious, jointed wrong. Too many elbows, too many hinge-points in limbs that should be simpler. They are pale, almost the color of the basalt when wet, and they move with a deliberate, mechanical patience—up the curved walls, across the moss islands, leaving behind trails of phosphorescent slime that glow faintly in the aperture’s steady glow and then fade, as if the chamber refuses to keep any record too long. They are both predator and prey in a rhythm compressed so tightly it feels like a single thought. One moment the pool is still, dark, reflecting nothing; the next, a sudden rustling in the vines, an explosive splash, the slime-trails converging in disruption, then silence. The consumption is quick. The noise resumes—clicking, scraping, the soft drop of condensation—before the echo of the splash has finished its long hollow decay.

The vegetation is not background; it is a participant with its own will. The dark green vines do not simply hang—they drip with intention, guiding moisture from the ceiling’s eye downward onto specific beds of moss, which cushion the floor’s depressions like thick, living upholstery. The moss is not uniform. Near one of the translucent pillars—the kneeling, humming ones—it grows in impossible symmetry, spiraling outward in patterns that mirror the pillar’s own silent geometry, twice as fast, twice as dense, as though being instructed. Ferns unfurl from seams where root and rock have ended their long argument not with victory but with a shared purpose: to hold the damp. Between these islands, the stone remains bare, warm, polished to river-glass, and the emptiness is not absence but breath—the necessary space that allows the density to feel overwhelming without becoming suffocating.

Then there are the remnants. They are not dead, though they show no face. The curved rib in the floor carries patterns that align with nothing human—angles that seem to shift slightly when not observed directly, though perhaps that is only the play of the uncolored light. The pillars stand at knee-height, translucent, emitting a frequency felt more in the teeth than in the ears—a low, constant implication of function. They maintain the climate: the warmth that should not exist at this altitude, the stillness that holds against the mountain’s forgotten wind. Near one, the air carries copper; near another, the moss listens and grows faster. The fauna understands them without understanding—nesting in them, feeding on the accelerated growth they provoke, building small architectures of slime and shell upon their bases. They are caretakers that require no care, or perhaps they are the original inhabitants, scaled wrong, operating on principles of nanoscale or cosmic patience, embedded so thoroughly into the erosion’s final form that they have become part of the geology itself.

Everything here cycles at a speed that suggests urgency within stillness. The condensation falls at irregular intervals, each drop echoing with a hollow resonance that the clicking wings seem to answer. The swarms shift with an invisible weather—perhaps responding to the pillars’ silent adjustments, perhaps to the predator-prey rhythms compressed in the pools. The fragments emit no movement, only the implication: temperature held constant, light sustained, growth accelerated in specific zones. The vegetation responds visibly, almost impatiently. The fauna fills every available margin—air, water, vine, pillar—overwhelming in number, overwhelming in noise, yet never spilling beyond the chamber’s negotiated boundaries.

This is what lives inside the mountain’s ending: not a single creature, but a congress. Small loud lives. Slow-crawling things with too many joints. Winged bodies clicking like seeds in a gourd. Moss that spirals by instruction. Ancient columns humming below the threshold of annoyance. And between them all—the smooth, vast emptiness of the polished floor, the warm still air, the aperture’s steady eye—holding them in a coherence that is strange precisely because it is complete. The mountain stopped being mountains and started being memory; this pocket is the memory’s heartbeat, still working out what comes after geology is done.

They persist. Damp, active, lush only where they have chosen. Overwhelmed with presence in a space that should be empty. A hidden room at the end of erosion, not haunted but inhabited—settled into its final form, loud with small lives, and silent with the quiet function of things that have long since forgotten they were ever built.
