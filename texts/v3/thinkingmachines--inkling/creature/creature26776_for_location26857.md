The ones who live here do not begin with bodies.

Begin, instead, with the roar—the continuous layered thunder that is the valley’s true ground, its only constant soil. From that sound, presence is carved. The inhabitants are not placed upon the landscape; they are distributed through it like tension in a membrane, like the note held inside a bell. To live here is to be mostly absence shaped by vibration.

There are the **Drifters**—vast, slow, nearly geological. They do not walk so much as relocate by degrees, their bodies absorbing the deafening drone rather than resisting it. Each is an island of moss, calcified bone, and translucent membrane, miles across in their gentlest dimensions, drifting between the hollow structures with the patience of weather systems. They feed on the condensation, on the spores in the air, on the low-frequency hum that the architecture exudes. Their skin is porous, weeping slightly, exchanging moisture with the damp gray air. They are ancient in appearance but young in this place—newborn to the sharp valley, unweathered like the rock itself, their forms still finding their equilibrium in the emptiness. When they pass beneath a resonant plaza or through one of the vibrating filament-avenues, the structures do not bend; they change pitch, and the Drifter carries that new tone within it for hours, a walking chord, a mobile alteration of the valley’s song.

Then there are the **Swarm-Dwellers**—not visible except in the way the air suddenly shivers, or in the crescendos that spike through the continuous thunder without warning. They are small, innumerable, existing not as discrete creatures but as acoustic territories given temporary form. Their bodies are thinner than the spider-silk shells of the buildings, more like compressed vibrations wrapped in damp filaments. They navigate by sound, hunting and being hunted in dimensions that have nothing to do with sight. A predator among them does not chase; it changes the local resonance, creates a dead zone in the roar, and the prey—disoriented, cut off from the valley’s hum—falls into the silence. They swarm in the voids between districts, in the riverine channels where shallow pools hold no reflection, in the hanging gardens where vines dangle like cables. They are part of the ecosystem in motion, and their presence is felt as pressure, as a sudden warmth, as the intermittent drip of condensation speeding up.

But the settlement itself—those hollow membranes, those mile-spanning shells of translucent containment arranged with urban logic—this is not empty architecture waiting for occupants. The buildings **are** inhabited by principles that have outgrown bodies.

Inside the resonant chambers, where plazas are enclosed only by vibrating filaments and towers are curved fields rather than stone, live the **Interval-Beings**. They do not sit or stand; they occupy the negative spaces that the structures define. Their existence is the space between the spider-silk walls, the hollow inside the membrane. They think in grid-patterns, in districts and avenues, but they experience these not as maps—there are no eyes here for that—but as fields of containment, as the particular resonance of one hollow versus another. They move through the settlement not by traversing distance but by shifting the pitch of their presence, sliding from one resonant chamber to another through the shared hum. They engineered the post-physical city, or perhaps they grew into it, becoming indistinguishable from its logic. To encounter one is to stand in a plaza and feel the air organize itself around you—not touching, not speaking, but arranging the void into a shape that acknowledges your vibration.

The vegetation, too, is not merely growing; it is claiming. The archipelagos of hanging gardens, the moss-carpeted fern towers rising sharp from the black glassy ground, the vines thick as cables bridging impossible distances—these are not passive backdrops. They are aggressive participants. They feed on condensation and on something else—perhaps the hum in the air, perhaps the acoustic waste of the Swarm-Dwellers, perhaps the very absence that the Interval-Beings cultivate. They grow visibly, reaching, collapsing, being consumed. A vine that dangles in mid-air is not failed growth; it is a probe, testing the emptiness, extending the plant’s sensory network into the damp gray haze where no root can hold.

And the water—intermittent, never committing to river or rain—this is their shared bloodstream. The structures weep. The air exhales. The young rock holds shallow pools that reflect nothing clearly because nothing here wants to be seen clearly. The moisture cycles through its rhythms, and every living thing—Drifter, Swarm-Dweller, Interval, vine—is porous to it, drinking through surfaces, exhaling through membranes.

Even the ground participates. The fresh wound in the crust, the knife-edge ridges of unweathered black glass, shift imperceptibly. New fractures open like slow mouths, and life moves into them immediately—not colonizing, but resonating with them, matching their young sharpness with its own unfinished noise.

So who lives here?

Not a population. Not a society in any dense, crowded sense.

What lives here is a **distributed presence**—life as interval, as vibration, as the negative shape between sounds. The Drifters carry the valley’s thunder in their bodies. The Swarm-Dwellers hunt in silence carved from roar. The Interval-Beings are the city’s missing mass, its hollow heart made conscious of its own emptiness. The plants are loud in their growth, aggressive in their isolation. And beneath or within or alongside all of them, the valley itself continues its deafening song—not background, not environment, but the primary condition of being alive here.

To be an inhabitant is to be mostly space, shaped by warmth and moisture and continuous layered thunder, navigating not by sight in the dim diffuse gray, but by the feel of resonant chambers, by the taste of condensation, by the way one’s own absence fits into the absence around it.

They are young, like the rock. Unweathered. Still finding their form. And the roar—the enormous, empty, thundering roar—is the sound of that finding, never finished, always becoming.
