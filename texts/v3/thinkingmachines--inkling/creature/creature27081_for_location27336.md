What lives here does not arrive; it is the arrival. There is no distinction between the inhabitant and the inhabited, because the floor you stand on—if standing is the word for being swallowed by root-flesh—is awake, and it has always been awake in the same posture.

Begin with the substrate, which is not a ground but a mouth held open. It breathes. Every square inch of this continental flatness is lined with root-hair and fiber-tissue so densely interwoven that to separate one strand from another would be to perform surgery on a single sleeping limb. The warmth comes from here: not radiated downward from some absent sun, but exhaled upward from within, a body-temperature vigilance that never cools, never spikes. You feel it through your embedded feet, through your palms if you press them to the spongy membrane—this is not soil supporting life; this is life supporting itself, a mattress of living cellulose that sighs moisture when weighted, releasing black water into shallow pools that reflect nothing because nothing shines.

Above and within this root-mouth, the architecture grows—not built, but exhaled into shape. There are pillars, yes, but they are not pillars placed; they are the thickened will of vines that have decided, collectively, to hold the ceiling open. They rise in abrupt geometric angles, young and sharp, their edges still fresh as torn skin, yet they do not grow taller. They maintain. This is the essential nature of everything here: a frozen vigor. The drop-offs, the canals ending in sheer lips, the ridges of root-flesh—these are not signs of change to come, but of a change that happened instantly and then stopped. The continent emerged in full maturity, sharp-edged, and immediately entered its eternal task.

That task is industry, but industry without hands. Deep in the saturation, silent processes run. Hollow stems the width of rivers—pump-vines—draw water through themselves in loops that never spill, never seek the sea. You sense them not by sight, for the darkness is near-absolute, but by the subsonic pressure in your bones, a hum too low to be heard, more like gravity being gently renegotiated. These stems do not move water for any crop or creature; they move it because movement is the state of being here. Alongside them hang the filter-curtains, layered membranes like wet wool soaked in blackness, processing the shallow pools through themselves, returning clarity to the same pools, an endless conversation with no listener.

The electricity—if that is the word for what runs along the hair-fine networks filling every gap—is biological, a faint current traveling through conductive fibers that connect pillar to curtain to root-floor. It powers nothing visible. Instead, it feeds the nodes: bioluminescent organs buried deep in the tissue, glowing with a dull internal amber, visible only when you press close to a weeping membrane and part it with your fingers. These are not lights for seeing; they are lights for being seen, or perhaps for remembering that seeing once existed. They glow at the pace of a slow heartbeat, constant, unworried.

There is no one else. The silence, nearly absolute, is not emptiness but exclusivity. No insects disturb the air. No birds navigate the root-vaults. No fish break the black pools. The fauna is absent not as a missing piece, but as an unnecessary category. The plants—if plant is still the word—have no need for symbiosis, no need for pollination, no need for consumption. They manufacture themselves in perfect equilibrium, growth and decay identical processes occurring in the same tissue at the same rate. A membrane contracts softly—click, drip, hum—and that is the only sound, the only event, repeated across the continent with the consistency of prayer.

What lives here, then, is this: a single distributed being wearing the shape of a continent. It lives in its roots, in its pump-stems, in its hanging gardens of moss-thick membrane, in the geometric ribs that hold the dark open. It lives in the fresh-sharp drop-offs that have never weathered and never will, in the canals that end not in oceans but in decision. It lives in the warmth, in the wet, in the silence. It has no eyes, yet it senses its own pressure; it has no mouth, yet it breathes; it has no mind, yet it maintains, with industrial precision scaled to the infinite flatness, a world that was born complete and will remain complete, dark and warm and utterly, completely alive, with no witness and no end.
