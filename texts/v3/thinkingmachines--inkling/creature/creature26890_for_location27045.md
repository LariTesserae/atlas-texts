What lives there is not a population but a condition—an inhabited hollow that behaves like a single, distributed body.

Begin with the walls. They are not bare; they are upholstered in skin. Thousands of translucent amphibians—frogs, newts, and shapes that borrow from both without settling—cling to the curved brick in a living mosaic. Their bodies are the precise color of weathered clay and wet mortar, cool and glassy, so that to look at the wall is to see it breathe. They do not hide; they dissolve. Only when the dome shivers with sound do they reveal themselves, shifting in slow, collective ripples, their limbs splayed like rootlets holding the structure in place. They are the chamber’s exterior made interior, the erosion given pulse. Their voices do not scatter but gather—layered, resonant croaks that find the shallow crown and turn it into a bell. They speak not to one another across distance, but into the same space, so that the air becomes a single vibrating membrane. To hear them is to feel the room think.

In the shallow water—never deep, always present—another order moves. Blind, finger-length fish, paler than bone, flash silver against the dark tiles. They have no need for sight because the chamber offers nothing to see; they navigate by vibration, by the mechanical click of their own bodies against concave brick. Their motion is abrupt, mechanical, like gears in a soft clock—darting, stopping, chattering in continuous percussion that fills the gaps between the amphibians’ pulses. They feed on what falls from above, and in feeding they keep the floor from ever being still. The water holds them not as an environment but as a medium they have made necessary.

Above, in the upper air near the gray slit of light, a third presence drifts: insects with bodies like hollow gourds, large and slow-winged, never landing, never resting. They do not swarm in chaos but in a sustained, collective hum—deep, unceasing, tuned to the dome’s exact resonance so that the brick itself trembles gently, distributing invisible spores across a world with almost no plants to receive them. Their wings brush the ceiling and leave no trace, as if they are not physical but acoustic—sound given temporary shape. They drink condensation; they fall; they are eaten. They are the chamber’s breath made visible.

But these are only the parts. What truly lives there is the loop they form together.

The amphibians breed in the water film; the fish consume what descends; the insects rise from the same moisture, feed, and fall back into the cycle. There is no outside. The water seeps, evaporates in cool air, beads on the arch, and returns. Every drip strikes the tile with a clear, bell-like tone. Every call echoes until direction collapses. The sound is not background but architecture—another wall, curved and invisible, holding the space together.

There is also the coherence, which feels like a kind of intelligence without mind. The frogs match the brick exactly. The hum resonates at the frequency that makes the dome sway just enough. The water remains always at the same shallow depth, calibrated by nothing visible. Everything belongs too well. It suggests that the builders—whose hand-pressed bricks and bone tools left only this—did not merely construct a shelter but seeded an equilibrium, and then stepped away, leaving the mechanism running.

So who lives there?

The translucent thousands live there, yes—clinging, croaking, shifting in unison. The blind silver fish live there, clicking through their perpetual shallow. The hollow-bodied insects live there, humming in the dark crown. The algae lives there, black-green in streaks; the single pale tuft lives there, in one high crack, drinking darkness.

But more precisely, the emptiness lives there—given form, given walls, given sound. The chamber is its own inhabitant, running on water and echo, maintained by bodies that have become indistinguishable from its surfaces. You stand in the center, surrounded not by absence but by a fullness that has refused to take shape—motion without object, noise without source, a world of one idea carried to its absolute, wet, resonant center.

What lives there is the idea itself: hollow, cool, loud, made, and complete—held together by nothing but curved, weeping brick and the continuous, layered pulse of things that have forgotten they were ever separate from the room.
