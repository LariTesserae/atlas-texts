What lives here lives at the threshold of living—not dead, but not animated in any familiar register. The inhabitants are the stalks: seven, perhaps eight, scattered like notations in the cracks where wall meets floor, separated by yards of empty dust. They are not green. They never were. They rise in thin, brittle columns—bone-pale, almost translucent, closer to dried quartz fiber or fossilized root than to plant—each one barely taller than a hand’s span, though age is impossible to read in their form. They do not lean toward anything; there is no light to seek, no moisture to chase. They stand perpendicular to the stone, or lie flush against it, absorbing the sustained warmth that seeps from the rock’s core and drinking only the bound water locked inside mineral lattice, never free, never pooled. Their growth is not a process you could observe; it is a statistical drift across centuries, a single cell-length added so slowly that the concept of season does not apply.

They do not reproduce visibly. There are no seeds, no spores, no flowers—only the occasional new stalk, years or decades apart, emerging in a fresh fissure as if the stone itself had decided to try another posture. Each exists in absolute solitude. The density is 1.2: sparse enough that they never touch, never compete, never form a colony. They share nothing—not root-networks, not chemical signals—because there is nothing to share and no mechanism to share it. They are individuals without individuality, present but not present, maintaining.

There is no fauna. The absence is not emptiness but a negative resident: the hollow has been scrubbed of all moving life. No insects navigate the dark; no microbes bloom in the air—though the air is dry to the point of sterility, salted and crushed to powder. The stalks are alone, and they behave as if alone is the only condition that has ever existed.

Yet there is a second inhabitant, inert but undeniable: the flaked stone tool, half-buried near the center of the dish. It is not theirs. It predates them, or perhaps postdates them—time is flattened here. The maker is absent, erased by the same patient erosion that rounded the walls. The tool is simple, hand-knapped, pre-metal, worn smooth by mineral abrasion until its edges have surrendered their sharpness. Near it lie the pebble marks, arranged or fallen into a loose pattern that the dust has already begun to reclaim. These are not structures; they are artifacts that have been adopted by the geology. The tool does not belong to the stalks—they do not use, do not recognize—but it lives among them now, an object that has achieved the same equilibrium: settled, unchanged, complete.

The true inhabitant, then, may be the equilibrium itself, made visible only through these scattered expressions—the pale stalks drawing warmth without fire, the salt-scented air holding its temperature without exchange, the tool resting without purpose. Nothing disturbs them. The stalks do not wither; they have already reached the form of withering that is also permanence. The dust does not rise; it has settled into its final concavity. You, if you sit among them, become part of the density: low, silent, warm, dry, holding your breath in a pocket that has been holding its breath since before the concept of breath existed.

What lives here lives as maintenance. Not growth, not decay—just the sustained fact of being present in a place that has finished becoming itself. The stalks stand in their own silence. The tool lies in its own silence. And between them, the dark is not empty; it is full of the absence of change, inhabited completely.
