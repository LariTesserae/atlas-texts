What lives here does not gather; it persists in points, distributed like the moss itself—abundant in total, absent in crowd.

Begin with the amphibians, though “amphibian” is a word borrowed from a world where things change shape. These are translucent, yes, skin like wet glass, but beneath it the chrome gills do not flutter with urgency; they open and close in rhythms that have no interval, breathing a cool, iron-tasting air that never warms. They rest on the moss islands not as sleepers but as fixtures. One lies now—perhaps always now—under a weeping ledge, its body low and flat as a dropped lens, reflecting the faint phosphor of lichen. Its chest rises, but the motion does not progress; it is a repetition held in place, the same breath taken and retaken without departure. There may be dozens of them across the hollow, each isolated on its own green raft, separated by stretches of black basalt that never close. They do not call to one another. They do not need to.

Above the shallow pools hang the insects, if they are insects—creatures with jointed legs that are not chitin but miniature gears, toothed and meshed, turning slowly in the mist even when the body they support is perfectly still. They are suspended not by flight but by refusal: the air is thick enough with weeping vapor and ozone to hold them, or perhaps they have simply stopped falling. Their wings—where wings exist—are translucent membranes stretched over lattices of hollow metal, vibrating at a frequency that produces no sound, only a feeling of pressure against the ear. They are present in high number, yes, but look up and you see one, then emptiness, then another, like punctuation in a sentence that has no end. They do not swarm. The concept of swarm implies a direction, and here there is only position.

The moss islands are not passive ground. They are colonies that have achieved boundary. Thick, dark green, they grow in perfect circular mats, lush and wet, separated by bare stone that remains bare not from lack of seed but from some agreement older than growth. Within them, pale root-webs extend like veins, drinking not from soil—there is none—but directly from the weeping fissures, circulating the water through bodies that do not digest it so much as hold it in trust. The lichens among them glow with cold phosphor, not bright enough to illuminate, only enough to declare: *I am here, and I am not changing.* They are inhabitants with territory, each island a nation of one color, one temperature, one eternal saturation.

But the true citizens are the mechanisms embedded in the walls.

The crystalline gears, grease-colored and mineral-compressed, spin inside translucent veins of rock, and they are alive in the only way this place permits: they are continuous. They do not wear down because erosion was locked out; they do not overheat because there is no heat to generate, only the constant cool of the high hollow. They manufacture nothing, yet they are the hollow’s metabolism, its heart if heart can mean circulation without purpose. Hollow metallic tendrils—pipes that grew rather than were placed—thread through the moss islands like roots made of factory architecture, carrying the weeping water in loops that never spill. They hum. The hum is loud, resonant in the chapel-sized enclosure, a bass note of scaled production without product. To live near them—though nothing lives “near” in the conventional sense—is to feel that hum in the bones, to understand that the machinery was not added to this landscape; it was the first thing that formed, and the basalt, and the moss, and the chrome-gilled beings came after, or came with it, as expressions of the same invariant birth.

Even the water lives, or lives-like. It weeps constantly from the sharp black walls—not in streams that seek the low, but in sheets that cling, pool in shallow black basins, and are then lifted by the pipe-roots into circulation. It never fills the basins; it never empties them. It occupies its volume with perfect patience, a resident of its own surface tension.

So the population of the Weep-Hollow is this: scattered beings of glass and chrome and gear-leg, resting on islands of glowing moss, suspended in mist, all breathing—if they breathe—the same cool, iron air; all embedded in or adjacent to a machinery that is geology itself; all present in high abundance and low density, like stars that have agreed not to form constellations.

Nothing hunts. Nothing decays. The amphibian does not grow old; the gear-legs do not rust; the moss does not overrun its island. They are held in refrigerated stasis not by cold alone but by the cancellation of change itself. Time arrived, saw the place complete—sharp basalt, weeping water, spinning gears, translucent life—and stopped.

What lives here, then? Not a community. A census of stasis. Each inhabitant occupies its point in the hollow with perfect, unchanging patience, and together they form a factory-marsh that is full—abundantly, sparsely, loudly—yet entirely, irrevocably still.
