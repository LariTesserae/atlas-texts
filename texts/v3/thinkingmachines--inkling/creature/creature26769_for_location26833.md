You do not see them at first—only the architecture suggests they must be somewhere, because nothing here is wild, and nothing is abandoned. The grid is too exact, the paths too deliberately worn, for this to be a ruin. The presence is distributed like the vegetation: sparse, disciplined, separated by long intervals of bare rock and still air. Then, slowly, they emerge into your notice—not suddenly, but as if they have always been in your peripheral vision and only now have consented to be looked at.

They are human in their bones, or were once, though the distinction feels less important here than in other places. They stand about five or six feet, wrapped in woven fiber that has never frayed, never darkened with use, colored in the muted grey-green of moss and unweathered stone. There is no metal on them—no ornament, no weapon, no tool hanging from a belt—only the materials that the valley allows: lashed wood, sinew-knotted rope, garments stitched with a patience that outlasts urgency. Their skin holds the cool of the air, not pale but mineral-tinted, as if they have spent so long among the sharp ridges that the rock’s youth has rubbed onto them without wearing them down.

They are few. For every broad terrace that climbs the valley wall, there may be one—standing at the edge, facing not the view but the exact center of the grid below, motionless in a posture that is neither guard nor prayer. In the central plaza of flat-cut slabs, others sit, not on carved thrones but on the stones themselves, backs straight, hands resting on knees in positions that do not shift with tiredness. Along the narrow lanes, some walk, always alone, never in pairs, never in procession. They follow the paths with absolute fidelity, stepping in the same footprints that have been repeated since the settlement was completed, leaving no new impression because the ground has already given all it will give. Their movement is not slow out of weariness; it is slow because speed is not necessary in a place where nothing arrives and nothing departs.

They do not speak. The valley holds sound at a distance, and they are part of that holding. You might stand near one—close enough to see that their eyes are open, clear, unblinking in the steady, unfiltered light, reflecting the still pools and the sharp ridges with equal calmness—and hear nothing: no breath that rasps, no footfall that strikes differently, no murmur of greeting or command. The birds perch on the walls in patterns that never shift, and the inhabitants share that silence. There is no hierarchy visible in their dress or posture, no elder and no child, because aging has been suspended along with everything else. They are all exactly the age they were when the equilibrium was reached, preserved in mature form, neither withering nor ripening.

What do they do? The settlement does not expand—they do not quarry new stone, do not stack new walls. The vegetation does not overgrow its pockets, and they do not tend it. The water collects in its basins, and they do not draw it with bucket or cup, though they may kneel beside a dark pool and remain there in perfect stillness, their reflection unbroken by thirst. They are not laborers maintaining a city; they are residents in the purest sense—those who reside, occupying the completed form without altering it. Perhaps they once built, with hand and patience, the terraces and the hollowed chambers, and then, when the work was done, they simply stayed, becoming part of the architecture’s intention, as fixed as the grid itself.

You notice, after watching long enough, that they do not interact with the small creatures that move through the open spaces—no startle, no chase, no feeding. The creatures follow their own eternal paths, and the inhabitants follow theirs, and the two patterns intersect without disturbance, like lines of a grid that cross without tangling. Even the broad-winged birds, silent in their hundreds, do not take flight when a walker passes beneath them. There is no fear here, no dominance—only the shared discipline of presence.

Their shadow, if the light is steady enough to cast one, lies where it lies, sharp-edged and unmoving, not because the inhabitants are motionless—some do walk, some do turn their heads with mechanical precision—but because the light itself does not pass, and the shadow obeys the same law. If one stands in a shadow, they are part of it; if they step into the open, they carry the coolness with them but do not warm.

The strangeness, then, is not in their shape but in their refusal to be part of a story that moves forward. They are not ghosts—there is nothing translucent or mournful about them—but they are also not alive in the usual, urgent way. They do not sleep, or if they do, they sleep standing, eyes open, in the same posture they hold while walking. They do not eat, yet they do not weaken. They do not reproduce, yet they are not the last of anything, only exactly the number that the settlement requires, spread thinly across the distance, maintaining the urban bones not with labor but with presence.

To live here, then, is to exist in a finished state. The valley is young in its rock but old in its completion, and its inhabitants are the proof of that paradox—human intention made permanent, walking the grid of a city that will never crumble, resting beside pools that will never flow, silent in a cool, thin air that never changes, part of the abundant, isolated, disciplined life that asks nothing and offers nothing but the exact, unending now of its own perfect form.
