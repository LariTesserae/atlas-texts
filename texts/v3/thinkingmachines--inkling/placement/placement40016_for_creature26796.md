The plateau does not end; it forgets to. You stand on a continent that has given up on falling, worn over epochs into a near-perfect flatness still suspended high above the weather of lower worlds—two miles of altitude rendered meaningless by the softness underfoot. Here the erosion is complete: mountains dissolved into memory, valleys filled and smoothed, rock ground to a kind of hidden substrate, then buried entirely. What remains is the settled-into-final-form, the oldest-stable-state, a world that has finished becoming and now simply persists.

And it is all plant.

Not a forest, not a meadow—those words imply separation between ground and growing thing. Here the vegetation is the architecture. A continuous, warm, spongy substrate stretches in every direction: interlocking roots compressed into walkable mattress, mosses grown to the depth of rivers, fungal plates that have replaced soil with living tissue. The density is near-touching—every step presses into layered life—but the vastness prevents crowding. You could walk for months and find only more of the same woven biomass, changing in shade and texture but never breaking into bare earth.

The air is thick with water. Not rain exactly—though rain comes, slow and warm—but a perpetual near-surface wetness. The plateau weeps. Moisture condenses on every surface, beading on vertical stalks that rise through the carpet like pillars of slow sculpture. In hollows, shallow pools sit motionless, dark with dissolved plant-matter, reflecting nothing in the dim light. The temperature holds at a patient warmth, never spiking, never chilling—just the steady metabolic heat of so much living mass breathing together.

Light is scarce. The sky is present as a pressure, a gray-green weight above, but direct sun does not reach the floor. What illumination exists filters through miles of suspended growth—hanging curtains of moss, translucent leaves, root-webs that dangle like cathedral drapery—casting everything in a greenish twilight. Shadows do not fall sharply; they soak into the surrounding biomass. You see by diffusion.

The fauna is everywhere and almost invisible. Abundant, yes—insects with translucent wings that navigate the root-columns; small, soft-bodied creatures that nest inside hollow stems; larger, slower beings that move through the carpet with the patience of geology, leaving only a gradual ripple in the moss. But the sound is barely there. A faint, constant exhalation from the plants. The drip of condensation. The occasional, distant crack of a dead stem settling. Nothing calls loudly. This is not an ecosystem of alarm; it is one of integration.

Above, the plateau generates its own weather—vast enough that mist rolls in banks hundreds of miles long, warm and clinging, then dissolves back into the vegetation without ever leaving the surface. There are no storms, only slow shifts: a deepening humidity over centuries, a gradual thickening of the carpet in one quadrant, a patient settling of water into new shallow seas of root and rot.

And scattered across this green continent, half-buried and half-emerged, are the remains.

Not buildings in any technological sense—there is nothing here of device or metal or intent as we understand it. The structures are older artifacts, occasional and faint: dome-like shells of petrified fiber, hollow and echoing, formed by some long-completed growth-cycle of the plants themselves. Circular marks in the carpet where immense columns of compressed matter once stood, now collapsed into craters filled with particularly dark moss. A single wall, if wall it is—an arch of hardened root-wood that has outlasted the living tissue around it, standing three feet high and covered in lichens that are themselves centuries old. They are scattered, partial, never gathered into towns. The built presence is ghost-light, a reminder that even this living world has its dead parts, its bones, its memory of form.

The strangeness lies not in spectacle but in continuity. Walk far enough and you begin to feel that the boundary between observer and substrate is thin. The plants do not grow toward light in urgency; they have already filled every available space and now grow in patience, replacing themselves with near-identical tissue, maintaining. The fauna move not to hunt but to circulate nutrients. The water does not flow to the sea but returns immediately to the body of the plateau. It is an ecosystem in its end-state, settled, warm, wet, dim, and vast—patient beyond human measure, quiet to the point of silence, alive in every cubic inch, and finished with change except for the slowest, most ancient rhythms of being.

What lives here is not a population but a continuity—an unbroken negotiation between root and breath, between the compressed dead and the softly urgent now.

Begin with the floor, because there is no other beginning. Beneath your weight, the mattress gives: not soil, but generations of root-hair compressed into a living felt, dense enough to hold your step yet porous enough to drink the constant weeping. And within that felt—always within, never upon—move the first inhabitants. They are barely articulated. Pale, segmented creatures no longer than a finger, translucent in the green twilight, lacking anything so decisive as eyes; they navigate by the chemical gradients of decay and renewal, threading through the fungal plates that have replaced earth. They do not burrow so much as dissolve into the substrate and re-emerge elsewhere, carrying dissolved matter in their bodies, leaving behind faint tunnels that close like lips behind them. They are the circulation of the carpet itself, its internal bloodstream made visible only in the occasional ripple, a slow twitch in the moss like a muscle remembering a dream.

Above them, in the vertical spaces—those root-columns that rise like slow sculpture through the carpet—live the ones with wings. Not insects in the sharp, armored sense, but translucent membranes stretched over fragile ribs, navigating the dim air without urgency. Their wings are not for escape but for hanging: they drift downward through the curtain-moss, land on the stalks, and remain for years, feeding on the condensation that beads on vertical surfaces, mating in the same position, dying into the fibers they clung to. Their bodies, when they fall, do not rot separately but are immediately incorporated—lichen begins on them before they finish settling, and within a season they have become part of the pillar’s texture. They are not visitors to the vegetation; they are its temporary expression, like flowers that have forgotten to open.

Then there are the nesters—small, soft-bodied, warm to the touch if you could hold one without crushing it. They live inside the hollow stems, not in colonies but in solitude, each occupying a chamber formed by the death of a previous root, lined with their own secretions into something like silk, though heavier, wetter, smelling of nothing. They do not sleep in rhythms you would recognize; they enter long pauses, metabolic slowdowns, matching the plateau’s patient warmth. Sometimes, if you press your ear to a pillar—though there is little to hear—you might detect a faint, wet shifting inside, not a sound made for communication but a byproduct of being: the slow rearrangement of a body that has no predators, no urgency, only the need to exist in darkness and moisture.

But the largest ones—those are the ones that change your sense of scale.

They are not beasts of prey or flight. They move through the carpet with the patience of geology, leaving only gradual ripples, as if the ground itself were breathing through them. Their bodies are low, soft, almost liquid in outline, covered not in fur but in a thick, spongy growth that mirrors the carpet itself—moss and fungal tissue that has colonized them as they colonized the floor. They are indistinguishable from the substrate except for their slowness, their deliberate, rolling progress. They do not eat in the sense of hunting; they absorb. They rest in shallow pools of root-water, half-submerged, and the pool darkens around them with dissolved matter they have released, feeding the very plants that support them. They are mobile islands, walking ecology, carrying their portion of the plateau’s metabolism across the flatness over decades, over centuries, leaving behind trails not of destruction but of slight thickening, a denser weave where they have passed.

And then—the remains.

Not dead, exactly, but finished. The dome-like shells of petrified fiber, hollow and echoing, stand in scattered isolation, formed by some long-completed growth-cycle when a cluster of plants decided—if decision is the word—to harden against their own softness. They are not tombs but shells, like the cast-off armor of a creature that never existed. Inside them, if you enter (and you can, for the openings are low and welcoming), the air is slightly different: drier, older, carrying the faint mineral scent of completed life. The nesters sometimes occupy these, not out of need but out of affinity, lining the interior walls with their silk-secretions until the boundary between artifact and inhabitant dissolves. The collapsed columns—those craters filled with darker moss—are not ruins but pools of intensified life, where the root-wood has fallen and decompressed, releasing centuries of stored moisture, and the moss there grows thicker, darker, almost black, feeding on the death of form.

There is an arch, somewhere, three feet high, covered in lichens centuries old. Around it, the larger ones gather—not in herds, for they do not gather, but in proximity, as water gathers near a low point. They rest against it, their spongy bodies merging with the moss-covered stone, becoming part of the architecture of memory. The arch is not worshipped; it is used, incorporated, made into a resting place for beings that do not distinguish between the living root and the dead wood.

Above all this, the hanging world: curtains of moss, translucent leaves, root-webs dangling like cathedral drapery, filtering what little light exists into this green twilight. And in that suspended realm live the smallest of all—creatures not of body but of filament, fungal threads that have achieved a kind of mobility, weaving and unweaving in the damp air, carrying spores not on wind but on the slow convection of warmth rising from the carpet. They are barely there, yet they maintain the connection between floor and ceiling, ensuring that nothing in this world is isolated, that every cubic inch breathes with every other.

What lives here, then, is not separate beings but roles within a single, vast metabolism.

The transparent wing-bearers circulate moisture downward. The segmented ones circulate matter upward. The hollow-stem nesters maintain the internal spaces, preventing collapse. The large, rolling ones redistribute density, preventing stagnation. The fungal filaments bind the vertical and horizontal into one tissue. Even the dead shells and fallen arches serve—the shells as shelters, the fallen columns as wells of deeper darkness where different shades of moss can evolve.

There are no voices. Only the constant exhalation, the drip, the occasional settling crack. The fauna do not call to one another because there is no need to locate; they are always already in connection, linked by the continuous body of the plateau, by the chemical language of the root-carpet that tells every inhabitant where the warmth is deepest, where the moisture has pooled, where a new stem has begun its slow rise.

If you stay long enough—if you sit until the boundary thins, as it will—you begin to feel that you, too, are part of the circulation. Your warmth joins the metabolic heat. Your moisture, your breath, your very stillness feeds into the substrate not as an intrusion but as a temporary addition to the weave. The plants do not grow toward you; they have already filled every space, and now they grow through you, in patience, replacing themselves with tissue that will outlast your observation.

This is the life here: not individual, not dramatic, not even particularly visible. Just the endless, warm, wet, dim persistence—the settled-in, the finished-form, the ecosystem that has completed its becoming and now simply continues, breathing together, in every direction, without end.
