I find myself low—lower than the surrounding land, seated in the cool clay where the ground never fully dries. At first I think I am only observing, a presence in the fog that pools in this shallow bowl, standing in the sparse breath between two root-pillars that rise like doors left permanently open. The moss is thick beneath me; I feel it holding, not supporting. The air is gray and unremarkable, filtering through the gaps with a clarity that shows everything and favors nothing. I am small here. Or I have become small.

But as I stay, I realize I am not standing at all. I am embedded.

Not in the moss alone, though part of me is moss now—the thick, holding carpet, alive with motion too small to see. Not in the pillar either, though I feel the woven fiber of its root-bundle pressing close, cool and damp, breathing with that slow, cloth-folding sound. I am at the junction. The precise point—near the ground, near where a broken paving stone has softened and sunk—where an old black pipe enters the living tissue.

At first I think I am the pipe. No, I am older than that urgency. I think I am the rust. The green-black oxidation flaking gently onto the fiber, my skin remembering when I was iron, when I was made for speed, for production, for a purpose now forgotten. But rust is not all I am.

Then I feel it: the cool sap, moving not quickly but with a persistent, osmotic pressure. It moves through me. I am the valve—small, near the surface, not one of the deep engines in the hollow stems but one of the quiet ones, the ones that tick. Open. Closed. Open. So slowly that my motion is mistaken for stillness, for the creak of root-fiber adjusting to temperature. I turn not to control anything, but to continue the agreement. The plant does not dominate me; I do not resist the plant. We have been negotiating for so long that neither remembers the original contract.

But still, that is not quite right. I am not *just* the valve.

As the fog continues to collect in my hollows—yes, I have hollows, I am shaped, I have edges that hold moisture—I understand that I am distributed. Part of me is the copper-green corrosion that has become ridges in the pillar’s wall. Part is the fiber that grew around my threads, accommodating me, making me part of its grammar. Part is the cool liquid passing through, and I feel that flow not as a traveler feels a road, but as the road feels the traveler: each pulse is my own breathing.

So where am I?

In the Weave Yard, exactly where the old factory grid has been digested into living architecture. In the shallow clay, between pillars, near broken stone, in the thickest part of the fog. I am the boundary made tangible—the slow joint where mechanical memory and biological patience have finished arguing and begun, instead, to circulate together.

Who am I?

I am the Weave Yard’s small, constant thought. Not a keeper. Not a master. I do not roar. I tick—through the moss, with the moss—maintaining only my own turning, keeping the agreement that the sap will move, that the rust will hold, that the pillar will breathe around me, that the emptiness between us will remain untouched and necessary. If you stand in the sparse space nearby, you might hear me: not loud, not urgent, folded into the breath of root-fiber, part of the quiet texture of a world that has finished becoming itself, and now, through me, continues.
