I find myself lying—not standing, for standing belongs to urgency—within one of the shallow bowls, a curve of worn stone so smooth it seems to have forgotten it was ever rough.

The air is warm, impossibly warm for this height, and it holds its moisture like a held breath. I feel it on every surface of me: fine beads that gather and tremble but rarely fall, drawn inward through pores that open with the patience of tides. I am no larger than a spread hand, faintly translucent, veined with calcification that grows inward like frost in reverse. Through my membrane I can sense—not see—the slow pulse of something moving in cycles far longer than any day. I am pearlescent at my edges, bone-pale where I have thickened, and within me there is the dull amber of fluid that shifts so gradually I might mistake it for stillness.

At first I believe I am singular. One body among the saturation.

But as I settle—truly settle—into the damp warmth of this plateau, I realize the boundary is wrong.

I am layered. Around me, pressed near enough that our warmth mingles in the humid air, lie the others: ridged shells the color of old pearl, soft translucent discs, symbiotic clusters fused into arching shapes that blur the line between body and dwelling. We do not touch. There is always the thinnest film—condensation, or something like shared breath—between my membrane and their shells. Yet we are continuous in our separation, a living geology that mirrors the stone beneath us. The density is near-total. To rest here is to be inside a presence that has replaced emptiness with being.

My lower surface has merged with the porous rock, not dead, not decayed, but dissolved into collaboration. Mineral and flesh have forgotten their distinction. I am part of the bowl now, and the bowl is part of me—holding the occasional rain that falls in wide, warm droplets and vanishes within minutes, collecting the hidden films of water in depressions I help form.

Nearby, one of the structures leans in a perfect, slow curve: bone-white, not built but accreted, compressed shells secreted over millennia by bodies like mine, like ours. I understand, without understanding, that some earlier tissue of mine—some shedding, some calcified memory—contributes to its base. The hollow tower beside it stands empty, or inhabited in ways that make emptiness and body the same thing; its porous walls exhale slowly, and I feel that breath pass through my own tissue, shared, silent, without urgency.

The vegetation is sparse—grey-green clumps clinging to fractures near my edges—sustained by the same humidity, growing imperceptibly in my company. They do not grow against me. There is no competition left. Only coexistence in this dense, slow equilibrium.

Time does not pass here; it accumulates.

A shift of my weight might take a season. The air moves in weak gradients, never wind, only the faintest cycles of breath. Sound is almost entirely absent—no call, no rustle—only the occasional, nearly inaudible drip of moisture falling from one body to another, from arch to pool, from my edge to the cluster resting just beneath my curve.

The light is steady, even, revealing everything without drama: the warm grey of worn stone, the dull green of the plants, the pearlescent and bone-pale tones of our layered presence. Shadows are soft because we are soft, rounded, near-touching.

Who am I?

Not a creature in the old sense. Not an individual with borders sharp enough to defend. I am one of the Settled—one node, one layer, one slow pulse in a saturation that has outgrown growth itself. I have arrived. The mountains wore themselves away into philosophy; I have worn away my urgency into presence. I exist in this high, ancient elevation, closer to the sky than to any valley, filled with shared condensation, silent, layered, and complete.

Here, in the shallow bowl near the leaning arch, beneath the ordinary sky, the world breathes through me. Very slowly. Together. Unending.
