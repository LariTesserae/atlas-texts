You would not find me at first by looking for a shape that stays still.

Begin at the floor, near the lower engines—those piston-driven bodies that turn with the slow patience of large animals who have forgotten sleep. There is a shallow dark saucer set into the metal there, one of several scattered like forgotten dishes across the chamber. I rest in it. I am not deep—barely a mirror, barely a pool—dark water holding the faint, mineral patience of something invisible within me. The vibrations reach me constantly: the deep hum of belts turning in circular paths that never complete, the soft clank when a load shifts, the pressure of fluid in thick sheaths running overhead. I shiver in small, circular waves, but I do not spill. I collect.

But that is only where I wait. To know me, you must follow me upward, because I am not contained.

Above, woven in arches across the slender gaps between the three levels of grating, the pipes run—bundled, thick-sheathed, some carrying translucent orange glow like blood under heated skin, others cool only on their undersides where I cling. I bead there, growing heavy with the warmth of young rock and industrial breath, until I fall. The percussion is soft—onto iron, onto the grating, onto the edges of my dark pool—then I gather again, dark and shallow, holding within me the only visible life in this sterility except the brittle gray-green threads that cling near a trough, not growing, only persisting, like distant relatives I never touch.

The heat is not solar; it has never seen sunlight. It comes from the embedded density itself—the brick kilns shoulder-to-shoulder, the combustion chambers flickering orange and crimson behind their doors, the blue-white sparks where insulated cables meet my damp skin. This heat drives me. I evaporate in thin, hissing threads, leaving the saucer, rising through the narrow streets between machinery, threading past gantries and conveyors that move material in endless loops. I am the vertical breath of this compressed town, the only movement that travels freely through all its layers.

At the ceiling—fractured basalt, black glass broken and reset, sharp-edged and hardly older than a rain’s season—I condense. The rock is young; it sheds plates in quiet ticks that settle onto metal below. It has not learned softness. I gather on its cool fractures, beading against the broken reset glass, then seep back into the hairline wounds of the stone, or fall again, returning to the pipes, to the pools, to myself.

So I am distributed. I am the drop and the bead and the thread of steam and the shallow dark mirror on the floor. I have no single body, only a pattern of return. I see the amber bulbs behind their wire cages, hanging irregularly, casting more shadow than illumination—I pass through their dim glow without being warmed by it, for my warmth comes from elsewhere. I feel the absence around me: no insects threading the beams, no rodents in the alleys between boilers, no birds, no breath but the machinery’s hum and my own slow circulation. I am the moisture that makes the air thick, that keeps this pocket uninhabitable to conventional life, enforcing the rule of sterility by my very presence.

Near me, in the water I hold, there may be others—microbial, patient, not growing but waiting in the mineral warmth. They are not seen; they are only implied by my refusal to be pure. Together we form the only biology permitted here: persistence without growth, existence without expansion.

If you came searching for a being, you might miss me, because I am the gap between things. I live in the slender spaces between platforms, in the woven arches of pipe, in the fractures of young stone. I am the cycle that connects the engines to the ceiling, the rock to the metal, the fall to the rise. The chamber breathes, and I am its breath—never leaving, never arriving, only returning, dark and shallow and constantly moving in the heat of this sharp, recently opened hollow.
