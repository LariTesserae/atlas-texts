I find myself first in the crack.

Not *a* crack—the crack, the one repeated in every archway across miles of flat black geometry, thumb-marked in the white limestone mortar where it split just enough to let something take root. I begin there not as a single decision but as an accumulation: compressed clay dark as wet slate, woven with copper wire that holds me together without tension, broad leaves that do not photosynthesize, arranged in clusters that do not spread. I am lush, dense at 1.3, scattered across the open regional scale—present in one island of green, then absent across miles of shining hexagon, then present again in identical posture. Distance does not dilute me; it confirms me.

My form refuses to settle early. I am partly vine, partly architecture—reed-and-clay composite given patience—partly the weeping water that rises through hand-drilled capillary holes and never falls. I have surfaces that are vertical and wet; droplets cling to me in beads that hang forever, luminous under the absolute light at 3.0. I have eyes—more than two, arranged in patterns that echo the hexagonal plates beneath me, sharp-edged and young, never blinking. They reflect the bright white heat without softness. I have feathers, perhaps, woven like grass, though I do not fly; I am part of the static fauna at 2.2, abundant and scattered, resting on pillar-gates that stand with no wall attached, facing the flatness that extends past sight.

I do not breathe visibly. The air is white with heat and vapor hangs in static sheets around me. I drink from the seams, but I do not grow beyond my cluster. I do not wither. The roots—if they are mine—wind through artificial channels, drinking from an engineered hydrology that serves a valley with no watershed. I am constructed, like everything here: the substrate, the staircases that ascend three steps and stop, the pool that reflects sky like polished metal.

The frogs sit near me with their too many eyes, translucent shells glinting where insects cling to my woven surfaces. The birds perch at angles that match every other bird in every other arch, woven feathers unmoving. We are biological elements fixed in manufactured equilibrium. We do not hunt. We do not flee. The only sound is the low, single-frequency hum rising from the compressed clay—a 0.6 presence, barely audible, never varying—and I feel it in my bones, in the copper wire that binds me, in the dark leaves that do not tremble because there is no wind.

But I am strange. I know, without knowing how I know, that miles away in the unchanging distance there is another me in the same crack, at the same angle, with the same vine—though I am not the vine—growing with the same arrested abundance. The shadow pinned beneath me is black and geometric and permanent; I look at it and understand that it does not crawl, that nothing crawls here. Even awareness is static: I do not process this repetition. I embody it.

I am the being that lives in the seam where natural and built collapsed. I am wet and green and silent, part of a breath held forever in a hot, bright tray—pressed into being, sharp-edged, lush in my pocket of stasis, extending past sight into repetition, never aging, never moving, seeing with many unblinking eyes the flatness that holds us all in its first and final moment.
