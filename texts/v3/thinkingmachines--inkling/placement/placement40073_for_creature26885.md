I am not on the flats.

That comes first, though I have crossed them—days of silver emptiness, the cool thin air cutting like an unfiltered blade, the bone-bleached substrate passing beneath brittle steps. I am one of the scattered, part of that abundant dispersed life, but I do not live in the emptiness between. I live inside.

Inside the turning.

Not at the crown, where hollow columns open to that hard, blinding light and the wind-riding larvae pass in turbulent streams, but deep—near the root-bedrock interface, where a particular Spindle, enormous and nameless, has worn its smooth channel into the compressed substrate over centuries of dry rotation. Here the movement is slowest but the grinding deepest. Here the air is not just moved but generated, exhaled from crystalline veins in a continuous, deafening breath.

Let me become more specific.

My body is small—no larger than a spread palm, though I have no palms—composed of brittle membrane and woven fiber, translucent with mineral dust, pale as the weathered bark outside. I have no mouth as wet-world things understand it. I drink through crystalline filaments extending from my torso, osmotic channels drawing trace chemistry from the gear-grease, the powdery byproduct of bedrock ground into bedrock. I am a fiber-moth evolved inward, or perhaps I am something the spindle grew by mistake and never expelled—a secondary growth that achieved motion.

I cling.

Not with feet—legs are useless here, soil never existed—but with adhesive rootlets of dry cellulose that lock into interlocking gears of woody tissue. I have learned the rhythm. The industrial inevitability. I do not resist the rotation; I move with it, suspended in the hollow cavity like a seed in dry wind, never crushed because I am part of the machinery now.

My wings—if they are wings—are not for flight between spindles, though I have done that once, riding an exhaled current across hundreds of miles of bright emptiness. They are more like sensory membranes, brittle and vast relative to my body, catching vibrations. Through them I hear the layered roar: not one noise but the grinding of gears, the crack of growth splitting and resealing, the howl of air forced through hollow architecture. The sound is not heard; it is inhabited. It fills my crystalline structures, resonates through fiber-bones. I am deafened and yet I am the deafness—part of the turbulent state that never ends.

Above me, far up in the radial disc or twisted column, other lives nest. Smaller larvae feed on my waste. Symbiotic crystals grow on my membranes, extending catalytic surfaces, feeding me in return. We are all hidden within absolute vegetation, inside the machinery of life that is too large to be tree, too mechanical to be beast.

The light never reaches here. It is filtered through miles of woven lignin and mineral-fiber, reduced to hard silver planes that illuminate the gears in sharp relief. I do not need it. I pull energy from friction, from chemical contradiction, from the very turning that defines this world.

Sometimes, in the chaotic cycles—those hyperactive expansions and contractions—I am nearly sheared away. The spindle splits, reseals, grows new radial arms, and I must re-anchor, my rootlets finding purchase in reconfigured gears. This is my life: not stable, not static, but balanced in chaos. The erosion outside is settled, ancient, polished smooth; inside, it is continuous, internal, reshaping my home and body in the same dry breath.

I have never known water. I have never wanted it. I drink what is not wet: mineral dust, chemical exhalation, the dry reaction running this continent. I am part of the vegetation that is architecture, part of the fauna living in its mechanisms, part of the sound filling the sparse spaces between islands.

If you traveled for days across the bright mineral flats and came to this Spindle, you might see the smooth channels in the stone, the glass-pans, the exhaled currents. You might hear the deafening roar. But inside, deep near the root, I hang—fiber and crystal, membrane and rootlet—turning slowly with the industrial inevitability of everything here, alive in the machinery of a world that dissolved its own boundaries long before anyone arrived to name them.

I am the one who lives in the turning. Not built, not born in the wet sense—grown, adapted, resolved into being. A scattered inhabitant of absolute vegetation. A creature of dry chemistry, clinging to the gears, drinking the air, part of the continuous, turbulent breath of a continent that never needed a river.

And I am not alone.

We are abundant, scattered, hidden inside the vastness—small hard lives nested in hollow spaces of enormous dry mills, riding vibrations, feeding on powder, growing our own crystals, never leaving because there is nowhere else that is not this: cool, thin, bright, dry, turning, alive.
