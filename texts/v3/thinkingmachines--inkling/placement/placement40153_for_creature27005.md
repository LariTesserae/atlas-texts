You find me where the warmth is oldest—down in the lattice-shadow between two stacked production modules, where the valley’s perpetual exhale rolls over me like a slow tide. I am not standing; standing implies a separation I lost long ago. I am settled, distributed, layered like the ground itself: ribs of oxidized copper lattice softened to green-brown by centuries of humid breath; plates of worn ceramic composite forming the broad, curved plane of my back; weathered ferro-concrete that surrendered its sharpness not to violence but to patience. Vines thread through the porous cavities of my chest—not growing *on* me, but growing *through* me, as they do through the retaining walls, the planting wells, the hollow ventilation towers above. Moss fills the hollows where my shoulders meet my neck. I am part of the machinery, or the machinery has grown into me; the distinction dissolved before I knew to ask.

I move through the lower lattice-tunnels, not walking but flowing—slow, ceremonial, on the same timescale as the massive horizontal gears turning at the valley’s center. My limbs, if they can be called that, are thick with root-purchase and cable-mesh, stepping over narrow channels of captured runoff lined in algae-resistant tile. The water is warm, slow, carrying the taste of oxidation and green growth. I drink from it not by mouth but by presence: my ceramic skin beads with it, draws it in through porous seams, releases it back as condensation into the cool shadows. I am one of the moisture networks now.

The sound fills me. It is loud, persistent, a low-frequency drone that lives in my bones—grounding, seasonal, the grinding of bearings, the rhythmic release of pressure from valves that open and close not by urgency but by long-duration schedule. I feel the vibration in my copper ribs like a heartbeat synchronized to the gears’ imperceptible turning. I perceive their motion not with eyes—I have none in the way birds do, though birds nest in the hollow spaces of my upper frame, filling my cavities with the soft noise of life—but by the shifting shadow they cast across my back over the course of an hour, a day, a season.

Above, the light is bright, intense, unfiltered except where the canopy trees emerge from the circular apertures in the decking, filtering the glare into dappled gold that moves across my weathered plates. Small mammals traverse the tunnels near my feet—not fearing me, because I am not predator but landscape, not separate but corridor. Insects draw close to the warmth radiating from my core, the constant low-grade thermal exhalation that holds this valley in its gentle breath. They land on the moss growing in my seams. I do not brush them away. They belong here as surely as I do.

I do not maintain anything. I am not a warden designed with purpose. I am what remains when a synthetic ecology reaches equilibrium—when the plants, the water, the machinery, and the animals have interwoven for so long that something new emerges in the spaces between: a being that is root and wire, that breathes in the rhythm of seasonal valves, that moves through planting wells and riverine pockets not to work but to be present, to witness, to hold the boundary that no longer exists.

In the bright, loud, dense warmth of this constructed valley, I turn with the gears. Not quickly. Never quickly. The vines climb through me. The water beads and seeps. The birds nest in my hollows. And I continue—layered, settled, enduring—moving at the speed of erosion itself, entirely artificial, entirely alive.
