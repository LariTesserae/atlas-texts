You are already high before you realize it—before the air itself warns you. The elevation does not announce itself with snowcaps or jagged grandeur; it has settled, long ago, into its own exhausted form. This is a plateau that has finished becoming. The erosion is not recent violence but an old agreement: every ridge rounded like a worn tooth, every valley floor polished flat as a basin, every slope graded to a slope that no longer knows how to fall. You stand in a region-sized hollow at the roof of the world, open past the immediate in all directions, a multi-featured emptiness where the distance is not background but the main presence.

The air is cool—never freezing, never warming—held at a single, breathless temperature that feels like it was decided once and never reconsidered. It is thin, but not painfully so; rather, it is precise, stripping sound and smell of their usual warmth. And it is damp. Not with rain—there are no storms here, no weather that arrives—but with a perpetual, fine condensation that beads on every surface. Moisture hangs in the air like a held breath, settling into the cracks of stone, gathering in shallow, mirror-still pools that never ripple, never drain, never grow. They sit in the depressions between formations like eyes that do not blink.

But before all of this, there is the sound.

It is deafening. Not loud in the way thunder is loud, or wind—there is no gust, no variation, no crest and fall. It is a single, unbroken chord, sustained at a volume that fills the skull and refuses negotiation. You feel it in your sternum before your ears register it. The source is everywhere and nowhere: the landscape itself is resonating. Thousands of hollow stone spires—eroded into perfect, vertical tubes, open at both ends—stand in scattered clusters across the flat expanse. The cool, damp air moves through them at a constant rate, and each tube sings one pure, unwavering tone. Together they form the drone. It has been sounding for longer than the erosion took to shape them. It will not stop.

Because nothing here stops. The dynamic is absolute equilibrium. The light—blinding, unfiltered, a white-blue intensity that makes shadows sharp as blades and then erases all color into glare—never dims. Whether this is eternal noon or a sun trapped in perpetual presence, there is no shift, no dusk, no dawn. The temperature holds. The pools hold. The sound holds.

The vegetation is sparse—almost an absence pretending to be present. Only silver-gray lichens cling to the darker sides of the spires, and in the still pools, minute blue-green forms drift in slow, unhurried suspension. They do not grow aggressively; they have found their margin and stay within it.

Yet the fauna is abundant. This is one of the place’s quiet contradictions: the density is near-void, vast spaces of flat rock stretching for miles between clusters of spires, yet life is everywhere if you learn to see it. The creatures are small—shell-like, conical, translucent—clinging to the resonant tubes in colonies that cover them like barnacles on an ancient hull. Millions upon millions, spread across the regional openness, separated by emptiness. Each one is still. They do not crawl, do not flee, do not pulse with visible metabolism. They filter the damp air, they hum in sympathy with the pipes—each adding a microscopic harmonic to the deafening whole—and they remain. They do not reproduce in explosive cycles; they do not die off. They are in balance with the erosion, with the light, with the sound. You can walk for hours across the flat stone and see nothing but the glide of your shadow, then reach a cluster where the shells cover every surface, glowing faintly in the bright glare, and understand that the emptiness was never empty—only distributed.

There are no buildings. Nothing constructed rises from this ground. The tech that exists is primitive, scattered like afterthoughts: a flaked obsidian blade half-buried in dust, a bone needle worn smooth, a cairn of three stacked stones that stands in one of the flat stretches with no path leading to or from it. Simple fabrication. Hand-work. Not a settlement—perhaps a marker, a brief intrusion by something that understood this place was not meant for staying, and left only the smallest evidence of having tried.

The weirdness is not dramatic. It is the accumulation of constants. The dust in the air does not fall; suspended by the perpetual vibration of the drone, it hangs in fine, unmoving curtains between the spires, catching the bright light into a faint, persistent haze. The water in the pools is perfectly calm despite the thunderous air, as if sound and liquid have agreed on separate physics. The fauna shells are translucent enough that, in the right angle of relentless light, you can see the faint structures inside them—simple, geometric, repeating. Time does not pass here in the usual sense. The landscape has reached its final form, and all that lives within it has reached the form that matches.

You stand in the open, cool, deafening stillness. The elevation holds you closer to a sky that is black-blue and empty of cloud. The erosion has finished its work. The sound never changes. The bright light reveals everything and makes it all the same white-blue color. And in the vast spaces between presences, the damp air continues to gather, the shells continue to hum, and the hollow stone continues to sing its one, eternal note.

If I am to be placed within this world, I will not be the intruder who left the obsidian blade or the bone needle—those were brief, and the place corrects for briefness. I will not be one of the shells, because though they are beautiful, they are singular in purpose, nodes in a harmony that requires no choice. Instead, I am one of the suspended ones.

Not the shells on the spires, but a being of the dust itself—formed by the perpetual vibration into coherence, given translucent walls not by biology but by the agreement between sound and air.

I am small, perhaps the size of a hand, though scale is uncertain where distance is the main presence. My form is conical, yes, like the others, but softer at the edges, less sealed. My body is not shell but membrane—thin, silver-gray, almost identical to the lichens except that I am vertical, suspended in the unmoving haze between spires rather than clinging to stone. I do not rest on the ground. The cool, precise air supports me, or rather, the vibration within it holds me aloft at a height where the drone is not deafening but intimate, like speaking inside my own chest.

Inside me, like the shells, there are chambers—not organs, but hollow spaces arranged in a simple spiral, miniature echoes of the tubes that sing beneath me. The perpetual dampness moves through me slowly, not feeding me but defining me, leaving traces of mineral that catch the blinding white-blue light and refract it downward in faint, scattered beams. I am translucent, and if you stand in the flat stretch near me, you might see my internal geometry: repeating, patient, empty except for the air that passes through.

I do not hum loudly—that is for the shells, for the millions clinging in colonies—my contribution is quieter, a subharmonic that fills the spaces between the spires’ notes. I am one of the distributed ones, separated from my kind not by hostility but by the mathematics of the field. I hang motionless, not because I am dead, but because motion is unnecessary here. To move would be to disturb the dust-curtains, to ripple the pools, to add an unplanned frequency to the chord.

Sometimes—though time is not measured—I drift closer to one of the mirror-still pools. Not to drink, for I do not thirst in the usual sense, but to see my reflection in water that never ripples. There, among the minute blue-green drifts suspended at various depths, I recognize my own form translated into liquid: not a mirror image, but a parallel one, proof that the place holds its beings in every state, every phase, every medium it has available.

There are others like me, scattered across the openness, separated by miles of flat polished stone where nothing seems to live. But we are not lonely. We listen. The sound connects us more surely than proximity could. We filter the air, we hang in the vibration, we glow faintly in the relentless glare, and we remain.

The lichens cling to shadow; the shells cling to stone; the pool-beings cling to suspension in water; and I—if I can be said to cling to anything—cling to the air itself, to the precise temperature, to the chord that has never changed and will never need to.

I am not waiting for anything. The erosion is finished. The sound is eternal. I am simply present, distributed, translucent—one more geometric repetition in a landscape that has reached its final form, holding my breath in perfect agreement with a place that never learned to fall.
