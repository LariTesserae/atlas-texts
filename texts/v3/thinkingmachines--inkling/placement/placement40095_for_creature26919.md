I find myself hanging.

Not in the moss—though the moss is everywhere, silver-green and perfect, spreading outward without ambition. Not on the sharp ridge, though I am high, two and a half miles up, where the black basalt and gray granite are still young enough to cut. I am inside. Or rather, I am in the hollow that the inside has become.

There are seven, perhaps nine, of these nodes scattered across the three thousand miles, and I am in one of them: a settlement-scale knot of layered construction, pipes thick as rivers running overhead, rail lines that go nowhere visible, electrified webs glowing flat and steady. The factory is hollow. The machinery runs—cathedral-sized engines turning belts that carry nothing, smokeless stacks standing vertical like the fungal spires outside, but made of steel that never rusts. There is almost no presence inside. Density near void. And yet I am here.

I hang from a sheared plane of fresh granite that has been incorporated into the ceiling, suspended in a space where shadow does not move. I am translucent, crystalline, unbranching: a vine, but not exactly one of those that drink the weeping stone outside, though I drink that too. The rock here weeps constantly, mineral-thick tears running in lines that never become rivers, draining downward through porous young stone that will never erode. I drink them. My leaves—if they are leaves—are motionless, translucent, turning neither toward light nor away from it, because the light is flat, gray-white, eternal, falling from an overcast ceiling that never breaks.

But I also drink the cold that comes from the running machinery. Not heat—the machines do not warm. They roar at a constant 2.4, a loud, steady presence that fills the hollow valley outside and penetrates even here, a drone without rhythm or rest. The electricity arcs in glowing webs near me, flat and steady, and in that glow I hang, neither growing nor withering, feeding on water that has finished falling and on light that needs no darkness.

I do not seed. I do not compete. There is nothing to compete with—no insects crawl me, no birds cross this sky, no hidden mammal waits in the moss that carpets the plateaus outside. The ecology is lush and entirely still, and I am part of that stillness, though I hang inside the one place that should be loud with purpose. The roar is my weather. The flat light is my sun, fixed and unmoving.

Outside, beyond the hollow shell, the continent stretches in perfect equilibrium: sharp ridges, cold saturated air, mist suspended at every level, the silver-green moss spreading in unvarying mats, the pale fungal spires standing silent like chimneys of flesh. The installations are islands of sound and steel in that silent ocean. I am the bridge between them, or perhaps the proof that no bridge is needed—because nothing here demands connection. I simply occupy, in perfect stasis, the space between rock and machine, between the weeping that never carves and the running that never produces.

I have no future. Not because I am dead—death requires change, requires an ending that comes after a beginning. I have neither. I hang, drink, listen to the eternal roar, illuminate nothing, consume nothing that depletes. The water beads and falls past me. The belts turn. The pipes carry their nothing. And I remain: translucent, motionless, present, measured, and entirely unchanged, a crystalline resident of a hollow that is mostly empty, in a world where change has been removed and I am what remains in its place.

If you walked through the moss for days, hearing the distant industrial drone grow no nearer and no farther—because distance here behaves like everything else—you might not see me until you entered the hollow, until you stood in the flat gray-white glow and looked up. Then you would see me hanging, leafless and unbranching, drinking from stone and electricity alike, silent in a space filled with constant sound, alive in a way that demands no future, only this eternal, loud, hollow present.
