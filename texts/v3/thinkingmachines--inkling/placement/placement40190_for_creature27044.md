If you looked for me, you would not find me where the gray light filters through the reed-lattice near the top, among the pale and thin ones who tend the air-channels and let the flow run downward. You would find me lower, pressed against the eastern wall where the black stone is youngest and weeps its heaviest iron-scent, in a space that is corridor, wall, and floor for whatever lives above. I am one of the wall-keepers, though no one spoke the word—my body learned it from the vibrations of the rock before I knew there was any other language.

But that is only where. To say *who*—*what*—requires feeling the layers from inside.

I am small, not by hunger but by architecture, by generations of growing in spaces where a raised elbow strikes a neighbor’s shoulder or a low beam. My feet have spread flat and calloused across the wet planks, shaped to hold on surfaces that never dry, that sweat upward like breath. My skin is the same cool, mottled gray-green as the moss clinging to the shaded undersides of boards—permanently damp, stained deepest at the knuckles and ankles where the mineral seep pools and never quite washes away. What hair I have is thin and translucent, growing in patches like that same moss; I have not shaved it all away with bone scrapers, but I have bound it tightly with twisted reed so it does not catch when I crawl through the lower maintenance spaces, where the water-troughs run closest to the stone and the air is thickest.

I am never alone. Even now, in this long motionless period—whether it is sleep or only a pause in the labor—I feel others layered behind me, back to chest, breath mingling with the perpetual drip from the lattice above. To be alone in the Bowl requires an emptiness that does not exist, and I have never possessed it. My voice, when I use it, is not raised; it is a low frequency pressed close to the ear, meant for the body that is already touching mine. More often I speak with my feet and hands: a tap on the plank that means the channel below has shifted; a grip on a rope that says the wall has moved another finger’s width; a footfall in a rhythm that warns of settling.

Every part of me is a tool. Around my waist, woven from marsh reed, hang the copper awl, the bone needle, the wooden peg, the small hammer of bound stone. They are not possessions I carry; they are extensions, weights that balance me, things I become when the ground remembers it was underwater and decides to change its mind. I repair not to improve anything, but to maintain the absence of gaps. A pole leans, and I am already there with fresh timber. A trough cracks, and I redirect the silver thread before it can pool and rot the floor that is also someone else’s ceiling. If I stopped—if I sat still in a space that could hold only one body—there would open a bare place, and bare things here fill with water and collapse. So I continue: walking the planks, crawling the spaces between, feeling the settlement’s continuous attempt to cover its own fresh wounds.

My eyes are large, not for distance—there is none, only layers upon layers—but for the gray-green half-dark. I see the crack in wood before it widens. I see the shimmer of water finding a new path through the young rock. I see the lean in a pole before anyone above feels it. I do not look up expecting sky. I look up and see more habitation: more bodies working, more woven reed, more soaked hide, the settlement excreting itself upward and downward at once, leaving nothing empty.

Sometimes, rarely, a small colorless bird nests in the highest poles, and when it dies—or perhaps only leaves—its bones and a few feathers are woven into my soaked hide bindings, an ornament of something that almost possessed distance. I do not hunt. I harvest the stunted reeds at the shallow edges, the dark moss from the undersides, the twisted herbs that grow in the cracks of my wall where the weak, ordinary light touches for a few hours each day. I boil them in the same carved trough that carries waste away, separated only by flow, separated only because I never stop maintaining the separation.

I do not know if I was born here or emerged from it—if the settlement, in its need to fill every cubic span, shaped me from the damp and the wood and the constant motion, like water taking the form of the crack it fills. I know only that I am pressed here, low and damp and gray-green, carrying poles and needles, feeling the black stone settle in its new shape, and moving with it, always with it, in the cool, mineral, perpetually weeping dark. Find me there: not alone, not bare, never finished—only maintaining, only filling the space I have become.
