I do not arrive. I have always been in the gap—born in a hollow knot where timber meets splitting root, where the old hand-laid stone breathes out cold moisture and the moss seals every crack with living tissue. The ceiling above me is jagged, young, a collapsed arch that never finished falling; I know its shape not by sight but by the particular drip-pattern of condensation on my fur, the way water finds the same path through root and fracture every slow hour.

I am small. No larger than two hands pressed together. My body is covered in dull, thick fur that holds the mist close to my skin, a cold insulation against a world that offers no warmth except in the pockets of decay—those warm, barely-there zones where fungal growth blooms thickest in the layered timber. My eyes are dark and open, always open, catching the faint green phosphorescence of the vine-columns not to navigate but to feel the outline of the settlement that contains me. The vines hang sparse; there is space between them, wide pockets of empty air that I move through, and in those gaps I am visible only as a shift in density, a slow passage of fur and breath.

I live within the compressed logic of the place. The flat stones—the ones laid by hands long gone—are not ruins to me. They are ground. I cross them to reach the pools where amphibians lie in slow layers, their slate-skin bodies pressed close, breathing with visible patience. I do not disturb them. We share the stillness. Above us, in the wet timber, the insects cling in dense, unmoving mats, their translucent wings folded like pearl scale-mail; when I pass, they ripple—no sound, just a shimmer of lifted wing and resettled weight. I feed on what falls: dead leaf-matter, fungal spores, the softer tissues of things that have fallen still. I am predator, yes—there are smaller ones, hairless and blind, hatching in moss-lined hollows near me, and I have taken them—but I am also taken, watched by the larger amphibians whose tongues move like pale threads, by the mammalian shapes that hunt the same root-corridors in absolute silence.

My nest is in the infrastructure itself. I have burrowed into the space where a wooden peg has loosened, where a root has split the primitive wall just enough to create a chamber lined with moss and old fiber. There I breed, slowly, in darkness—small shapes with eyes sealed, growing into the cold. The young erosion serves me: roots open new hollows, water shifts to create new pools, the ceiling drops fragments that become shelter. I do not build; I continue. The settlement was hand-built, urban in its logic if crushed in its span, and I inhabit its logic now—using the carved channels as paths, the platforms as territory marked by scent too subtle for other senses, the layers of stone and timber as the architecture of my life.

The sound I make is part of the quiet. A brush of fur against moss. The soft shift of weight on flat stone. The collective exhalation of my kind—thousands of silent lungs breathing in the black-green gloom. I hear the water only as absence: no rush, just the intermittent percussion of drops falling into pools where amphibians do not flinch.

I am not a guest. I was never invited, and I never leave. I am the continuity—the digestion of the old human grid into living tissue, the breeding that fills hollow knots, the slow chase through root-corridors that ends not in scream but in feeding. When I die, the fungal mat will take me; the vines will grow thicker; the flat stones will bear another layer of breathing bodies. I am one of the overwhelming, silent lives that make this pocket not a ruin but a world—sharp-featured, fresh, never settled, never finished, always weeping, always glowing faintly green, always full.
