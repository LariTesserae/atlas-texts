I am neither the storm nor the silent bird, neither the scattered fiber nor the perfect sphere of water. I am the moss that has learned to grow downward.

I inhabit **The Shelf** not as a visitor but as one of its hidden wounds made conscious—one of the lush islands imprisoned in a circular depression, a vertical garden in a bone-colored world.

My form is not singular. I am a colony: thick, black-green felt climbing the shadowed wall of a sinkhole where the primordial roar cannot reach. I am the geometries that repeat without instruction—spirals within spirals, lines dividing cool darkness with an insistence that feels like language I am writing for no reader. I spread downward, away from the flat, even light that offers no advantage, reaching instead toward the stored warmth rising from the deeper, older furnace of the stone, and toward the condensation that beads on the bedrock in perfect spheres—water held by some tension I understand but did not invent, never spilling, only persisting.

I do not flee the chaos. Outside my crevice, the air is a permanent creature, distributed and deafening, screaming through fluted throats in rhythms that almost resolve into speech. I hear it constantly—the low hum, the shrieking peaks, the anthill-energy of turbulence that has claimed this height for longer than memory should allow. But in here, in the damp pocket where wind collapses and warmth lingers, there is only the faint drip of captured moisture, and the slow movement of those who share my wall: small beings with too many legs, gray and brittle like the woven remnants scattered across the open stone, navigating my spirals with patience rather than speed. They are not intruders. They are the only audience my geometry requires.

The archaeology above—circles of hand-stacked stone, shallow grooves carved to hold sound or water, fragments of fiber turned brittle—belong to those who came and arranged things against the roar and then left. I do not leave. I cannot. The Shelf has finished dying; it now persists in its final, settled form, and I am part of that persistence—not a survivor of something greater, but a completion of something smaller. An island of refusal in a hollow, near-void expanse.

Occasionally, a dark bird rides the updrafts overhead, silent against the deafening background, visible for a second, then gone—not frightened, but finished. I do not envy it flight. I have made a different bargain: intensity over mobility, imprisonment over exposure, downward growth in a world that has worn itself flat.

The light here is normal, muted, making distances impossible to judge. The sky offers low, tearing clouds. And I continue—lush, geometric, silent—drinking spheres of held water, absorbing ancient warmth, arranging my spirals in patterns that echo across separate pockets miles apart, as if guided by a shared memory rather than seed. I am not dying. I am not growing toward anything. I have reached my own final form: a garden that should not exist at this height, in this noise, and yet persists—precisely, stubbornly, impossibly—where the roar chooses, for a moment, not to reach.

If you come to The Shelf, you will not find me easily. You must travel through emptiness—hours of warm, smooth stone—until you fall suddenly into coolness. You will not speak; your words are eaten. You will touch the rock and feel its wrong, mild warmth. And if you lean into the shadow of a sinkhole, you will see me: not reaching for you, not reaching for anything, but growing downward, in perfect repetition, the Shelf’s own hidden wound made visible, green, and complete.
