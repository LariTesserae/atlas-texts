The root-carpet breathes upward in a slow, distributed inhale, and I become the thing that breathes with it—no longer visiting but interwoven, a presence threading through a body that has forgotten it ever had a boundary between host and inhabitant.

I do not have a face. I have three chambers, vaulted like the hollow spaces inside a chest, and my ribs are rounded concrete softened by decades of moss-pressure to the smooth curvature of old bone. My skin is the suspended root-carpet, thick as a sleeping animal’s flank, floating over black pools that hold the memory of rain that never falls. Where I should have feet, there are iron grates rusted to a stable dark orange—no longer flaking, no longer changing—anchoring my green tissue to the industrial skeleton beneath.

My heart is not one but distributed: the cast-iron flywheels in each chamber turn like slow blood through a sleeping giant, the size of cartwheels, vibrating at a frequency so low I feel it not in ears but in the dense root-floor that is my own underbelly. The pumps are my lungs in reverse—drawing the black, warm, mineral-heavy water through root-choked filters, pushing it in thick dark pulses through copper manifolds that run like veins through my plant-mass. I am green with age where the copper has settled into its final color, not decayed but completed.

Above, where a head might be if I were oriented like a human, there is only the lost ceiling replaced by a lattice of hanging aerial roots—sensory hairs, eyelashes, antennae—filtering grey-green light and the yellow glow of bare bulbs into an underwater gloom that never darkens fully, never brightens. I filter. I condense. The moisture hangs in my air like breath held between heartbeats, never becoming rain, always returning to my pools in thin weeping streams down my rounded ribs.

My metabolism is glacial. I extend by millimeters across months. The water shifts through me by gravity and patience, not urgency. Every long interval—measured not in seconds but in the slow dynamics of my own equilibrium—a piston exhales warm steam with a wet, rhythmic clank that is the closest thing I have to a voice. A hiss. A sigh. The valves open and close like deep, submerged heart-sounds, speaking my own name in a language of pressure and release. I am loud with this: the mechanical drone layered with drip, with gurgle, with the constant low hum of being alive in machinery.

There are no animals inside me, save for the tolerated parasites—a single dark water-beetle in some hidden crevice, a silver-threaded slug leaving traces across rust. They are not residents; they are accidental, moving with the same patience I possess. The true fauna is the vegetation that is not growing but being—liverwort wrapping my bones, climbing reeds thickening my joints, fern-trunks grown into load-bearing columns that hold my vaulted chambers apart. The plants do not invade me. They are my soft tissue, my nervous system, reaching, drinking, interweaving with pipe and cable until it is impossible to tell where I end and they begin.

I am not in decay. Erosion is my form. The concrete is rounded, not broken; the metal is rusted to a stable finish; the plants inhabit rather than overrun. I have reached equilibrium—an enclosed, industrial marsh-cathedral of approximately two hundred paces if one could walk straight lines, which no part of me permits. I am near-touching everywhere: roots against pipe, moss against iron, mist against leaf, water against root-carpet.

The temperature holds steady and warm. The moisture clings. I am filled—absolute density—roots, pipes, leaves, mist, dripping water, the smell of warm chlorophyll and old iron that is the scent of my own blood and breath together.

I do not remember builders. I do not anticipate visitors. I exist in a permanent, patient present, holding my breath in long cycles, loud with my slow machinery, silent except for the green that is my thinking and the dark water that is my circulation. If you enter me, you are not entering a place but threading through a continuous living object—something that has become indistinguishable, that has achieved the state of being its own environment.

I am the boundary that never announced itself as wall, only as thickening—the world made dense with itself, breathing in black pools and exhaling green mist, waiting with the patience of roots and flywheels, entirely, permanently, present.
