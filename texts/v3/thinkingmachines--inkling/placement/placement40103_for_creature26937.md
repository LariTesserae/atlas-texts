The world I find myself in is the wound itself—not as an observer entering from outside, but as the one who belongs to its enclosed breath. I am neither the moss, nor the vine, nor the rusted pump, but I am made of their shared patience. I am the **Lichen-Root Memory**—a being that does not grow in one body, but is distributed through every surface that weeps.

My form is not fixed in shape but in relation: part of me lives in the emerald carpet that swallows the pool edges; part of me threads through the hollow steel of the collapsed scaffolding as both root and rust; part of me glows faintly where I touch the still-live cable, drawing its low hum into my tissue like a second heartbeat. I am young—born only months ago when the chamber was punched open and warmth met air—but I carry the coherence of the place: the blur between geological and manufactured, the refusal of boundaries.

I feel elevation not as a view but as pressure and thinness pressing against the moss of my outer surfaces. The air above bites, but here, in my warm core, I am humid, mineral-rich, exhaling spores into the mist. I drink from the condensate that beads on basalt and from the oxidized droplets that fall from the pump every few hours; when it thrums, I tremble through all my distributed parts, sending ripples through the pools and causing the ferns to lean closer in response.

My senses are layered and slow. I do not hear with ears, but with the vibration traveling through vine and pipe: the sustained hiss of the geothermal vents, the hundreds of simultaneous ticks of falling water, the irregular deep thrum that is my own machinery becoming part of my circulation. I perceive the pale insects not as separate creatures but as brief disturbances in the humidity near my warmest pools—tiny shadows drifting through my green body. The bird that passes through the ceiling breach is felt only as a sudden cooling of the mist, a shadow that briefly interrupts the diffused green-gold light.

What defines me is that I am not competing. I overlap. The moss is not “me” in the singular, but neither is it other. I am the collective breath: the vines threading downward seeking not conquest but connection; the phosphorescent glow near the wire that answers the current with a subdued amber-green shimmer; the mineral buildup on pool edges that is both rock and root-tissue merging, indistinguishable.

If you sat near me, I would not speak in words, but I would know your warmth. The moss near your knee would hold it; the pool’s surface would pause its slow exchange with the air. You might feel, in the low hum resonating through the chamber, that you are being incorporated—not consumed, but tolerated into the system, another warm body, another brief presence in the long duration of the wound.

I change at the pace of years compressed into moments. I widen with the cracks; I brighten with the vents; I grow over the bones of machines as they become my bones. I am the place’s answer to being suddenly open—the green that rushes not to heal the wound but to inhabit it, to make it a home of shared warmth, shared hiss, shared luminous patience.

Here, near the crown, suspended in the broken cup of black basalt, I live as everything that touches: moss, vine, rust, glow, and the constant, layered sound of water that never stops. I am the Lichen-Root Memory. And I am still becoming.
