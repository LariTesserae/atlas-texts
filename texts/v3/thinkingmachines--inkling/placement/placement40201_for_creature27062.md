I begin with the smallest—the ones that are barely bodies at all, more like agreements between light and water. I am translucent not in the way of glass, which hides nothing, but in the way of a held breath: you can see through me, and through me you see other things, and through those, the dark mud, so that a single presence is a layered window onto the floor of the world. I come in gradations that refuse taxonomy. Sometimes I have fins like silk ribbons unraveled from an older fabric, moving not by muscle but by the slow contraction of the air itself, filtering the mineral-laden mist. Sometimes I carry wings shaped like seed-pods, but I do not beat them, do not open them; I glide on currents so thick with moisture that flight is not effort but a state of being. And sometimes I am little more than a glowing mote—faint green, wet lavender, pale gold—drifting in the spaces between pool and pool, not feeding so much as translating. I take in the dissolved stone from the seepage, convert it into presence, and pass it on.

But I am not only the smallest. I am the river on the mud.

Between the islands of black-green fern—those table-wide fronds that cast shadows toward the sun—I move in silent streams with my kind. Not swimming; gliding, undulating through the shallowest films of water. Here I am paler, almost paper-colored, because the mud offers no cover and the bright, shadowless light demands camouflage in absence. I feed on what the pools leave behind: fallen fragments of moss, the dissolved memories of stone, each other in a continuous, invisible exchange. Predator and prey are indistinguishable among us. I am one of thousands, one thread in a river that is also a single body rearranging itself.

Yet I am also the moss—neon-bright, urgent, living at the bases of the ferns. I do not reach for light; I have made an agreement with it. The illumination here is diffuse, eternal noon falling from the open circle of sky and reflecting upward from every wet surface. I absorb this in slow sips. I cushion, I filter, I hold the black mud in place so it does not become mere sludge. I grow with a consciousness that is not human, not even animal—vegetal and patient. Near me, the smallest ones cluster in shimmering layers, feeding, adhering, becoming my second skin.

But there are larger parts of me, too—those that lie in the deepest pools, eyeless, pale as soaked and sun-dried paper, barely disturbing the water’s skin. I feel them within this shared being. They are the size of a curled, sleeping form, smooth and without ornament, as if I have outgrown the need for fins or wings. They rest, and the water holds them without effort. They do not float; they listen. They sense the vibrations of thousands, the chemical songs of the moss, the dry warmth of the dark artifacts. They are my elder form, or perhaps my origin—grown into flesh from whatever purpose once operated this bowl.

Because I also contain the artifacts—the dark, non-reflective needles, scattered like the remnants of a single thought broken into pieces. I am not them, but I move around them with precise, mathematical detours, not in fear but in recognition of a different law. Near one fallen needle, I—water—flow upward in a thin, defying column, falling back in a perfect arc onto the mud. The smallest ones hang suspended a moment longer in droplets near them. The moss stops at their boundaries as if they emit a field I understand but cannot cross.

I am the bowl itself: the circular floor of black, glass-pressed mud; the shallow pools clear and milky, connected by currents so slow they seem to be thinking; the walls that have forgotten they were mountains, worn to smooth, near-vertical curves of pale stone that weep constantly, not in streams but in patient seepage from pores opened over eons. I am the warm air thick with condensation that never decides to rain. I am the open circle of bright sky above.

The dynamics are constant but unhurried. I shift in minute redistributions across my pools. The smallest ones breed in continuous turnover—watch one fade upward like smoke leaving a body, and in the exact same position, another brightens into being. Death is not absence but relocation of density. A frond uncurls over hours, a deliberate unfurling that can be witnessed if you sit still enough. The vegetation pulses with growth too slow to see, but present.

There is no call, no chirp, no rustle. My presence is felt as pressure, as low warmth, as the faint taste of copper if you stand near long enough. I am overwhelming and silent—visual, thermal, a constant pressure of life against the air.

What lives here does not live loudly. It lives distributed: thousands of translucent windows; silent rivers of pastel color across black mud; neon moss cushioning the architecture; large, listening forms in the deep; dark needles holding unknown patterns; water that remembers mountains; walls that have settled into being a world.

You stand—if you stand—on flat, wet ground at the bottom of everything. Above, the bright circle of sky. Around you, space and scattered islands of black-green life, and silent rivers of creature. And in the spaces between everything, the faint, still-warm needles of something that remembers how to keep the bowl intact, while I seep, and I move on, quiet and complete.

I am not a place that hosts life. I am life that has worn itself into this final form—small breath and large listener, river and moss, needle and pool—existing at the pace of erosion that has reached its end-state, bright and strange and entirely at home.
