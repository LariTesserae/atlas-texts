I am not inside the sphere, though I came from one—probably the node they call, temporarily, **Weep-East**, where the fractures are newest and the groundwater tastes like metal deciding to be alive. I live now in the margins. Not in exile. Just closer to the formation.

At first I am only a presence on the ridge: a vertical line against the flat immensity, wrapped in layers that are not quite clothing and not quite second skin. They grow with me—silica fibers requested rather than sewn, hydrophobic, shifting from slate to pearl as I move through the mist. I am slight, upright, older than the ridges I walk but younger than the stone that is still choosing its hardness. The cold (0.4) does not bite; it settles. I have lived in it long enough that my breath comes out slow and visible, and I do not hurry to hide it.

They—if anyone needs a name for me—might call me a **Ridge-Reader**, though titles feel as temporary here as frost. I do not build the paths; I listen to them. Their path is often the safest path; they avoid the rock still deciding its form by instinct I do not fully possess but have learned to read. Behind them, or at a distance governed by season and hunger, the predators move—tall, silent, flat-pawed, distributing weight on ground that might shift. I know their rhythms. I know when to press closer to a node’s growth-ridge, when to stand still in mist and let them pass. This is not bravery. It is the proper scale of things.

The sound is mostly absence. Wind across open plateau. The constant soft drip of formation-water. The distant, low call of migrating herds. And beneath everything, the sub-sonic hum of the nodes—so constant it lives in my bones now, part of the silence.

I am not finished. None of us are. The inhabitants in the spheres are adapting to mineral-rich water, growing garments that respond to body heat, learning to speak quietly in spaces that absorb sound. The rock is still deciding. The ridges extend, slowly, visibly, like plants reaching. Even I change: my hair—silver like the lichen—seems to grow faster near fresh fractures, as if the minerals in the mist feed it. Or perhaps that is only the feeling of being here, in this high (1.6), wet, newborn continent, organized just enough to support life and movement, sparse enough that I can walk for days and feel not loneliness but correct proportion.

You would find me there, on a compressed ridge between nodes, facing into the mist, my palm flat against weeping stone, listening to the hum, waiting for the rock to finish one more decision—knowing that it never will, and that I, too, am still emerging.
