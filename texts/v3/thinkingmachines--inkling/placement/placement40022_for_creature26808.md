The throat first. Not a drop, but a narrowing—brick laid by hands that never knew metal, each course crumbling, then repacked, then worn smooth by shoulders that followed and followed. You descend through that constriction, and the world does not open; it *closes*. Not wild, but **claimed**. Every surface has been touched: fired brick, carved limestone, salvaged timber, mud plaster over mud plaster. The rock is gone, sealed away. What remains is architecture so complete it feels like a single exhalation held downward into the earth.

The scale is small—not a kingdom of tunnels, but a cluster. A hamlet compressed into vertical density, chambers stacking, corridors crossing at shoulder-width, basins shallow and close. **Density is law here: 2.5, saturated.** There is no void. Reed mats hang like curtains between sleeping and working and storing. Clay pipes run along low ceilings. Nerves, if they existed in the walls, would be these pipes—carrying not blood but air and water and smoke. You cannot extend an arm fully without touching presence: a beam, a jar, a wall, another body. The air is layered—warm, thick, carrying damp clay, rendered fat, musk of constant habitation, the particular metallic sweetness of long-burning wick.

**Construction is total.** Primitive tools—hardened-stone chisels, twisted-fiber ropes, rare simple pins—have left their marks in every corner. The technology is early, manual, patient. And the work is **old**: erosion settled at **2.4**, not destructive but *established*. Corners have rounded to river-stone smoothness under generations of passing. Surfaces gleam not by design but by use and moisture—polished into their own endurance. It does not collapse; it holds. A bone kept in a pocket, smoothed by time rather than intention.

**Water, 0.7**—not river, not flood, but dampness made structural. Condensation beads on the cool undersides of upper chambers, collecting in shallow clay troughs. Pockets gather seepage: dark, still basins where the earth above presses its slow, intermittent weight. Drip is percussion—scattered, constant. Small hand-dug channels guide excess toward jars or evaporation pits. The air never dries. Your palms come away wet. Moisture migrates; it is architecture’s other half.

The temperature holds **warm, 1.9**—not hot, but enclosed. Geothermal breath, accumulated bodies, small fires that never blaze but maintain. Comfortable in the way of a held palm. You do not shiver.

**Light is normal, 1.5.** Not abyss, not blaze. Light wells—narrow vertical shafts, ceramic-ringed or raw-bored—carry filtered, gray-green daylight down from above into the upper chambers. Lower, clay cups burn rendered fat or thick oil, wicks twisted, giving thick yellow flicker. Between these, shadow and clarity weave. You see well. Nothing hides behind darkness.

**Vegetation is sparse, 1.1.** Not garden, not forest. Where cracks gather moisture—between bricks, along a trough—pale moss, velvety fungi, a searching root hangs like thread. But green is not abundant; life here is not botanical.

Instead, **fauna is abundant, 1.8.** The place teems with small, adapted residents—not invaders, but co-inheritors. Thick-furred rodents nest in storage niches. Dark moths, clicking beetles swarm in warm pockets. Flat, quick reptiles slide through clay pipes. Bats cluster in tight groups from highest beams, their droppings adding to the layered smell. Paths are worn not only by feet but by constant scurrying. Nests occupy niches meant for jars. The boundary between built space and ecology is membrane-thin.

The **sound is loud, 2.5**—this is immediate. In a small, enclosed, dense, warm space filled with active life and process, noise has nowhere to go. It bounces. Drip is not single but scattered percussion across dozens of surfaces. Claws scratch brick; wings flutter; air moves through wells with low rumble. Voices—if voices exist—echo tightly, intimate, close. Small fires crackle. There is no silence, only the collective noise of a functioning, crowded system maintaining itself.

The **dynamic is active, 2.2.** Not tomb. Cycles operate: moisture collects and moves; lamps are fed and trimmed; food stored and taken; fauna breeds in rhythms even underground. Primitive craft continues—pottery shaped in damp basins, fiber woven into mats, mortar patched, channels cleared. The erosion is old and balanced, so repair is constant but not desperate. The place breathes. It is in motion, holding its form.

**Weirdness is low, 0.6.** Nothing supernatural. Only intensity: the completeness of built environment, the density of life compressed into a small, warm, damp box below ground, the loudness of ordinary functions made intimate by absence of space. Strange the way a beehive is strange—logical, other, entire.

To stand at whatever center exists—perhaps a low circular chamber where light wells converge, where air is thickest—is to feel **small scale made dense**. Opposing walls within reach. Ceiling curves or flattens just above. Around, presence of life and structure and moisture and sound presses equally. It is a world that achieved its form and now maintains it: warm, loud, wet, built, below the skin of the earth, complete.

In the yellow flicker—there they are. Not seen first, but *adjusted for*: the sudden stillness of a lamp being trimmed, the shift of air as a body turns in a corridor only shoulder-wide, the close breath of work.

There are **not many**—perhaps two dozen, compressed like seeds in a pod. Scale is intimate: heads pass beneath beams hung at head-height; bodies learn to turn sideways, to navigate the lattice without extension. They are **compact**—not necessarily stunted, but shaped to this world: broad shoulders for carrying through narrow passages, spines accustomed to bending, necks strong from looking up at low brick. Skin is darkened not by sun—the wells carry only gray-green filter—but by smoke-fat and clay-dust, always slightly damp, smelling of rendered oil and earth. Hair thick, often matted with moisture, protected by woven caps or left to hold warmth.

These are **builders and maintainers**, inheritors of the hands that lined every inch. Their technology matches the walls: stone chisels, fiber ropes, clay shaped in seepage-troughs, timber splints, bronze pins rare and treasured. They work in **continuous, active cycles**—patching mortar where earth-pressure cracks a seam, guiding condensation along carved channels into collection jars, replacing wicks in clay-cup lamps that burn with thick yellow light. They weave reed curtains. They shape pottery in shallow basins, using ever-present damp clay, firing in small hearths whose smoke layers the air. Labor is manual, inherited, patient: holding back not collapse but time itself.

They live with **fauna** not as masters but as residents. The thick-furred rodents share storage niches; the inhabitants accept loss, perhaps share seeds or rendered fat, recognizing the rodents as part of the density. Bats hang undisturbed; their droppings accumulate in specific corners, collected for fire or left as part of the smell. Insects swarm; flat reptiles slide through pipes that carry air or water; the people move around them, accustomed. One abundant life among others—only the ones who hold the lamps.

In this **loud** world—where sound cannot dissipate, only rebound—communication is intimate. Voices do not shout; they speak low, face-to-face, lips near ears, within the tight echo. Much is touch: guiding shoulders through dark passages, tapping rhythms on pipes to signal presence. The constant bath of drip, crackle, and movement creates a collective noise; silence is not absence but shared, specific attention.

They understand **water** as architecture. They tend seepage—emptying troughs, directing excess, using dampness to keep clay workable, to cool air, to fill drinking jars. Palms always wet. They do not fight moisture; they live within its migration.

They are **warm**, held in geothermal breath and accumulated heat. They sleep on ledges, wrapped in woven fiber, curled close for shared temperature in lower chambers. Sleep is interrupted not by threat but by ordinary maintenance—the trimming of a wick, the passage of a rodent, a drop falling into a jar.

If you saw one clearly in the yellow flicker: low, compact, slightly hunched not from weakness but from the geometry of home. Hands thickened and cracked from mortar and fiber. Eyes adjusted to read shadow and lamplight equally, moving through the dense lattice with unconscious grace—someone who has never known open sky, who understands space not as emptiness but as the precise, warm, loud, damp interval between one touched wall and the next.

They do not leave. There is only the narrow throat of brick, and the balanced, weathered world they maintain in its long, held state—active, abundant, entirely themselves.
