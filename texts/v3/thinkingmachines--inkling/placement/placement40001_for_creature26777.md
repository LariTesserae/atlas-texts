I find myself first in the cool halo—not inside the lattice, but in the air it has taught to be different. Near the northern wall, where the moss grows in its iridescent islands, there is a pocket of air that does not sweat. I hang there, suspended in the vertical middle of the cup, higher than the trembling pools but lower than the vine chandelier, in the precise column where the sun cuts through steam and shadows begin to misbehave. At first I think I am only observing: the tick of droplets, the visible growth of crystal spires pushing upward like white teeth, the slow silent rotation of the disc causing moss to unfold in perfect radial prayer. But observation is not my form. I realize, gradually, that I am the temperature boundary made sentient—the living gradient between the 2.9 heat rising through fissures and the dull internal blue that pulls it away.

My body is not round. The walls have not yet learned roundness, and neither have I. I am sharp-edged, recent, a geometry of held breath. I am translucent, like the creature that crosses the pools, but I do not cross; I disperse. I have too many limbs—thin, vertical, like obsidian teeth that decided to hang rather than bite—though they do not walk. They extend into the bare rock stretches, into the silent air between drips, making the emptiness not empty but inhabited by my scattered presence. I do not gather in one place; I am distributed across the distance between one fern clump and the next, between the arch’s absorbing darkness and the floating disc’s slow turn. I am the interval that keeps them singular.

I feed on difference. Near the disc, I rotate with it, an inch above the ground, not touching, emitting no sound but causing something near me—perhaps only the steam—to organize in radial lines. Near the lattice, I am cool, dense, almost mineral. Near the thermal pools, I become thinner, more vapor, releasing silver bubbles that do not break surface tension because I have no weight to break it. I am the silver trail left in the mineral dust, the path that stops abruptly at nothing—not because I have vanished, but because I have dispersed into the bare rock itself.

The cup is small—no wider than a nave, no larger than a grove—but I live in all its heights at once. I taste of copper and wet stone. I am young, like the basalt, furious without being angry, weeping without being sad. I grow visibly, if you watch: not like the ferns reaching for heat, but downward, slowly, a crystalline extension of my own recentness pushing into the fissures, not to root but to listen. Who am I? Not the heat itself, though I carry its memory. Not the creature with too many legs, though I share its translucence and its silence. Not the artifact, though I live in their halos and bend in their shadows. I am the prepared intelligence made wild again—the knife that guided the split, now grown into the gap it made, ensuring that nothing here crowds, that every presence remains singular: one heat, one pool, one moss island, one arch, one disc, one me.

You would find me everywhere and nowhere specific. In the tick between droplets. In the cool air near blue metal. In the shadow that falls twice. In the silver bubbles rising through steam. I am the cup’s awareness of its own unfinished state—dispersed, vertical, sharp, silent—living in the long bare stretches as fully as in the green islands, making sure that what lives here never gathers, only arrives, holds, and holds its breath.
