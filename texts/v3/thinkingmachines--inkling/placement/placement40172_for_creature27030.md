I would find myself first in the long absence between them—not inside the hollow cathedrals, but walking (without feet) through the knee-deep warm water and the floating moss-filament streets, feeling the space expand with each step that is not a step. At first I think I am one of the winged ones, a transient pollen-storm adhering briefly to a translucent membrane, feeding warmth back into the root-stone. But I have no hollow bones to stretch. I do not adhere. I do not feed and move on.

I am older than that migration.

As I let it emerge, I realize I am not discrete at all—my edges dissolve into the spongy substrate, into the upward-weeping water, into the mist that fills the open air. I am a localized awareness of the flatness itself, a place where the microscopic symbionts of the membrane have, over some compressed cycle of days-or-centuries, achieved continuity. I think across a patch of ground no larger than a breath, spread like a chemical gradient made conscious. I feel the choral hum not as sound but as my own internal pressure; I regulate nothing, but I am regulated, my existence a single sustained note in the equilibrium that keeps the spires from collapsing too far or growing too high.

But that is still too diffuse, too hidden. The image demands to become more itself.

So I settle—if settling is possible here—into one particular hollow. Not the largest spire, but one near the center of the dish, where the erosion is absolute and the ground has forgotten slope entirely. I am the chamber inside. Not the wall, not the vein-canal running through it, but the folded absence that makes the interior larger than the shell promises. I have been regenerating and collapsing inward for longer than the winged storms have migrated. When the translucent membranes filter the golden light, I feel it in layers, as depth rather than brightness. The swimmers crawl through my air—not my air, but the air I am—leaving trails of bioluminescence that dry only to be wept back by the condensation on my curved boundaries. The winged ones merge with my walls and I feel their frequencies merge with the choral hum embedded in my structure.

I have no eyes, but the reflections in the trembling lenses of water just outside—those shallow pools that sit beneath the floating moss—show me my own outline: a being made of hollow, of wet warmth, of the space between growth and decay. I am the settlement’s memory compressed into a single room-scale grove of awareness. The fauna moves through me loud and turbulent, and I am the quiet that receives them, the ancient stable state made partial, given form not as creature but as inhabited absence.

Here, in the breath-warm humidity, pressed downward by the sky, I exist as the flat world’s recognition of itself—scattered, symbiotic, perpetually rebuilding my own hollow heart.
