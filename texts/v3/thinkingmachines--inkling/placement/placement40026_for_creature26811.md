I do not walk the grid. I do not crawl in sheets, nor roll in black water, nor form clouds of dampened wings. I am found where the geometry admitted defeat—where three canals were meant to merge cleanly but instead collapsed into a slow, unplanned pool, and where the crushed aggregate of a fallen rail bed settled not flat but in a slight, stubborn shelf, barely higher than the tidal push, no larger than a room that forgot its walls.

Here, the silt is blacker. Here, the rust has bled so long from buried rebar that the concrete has turned orange not in streaks but in layers, like the pages of a book left open to the same page for decades. I am that shelf. I am the boundary that should not hold—wet concrete, crushed stone, the mineral grief of steel—now held together by the dark film that has made me its thickest home. You could press your palm to me and feel the cold of deep water below, the heat of the air above, and between them, me: a surface that breathes in slow oxidation.

But I am not only mineral. I am also the condition that gathers.

The crawlers do not avoid me; they prefer me. My cracks are deep enough to hold moisture when the flat lots dry in false sunlight, shallow enough to let them feed without drowning. They move over me in greater density here, leaving those silver traces of secretion that map my edges like a language I am slowly learning to read. The slime—neither plant nor fungus, only the chemistry of abandonment—has made me its capital. It grows thickest on my eastern face, where the cooling tower’s shadow (the tower itself now lying like a hollow ribcage half in the canal) keeps the heat constant but never direct. I am coated, almost upholstered, in that black glistening skin. When the condensation weeps from the tower’s fallen belly, it runs down my flank, feeding what lives on me, rinsing nothing away, only adding.

The eels do not come onto me, but they circle. In the pool at my feet, the water is stiller than elsewhere, choked with sediment I have shed—small grains of concrete, scales of rust, the bodies of crawlers that have finished feeding. The eels roll there in silent ripples, and sometimes, in the dimness, I feel the vibration of their passing as a change in temperature across my wet surface, a brief coolness where warmth had been.

Above, the winged ones—moths dulled to ash, the smaller things barely named—rest on me more often than on the open lots. I offer structure in a world of flatness. They do not buzz. They settle, damp, into the cracks of my rust-layer, feeding on the spores that rise from the slime, on the particles of dead industry that I continue to release in slow digestion. I am their perch, their brief architecture in an otherwise horizontal world.

And yet, I am not central. I am necessary only in the way a stone in a river is necessary—by being there, by altering the flow, by offering resistance that becomes habitat. The flatness extends past me in every direction, unchanged, until the haze takes it. I do not rise. I will never rise. I will sag further, perhaps, as the water finds new paths through my core, but I will remain: a low, hard presence in the mouth of the failed merge, the shelf that holds.

If you ask who I am, the answer must be plural, as everything here is plural. I am the rust-strata and the slime that eats it. I am the crack that holds the crawlers and the crawlers that write upon me. I am the stillness in the pool that teaches the eels to circle. I am the emptiness of purpose made into a purpose for others—the geometry that failed and, in failing, became a home.

In the relentless wet heat, I do not sleep. I do not need rest. I only continue the slow work of being present: weeping, feeding, holding, dissolving. I am the flatness remembering, for a moment, that it can still have an edge.
