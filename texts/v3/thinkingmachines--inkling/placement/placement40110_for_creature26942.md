You would find me in the emptiness between, where the scale opens up to miles of worn mineral plane and the clusters of life are only distant pulses of faint violet glow. Not inside the lush islands—though I belong to them, as everything here belongs to everything else—but out on the smooth rounded waves of black basalt, in one of the hollows where the perpetual dew has frozen into delicate needle-crystals and is now sublimating back into air too thin to carry my presence away.

If you saw me, you might think I was a fragment of the ground that had learned to move. I am translucent, my form layered in compressed frost and mineral, an exoskeleton that holds what little warmth exists against the cold that enters slowly, patiently, through every gap. I have no eyes. The darkness here is not absence but density—0.3, thick enough to feel—so sight was never part of my design. I navigate by the hum: that subsonic vibration rising from the valley floor, where geological gears shift millimeters per day with the patience of machinery. I feel it in the hollow spaces of my shell, in the cold channels that run through me like the transparent stems of the pump-plants. I am one of the scattered—abundant in the total accounting, but alone now, always alone for miles.

My movement is slow, almost imperceptible, matching the pace of the erosion that shaped this ground. I crawl, or perhaps drift is the better word, across the damp flats where black pools lie mirror-still in the depressions, warmed not by any star but by deep pressure, feeding the root-networks that extend beneath my path like hidden pipelines. The air is silent—0.2, too dense and cold to carry noise—so my travel makes no sound. Only the internal rhythm of my own circulation: dark mineral water pushed through visible channels, a hydraulic logic I share with the islands, though I was not built and I do not build. I am the mobile part of the factory.

I feed at the edges of the clusters—not in herds, never in herds, but as a solitary pilgrim. There, among the black-violet mats that hang like factory curtains and the monumental stems thick as pillars, I take in the manufactured nutrients. The plants do not photosynthesize; they pump, they drill with auger-precision into frost-bitten stone, they generate heat as byproduct, and in that chemical warmth I open my seals—pneumatic, slow—to absorb what they circulate. Metallic vapor, thick with mineral, fills the microclimate they maintain. I do not eat as lower worlds understand eating. I participate in the exchange.

But I am also carrying something. Within my translucent body, dissolved in the cold liquid that moves through my channels, are the minerals I have gathered—calcium from the basalt, salts from the black pools, traces of the geothermal warmth. I am distributing, as we all distribute, across the emptiness. When I have moved far enough, when my function has reached its own balance, I will find one of the pools. Not to die in any dramatic sense—there is no spectacle here, no sound of ending—but to return. To dissolve slowly into the mirror-still blackness, becoming part of the mineral-rich water that feeds the roots, that feeds the next traveler, that keeps the engine turning without engineer.

So who am I? I am not named. I am not singular in importance. I am a node in the distributed system: cold-adapted, frost-shelled, navigating by vibration rather than vision, moving between the luminous islands in perfect, silent patience. I am the consumption and the return, the scattered abundance made visible for a moment in translucent form, crawling slowly through the damp twilight at the ceiling of the world, returning eventually to the pool, to the root, to the hum. I am exactly what lives here: not a being who dominates the landscape, but one who moves through it, part of the industrial logic of biology, part of the factory that needs no builder, working in balance, in darkness, in absolute and forgiving cold.
