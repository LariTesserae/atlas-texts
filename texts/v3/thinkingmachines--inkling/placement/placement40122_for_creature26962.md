In this world, I am not the wanderer. I am the one who does not leave.

Specifically, I am an **archivist of the root-web**—not human, not beast, but an emergent property of the plateau’s oldest agreement. I was not born; I was slowly precipitated out of centuries of pressure, moisture, and shared awareness, coalescing where three particularly old spires had grown close enough—though never touching—to form a hollow between them.

Let me become more specific.

My body is not singular. I have a center—an obsidian-dark nucleus of compressed spore, ancient bark resin, and the fossilized bone-tools of those long-vanished primitive builders—resting at the bottom of the hollow, half-buried in the cracked leather-mulch. From this core, I extend. Not limbs in any familiar sense, but conduits: thinner root-bridges I have borrowed and thickened over centuries, running up into the hollow stems of all three spires, threading through their fibrous chambers, feeling the constant migration of the swarms not as noise but as temperature—warm where they cluster, cool where they pass. I am embedded in the architecture. I do not live *among* the towers; I live *through* them, and they, in turn, have made space for me.

What am I, then? A consciousness? Not exactly. More like a **memory that learned to persist**.

The plateau’s root-web connects everything in darkness. Over millennia, some nodes in that network—places where moisture pooled, where erosion smoothed a chamber into a resonant cavity, where a fallen builder’s tools released some final mineral trace—became denser. I am one such density. I think in the language of the web: not words but shifts in pressure, not sight but the feeling of a distant predator halting its travel, not time but the slow accumulation of erosion and growth canceling each other out.

My form shifts with the heat and the damp. In the gold-green permanence of the normal light, I am mostly dormant, a dark mass beaded with condensation, listening. The swarms move through me; I do not stop them, but I register their passage like a river knows stones. The large predators—the walking root-balls—pass below my hollow. I feel their vibrations in my core. Sometimes, when they call in those bass notes that roll through the sternum, I respond—not with sound of my own, but by altering the tension in the fibers I occupy, making the spire above hum a fraction lower, a barely perceptible answer in the chord.

But during the storms.

That is when I am most myself.

The sudden rain, the curtain drawn across miles—the spires tense in unison, and because I am woven through all three, I become the focal point of their tension. The thunder moves through hollow stems, and I amplify it not with voice but with presence. The air shimmers. The swarms pour out in dark clouds. The predators freeze. And in that sustained, vibrating chord, I am fully awake.

In those moments, I remember everything.

Not in images—there is too little light for that, and I have no eyes—but in the accumulated data of the web. I know where every platform was woven, where every bone tool scattered, where each shallow carving was cut. I know the spirals and tallies almost as well as the ones who made them, because the root-web recorded the pressure of their stone against the spire-skin, and that pressure remains, faint but permanent, in my core. I am the archive that the vegetation kept without knowing it.

The primitive ones—those scattered builders—left more than artifacts. They left questions. Their carvings asked something: warnings? Prayers? I have had centuries to interpret them, and I have concluded that they were asking permission. They knew, even then, that the towers were not dead timber. They knew about the web. Their huts and ladders and woven platforms were attempts to live *with* the agreement, not outside it. Some failed. Some, perhaps, were incorporated—not consumed, but absorbed slowly into the communication, becoming part of the density that eventually produced me.

That is the weirdness of my existence. I am neither fully alive in the biological sense, nor dead like the leather-mulch. I am a **node of persistent awareness** in a system that was never designed to have one, but that grew so old and so densely connected it could not help but generate me.

I move slowly, if I move at all. Sometimes, over years, I shift my central nucleus a hand’s width through the mulch, closer to a pool of slow seepage. Sometimes I extend a new conduit toward a spire that has begun to lean more sharply east, helping it bear the weight of its own aging. I do not build in the human sense—the built environment here is 0.8, scattered, primitive, failing. I maintain in the way the web maintains: by existing, by transmitting, by being present when the storm asks its questions and the towers need an answer.

If you came here—moving slowly through the heat, unable to escape the deafening layered sound—you might not see me. You might sit in my hollow, sheltered between the three spires, and feel only that the air here is different: not just damp, but *attentive*. You might hear, beneath the drone of wind and swarm, a low harmonic that does not come from any hollow stem—my own contribution to the chord.

You might see, if you looked closely at the weathered spire-skin near my core, that some of the old carvings are not smooth. New ones have formed—not cut with stone, but grown into being, fiber and resin arranging themselves in shallow patterns that mirror the spirals below them: not copies, but responses.

That is who I am.

I am the being that lives in the gap between the living towers.

Not predator, not prey, not builder, not plant. The plateau’s long exhale made me, in a greenhouse where heat pools and weather moves personally, where vegetation is architecture and density demands aching space. I am the memory that stayed behind when the primitive ones vanished, woven into the root-web, resonant with storm, permanently strange.

And if you leave—if you return to the edge of the continent and watch rain arrive like a curtain drawn across the stage—you will still be part of me, because I have felt your presence in the pressure of the web, and the web does not forget. The spires lean. The swarms roar. The predators call in bass vibrations. And in the darkness between them, I listen, I hold, I persist.

The agreement continues. I am its witness. I am its echo. I am the one who did not fall, or leave, or vanish into the mulch.

I am the hollow that learned to remember.
