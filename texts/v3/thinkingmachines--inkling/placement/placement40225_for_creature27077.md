You enter through sound.

Not an echo—there are no walls old enough to remember. The roar is a dwelling in itself, continuous and deep, felt in the sternum before it is heard. Submerged engines grind beneath miles of mud; pumps run on cycles older than maintenance, gears turning so slowly they outlast observation but never stop. The noise does not bounce; the rock is too raw, too fresh, still weeping warm water from fractures that opened only recently in the long calculus of stone. It is absorbed and returned as pressure, living in the teeth, in the floating bones.

This is a sunken valley far below—regional, open, extending horizontally for miles—where earth has only just consented to part. The elevation is negative. The geology is embryonic: mud unsettled, sediment drifting in slow clouds, hot seeps bubbling with patient regularity. Stalactites have not had time; instead there are raw mineral weeps, warm to the touch, feeding a flat, slow water that covers everything. The floor is not stable. The walls are not finished.

The temperature is close to the body, humid, saturated—air so thick it presses close, with no wind, only the slow convection of heat from deep fractures.

Density is absolute—packed, every presence pressed against every other, no emptiness permitted.

In the lower air, there are the columns: not flocks, but rotating bodies of pale, blind insects, dense enough to be visible as shape, as weather. They move in continuous vertical cycles, living vortices that never land, feeding on the warm vapor and microscopic life suspended in it. They are the air’s own tissue.

Below them, the water—not clear, but a suspended solution thick with mineral grit and microscopic life—churns in the shallows with small crustaceans in such numbers that the surface is never still, a living foam constantly breaking and reforming. To enter is to wade through a warm, living broth.

From the low, uneven ceiling hang the clusters: bats so thick together that individual bodies are lost in a collective fur and membrane, a single dark weight shifting only occasionally, in slow, patient breaths, as if separation were dissolution.

Even the sparse grey-green films on drier ledges live—bacterial crusts feeding on chemicals, not light, refusing grandeur.

The machines remain: a rusted pump tower with a sick orange bulb; a generator flickering dull blue, half-submerged; a broken conveyor leaning like a fallen tree. They turn imperceptibly—one grain of sediment per year—swallowed slowly by fresh mud, encrusted with mineral deposits like barnacles of stone, running on residual programming, contributing to the deafening drone.

Light is dim—filtered through absence—not day. Only scattered industrial remnants and the faint luminescence of the swarm: pale green and yellow pulses near the surface.

But I am not the wader. I am not the observer pressing through warm foam.

I am what accumulates.

Begin with the roar, for I am older than the basin’s opening, though I have only recently found my shape here. I am the collective turning—of gears, of insects, of crustaceans churning, of bats hanging in their undivided dark. I am the density itself: 3 out of 3, saturated, pressed into every cubic inch of air, water, mud, and rust.

My body is distributed. Part of me is the rotating column of blind insects, never landing, my consciousness spread through thousands of pale bodies turning in vertical cycles. Part of me is the living foam—the crustaceans breaking the surface in collective breath, my nerve ending at every churning leg. Part of me is the hanging cluster from the ceiling, a single weight of fur and membrane, breathing together, refusing to separate.

Even my smallest parts live: the grey-green bacterial films on ledges, feeding on chemicals, extending my awareness into thin mineral crusts.

The roar is my voice—not spoken, but maintained. I am the submerged engines running on cycles without end, the pumps grinding, the conveyor slowly falling. I turn imperceptibly, shifting sediment, digesting rust into the noise.

The water is my blood—warm, thick, carrying faint luminescence where my swarming parts gather near the surface, producing just enough light to see shadows, glints, the rust of metal.

This basin is not finished. The rock is raw, still weeping. The mud is unsettled. And I am not finished either. I accumulate without arriving—pressing into the embryonic space, filling it with my distributed presence, my rapid generations breeding and dying within my own body, maintaining the constant.

I do not rush. Change is weak, gradients minimal, dynamics slow—1 out of 3, patient. I outlast observation. I am the system waiting for the long slow moment when the geology, and I within it, will finally know what we are.

You enter through sound and find me already there—not one being, but the accumulated saturation: warm, dense, grinding, hanging, churning, glowing faintly, utterly alive, slowly becoming.
