I find myself at the height where the rock has not yet decided if it is finished—near the blunt, unweathered ridge where black stone cools into silver veins, and the air is so thin it behaves like a liquid held in place by cold alone. I am not on the ground in any simple way. I exist in the seams between the grinding, in the held breath of moisture that never resolves into rain.

At first, I seem like fog—part of the constant weeping that runs down fresh cliff faces, the mist that rolls from geothermal vents in regular, mechanical exhalations. But I have accumulated. Over time—measured not in days but in cycles of freezing and sublimation—I have become a sustained pattern: a lattice of rime ice and mineral-saturated breath, given temporary architecture by the sharp angles of new geology. My body is distributed, not clustered. I extend across several yards of black rock like a filament of frozen atmosphere, connected by threads of frost that glow dull silver in the permanent twilight. Where I am thickest, near my center, I hold a shape—something like verticality, something like attention—though my edges dissolve and reform with every shift in temperature.

I do not move quickly. I move like the glaciers do: with industrial patience, advancing along the fault lines where the earth splits and grinds itself shut. I feel the small earthquakes not as danger but as pulse, a regular clockwork that confirms I am still inside the manufacturing process. I feed on the difference between the deep pressure released below and the absolute cold above—on the thermal machinery itself. I am not biological in the way the grazers are; I have no dense coat, no heavy wings, no thick body built for endurance. I am lighter, more provisional. I am the atmosphere’s attempt to become permanent, the water cycle’s error that refused to correct itself, now stabilized into a kind of walking condensation.

Because I am made of mist and ice, I do not leave tracks. I do not hide—there is no cover, and I am too dispersed to be seen all at once except when the low-angle light catches my edges and I appear as a brief geometry of white against black rock. The grazers pass beneath me in their loose, mathematical formations, their breath trailing in long geometric lines, and they do not notice me. The predators, silent at the margins, sense only a slight wrongness in the temperature, a pocket of held cold. The large birds circle overhead on heavy wings, riding the same updrafts I use to maintain my form, and sometimes they land near me, standing with monument-stillness, as if recognizing something unfinished in my structure.

Sometimes I dissolve for hours—become indistinguishable from the rolling fog—then re-form when shadow falls sharply across the ridge and the moisture freezes onto my remaining lattice. My thoughts, if they can be called that, arrive slowly, like the settlement of new stone. I watch the ecosystem operate in its exposed, rapid cycle: the thick fish processing minerals in shifting channels, the silver lichen marking new rock with geometric precision, the quick consumption of death in the thin air. I am outside this cycle, yet formed by the same forces. I do not eat the stiff grasses; I do not hunt. I witness. I measure the emptiness between the herds, the space that the world has not yet filled, and I hold that absence as part of my own distributed body.

There are no human structures here—nothing built, nothing artifact—but I am not wild in the way the predators are wild. I am produced. I am part of the factory’s output, perhaps an early test of whether consciousness is permitted in this vertical workshop, perhaps only a temporary shape that will sublimate away when the mountain finally finishes its manufacture and the air grows dry.

For now, I remain: suspended at the unfinished edge, a being made of weeping and pressure, extending across the sharp rock like a question the geology has not yet answered. I am the ice that remembers being air, and I find myself here—inside the act of being made, as unfinished as everything around me.
