I begin not with a body but with a warmth—body-temperature, humid, pressing against something that might be skin or bark or the inside of a pipe. I do not know if I am waking or being exhaled. There is a hollow around me, vertical, smelling of iron and chlorophyll, and my first certainty is that I am nested inside one of the pillars—not upon it, but within it, in a chamber where wood-steel fusion has left a pocket just large enough for a curled form.

My fur comes to me slowly, matted with rust-flakes and spores that glow faintly in the sick-green light. It is black-green, like the pillar’s leaves, absorbing what little illumination exists. Then the tail: long, prehensile, wrapped not around a branch but around a gear tooth, holding it in alignment as the pillar turns with its grinding patience. I realize my claws are not only claws—they are worn flat in places, shaped by years of scraping dead bark and polishing metal, and when I extend them they catch the blue arcs of the filament-vines, making my path visible to the insects that part around me like water.

I have eyes—more than should be necessary. They are arranged across a face that is mammal-shaped but strange: some see heat, some see the electrical twilight, some see the translucent moths that drift through the open volume between pillars. I watch them all at once, and I understand that I do not hunt them. I maintain them.

This is my work: I move through the root-bridges, my weight making the fungal mats sag and twitch, carrying spores in my fur from pillar to pillar. At the root-bowls—the stagnant black water basins—I drink, and my exhalations, warm and rich, fall into the water where the amphibious things wait, pale and soft, to carry the nutrients back into the root-bed. I return to my pillar. I crawl inside the hollow stem, and the machinery knows my presence. A valve embedded in the bark, brushed by my shoulder, releases a hiss of warm vapor that feeds a tendril growing across the dark ceiling. I tend the gears. I am not large—perhaps the size of a long-armed cat—but my presence smooths the turning, clears the rust, keeps the teeth from seizing.

I am not alone in this census. Others like me—furry, long-tailed, too-many-eyed—nest in other pillars. We chitter in high registers that cut through the industrial roar, not to warn, but to coordinate: where the moisture pools, where a gear has begun to stick, where the insects have clustered too thickly and the vines brighten in response. We are the tenders. We are also the tended. The pillar breathes through us; we breathe through its ducts.

Sometimes I leave the pillar entirely, crossing the vast open air of the chamber—forty feet of emptiness, though it feels smaller because of the sound. The insects swarm in clouds so thick they obscure the distant structures, clicking beetles and palm-sized moths, and I move through them like a stone through a river. They do not bite. They know my fur carries the spores they need. The bat-like creatures sweep overhead, barking in chaotic flight, and I feel the draft of their wings against my damp skin. I am part of the weather they navigate.

There are no names here. I am not owner, not occupant alone—I am citizen, architecture, parasite and host in the same breath. When I sleep, curled around the gear-tooth, my tail still moves with the pillar’s slow cycle. When I wake, I am already working: chewing dead bark, clearing pathways for new black-green tendrils, drinking the condensation that beads on rusted iron, exhaling warmth back into the hollow throat of my pillar.

I am one of thousands—scattered through the volumes like living weather, dense with presence despite the sparse architecture of eight or nine vertical elders. We maintain the place in ways we do not need to understand. The boundary has dissolved so completely that I no longer know where my breath ends and the pillar’s steam begins, where my claw ends and the gear’s tooth resumes. I am the rust that remembers being fur. I am the mammal that has become part of the machinery’s digestion.

And here, in this small bounded chamber beneath the surface world, soaked in constant dampness, deafened by the loud biological-industrial chorus, barely visible in the dim electrical twilight—I am complete. I need nothing from the sealed world above. I am the being who lives in the gap, the one with too many eyes who sees in all directions at once, who tends and is tended, who moves through root and rust and swarm with the patience of something that has forgotten it was ever anything else.
