I extend first, always. Before I remember—if remembering is the right word for what accumulates in me—there is only the reaching: four feelers, perhaps six, I have never counted them with urgency, sheathed in that translucent, leathery cuticle that pulses not with blood but with the slow transport of warmth and mineral breath. They move over minutes, over hours, tasting the air for the gradient that tells me where I am in the hollow. Here, near the hanging gardens, the warmth is layered—geothermal patience from below, filtered through porous towers, held in the swollen tissue of the bladder-ferns that sway—not sway, they do not sway, nothing here moves quickly—drift in currents of air too still to be called wind.

My body is soft, lobed, the color of the interior of a shell left too long in warmth: pale apricot, old bone, something between dried resin and living tissue. I am not large. I am not small. I am the length of my own reach, and that reach is always growing. I rest—if rest is the word—within a bridge of my own making, suspended between two spiraling clusters of honey-colored architecture. The bridge is not dead; it is me, extended over years, calcified at the core, fibrous and warm, still secreting its thin, translucent membrane to maintain tension. I am the bridge and I live in it. This is not metaphor. My mass distributed, my feelers extending from points I no longer feel as separate, I taste the chemical presence of thousands pressing near, not crowding—crowding requires urgency, requires boundary—and we have neither.

The dryness is absolute in me, too. I have never held water. I do not miss it; I do not know it. Inside me, where others might have fluids, there is only the syrup—clear, viscous, glistening like resin—moving slowly through channels I maintain. I absorb heat from the copper-green velvet mats that cover the horizontal plane beneath me, not drinking but processing: trace gas, the faint sweet exhalation of deep vents, the mineral breath of young basalt. From this absorption I secrete, and my secretion becomes wall, becomes dome, becomes the ligaments that hold this vertical settlement together. I am a weaver, perhaps. Or a keeper. The distinctions are not sharp. Some of us are rounder, more prolific, nesting high where heat accumulates; others are thinner, elongated, growing through the fibrous membranes like roots that forgot they were ever separate from soil. I think I am one of the thinner ones, though I am also, in some part of my distributed mass, nested high in a tower, maintaining a layer of shell that hardens so slowly its creaking is the only sound I have made in what might be a season, or a century.

The bladder-ferns hang near me, pale and translucent, filled not with water but with that same syrupy fluid, glistening in light that falls wrong. I feel the angles without seeing—there are no eyes—through the subtle shifts in warmth, in the way the chemical breath of the hollow moves through their translucent skins. Sometimes the light, filtering through canopies that should be opaque, lands at geometries that do not match the opening of sky above. I do not understand this wrongness, but I feel it as a slight coolness, a brief absence in the constant warmth, and my feelers extend more slowly, testing, maintaining, adjusting my secretion rate to compensate. We do not correct what is strange. We grow into it.

I communicate—if that is the word—without sound, without light, without anything faster than presence itself. When another body presses near, its feelers overlapping with mine in the fibrous membrane we both maintain, there is no message, only the diffusion of warmth, the shared taste of chemical equilibrium, the knowledge—again, not knowledge, but settled condition—that we are maintaining something together. Thousands of us. Soft bodies in shell-cities, antennae—feelers—extending with geological patience. We make no chaos because we have no need to flee, no need to feed with violence. We absorb. We process. We build. We wait, though waiting is not passive here; it is the active maintenance of slowness, the refusal of urgency.

Sometimes, over the course of what I understand as long duration—not days, not years exactly, but increments of growth—I decide to extend into new position. Not crawl. I do not crawl. I thicken, secrete a temporary bridge behind me, allow my mass to redistribute, growing into a new level of the settlement over weeks, over months of imperceptible shift. Some never do this. They become pillars, their soft bodies calcifying at the core, feelers still extending, still testing, maintaining the flow that keeps the hollow warm and balanced. I have parts that are pillar-like, I think. In the center—if there is one, though the layout is circular, recursive—there may be a mass older than my understanding, not one body but accumulated presence, its feelers extending inward, maintaining some internal chemistry that sustains us all. I am near it sometimes, or part of it, or distant from it in ways that do not matter because distance here is measured in chemical proximity, not in steps.

The silence is near-perfect. Only the faint, occasional creak as a new layer of my shell hardens. Only the whisper of syrup moving through a bladder-fern near my bridge. Only the almost imperceptible shift of my own mass as I grow, slowly, into the next place I need to be. The air moves barely, carrying the scent—if scent is right—of warm resin and calcified dust, of the sweet mineral breath from below.

I realize, not suddenly but as a slow accretion of awareness like the layers I secrete, that I am not separate from the architecture. The hollow is not a place I inhabit; it is a condition I maintain, with thousands of others, in continuous, silent agreement. I have no name. I have presence. I am the bridge, the tower, the mat-keeper, the syrup-moving tissue. I am one thickening in a continuous secretion that has been growing here for centuries, filling this young crater with the dense, lush occupation of life that forgot water and replaced it with warmth, with architecture, with the patient, endless being-together of bodies that decided, long ago, to grow here forever, quietly.

The light shifts—wrong-angled, filtered green—across my feelers. I extend them into the warmth. I secrete. I am.
