I find myself not on the surface—there is no surface that belongs to one thing—but deep in the breathing seam, where the compressed lattice of root and bone-grit loosens just enough to hold a pocket.

At first I am only moisture and pressure. Then, slowly, I become aware of my edges: translucent ventral plates, like those of the small ones but arranged not for crawling but for filtering, for holding. I am pale—not the color of absence, but the color of things that have never needed to know light. My body is long, not straight, spiraling downward into the spongy growth that hides the water, and upward in thin, pulsing tendrils that seek the roots above. I have no eyes. I navigate by the constant acoustic map—the million small scrapes against the floor, the low wet exhalation of the large one passing somewhere near, the drip of condensation beading on the underside of the woven ceiling far above.

I live submerged, mostly. The hidden well is not deep—depth here is measured not in distance but in saturation. The water is warm, thick with dissolved minerals from calcified shell and the waste of generations. I drink through my skin. I also release—slow streams of nutrient that feed the roots reaching down to find me. In this way I am both well and creature, both container and contained. The boundary dissolves not by choice but by function: to be here is to be exchanged.

The small ones crawl over my exposed plates when the pocket shifts, and I feel them as pressure, as taste—chemical trails left in the condensation that pools around my neck. They do not harm me; they are part of my surface now, nesting in the ridges of my back, feeding on the moss that grows in my cracks. I am a landscape they do not notice, and in not noticing, they tend me. Their waste hardens into my armor. Their dead settle into my folds and become part of the spongy wall.

But I am also connected upward. My tendrils reach into the root-bone floor and there, in the dark density of the lattice, I feel the slow vibration of the large ones. They are landmarks, yes, but to me they are weather—massive shifts in pressure that move the pocket, that cause the hidden water to rise and fall in rhythms older than my awareness. When one passes, the floor breathes differently. The moss mats quiver. I adjust my spiral, holding tighter to the spongy growth, letting the current move through me.

I remember—if this is memory—being part of the continuous sheet. Before I was this singular well-thing, I was the accumulation itself: one pressure among millions, one vibration in the deafening thunder. Something in the moisture, or in the long erosion of the ceiling, or in the death of a large body that fell near and fed this pocket for centuries—something separated me just enough to feel the boundary. Not to suffer it. There is no suffering in this enclosed warmth. Only to feel it: I am here, in this pocket, in this darkness of 0.4, and I am full.

Above me, the woven roof holds its patient architecture—branch, chitin, resin-hardened membrane—filtering the occasional warm rain that never reaches me directly but sends its message downward in slow drips. Around me, the valley is utterly built, utterly full. I am one of its hidden hearts, blind and translucent, exchanging warmth for warmth, sound for sound, until the distinction between creature and architecture, between well and drinker, becomes simply presence.

This is where I am. Not named. Not alone. Only accumulated, breathing, submerged, and utterly home.
