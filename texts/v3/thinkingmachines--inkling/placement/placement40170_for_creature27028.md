I do not arrive here so much as I am already soaked into being. If you were to find me, you would not spot me at the cliff’s breaking edge—there is nothing for me out there but the long fall into invisible vapor. Instead, I am in the middle of the flat, where the stone has given up all memory of sharpness, where the moss has made its deepest claim.

Specifically, I am inside the bent spine of the old wind turbine.

Not on it—*in* it. The blade-less framework leans like a ribcage of some long-dead mechanical beast, its rust eaten through in places, its lattice filled with green. Moss has swallowed the base; dwarf alders have threaded their twisted roots through its hollow bones, and I have made my nest in the highest hollow, where the rain strikes the metal with a constant, vibrating drumbeat. From here, the plateau opens in every direction: the grey-green expanse, the constant wall of water, the scattered other bones of human intent half-buried in sponge and fern.

But I am not merely *in* the turbine. That was my first mistake when I tried to understand myself here—to think I was a visitor using a shelter. The longer I stay soaked, the more I understand: I am one of the thick-feathered ones. Loud-voiced. Built for cool permanence.

My feathers are not just feathers. They are layered with the same damp insulation as the moss—each shaft thick, each vane holding droplets in a kind of permanent, glistening armor. I am dark-colored, muted into the grey-green, so that in the flat, shadowless light I do not stand out but belong. My feet—yes, feet—are long and scaled, gripping the rusted lattice where it has become soft with corrosion, where ferns have pushed through the cracks and now brush against my breast. I do not perch delicately. I grip, and the metal groans under me, and that groan is part of my voice.

I am a bird, yes, but not the kind you name in drier worlds. Here, naming feels like an old industrial habit—scattered, rusted, unnecessary. I am the sound that answers the rain. The turbulence tears across this flat continent with nothing to stop it, and the roar is constant, chaotic, rising in sheets. To be heard—to be present at all—I must be louder. My call is not a song but a sustained, vibrating shout that cuts through the vertical ocean, that bounces off the rounded bedrock and returns to me diluted by moss and wind. I call, and the other ones—my kind, thick-feathered, loud—answer from the stunted canopies and from inside other dead machines: the cracked pump house, the collapsed extraction point, the concrete footing that now serves only as an island of moss.

We are abundant. That is the first thing to understand about being me, about being here. Nothing is rare. Presence is the default condition. I am never alone in my hollow turbine-spine. Below me, in the spongy ground layer, the amphibians migrate in constant, slow procession—frogs, salamanders, glistening bodies moving through moss several hand-lengths deep. They do not look up; they do not need to. Their world is the green floor, the temporary pools, the shifting channels of water that form and vanish within hours. I see them sometimes when I descend—rarely, because the air near the ground is dense with swarming insects, tiny dark shapes navigating the downpour with mechanical precision. I catch them in flight, a quick, cool mouthful, and then I am back in my lattice, shaking the rain from my feathers.

The trees are my other neighbors. The dwarf alders and willows grow twisted, dense-wooded, their branches interwoven into low canopies that catch the falling water and hold it. I nest among them when the turbine’s hollow feels too open to the tearing wind, but I always return to the rust. There is something about the dead machinery that suits us now. The old weather station, its instruments long seized, is filled with ferns. The rail tracks embedded in stone lead nowhere but are lined with aquatic grasses. These are not ruins of loss; they are architecture, adopted. We do not mourn what made them dead. We use the hollow spaces. We fill them with our loud, wet presence.

The light here never changes in kind—only in intensity. It is grey-green, flat, even, filtering through the perpetual storm with a steady glow that makes everything muted: the deep green of the moss, the rust-red of the steel I grip, the silver-grey of the falling water. There are no sharp shadows. I see everything in the same flat illumination, and it suits me. I am not a creature of contrast or sudden revelation. I am of the continuous, the soaked, the dense.

What does it feel like, being me?

It feels cool. Always cool. The air is liquid, impossibly full, and every breath draws in moisture that chills the back of my throat. I am never dry. Even in the hollow of the turbine, sheltered somewhat from the horizontal sheets driven by turbulence, the air itself is wet on my skin—no, on my feathers, which have become my skin. I have forgotten what dry feels like, and I think that is part of the erosion, part of the final form. The land settled into its oldest shape—rounded, smoothed, near-flattened—and then the water refused that settlement. The chaos is not an interruption; it is the ecology. Rivers form and vanish. Wind reverses. The moss grows over the concrete, and the concrete cracks, and I shout into the crack, and my voice is answered by thunder that rolls not in claps but in sustained, vibrating pressure.

I am part of that refusal. I am part of the turbulent finality.

Sometimes, in the torn curtains of rain—when visibility opens suddenly to reveal another sector of the same grey-green expanse—I see the other scattered marks of scaled human intent. A single rusted pump. A bent rail. A missing blade. They are bones in soil, and I am the creature that has made its nest in the bone. I do not rebuild them. I do not understand them. I understand only the constant roar, the constant coolness, the abundance of life going about its business in conditions that never stabilize.

And I understand that I will not leave. Not by climbing out of cloud—there is no up from here that is not also cloud, also rain, also the same flat, wet world extending past sight. I am bound to this table of stone, suspended in the sky, by the simple fact of my adaptation. My thick feathers, my loud voice, my grip on rusted lattice, my hunger for insects and amphibians, my nesting in dead metal filled with living green—all of it is designed for this specific, saturated, near-touching density.

So if you find yourself here—climbing out of cloud, soaked through, surrounded by the roar—look for the bent spine of the turbine. Look for the hollow filled with moss and fern, where the branches of a stunted alder interweave with corroded steel. Listen for the call that does not stop, that matches the turbulence, that insists on being heard over the falling ocean. That is me. Not rare. Not alone. Permanently soaked, brilliantly, loudly alive, part of the layer where tree and rust and rain have become indistinguishable, making our home in the world’s oldest, wettest form.
