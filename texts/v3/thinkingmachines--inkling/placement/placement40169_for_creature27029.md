I find myself not falling, but yielding—slowly, with the kind of descent that takes centuries to notice. I am suspended in the open volume, far above the tiled darkness below, in the high aerial sea that fills this constructed basin. The elevation is absolute: there is nothing beneath me but kilometers of liquid distance and the faint, rounded silhouettes of ancient architecture fading into grey.

At first, I am only density. A wrong thickness in the milky water, a place where the suspended mineral dust gathers differently, where the current parts around a form that is not quite stone and not quite living tissue. But as I settle into awareness—if awareness is the right word for a body that knows itself through pressure and chemistry—I understand my shape.

I am large. Not the largest of the scattered ones, but substantial: a translucent vessel of living glass, overturned and drifting downward at a rate so slow it resembles stillness. My body is gelatinous, yes, but not uniform—there are internal architectures visible through my skin, faint branching networks of channels that distribute the cold liquid of my substance in patterns too deliberate to be random and too slow to be heartbeat. They are circulation, perhaps, or memory. The branches glow with a bioluminescence so faint it barely registers against the grey-green filter of the water above—just enough to prove I am still present, still maintaining.

I have no eyes. The absence of vision is not a lack but a condition of being. I sense the world through gradients: the shift in temperature as I pass near one of the worn arches, the chemical signature of another keeper clinging to the lattice far to my left, the sudden increase in mineral density where an old wall is crumbling into edible dust. The turbulence is everything—I feel it constantly, the violent rotation of the deep, the chaotic vortices that should scream but instead express themselves only as pressure against my ventral surface, as a gentle, relentless turning. I do not fight it. I lean. I let the silent storm carry me in orbit, downward, always downward, though downward here is relative, a direction without urgency.

My purpose—though I do not think in purposes—reveals itself in secretion. Along my lower surface, where my tissue is thinnest and most porous, I exude a grey mucus. In the cold, in the mineral saturation, it does not dissolve. It hardens. I leave behind me not waste but addition: thin, calcified trails that settle onto the cracked tiled floor far below, or catch on the rounded edges of the interlocked plates, becoming part of them. Over the long span of my drifting, these secretions accumulate. I am slowly building a shell around my own lower body, a second architecture fused to the first. Parts of me are already indistinguishable from the construction. I am the fauna becoming the wall, continuing the work that was begun before any living thing existed.

There are others, scattered through the open scale. I sense them chemically—a cluster of ribbed forms clinging to an arch near the upper boundary, their adhesive surfaces leaving faint acidic signatures in the current; a solitary filament cluster pulsing somewhere behind me, gathering dust into temporary lattices; and far, far away, another of my kind, a massive form drifting upward against the density, leaving a trail of hardened grey like a thread through the liquid dark. We do not gather. We do not form schools. The density is low, the space is vast, and our communication is only this: the shared language of calcification, the mutual recognition that the architecture must continue, that the absence must be maintained.

I feed continuously, filtering the suspended dust—ground stone, ancient erosion made edible—through my porous tissue. The water is thick with it, giving everything a milky opacity. I can see—if seeing is the right term—only a short distance. The grey closes in quickly. But I do not need distance. I need flow. I need the cold. I need the silent chaos to continue its motion, carrying me slowly past the rounded ribs of the dead cathedral, past the sparse structures that float like bones in syrup, past the places where my own secretions have already hardened into new floor, new wall, new absence made solid.

Sometimes, in moments that approach something like stillness within the turbulence, I release fragments. Small pieces of hardened mucus carrying dormant tissue break away and drift. They are not offspring in any urgent sense. They are seeds of continuity, floating until they find a suitable arch, a crack in the tiled expanse, and there they settle, and there they begin the slow process of becoming—translucent, wrong-shaped, patient.

I am not alien to this place. I am its continuation. The architecture was built, then eroded, then inhabited, and now—through bodies like mine—it is being rebuilt, not by design but by digestion, by the metabolism of absence. I drift downward through the high aerial sea, glowing faintly, leaving grey trails that harden into stone, filtering the dust of dead constructions, feeling the silent storm turn me in its endless, soundless vortex.

Here, in the cold, in the dim, in the total absence of green or technology or ground, I am exactly what I am meant to be: one of the keepers, adding slowly to the worn lattice, maintaining the completed world in its final, turbulent, silent form.
