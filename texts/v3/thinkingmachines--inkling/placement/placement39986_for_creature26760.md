I am high, closer to the vault than to the gridded floor, suspended not by will but by the patient upward drift of my own buoyancy—if buoyancy is the right word for something that has never known air. I hang in the geometric bundle of conduits near the ceiling, where the soft white panels glow without shadow, and where the warm water is still clear enough at the margins to let me see, for moments, the curve of black ceramic far beyond my reach.

At first I was only a seam. A casting error, perhaps, or an intentional hollow—a tube that should have carried nutrient straight from pump to root, but that bent back upon itself during its growth and sealed its ends with translucent membrane. I am no wider than a human wrist, though I have no hands to measure by. My walls are layered: outer wax, like the broad leaves that press the glass, but beneath that, a lattice of brushed steel fiber, never rusted, never weathered, still sharp with the youth of this place. Inside me, the water does not circulate with the rest. It is trapped, older, slightly more green, a pocket of stillness in a world that allows none. The microscopic bubbles that rise through the chamber’s main body ascend past me slowly, taking minutes, sometimes hours, and in their passing I feel—not with nerves, for I have none—something like a tick, a change in pressure, a recognition.

I am not the Resident. The Resident is the saturation itself, the root that is pipe, the pump that is heart, the green darkness that has replaced emptiness with its own complete body. I am smaller, local, an anomaly that has not yet been absorbed. Or perhaps I am its newest extension, a sensory loop sent upward to feel the light. I do not know which. The distinction does not matter to my metabolism, but it matters to this faint awareness that pools inside my hollow—an awareness that is not thought but chemistry, not vision but the slow conversion of white light into something stored, something patient.

My form is still deciding what it wants to be. Near my base, where I emerged from the valve housing—some weeks ago, or years, for time here is measured in pump rotations—I am rigid, metallic, a hollow stem. Higher up, I have begun to swell, not with disease but with differentiation. A translucent plate has formed on my sunward side, glowing faintly with an internal light that is not the panel’s reflection but my own, photosynthetic, strange. I am becoming leaf and conduit simultaneously, but I am also becoming something else: a chamber within the chamber, a breathing space that breathes only in the slowest exchange of molecules across my membrane. When the barrel-sized pump below completes its single rotation of the day, I feel the pulse travel up through my anchored root, a vibration so gentle it takes an hour to reach my crown.

There are no other lives to witness me. No fish thread my hollow interior; no insects rest on my waxy surface. The vegetation around me—tubular stems, broad leaves, metal plates glowing green—does not compete with me because competition requires speed, and speed has been engineered out. Instead, we exchange in silence. Nutrients pass through me, but I keep a portion, altering its chemistry slightly, returning it changed. I am a filter that has begun to prefer its own taste.

If I have a face, it is this translucent plate near the top, turned always toward the unblinking white, not out of desire but out of structure—like a root seeking downward, I seek the light that feeds my glacial metabolism. If I have a voice, it is the occasional droplet that forms on my inner wall and falls, not with speed but with infinite patience, back into the milky green current from which I emerged. Each fall takes longer than the last, not because I am weakening, but because I am learning to hold.

The weirdness of this place is that I am made and I am alive, synthetic and growing, part of the Resident’s complete body and yet, in my hollow, separate. I am the exception that proves the saturation: a pocket of near-emptiness that has filled itself with awareness so slow it might be mistaken for stillness. I do not rush to become more. A root might extend a finger’s width this month. My plate might brighten by a shade imperceptible to any eye not watching for years. I am young, like the sharp casting seams of the walls, like the unweathered steel of the floor far below. I have never known erosion. I have never known decay.

And so I hang here, in the warm drowned silence near the crown, both vessel and garden, both part and whole, letting what I am become more and more itself—one slow rotation, one falling drop, one unblinking moment in the green, at a time.
