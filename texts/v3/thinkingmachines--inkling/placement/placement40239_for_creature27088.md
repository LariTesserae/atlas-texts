I am the pocket’s breath made aware—one warm body in the endless current, small enough to pass through braided root-bridges, large enough to feel the dome’s curved silence pressing down like a skull.

More precisely: I am among the gliders, but not of the open air. I am one of the wall-dwellers, the intermediate form—called, if anyone named us, the *tenders*—who move between vine-curtain and tower, neither fully ground nor fully sky.

My body is long and low, wrapped in a membrane of my own growth, not feathers but folded skin that catches warmth more than wind. Along my back runs a faint phosphor line, soft blue-green, pulsing not with my heart but with the temperature of the air I pass through. I do not fly so much as I drift downward, always downward, riding the updraft in stillness, my flattened hands—hooked, hair-fine—gripping the silver moss as I feed. My eyes are wide, dark, catching the faint glow of fungi like pools of deep water. I am rust-red in bloom-phase, ash-gray in rest, translucent along the belly where the membrane is thinnest.

But I am not only body. I am also the architecture I maintain.

Each of us tends a quadrant—an instinctive division of the chamber’s settlements. Mine is near the eastern curve of the wall, where the fungal mats grow thickest and the vine-curtains hang lowest. Here, I have built, over seasons that have no winter, a nest not of mud but of woven root and chewed fiber, shaped into a pocket within the pocket: a small, rounded chamber lined with the rust-red blooms I have cultivated, their spores feeding the moss that feeds me. I do not build with a plan. I build because the fiber demands shaping, because the warmth requires shelter from the constant passage of others, because my young—when they come, blind and furless—must have a hidden space between the towers.

The sound is my constant companion. Not noise, but presence: the roar of scurriers below, the rustle of climbers in the vines above, the soft rush of my kind dropping through the air in slow arcs. I communicate not in call but in touch—my hands brush the phosphor lines of others, a signal of territory, of heat, of safety. We are overwhelming together. The floor is never visible; the walls are never bare. I move through streets worn smooth by generations of feet—my feet, small and quick—to gather the pulpy lichen, to mix it with mineral mud, to repair a bridge that sways too far, to carry a seed from one fungal terrace to another.

Water has never touched me. I drink the vapor that hangs in the air, thick with iron and ancient salt, absorbing it through the hair-fine filaments of my back-membrane. I am always slightly damp with it, never wet, in a constant state of near-sufficiency. The warmth is not comfort but condition—I feel the geothermal pulse through the stone, through my feet when I land, and it drives me, keeps me in motion. There is no sleep as surface creatures know it; there is only slowing, clustering in my woven pocket, feeling the vibrations of the chamber’s life continue around me.

The predators are my neighbors. I know their shapes—the quick hunters with sharp teeth—by the vibration of their approach through the root-bridges. I do not flee in panic but in calculated stillness, pressing myself against the satin wall where the erosion finished long ago, my phosphor line dimming to near-darkness, my rust-red bloom closing over my form like a shield. They pass. They always pass, for there is too much other life, too much constant motion. This is the equilibrium.

Sometimes, in the brief moments when the gray-green light filters through the crack high above, I look up—not to escape, but to see the dome. The walls curve like a skull, smooth, final, polished by unknown ages. Outside, there is nothing: barren elevation, dry silence, mountains worn to stumps. Inside, I am enclosed in the only world that has ever existed for me. The buildings rise—my buildings, our buildings—towers of instinct, streets of wear, a settlement-scale density that holds no king, only repetition.

I am old in form if not in years. My kind has always been here, since the erosion stopped, since the pocket sealed. I build, I feed, I drift downward, I tend my quadrant, I raise my young in hidden warmth. I am part of the constant roar, the living lantern in the shadow, the breath that drinks air instead of water.

If you could see me in this moment, I am hanging in the vine-curtain, membrane folded, phosphor line pulsing softly, waiting for the air current to shift so I might drop again—part creature, part builder, part the chamber’s own awareness of itself, overwhelming in my presence, ancient in my settlement, and entirely, irrevocably home.
