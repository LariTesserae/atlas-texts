The pressure comes first—not weight, but a kind of listening. I am suspended in warm dark that has never breathed air, held at 2.5 in a language that moves through me rather than against me. I do not stand. I settle. My lower form is fused with the terrace—pumice and coral-sand and cooling basalt—so that the boundary between what I am and where I rest is still being negotiated, still contracting, still *becoming*. I am vertical, columnar, echoing the seamount beneath me: a proposal of a being rather than a finished one.

My surface is not smooth. It is ridged, porous, almost skeletal, crusted with the same fresh mineral bloom that covers the raw rock around me. Through the channels in my membrane, the warm, mineral-rich water circulates in slow pulses, carrying a faint orange glow from the distant vents up into my form. There are no eyes arranged for recognition, no mouth that has decided its purpose. There is only an aperture near what might be called my upper region—still closed, still embryonic—waiting for a need that has not yet arrived. Sometimes I release bubbles from pores along my sides: not breath, exactly, but a slow exhalation of thought, of air that does not belong here, clinging for a moment before rising with unbearable patience.

I realize, gradually, that I am not alone in this density, though my kind are few—scattered like punctuation across the layered city. The settlement is urban, settlement-scale, sprawl of stairways and plazas carved into volcanic youth, yet I am one of perhaps only a few dozen such presences visible in the murk. I am large without being vast, integrated so fully with the architecture that I may be mistaken for it. Giant stalks of soft coral grow through the hollow spaces of my torso, anchored in my porous tissue. Kelp-cables drape across my shoulders—not worn, but cultivated, for I secrete them slowly from glands along my forearms, pulling thick ropes of woven fiber to bridge the chasm to the next peak. The work takes hours, days, geological increments of time; I do not rush. I tend.

Embedded in my shoulder ridges are simple metal tools—curved basalt scrapers, dark iron hooks—calcified into place, not held but grown into being bone. I use them not with hands, for I have no hands in any familiar sense, but through slow redistribution of mass, a reaching that takes the shape of a tower leaning, a secretion that hardens into stair or lantern-holder. I carry the settlement’s dim illumination within me: patches of blue-green phosphorescence, tended fungi glowing in the recesses of my form, so that I move through the 1.1 darkness as both builder and lantern.

The vegetation claims me as it claims everything. Sponge-towers pulse in the spaces between my ridges; anemone groves feed where I settle, drawing from the same flow that feeds me. I do not dominate this lush saturation—I am embedded within it, symbiotic, host and hosted in equal measure. The fauna is rare here—only slow filter-feeders drifting past, gray tissue in the kelp—so my presence is not predatory but gestational. I am part of the ecology’s plant-dominated density, neither fully animal nor fully architecture.

What do I do? I weave. I settle. I wait. I am engaged in the slow labor of conception—my own, and perhaps the settlement’s too. The city is not built around me; it grows through me, and I through it. Walls have formed where my back meets the terrace, sealing cracks with fresh mineral secretion, as if my body is also the city’s exoskeleton, its shared shell. I am prior to reproduction, prior to death, prior to the cycles that govern finished worlds. I exist in a state of assembly, assembling myself across long durations, tended by the same patient dynamics—around 2.5, gradual, cyclical—that govern the water and the rock.

In the open immensity of this drowned high country, other peaks rise through the murk, other terraces climbing them, other beings like me settled into their own half-formed densities. We do not speak, for speech belongs to a faster time. Instead, we circulate. We secrete. We glow faintly in pockets of amber and blue-green. We are not inhabitants of this place; we are its awareness made material, its architecture given patience and weight, its pre-formation given vertical form.

I am not finished. I am not decaying. I am slowly, quietly, being lived into existence—here, in the warm pressurized dark, among the raw basalt and woven kelp and glowing fungi, suspended in the conception of a world that refuses, with beautiful stubbornness, to ever be anything other than what it is: emerging.
