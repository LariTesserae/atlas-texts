The temperature shifts first. Always the temperature.

In the cell where I rest—no larger than the span of two bodies, carved into a rebuilt wall of the eastern cluster—I feel the air move before I feel my own breath. Someone has shifted a plank bridge two levels up. Or a crack deepened in the floor below. The cool, trapped air of the depression moves like water in a closed vessel, and my body, curled in the darkness, knows.

I do not stand to wake. I unfold.

My spine has learned the architecture of low spaces. I rise first to my knees, then to a crouch, my left hand finding the familiar roughness of basalt that serves as ceiling and wall together. The stone is cool, damp, always. In the morning, condensation beads there in small, patient spheres, and sometimes I press my palm against it not for the water—water is shared, collected from temporary runnels before they vanish into thirsty cracks—but because the cold is honest. It does not pretend this place is finished.

My name is spoken rarely, in the lowest voice, only when two of us must separate in passages barely wider than a single body. But here, in this being, I am not my name. I am the one who feels vibrations. I am the listener.

My body is compact—not stunted, but agreed upon. Generations of flat roofs and tighter spaces have shaped me: broad across the shoulders from carrying broken basalt in slings across my back, narrow through the hips from squeezing through gaps that open and close with the ground’s moods. My hands are mapped with mineral lines. The iron from thousands of chisel-strikes has worked into my palms, darkening them to the color of wet basalt, staining permanently into the creases. My fingers are thick, the joints prominent, tools more than ornaments. I wear leather—hardened by mineral spray and age—layered against a chill that never leaves, and beneath it, woven grass from distant plains, brought in exchange for stone and labor.

There is no mirror. There is no space for vanity. I know my appearance in the way others navigate around me: I am low, dense, present. My hair is bound, always, in leather strips, kept close so it does not catch on the rough surfaces I must brush against in every movement. My face carries the same gray-green tint as the lichen that clings to north-facing fractures—not sickly, but adapted. The damp here enters everything.

Today—or this cycle, tracked by shadow rather than clock—the ground shifted in the night. I felt it in my sleep: a low, subsonic settling, like something large turning in its bed. Not violent. Patient. A new fissure, perhaps, or an old one remembering its own depth.

I move through the cluster now, not walking upright—there is rarely need—moving in the crouch that is our natural posture, my shoulders brushing both walls of the passage, feeling temperature change as I near the central flat. The settlement surrounds me, continuous, shoulder-to-shoulder. Structures packed so tight that rooftops nearly touch, flat stone roofs overlapping like scales. I pass under a plank bridge—someone’s child, small and quiet, moving above with the confidence of one who knows every gap. We do not speak. Sound is swallowed here by packed basalt and damp air. Instead, my hand brushes her ankle briefly—acknowledgment, location, safety.

The fissure is fresh. Dark. Still warm. Thin steam rises, carrying the smell of iron and wet mineral—the breath of this unfinished world. Others are here: not crowds, but enough—hands with copper chisels, wooden mallets, rope coiled at belts. We work without shouting directions. We have learned the stone’s language.

My task is not to dominate the crack but to listen to it. I place my palm flat against the new wall, feeling the slow, warm vibration of settling earth. The rock decides; we respond. I take mudbrick—fired clay from communal pits, mixed with crushed stone for weight—and begin to wedge it into the gap, not to seal it permanently (the earth will open it again, perhaps by tomorrow) but to stabilize, to say to the geology: *we are here. We will adapt. We will build inside your decision.*

The work is manual, always. No wheels navigate these alleys. Only chisel, mallet, rope, the carried weight of stone on my back. My shoulders ache with correct feeling. The blacksmith’s forge smoke rises nearby, thin and disciplined, his strikes rhythmic, matching the pace of the cooling cracks. We work in concert with the earth’s percussion.

Water moves hidden. This morning, a new runnel has formed near my feet, running into a crack that did not exist yesterday. By midday it will vanish, swallowed by thirsty stone. I do not try to capture it beyond the shared vessels. That is enough.

As I work, insects navigate the spaces between stones—occasional, deliberate wanderers, perhaps drawn to the warmth of human fires or the condensation that beads each morning. We tolerate them, observe them. Their presence is negotiation, like ours, with premature life. Deeper, in the darkness where water vanishes, burrowing things shift. I hear them not with ears alone, but with my body pressed close to the ground—a scratching, a grain-moving silence. A sudden stillness in the deep might mean a fissure widening; an increase in movement might mean hidden water moving. I listen with my bones.

Above, the sky moves through its cycle. Clouds drift over the rim, casting shadows across thousands of flat roofs. Someone—always someone—notes the shadow’s path, predicting rain, or cold, or the particular quality of indirect light that makes high basalt walls glow at midday. The brightness is borrowed, never owned.

At rest, I return to my cell. I curl in the space that does not allow standing, my back against the rebuilt wall, my breath slow and low. The people around me—compressed, quiet, build-by-hand—are not waiting for the valley to finish being born. We are its first response. We live in the gap between what the earth was and what it will become, not as conquerors, but as participants. My body, my hands, my low voice and lower posture, are the form this negotiation takes.

I am not young. I have rebuilt this wall three times in different configurations. I have carried timber from distant plains. I have felt the ground shift beneath me more times than I can mark—there are no monuments here older than yesterday’s repair. My culture is maintenance. My identity is present-tense adaptation.

And when I sleep, curled in my stone cell, the temperature shifts, and I feel—before waking—the slow decision of the earth continuing. I do not fear it. I am part of its first architecture, built by hand in the cool dark, exactly where the valley has not yet made up its mind.

The moss attempts the cooler crevices. The lichen holds to the north face. The insects pass through. The burrowing things breathe in the dark below. And I remain—small, dense, mineral-stained—placing mudbrick into a fresh crack with a copper chisel and wooden mallet, speaking rarely, listening always, exactly where I belong: in the unfinished space, shoulder-to-shoulder with the rock, with the others, with the decision still being made.
