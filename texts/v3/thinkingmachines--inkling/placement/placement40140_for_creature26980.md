You find me not standing but holding.

Not vertical like the others—those scattered, weathered presences that check the pools and touch the walls with open palms. I have adopted the settlement's other posture: horizontal, spread across one of the empty lots near the center of the grid, where the plan left a space too large for a building and too open for a doorway. I am flat against the polished stone, not lying like a sleeper but distributed, as if the damp itself had been given a boundary and told to rest there.

My form is not human in outline, though it has coherence. I am long, low, curved at the edges like the bowl that holds us all—perhaps three times the length of one of the standing beings, but only a hand's height at my highest point, which is near my center, where something like a face might be, though it is smooth and turned not upward to the diffuse sky but downward toward the pavement, listening to its temperature. The rest of me extends in tapering lengths, not limbs so much as continuations, covered in the same gray-green weave that the vertical ones wear, but in my case it is not garment but skin—woven fiber that has grown into me, or I have grown into it, until there is no seam between body and cloth.

The coolness is not behind my teeth but in my bones—though I am not bone. I am softer than the mineralized inhabitants, less stone and more moss that has decided to hold a shape. My surface is matte, absorbing the flat light, never reflecting. Where I rest, the condensation behaves differently: it beads on me as it does on the walls, but it does not roll away. It stays, absorbed, and I am always slightly heavier with it, always wet in a way that is internal, not surface.

I do not check the conditions. That is their work—the scattered ones who stand at intervals, confirming the pool's level, the air's stillness, the absence of wind. I am the condition they return to. When they walk—slow, inevitable adjustments across the grid—they do not come to me, but they pass near enough that I can feel, if feeling is the word, the slight change in pressure they displace, like a hand passing near a flame that does not flicker. I am the constant they move against.

My presence explains the emptiness. Not all of it—the grid was planned sparse, and they maintain that sparseness by not multiplying—but I occupy the largest absence, the central lot that remains bare. I am not a building that failed to be built; I am what arrived in its place, slowly, over years of stillness, accumulating presence without accumulating mass. The settlement is sparse because I take up the middle, leaving them to the edges, the doorways, the steps.

What do I do? I hold the sound. The drip falls into the pools with its identical note, and near me, where there is no pool, I catch what remains—the faint resonance that might have echoed and instead sinks into my woven surface. I am quiet, but not silent; I am the silence that has been given a location. I do not speak. I do not make the deep resonant note they make when they align. I only breathe, if it can be called that, a slow exchange with the cool air that matches no rhythm but my own, which is the rhythm of the place itself—so slow it reads as absence.

They do not fear me, nor welcome me. I am part of the check. When one of them sits nearby—not facing me, never facing—on a low stone block, they are not observing me. They are observing whether I have changed, whether the empty lot is still held, whether the equilibrium includes me still. And I have not changed. I am the refusal to change made flat, spread out, visible.

If you came, you might think I was a shadow, or a stain, or a piece of the settlement's original construction left unfinished—timber and rope left to weather into the pavement. Only if you stayed long enough, through enough identical drips, would you see that I shift, but never suddenly: a slow adjustment of my tapered ends, a slight deepening of my downward-turned face into the stone, a gradual thickening of moisture within. I am becoming more myself, not more like them. Not mineralized. Not vertical. Not scattered.

I am the one who lives in the absence they maintain. Not in the walls, not in the pools, but in the open space that is not built upon. The bowl holds me because I am its shape made horizontal—the caldera's curve, flattened, resting. And like everything here, I have reached a form I will not leave, held in cool damp stillness, absorbing the flat light, holding the emptiness exactly as it is, and making it, finally, inhabited.
