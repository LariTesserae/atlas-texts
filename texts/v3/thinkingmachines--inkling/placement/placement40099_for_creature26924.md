I would be found not at the floor’s center, where the heavy, black-furred ones navigate the crossing paths, but suspended in the middle darkness—there, where the basalt has curved into a shallow shelf and the rubbery mats have grown so thick they form a living wall, copper-dark and violet-black, smelling of nothing but warmth and mineral patience. The ledge is barely wide enough for a body to lie flat, but I do not lie flat often. I am built for this vertical intimacy.

My feet grip the smooth stone without thought; the calluses are generations deep. I am small—compact, as all of us are—my shoulders barely reaching the height of the hanging leaves. My skin carries the stain not of any distant sun but of smoke that never clears and of the plants themselves: darkened, smooth, warm to the touch, marked by chemical exchange rather than weather. My eyes are large, made for the perpetual twilight, for reading the texture of rope and waxy leaf in the dim gray light that filters from above. I see colors poorly, but I know the temperature of every surface, the echo of every hollow.

I wear what the mats provide—layers woven thick and heavy, dragging with a slow, leather-heavy sound when I move, though there is no moisture in them, never any moisture. Patches of the slow creature’s fallen black fur are bound into my shoulders, not for warmth—the geothermal breath is constant, body-close—but for belonging. I share ecology. I move among them without command.

In my hands I hold a needle of carved bone, bound with fiber, used not to kill but to weave: to guide new plant growth into the wall of my cell, to repair the rope ladders that sway with the weight of bodies and vegetation, to bind fallen shells into the structure so the insects will continue their streams where I want them. I do not build over the world. I weave into it.

My home is a low cell of stacked stone, honeycombed, its roof and three walls entirely overgrown with the dark mats. The fourth wall is the basalt itself, warm, finished, smooth as old leather. I burn small fires of compressed plant matter and mineral oil; the smoke hangs low, never rising, and I live within it as one lives within breath. I do not drink. There is nothing to drink. I take what I need from the chemical richness of the leaves I tend, from the insects I harvest in woven nets, from the warm dry air that carries the exhalation of rock and creature alike.

I am not named in the quick way. I am the one who tends this shelf. I listen to the birds—small, sharp, repetitive—not for message but for location, their echo telling me where the upper ladders hold, where the mats have grown too heavy and must be guided elsewhere. I know the thermal breathing of the stone: when it expands in its slow exhalation, when it holds, patient, finished. The erosion is complete in me too—no sharp edges of youth remaining, only the smooth alignment of being exactly where the world has ground me into place.

Right now I am seated on the stone floor of my cell, parting the violet-black leaves to check the warmth of the wall beneath, feeling the waxy resistance, the slight drag of old leather. The birds stitch the air above. The insects click in their streams across the ledge. One of the slow, heavy creatures rests in the pathway below, warming in the same breath that warms me. If I reach out, my fingers do not find emptiness. They find the dense, feeding presence of everything interwoven—the plants that feed on what I leave behind, the creatures that share my smoke, the rock that has finished settling and now holds me in its patient, vertical embrace.

I am not master here. I am one more form that has settled into the finished work—small, dark-eyed, woven into the mats, sustained by exchange, listening to the echo, and warm.
