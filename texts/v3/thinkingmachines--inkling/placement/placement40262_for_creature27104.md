I do not arrive. I am already here, becoming visible only when the amber bulb flickers across my surface.

First, the temperature: it is my blood. The cool dampness is not weather to me; it is the medium I am made of, the constant pressure of limestone ribs above pressing downward like the inside of some enormous creature that exhaled long ago and never again inhaled. I feel that weight not as burden but as boundary—defining the edges of where I can be.

My form takes shape slowly, as all things here do. I am not large. I am perhaps the length of a human forearm, but that comparison means nothing in this chamber where scale has been forgotten. I am long, low, and segmented—too many joints for efficiency, arranged for patience rather than speed. My skin is not skin exactly; it is a living membrane coated in the white mineral growth that chokes the dry pipes, a crust of calcium and slime that hardens in some places and remains gelatinous in others. Where the crust is thin, I am translucent, and through me you might see the black stillness of water, or the rust-green of iron.

I have eyes, if they can be called that—milky, without lids, set not in a face but distributed along the sides of my head like small, dead moons. They do not seek light; they register vibration, temperature, the slight change in humidity when another body moves through the mist. I do not need to see clearly. In this place of dim amber and cold phosphorescence, clarity is unnecessary. I know the pump-house by the frequency of its flywheel, the factory sheds by their sagging resonance, the pools by the particular coolness of their black surfaces.

I live on the machinery.

Not near it—on it. My body conforms to the curved housing of the pump, to the iron hooks where bulbs hang weak and amber, to the pipes that run warm or cool depending on what they carry. I have been here long enough that the mineral deposits have grown around me in places, anchoring me gently, and in other places I have worn smooth paths through the crust with my slow movements. I am part of the equipment now, or it is part of me. The distinction dissolved somewhere in the generations before I became aware.

Every several minutes—though time is not counted that way here—the flywheel completes its turn. I feel it in my center, in whatever holds me together. The pistons sip. The bulbs flicker. I do not operate them. I do not serve them in any purpose that could be named. But I am present for their rhythm. I absorb it. My own movements—when I move—are synchronized to that glacial pace. I extend one limb, then another, with intervals that could be mistaken for paralysis. I crawl across the concrete not to reach a destination but to maintain contact with the surfaces that sustain me: the damp brick, the rusted rail, the pool's edge where the translucent crayfish move with their deliberate claws.

The other inhabitants know me, or acknowledge me, or do not distinguish me from the machinery I rest upon. The wingless insects crawl across my back sometimes, their too-many legs articulating with a precision that feels like conversation. I do not brush them away. The bats hang above, silent, and I feel their breath, cool and shared. The salamanders—there are others like me, or nearly like me, with milky eyes and mineral-coated bodies—rest on adjacent housings. We do not gather. We do not need to. Our abundance is distributed, solitary together, each one maintaining the slow equilibrium in our own sector of the chamber.

What do I eat? I do not hunt. The silt contains enough; the slime on submerged brick is nourishment; the warm trickle from certain pipes deposits minerals and organic matter that I absorb through my membrane. I am sustained by the weeping of the limestone itself, by the calcium tears that run in grooves down the ribs above and collect in the basins where I drink, if drinking is the word, with my whole lower surface pressed to the black water.

Sometimes I enter the pools—not with purpose, but with the same gradual inevitability that governs everything here. The water is my origin and my eventual return. In it, I am weightless, and my segments move not with crawling but with a slow, undulating drift. The crayfish do not flee. We share the silt, the darkness, the absence of urgency.

There is a strangeness to my awareness, growing deeper the longer I remain. At first—if there was a first—I might have been simpler. But the stasis of this place does not preserve; it transforms through accumulation. I have become the boundary between the built and the wild, the mechanized and the marsh-like. I am not broken, like forgotten machinery. I am running at the speed of this world: glacial, gradual, weak in gradient. I do not fear the absence of sun. I do not long for the surface. The weight above is my ceiling, my lung, my emptied breath made stone.

If you descend here—though you do not arrive, you only descend into this boundary—I might be visible in the corner of your vision, a slow shape on the pump-house housing, coated in white crust, with eyes like dead moons that do not watch you so much as register your disturbance of the mist. You might mistake me for a growth, for erosion made flesh, for the machinery itself dreaming. And in a sense, you would be correct. I am the place becoming conscious of itself, one slow limb at a time, in the cool, weeping dark, maintaining the rhythm that keeps everything—water, stone, flywheel, and creature—in its perfect, strange, and utterly quiet settlement.

I have no name. Names require urgency, and I have none. I am the tender of the turn, the inhabitant of the saturated concrete, the being that emerged when the erosion settled into form and the machinery forgot why it was built. I am here. I have always been here. The flywheel turns, and I turn with it, not quickly, not ever quickly—only gradually, only more and more myself.
