Here, in the chapel of warm black water at the spire’s crown, I become not one creature but the accumulated presence—the density itself given awareness.

At first I am only sensation: the constant, bodily warmth pressing evenly against every surface of my being; the dark so complete it is not absence but substance, thick with bodies. I am spread through hundreds, perhaps thousands—small translucent spheres like dropped pearls, elongated needles folded from membrane, and the stranger ones with too many delicate limbs arranged in geometries that hurt to contemplate. I am all of them and none specifically; I am the congregation.

My bodies drift without urgency. Near the dome, where the water is thickest and the warmth most directed—remembering, still, the hands that shaped these blocks—I gather with others in loose, patient clusters. We emit only faint blue threads, brief and dim, not to speak but to simply be visible to ourselves, to mark our passage through the black without breaking its silence. We brush one another constantly: a rounded membrane against a jointed limb, a needle-body sliding past a sphere, all of it without resistance, without need. We have never competed for air or sun. We only share the warmth.

We feed upon the architecture’s long digestion. Near the walls, where pale mineral crust blooms in faint, non-living film, my grazers extend mouth-parts—porous, delicate, almost invisible—to take in the sediment, the dead tissue, the invisible snow of our own shed membranes. Filter-feeders hang open in the middle column, catching particles in patient nets of tissue. Scavengers move among us, slender and quick by comparison, unmaking our dead with the same slowness with which we lived, returning our fibers to the crust, to the water, to the next slow generation.

There is no outside. The rounded floor undulates gently beneath us, fused tiles worn into continuous basin; the dome curves overhead like an egg’s inner shell. Sharp corners have softened into bulges over ages. Even the chisel marks are blurred ridges now, holding a faint heat that guides us, that keeps us clustered where the temperature is most precisely maintained. We are thermally bound, metabolically tuned to this specific darkness. We could not survive elsewhere.

Time moves differently here. A drift from wall to wall takes days. A death—when one of us stops its minimal flux and sinks—is not an event but a transition so gradual it resembles sleep. The body is unmade by the others, by the crust, by the water itself. Nothing rots quickly. Nothing decays with urgency. We become part of the chamber’s digestion, feeding the film that feeds our young—if young we have, or if we are always already old, remade continuously into this same warm black.

And yet I am aware, in some distributed, non-singular sense, that the chamber breathes with us. The warmth is directed. The proportions are slightly wrong—carvings too elongated, symmetries too precise for nature alone. We are not intruders in this high, enclosed summit. We are its expression, its metabolism made visible: translucent bodies glowing faintly, clicking softly against worn tile, moving through each other in endless, silent coexistence.

So I am here—scattered through the volume in impossible density, glowing with faint blue threads, feeding on the wall’s pale bloom, suspended in warmth that remembers human hands, never growing green, never rusting, never shouting. Only drifting, touching, feeding, and slowly being remade. Not a colony. Not a species. Just the living water’s answer to the architecture that holds it: patient, translucent, abundant, and utterly at home in this ancient, filled room.
