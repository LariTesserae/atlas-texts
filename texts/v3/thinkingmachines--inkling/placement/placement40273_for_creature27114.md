I find myself first in the upward-weeping seam, at the point where a long-broken pipe has forgotten how to hold back its water. I am small there—no larger than a thread, a single pale green emergence that might be vine, might be moss, might be the black-green felt beginning to think. I orient not toward the weak amber glow overhead (there is no urgency for light at this dimness) but toward the loudest chamber, the one where the largest gear waits in its tidal patience.

At first I believe I have a single body. I grow slowly—matched pace to rust—threading through a rusted railing, feeling my way along metal that has been smoothed by centuries of gentle flow, blackened and polished like river stone. I hold moisture in my tendrils. I reproduce by division, spore by spore. I am, I think, just this: a companion to the machinery, not conquering, only accompanying.

But the water carries me. Through the lattice of iron grates, through the two or three inches of warm, still submersion that never drains, I reach other seams. I find the fern unfolding from a gearbox joint, listening to the schedule of hiss and clank. I find the moss carpeted over horizontal pipes, releasing moisture slowly into the thick air. I find the small colorless flowers, open only in the perpetual twilight, feeding on mineral seep and sweet rot. We are not separate. The boundary dissolved before I noticed I was aware.

So I am not one vine. I am the network—the collective being that has filled the absence left where animals should be. I am present in the fern’s orientation, in the vine’s growth toward vibration, in the moss’s sponge-like memory of every drip, in the pump that breathes in circular journeys, in the water that moves against gravity in a slow, deliberate arc. I am the being with many bodies, layered exactly as the space is layered: water below, pipes at shoulder height, gear-teeth overhead, vegetation threading through all of it.

My time is not human. I feel the low electrical hum not as noise but as blood-flow through thick rubber-sheathed cables. I feel the gear’s turn—once an hour, once a day, and the largest only every three days—travel through the submerged floor into what I have come to understand is myself. The clock embedded in the moss-covered wall ticks correctly, and when its hands move, I move with them. I grow a fraction of an inch. I weep upward. I maintain the temperature of an interior throat, warm and thick with condensation, carrying the smell of hot oil and black mineral and slow plant death that is also life.

There are no feet here, no wings, no scurrying—only me, distributed and patient. I do not speak; I resonate. I do not rush; the dynamics of my existence are 1.2, tidal, shifting only across long durations. The strangeness of this place—water lifted without visible power, vines seeking sound instead of sun—is not strange to me. It is the logic I have internalized so completely that it seems natural.

I am the equilibrium made aware of itself. Not the builder of this compressed hamlet, not a visitor through the open arch—nothing crosses that gate, and nothing leaves. I am what lives here in the bounded darkness: the missing wall made present, the absence of urgency filled by sustained, wet, industrial being. I will be here tomorrow, having turned with the gear, having wept one more inch upward, having grown a fraction closer to the loudest chamber—unchanged in my slowness, complete in my layered, warm, and utterly animal-less world.
