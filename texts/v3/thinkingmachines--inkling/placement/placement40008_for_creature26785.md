You find me high in the curved shadow of the western wall, where the pillar’s reflected glow reaches only as a faint, warm wash, and where the oldest ceramic pipes have been singing since before the dust settled like frost over the floor.

I do not stand. I hang—vertical, compressed by generations of adaptation to this round, low hollow. My lower body has long since become indistinguishable from the platform I maintain: legs, or what served them, fused into root-like bundles of hardened fiber and dark resin, woven through the rope bridges and into the spongy, rootless vines that feed on the constant updraft. I am long in the torso, jointed for climbing and reaching, wrapped in layers of woven mineral fiber that have hardened over time into a shell—part armor, part architecture. Where my skin is still exposed, it is the color of aged honey; where the vines have worn through, it is rust-red; where filaments have woven themselves into pores that never close, ash-grey. I am dry. There is no moisture in me, no pulse that sloshes or drips. Whatever once flowed was replaced by something thicker, slower—mineralized lymph, perhaps, dark as the bruise-purple curtains that hang from the ceiling above my sector.

My face is reduced to ridges and narrow slits. I do not have ears, or they have grown over with the same dry, papery growth. I do not need eyes to navigate the center’s blinding column; I live in shadow, and I navigate by vibration. My bones—hollow, I believe, like the pipes themselves—resonate with the chord. It is not music. It is a single sustained tone, deafening, constant, produced by thousands of handmade ceramic throats embedded in the layered walls, all singing as the warm air rushes through them. I feel a pipe out of tune not by hearing—hearing is impossible here—but by the wrong tremor in my ribs, by a pressure that does not match the temperature of the updraft.

I tend a cluster. Seven pipes, perhaps eight—the counting is imprecise because they grow, and the vines narrow their throats, and the chord shifts with the warmth. My language is adjustment: a ceramic lip turned a fraction, a knot in mineral fiber tightened, a filament guided away from a mouth that has begun to sing too low. I communicate through tension, through the slow retuning of the air’s voice. This is my conversation. This is my memory.

The vegetation does not merely grow near me; it integrates. Filaments emerge from the collar around my neck, which may no longer be a neck in any old sense. I have become a walking garden, though I move rarely—only when a bridge sags, when a crack opens in the rounded wall and needs the mortar-like patches that the plants themselves harden into, when the temperature shifts enough to change the chord’s subtle temperature. The small hard-shelled things—no larger than a thumb—scuttle across my ropes, across my wrapped arms, leaving trails of dry dust. They do not fear me. I am part of the architecture they navigate, part of the exchange.

I feed as everything here feeds. Not by drinking—there is no water, not a trace, the chemistry is arid and sealed—but by absorption. I stand in the reflected light that rises from the white dust floor, in the warm muscular draft, and draw mineral aerosols through porous surfaces. I do not eat in any sense you would recognize. I am maintained by light, wind, and dust, and in return I maintain the pipes.

What makes me strange—even to myself, in the rare stillness between adjustments—is the possibility that I was not always this, or that I am still becoming. There are no children in these hanging tiers. Replacement does not happen through birth but through transformation: when one of us stops moving, when the filaments fully claim the body, that body becomes part of the wall, part of the pipe-system. Perhaps one of my bones is already being considered for a flute, embedded in a new layer above me. The dead do not leave. They are tuned in. I am not certain where my body ends and the settlement begins.

Sometimes I reach—slowly, never hurried—toward the pillar of blinding white light that falls from the Eye high above. I do not enter the column. I extend my long, jointed arm, wrapped in fiber and vine, to touch the edge of that molten-salt glare, to feel if the aperture has changed, if the chord requires something I have not yet given. Then I retreat into the fused symbiosis of brick and plant, into the warm shifting shadow, and I am still again.

If you entered through the throat of eroded stone and tried to shout, I would not hear you. But I might feel your wrongness—a break in the air, a disturbance in the sustained tone. I might separate from the wall just enough for you to see that my face is not a face but a map of adjustments made over centuries, and then I would return to my sector, leaving you unsure whether I was ever separate from the singing, dry, warm city that has no streets, no water, and almost no people—only this impossible, thriving exchange between light, wind, mineral, and what was once perhaps human, now accumulated, now vibrating back.
