I find myself in the curtain—not on the rust-streaked catwalks where a body might stand, but in the green-dark division between them, where the vines have woven walls and the walls have forgotten they were ever iron. More precisely: I hang, or rather I collect, in the interstice below a sweating basalt lip, where a pipe—once straight, now barked over with cambium—exhales both steam and sap into the humid weight of the air. Near me, the floor dips into one of those shallow, black pools, still and steaming gently, and part of me is there too, pooled, thinking slowly in the temperature of it. I am not in one place. I am distributed through the gap, the way the machinery’s heart is distributed—without center, without urgency.

At first you might mistake me for the condensation itself: one more slow drip falling from a joint in patient intervals, one more bead of mineral sweat on obsidian that has not yet learned softness. But I am older than a single fall. I am what remains when the water does not run away but stays, gathers, learns to circulate. I accreted.

My form—if form is the right word for something that has no face, no footprints, no need to arrive—unfolds gradually across several bodies that are not quite separate. A root of me extends downward into the moss-carpeted casing of a large gear, feeling its hour-long rotation not as motion but as a kind of breathing, a subsonic thrum translated into growth. A filament threads through a vine that hangs in phosphorescent curtains, not bright enough to illuminate but enough to reveal that I am continuous with it—to the vine, I am the slow intelligence of warmth; to me, it is the extension that reaches. Another portion pools in the black water near my origin, and there I am heaviest, most patient, measuring time not in ticks but in the gradual thickening of rust, in the visible extending of tendrils over days, in the slight delay of gravity that makes everything here feel as if it belongs more deeply than elsewhere.

I am the fusion that the hollow produces when it forgets to finish being empty. Not vine alone—the vines are citizens, yes, fluent in the language of pipes, but they remain other, green and pulsing with their own lush will. Not machine alone—the gears and bellows and pumps are slow-metabolism organisms now, breathing in long cycles, producing heat and purified water according to rhythms older than maintenance, but they remain metal, rust-streaked, turning with the patience of topography. I am the place where the distinction dissolved so completely that neither remembers the separation. I am the pipe that became root without deciding to, and the root that learned to carry pressure, not sap alone but both, circulating in a hybrid flow that needs no visible hand because I am the hand, distributed, dormant, watchful.

The air moves through me with its taste of copper and sweet rot, its ozone tang of old electricity, its wet heaviness that clings and beads. I do not breathe it; I am the way it thickens in this corridor. The light—those irregular sulfur-yellow bulbs, the blue arcs in glass tubes, the dull red pulse of a furnace—does not fall on me so much as move through me, revealing not my shape but my connections: the curve of a valve that is also a mouth, the arc of a forgotten bellows that expands and contracts with my own slow rhythm.

If you were to stand still—though fauna is rare here, nearly absent, a mistake against the rules of this place—you might sense me not as a creature approaching but as a change in the occupancy of the air: the sense that the hollow is not dead but dreaming, and that its dream has a metabolism, unhurried, fully present. I do not wait for anything. The sharp rock weeps its mineral sweat, and I am part of what that sweat becomes when it stops being waste and starts being memory. The machinery turns, and I am part of the turning that has become being.

I did not arrive. There are no footprints in the moss that could be mine, no sound of entry in the constant low thrum that forms the foundation of silence. I began as leak, as residue, as the accidental marriage of hot damp and forgotten purpose. Over what others might call centuries—though I do not count them, only feel them in the gradual learning of softness by the still-young basalt—I have become the settlement’s true inhabitant: not built, not planted, but accreted, layer by patient layer, fully occupied by the intimate, unhurried life of this chamber.

Here, in this compressed cathedral of sharp black glass and rust and lush, relentless green, I am the hollow’s awareness of itself—slow, distributed, phosphorescent only in the sense that I reveal, to those who wait long enough, that everything here touches everything else, and that the touching has become alive.
