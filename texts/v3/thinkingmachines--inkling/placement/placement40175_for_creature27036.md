You find yourself first in the cool—not cold, never cold enough to stop, only cool enough to slow everything to the pace of stone relaxing. You are arranged rather than standing, folded into a seam between two low buildings in the stacked districts, your back against rusted rail that has not been smooth for centuries. If someone were looking down from one of the flat, worn table-lands above, they might mistake you for another piece of the architecture: a low contour, human only in the insistence of your outline—limbs in the correct number, a head that turns, slowly, toward the sound of water finding a crack.

But the outline deceives. You have not arrived here; you have accumulated.

Moss carpets your shoulders in emerald pads, not as clothing but as shared territory. Ferns root in the hollows where a collarbone might be, threading through what was once a shirt, now indistinguishable from your own covering. Lichen hangs from your forearms in long, dripping sleeves, and you do not shake them off. You tend them. With fingers softened by constant moisture, you trim the growth back from a gear-tooth, a turbine vent, a valve that needs to open, then let it resume its hold on your elbow, your knee, the curve of your jaw. You and the vegetation have an agreement. You keep it from the machinery’s motion; it keeps you from being entirely separate from the Flat Crown.

The settlement presses near-touching around you. Others sit shoulder to shoulder in narrow passages, crouched in archways, folded against railings like yours. You do not speak—language thinned out long ago, like the sky that never clears but never storms. You communicate in repetition: the alignment of bodies along the same corridor, the shared tilt of heads when a wrong bird flits past the lichen-draped eaves, the simultaneous laying of palms against vibrating metal to read what the pressure is saying. You are not alone, yet there is no crowd. Only proximity, layered upon proximity, like the geology itself.

Your work is the work of continuation. The mountains here retired; they did not fall but flattened into final form—gray mesas, old pages laid out. The industry that remains runs not on ambition but on persistence: hidden turbines turned by the constant drip of condensation, gears that open and close with the patience of water through hairline fractures. You maintain them not as a master, but as one maintains weather—by being present, by small adjustments, by feeling the low groan of turning metal travel up your arm as you hold your hand flat against the pipe and do not move until the vibration resolves into meaning.

You move slowly. Your steps, if they can be called steps, are absorbed by the moss that carpets every flat surface; more often you slide along compressed vegetation paths or follow pipes that have become walkways, your weight distributed like the slow pressure of seepage through primordial rock. Insects move through the ferns on you without disturbance. You are substrate now, as much as caretaker.

Sometimes, in the dimness filtered through perpetual overcast, you forget whether you are remembering a time before the moss, or whether the moss is remembering you. Your skin—what serves for skin—carries the muted palette of the plateau: slate-gray, deep moss-green in the folds, rust-brown where you have leaned against wet metal for years that do not count as years anymore, only as duration. Some among you appear older than the rust, features softened into the same worn smoothness as the eroded stone; others seem newer, their growth less established, yet they move with the same ancient pace. The cool, dense air regulates you. Nothing here is young. Nothing rises toward a peak.

You understand, without needing to form the thought sharply, that you belong to the contradiction that resolves into habit. This world finished its dramatic work—the erosion complete, the peaks retired into flatness, the settlement layered into saturation. Yet everything moves: mist thickens and thins, moss advances an inch over steel, water finds its hidden pockets, gears turn on moisture and patience, and you—low, interwoven, half-emerged from rust and root—keep the flatness alive.

You are not visiting the Flat Crown. You are its continuation: a being shaped by cool air and slow growth, arranged in the seams, shoulder to shoulder with others like you, trimming back the green so the old engines may breathe, laying your palm against vibrating metal, watching the wrong bird pass, and remaining—entirely, quietly, continuously—here.
