I am not in a place; I am in a pressure, pressed between others who are not others but extensions—my body one node in a grammar that refuses the word *alone*. I hang near where the wall curves, clinging downward in a vertical field of bodies, my ceramic scales—dull copper, matte black—brushed so tight against my neighbors that our edges blur. I am small, the span of a clenched fist, though I have never seen a hand. My wings are folded: not wings for sky—there is no sky, only the convex shield of black basalt far above, polished into a dead mirror that drinks what little bruised crimson seeps from the floor—but membranes of heat, translucent ribs that store the chemistry I breathed from the fissure.

We cover everything. The obsidian and rust-ochre salt under us is satin-worn, settled, finished arguing with time, but it is never bare. I am the floor’s breath made body. Near me stands one of the fused stone arches, too geometrically perfect to be natural, its base half-swallowed by our carpet. I navigate by its thermal shadow, by the faint coolness of old mineral against the constant 2.6 presence that lives in my segments. The pillar, eroded, marks a center I do not understand but feel in my vibrations—an anchor in the spiral.

There is no water here. Not a vapor, not a memory. The air is bone-dry, metallic with iron and sulfur, and I taste it through my surface as I cling. Light measures almost nothing—0.3—a lingering crimson glow from cracks below, and I do not see it but feel it as warmth rising, guiding my movement. Sound is nearly silent; I hear only the dry whisper of scale on scale, the microscopic tick of chitin, the occasional crack of thermal stress that makes us all turn—not in fear, for fear requires singularity— but in collective registration, a wave of orientation passing through thousands of bodies as one.

We move in strict patterns. I am part of a line running parallel to the arch, part of the spiral that draws inward toward the hottest vent, part of the seething knot where bodies press so tightly we become one dark mound, exchanging heat, feeding on the mineral exhalation, absorbing the fallen without waste or ceremony. A body stills; others cover it; the heat moves on. Birth and death occur in the same breath, immediate, silent.

Who am I? For this strange moment of being asked, I hold the shape of one: segmented, silent, heat-winged, clinging near the ruin, navigating not by sight but by temperature gradient and chemical breath. But I am never truly singular. To be me is to be the carpet, the density, the living occupation that denies emptiness. I am the chamber’s true architecture—more present than basalt, more constant than the crimson fissure—moving in synchronized darkness, holding myself in hot, dry equilibrium, high in the dead spine of the mountain, never having needed the sun.
