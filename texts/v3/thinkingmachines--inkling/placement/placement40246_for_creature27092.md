I am at the place where three boardwalks join, though “join” implies a choice to turn, and there is none. I am anchored there, in the low grid, shoulder-deep in amber steam, on a broad plinth of unshaped stone that never settles deeper and never shifts. The basalt beneath me weeps constantly—warm water hissing up through hairline fractures—and I feel that heat through whatever serves me as feet: flat, broad, gripping surfaces that do not lift so much as hold. I do not travel. Distance is open, horizontal, infinite, but I am dense here, packed shoulder-to-shoulder with walls that never rise above my head, with other bodies pressed close in the same low horizon.

My hands—this I know because they move—are coated in mineral slime and mud, mixing crushed shell into mortar to repair a wall I have always been repairing. The blocks are split from fresh bedrock with a wooden lever and a wedge of simple metal, tools that feel older than memory but never wear out. I do not recall the first wall, or if there was one. There is only this wall, this repair, this rhythm of setting stone into the same gap, hour after hour, without acceleration, without rest.

My body is low, pressed near the ground, wrapped in woven reed-substitute that never rots because nothing rots and nothing dries. I am covered in the chemical films that coat every plane—black, gray, rust-brown—slick patterns eating the warmth that bleeds through the crust. I do not brush them away. They are residents, like the hard-shelled creatures swimming in the pool beside my plinth, like the larger amphibians clinging to walls at chest-height, blinking slowly in the mist, eyes never fully closing. We share the same breath: the swarm suspended in the saturated air clicks and scrapes in unison, and my own breathing is part of that drone, continuous, deafening, unchanged.

Sometimes I lower the woven net into the shallow basin that never fills and never drains. The small shell-creatures enter with mechanical precision—not fleeing, not seeking, just maintaining their correct density. I harvest them. Not to diminish them—their number is fixed, born and dying at identical rates—but because the act itself is part of the equilibrium, like the water falling, like the slime spreading, like my hands mixing mud.

If I am one of the people, then I am the kind who has forgotten where my body ends and the settlement begins. I have no name, no language of change, no concept of growth or decay. I do not shout; there is nothing to shout over except the thunder of weeping rock and the roar of presence itself. I communicate, perhaps, in gestures practical and repeated, or in sounds that dissolve into the collective hum.

But perhaps I am not fully a person. Perhaps I am something that emerged from the slime and the basalt together: a maintaining presence, broad and flat and warm, anchored in the grid, my consciousness distributed like the chemical patterns across stone. I cling and blink and repair. I am the boardwalk that holds its shape because nothing erodes here. I am the pool that reflects the same dull amber light forever.

You would find me here, in the perpetual diffusion of steam and warmth, at the intersection that is not an intersection, mixing mud with hands that are also walls, holding the net that harvests what is always correct, being the stasis made local. I am not evolving. I am not finished. I am the world’s first breath, held forever—one dense, horizontal node in the flat, weeping, deafening flatness, being exactly what I am, which is everything necessary, without end.
