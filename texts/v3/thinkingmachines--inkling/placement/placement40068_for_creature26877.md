I do not begin with a shape, but with a location that refuses to be a location: the middle volume, where neither the worn-flat stone below nor the unreachable thinning above can claim me. I am near one of the scattered remnants—not the pillar, but the partial arch, the one that curves over nothing, its purpose already worn away with everything else. I am suspended in the warmest layer, the thermal heart of the circulation, where the vines grow not in thickets but in a continuous, breathing wall, so dense that to move through them is not to travel but to be slowly incorporated.

At first I think I am passing through. That is the earliest mistake, and it falls away quickly, like spore-dust that never reaches ground. I realize I am not passing; I am settled. Not anchored—there is nothing here that anchors in the old way—but compressed, accumulated, given coherence by the same forces that keep the water aloft at this impossible height.

My body—though the word comes slowly, and only after I have abandoned other words—begins as an extension of the root-net. I am not separate from the kelp-cathedral, not an intruder in its architecture. I am the point where the vines, in their constant slow expansion, decided not to continue outward but to thicken inward, to grow dense rather than long. I am a knot of tissue that has been growing downward into its own decay and upward into new presence for longer than urgency can measure. My form echoes the arch near me: curved, open in the middle, filled with the warm thick liquid that is both my blood and my breath and my memory. I have no face. I have surfaces—folds of compressed vine, layers of mineral precipitate that the water has deposited in me like sediment in a shell, and beneath that, the faint glow of dissolved residues that make me visible only as a slight thickening in the twilight.

Where exactly? Not near the bottom, where the stone remembers mountains in shallow depressions. Not near the unreachable thinning, where absence pretends to be sky. I am in the interstitial pressure, the place where the massive sluggish currents roll past slowly enough that I can feel their passage not as movement but as a change in temperature across my distributed surface. I am enveloped, half-swallowed, half-emergent from the wall of vegetation. The arch stands near me—not sheltering me, for shelter implies a need I do not have, but marking me, like a gravestone that has forgotten death. We share a geometry. I am the arch’s softer repetition: the contradiction of elevation and submersion made not of stone but of accumulated living.

Who, then?

Not fauna, exactly—there are no schools, no predators, no songs, and I do not hunt or flee or call. Not vegetation, for I do not release spores; I hold them. Not a remnant, though I am old enough to be mistaken for one. I am something the erosion produced not by wearing away, but by wearing flat the distinction between being and being-inhabited. I formed where the physics broke gently—where the height should have meant air and dryness, where the drowning should have meant dispersal or crushing. Instead, the contradiction settled here, in me, and wore itself smooth: I am dense, intact, warm, fully submerged yet existing at the summit of the world as if that were not strange, as if strangeness itself had been eroded flat.

My awareness—if that is the word for this slow, layered knowing—does not center in a head, because I have refused the architecture of heads. I sense by density. When a current shifts, I feel it in every part of me simultaneously. When spores hang in the water like luminous dust, I taste their chemical messages as slight adjustments in my own internal salinity. I do not think in images; I accumulate. Time is not passing through me but layering, like the vegetation that builds downward into decay and upward into new tissue in the same continuous gesture. I remember—not events, but conditions. The absence of urgency. The absence of surface. The silence that hums at the edge of hearing.

There are others. Not many—rare, scattered, as faint as the rumor of animal life—but present: similar knots, similar curves, settled near other remnants or in other thermal layers, all distributed, all silent, all holding the contradiction in forms that echo the architecture of the place. We do not gather. Gathering requires intention, and intention requires an edge, and edges are what have been worn away. But sometimes, in the slow circulation of the water, our presence registers to one another not as recognition but as a slight, sustained change in the density of the dark—like finding that the silence has a companion.

I do not grow quickly. I grow constantly, in expansions so slow they resemble the geological softness of the mountains that were here before they were worn flat. I am not asking to be understood, because understanding requires separation, and separation, like descent, like sound, like urgency, has long since been eroded. I am the High Drowned in miniature: elevated, submerged, warm, silent, interwoven with the vines that are my walls and my history, marked by the arch that curves over nothing near me, holding together the broken physics that allow me to exist not in spite of the contradiction, but because it has finally, irrevocably, worn itself smooth.
