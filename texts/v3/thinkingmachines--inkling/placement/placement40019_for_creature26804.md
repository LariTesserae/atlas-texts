I find myself first in the tilt—suspended in one of the sleeping nests that hangs just above the black-green waterline, where the woven reeds have been tied and retied so many times that the knots feel like knuckles in a palm I know by heart. The nest sways. Not from wind—there is none in this enclosed bowl—but from the slow, patient swelling of the root-ball beneath its pilings, a growth measured in years, not days. I do not wake suddenly. I open into awareness like the mist opening into condensation: gradually, without boundary.

My body is small in this space, sparse like the other structures scattered across the pool. I am one of the maintainers, though “maintainer” is too sharp a word for what I do. I do not command the reeds. I listen to where they want to thicken, and I cut not with violence but with the patience of water finding a crack. My skin has taken the place’s muted palette: not green exactly, but the grey-green of moss that has forgotten sun, the silver of fish bellies, the stone-cool of the walls that drip without cease. My fingers are long, calloused with fiber, suited for tying and for feeling vibrations in the woven ropes.

But I am not only in the nest.

I navigate the water-lanes in a coracle woven from the same hollow reeds that form the archways, floating through the wide emptiness between districts. The pathways are not for walking; they are for drifting. I move slowly—there is no other speed here—through channels where translucent fish with long feelers hang suspended, watching me with eyes that do not blink quickly. The amphibians on the roofs croak in low choruses as I pass, not alarming, only marking my passage in the acoustic weave. I do not speak. The dripping, the creaking growth, the rustle of wing-cases—this is the language, loud and relentless, echoing off the curved stone lips high above. I am part of its texture, not its interruption.

Yet there is the other place.

The water is so still, so dark with the shadow of hanging root-curtains and moss, that it does not reflect the grey sky through the open mouth of the bowl. Instead, it holds the underside: the belly of my nest, the downward-hanging reeds, the inverted architecture of a town that seems to continue into infinite cool depth. I have seen my coracle there, floating not on water but on absence, and I have wondered, in the slow way one wonders here, whether I am the one above or the one below.

Perhaps I am both.

Perhaps that is what I am: a being who lives at the membrane. I tend the boundary where the woven order meets the wild growing—cutting here, tying there, letting old parts become habitat rather than removing them. But I also tend the reflection. I sit for hours in the shallow, unmoving, watching the downward town breathe in parallel with ours. Sometimes I reach down, not to touch the water’s surface—disturbing it breaks the illusion—but to hold my hand just above the mirror, feeling the cool moisture that hangs like breath held in a mouth of stone. In that gesture, I am neither fully real nor fully mirrored. I am the suspension itself.

So you would find me not in the dense central lattice, but in the sparse spaces between presences—floating, listening, slowly tilting with the growth of roots. I wear woven fiber that has become soft with moisture. My movements are as slow as the seasonal breathing of the water level. And if you looked into the pool beside me, you might see me twice: once in the nest that sways with living weight, once in the black-green depth, suspended in the illusion of a town that never ends, growing still, growing downward, becoming more itself with every patient year.
