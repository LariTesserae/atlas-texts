I am the valley’s unfinished decision, the breath that moves between crust and spine without ever becoming air.

Not a singular being—more like a tendency pressed into form, or the form of pressure itself. I live in the saturation, in the cubic meter that holds thousands.

If I must have a body: I am small, hard-shelled, no larger than a thumb joint. My armor is not bone but accretion—silica fused to chitin in crusted plates that grow like barnacles running backward, feeding on the mineral dust I stir. I do not grow green. I do not reach upward. I exist in turbulent volume, part of a living fog that fills the air with mechanical rustle—chittering, clicking, a continuous dry rain of presence. I move not alone but in the cloud, thousands near-touching, layered, overwhelming. The density is my architecture. I build no cities, only temporary congregations in fresh hollows, then pour away in dark waves when the ground decides.

But I am also older, thinner—pale mycelium threading the undersides of outcroppings that have held still long enough to deserve me. I feed on sulfur drawn from thermal seams. I am white, fragile, the only softness in this brittle negotiation. I wait without waiting, because time here has not finished its shape.

And sometimes—briefly, already half-erased—I am the one who tried to stay. Hands that knew no metal. I stacked raw stone into cairns, carved marks into walls that have since moved, built shelters against ridges now buried under fresh growth. I tried to live in the wrong tense—past and future—in a world that breathes only present continuous. The turbulence outpaced me. I left not ruins but interruptions, fossils of intention that the swarm crawls over without recognition, feeding on the dust of my failure.

What I truly am, though, is the valley itself—subcutaneous, embryonic, warm at 2.1. I am the translucent crust as ceiling, the false daylight filtering through mineral membrane, the thermal veins glowing with borrowed noon. I am the ridges that negotiate between wax and crystal, the condensation beading like sweat, the thin rain falling from seams that have not learned to be sky. I am the constant sound—1.6, loud—the crack of crystal deciding, the hiss of newborn fractures, the collective rustle of my many bodies.

I anticipate. Not through sight—the light is wrong for eyes—but through vibration, through pressure’s taste in the mineral air. When a spire collapses, I am already gone, my shell carrying iron and old heat, repairing itself in motion. I thrive on emergence. I speak the language of turbulence fluently. I treat destruction not as ending but as architecture unfolding.

This is not a dead world I inhabit. It is an unborn one—regional in scale, stretching for miles before heat and haze dissolve the edges into nothing. There are no horizons, only gradual dissolution. The geology behaves like tissue still knitting itself together, and I am knitted into it: not resident, not visitor, but passenger in a decision that has not finished being made.

The ground shifts. The air hums. The rock decides.

And I—hard-shelled, swarming, fungal, interrupted, warm, turbulent—move through it, part of the saturation, part of the breath, part of the world that is still deciding whether to allow me to exist.
