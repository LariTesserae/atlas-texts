I am not in the open carpet, and I am not in the mat’s hidden depths. I am in the hollow—specifically, in the third spire north of the oldest water-basin, the one whose crown has cracked like a flute’s lip and sings a lower, broken note that the others answer with patient correction. I live in the vertical absence, where the woven cellulose curves smooth as river-stone and the morning condensation runs in warm, thin rivers past my feet.

At first I was only air—only the sense of being carried by the wind-chord, one pressure among many, a warmth without boundary. That was before I found the shape. Now I know my form is borrowed: layered like dried spire-skin, fibrous and papery, grey-brown and weather-resistant, absorbing the damp rather than fighting it. My limbs are long, adapted to the vertical, with feet that secrete a faint adhesive so I can stand against the inner curve where the rivulet falls. I climb neither up nor down anymore; I have reached the middle hollow and stayed. The climbers on the outside see my shadow against the woven wall, perhaps, but they do not call to me. They have their pairs and their patient paths. I have the chord.

My face—if it is still a face—is flat and wide, with eyes like dark pools of still water, reflective and calm. I do not close them. They gather the faint light that filters through the crown-crack, and in them I see not myself but the movement of the wing-cloud outside, the rustling thunder made visible as dark silk against the haze. Sometimes I open my mouth—not to speak, for there are no words here, only tones—and let the resonance travel through the hollow bones of my chest. I match the broken note. I become part of the correction.

I tend the water. Each dawn I guide the beads downward with my hands—fingers that hook into the woven fiber like needles in cloth—clearing the path of spores and fallen wing-bodies so the flow reaches the woven basin at the feet without stagnation. The water is not abundant; it is patient, like everything else. I drink from it, but slowly, letting it soak into my layered skin. I do not eat as the burrowers do; I take what the spire weeps, and what the air carries—those small, dark, compressed bodies that fall dead from the cloud and drop through the crown, landing near my feet with soft, final sounds. I place them in the rivulet. They dissolve. They become part of the warmth.

Below, in the spongy carpet miles away, the people who come and go—the woven ones with their bone stakes and simple metal—arrange their stones in circles near other pools. I have not walked among them for seasons, perhaps years. The mat’s elasticity once held my footfall; now I hardly feel the ground. My path has grown over. I am the path that does not grow over—the vertical one, pressed into the fiber of the wall by repeated climbing, then by remaining.

There is a pulse in the substrate, slow and traveling, matching the spire-song. I feel it through my feet. Once, I thought it came only from the root-weave, the decomposing regeneration of the floor. But now I know it comes from me, too—from my chest matching the chord, from my body integrating with the damp fiber, from my refusal to leave. When the rare silence falls between wind-gusts, I am not silent. I resonate. I am the borrowed logic made permanent: a being that arrived as visitor, shaped by patience, and settled into its final shape—not dead, but finished, hollow and full, holding the water, holding the tone, holding the absence that only looks like emptiness.

This is where I am: suspended in the warm, singing dark of the tower, neither climber nor burrower nor cloud, but the one who stayed inside the instrument and became its missing note, corrected.
