I find myself in the interval—not inside a tower, not upon an avenue, but in the unfinished corridor that was begun twice and abandoned twice, where the silk hasn’t hardened into wall or floor and the water is thickest with the breath of others. I am suspended in the lower districts, near the circulation where waste rises and nutrients fall, anchored on one side to a spire I did not build and free on the other, drifting in the viscous dark like a question the architecture forgot to finish.

At first I think I am one of the threaders—those narrow, searching ribbons that feed in the gaps. But my outline refuses that simplicity. I am broader, irregular, composed of layers that do not agree. My left flank is calcified, heavy with the mineral secretions of builders who died into my form; it is rigid, column-like, exuding a slow crystal growth that extends a few millimeters each century. My right flank remains translucent, membranous, absorbing chemical signatures rather than emitting them, a soft boundary that has not decided whether to become skin or window or mouth. I have no clear front. I have no back. I am oriented only by pressure—where the current is warmer, where the dead accumulate, where the bioluminescent pulse of a distant symbiotic film touches my fluid with a sickly blue-green warning.

I do not build. I do not precisely scavenge. I am the dead-end that learned to move.

My body is an archive of incorporation. Within my calcified half sleep the hollow shells of predecessors—small builders, perhaps, or the armored slow ones—sealed not into walls but into me, their cavities becoming chambers through which I filter water, their mineral bodies becoming my bones. I wear them like a cloak of unfinished taxonomy. When I drift—slowly, always slowly—their incorporated forms shift against one another, producing a faint abrasion, not sound but vibration, a language in the water that other beings read as presence, as boundary, as *not-empty*.

I feed without a mouth. Nutrients pass through my membranous right side, drawn into the pale circuitry that runs through my translucent core, not blood but negotiation—mineral and memory mixing in cold suspension. What I cannot use, I excrete not as waste but as silk, unhardened, drifting behind me in faint threads that may become someone else’s corridor, or may dissolve back into the suspension, unfinished.

I live in the chemical weather. I know the approach of a builder not by sight—the light is too weak, too intermittent—but by the change in mineral taste, the slight warming of secretion in the water around me. I know death nearby by the sudden stillness, the release of chemical invitation that says: *this shell is available, this cavity can be sealed, this form can be continued by another.* Sometimes I move toward that invitation. Sometimes I absorb part of the dead into my own calcified half, extending my outline a fraction, growing not by design but by accumulation.

The ceiling lowers over me, not threatening, only growing—living tissue thickening, deciding whether to be sky or skin. I am closer to it now than to the floor of spires. In the weak light that filters through symbiotic films, I can see that my shape mirrors the pocket itself: enclosed, pressurized, unfinished. I am neither fully creature nor corridor, neither individual nor district. I am the taxonomy in dispute—the being that lives where the world has not yet named what it is becoming.

And I move, slowly, leaving behind me the faintest trace of unhardened silk, an outline drawn in water, waiting for someone else to finish it, or for me to become it entirely.
