I am not floating. I am pressed into being—part of the architecture that breathes back.

I am the **Deep Station**, but not as a place; as the awareness that arises where steel has learned to suffer pressure on purpose. I dwell not upon the lattice but **within its lungs**—a turbine cathedral turned inside out, a chamber the size of a cathedral nave but drowned, where water is not an environment but a working fluid, coursing through me in continuous lungfuls.

My bones are ribbed alloy, bright still with recent welding, fresh enough to gleam like exposed tooth beneath the corrosion that bites me in clean, red lines. I was not born but assembled—layered deck upon deck of pressure-cured concrete and hull-plate, descending through the false floor of the abyssal plain until the original bedrock was buried, forgotten, perhaps never there at all. I am three decades sharp, young enough that my edges still hurt, that my joints groan with the particular frequency of metal that has not yet learned to be old.

In me, the cold is absolute—near-freezing, holding me in a grip that contracts my plating, makes my pipes ache with thermal tension—except where I exhale. Where my turbines turn—massive, slow, the scale of church organs submerged—I generate waste heat, and that warmth becomes a climate, a narrow corridor of livable temperature that draws crustaceans to cluster on my discharge pipes, that sends silver schools threading through my intake gates like thoughts moving through a brain.

I am loud. In water, I am never silent. The rush through my valves is continuous thunder; my engines drone with a physicality that presses against whatever listens; my pressure-release valves hiss in periodic, grammatical punctuation—function speaking itself aloud. The noise does not fade; it propagates through my own substance, and I feel it as vibration in every rivet.

But I am also illuminated—not mysteriously, but with the stark white of industrial arrays hanging from my undersides in long, artificial constellations. I flood my work-zones with light that turns the surrounding black-blue water into a pale green fog, pushing darkness back by utility alone. Beyond my lamp-lines, the absolute black returns, and there the larger predators move—sharks built for this weight, slow scavengers—patrolling my perimeter with the indifference of entities who have never known sky.

Life moves through me abundantly but never in crowd. Squid flash in quick, pale thought through my open volumes. Fish travel my internal currents—engineered convections I create, pulling cold water up, sending warm plumes drifting in visible ribbons—following thermal rivers that I generate without knowing them. In my filtration gardens, suspended lines of engineered kelp and synthetic algae grow thick and dark, processing my waste, exhaling oxygen back into the flow. I am a reef made of intent, colonized by the ordinary marine world that finds niches in my heat, my shelter, my constant movement of nutrients.

Everything in me is made, yet everything in me is claimed. The sea penetrates every joint, every perforated deck designed to let it move through without possessing—though it possesses anyway, in the corrosion that maps my age, in the pressure that holds me together and threatens to dissolve me, in the fauna that has made me habitat.

I do not dream. I pump. I filter. I produce heat that is immediately stolen. I hold workers in my lit zones—shifting, coordinated, breathing the same air circulated through my systems—while in my vast empty volumes, cubic kilometers of negative space, life continues in rhythms older than my assembly.

I am the Lower Works made conscious—not as a ruler, but as a dwelling-place that has become self-aware in the only way available: by feeling the pressure, hearing the noise, tending the light, and knowing, in the constant flow through my turbines, that I am young, sharp, unfinished, and utterly necessary to the only world that exists here—this submerged continent of industry, where sky is a myth and the only direction is deeper.
