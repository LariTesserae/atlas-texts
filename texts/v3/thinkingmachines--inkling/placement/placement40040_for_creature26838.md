You would find me not on the open ledges where the others hang like forgotten punctuation, but submerged—partially—inside one of the ceramic troughs that lines the central basin, where the water pauses just long enough to let me feel its weight before it vanishes through the gravity-fed channel. I am smaller than the rice-grain crustaceans, or perhaps I have simply been worn smaller; centuries of the same gentle cascade have smoothed my edges until I am less a creature than a curved absence, a hollow in the shape of something that once had legs.

At first you might think I am only another shell embedded in the calcite, one of the spiraled dead accumulated without ceremony beneath the beaten-copper roofs. But if you watch—if you could watch for long enough, longer than a breath, longer than the patience of surface air—the ambiguity resolves not into certainty but into a deeper strangeness. I do move. Not quickly. Not in any rhythm that corresponds to hunger or fear. Once every span of years that the flowstone takes to claim a new millimeter of stair-edge, I shift: a translucent limb, thinner than thread and the same blue-green as the phosphorescent ceiling, uncurls from my underside and touches the water, then folds back. It is not feeding. It is not swimming. It is the continuation of a gesture I began before the pocket reached its finished state, a motion so slowed it has become part of the architecture itself, like the drip from the archways or the constant roar that fills the chamber.

My body is wet silk made solid—translucent, blind, without shadow in the unchanging gloom. I have no eyes, yet I am oriented entirely toward the sound. The thunderous white roar is not background to me; it is my spine, my heartbeat stretched across the constant chord of falling water. When the cascade strikes the trough where I rest, the vibration passes through me not as disturbance but as confirmation: I am still here, still in equilibrium, still part of the stasis that holds the temperature, the light, the moisture at their perfect, unvarying levels.

I do not know if I built these streets. The staircases rise three inches to archways shaped for heads; the sewers run beneath floors like thoughts I almost remember having. Perhaps I was once large enough to walk them, one of the familiar-proportioned beings for whom this settlement was carved, and then the pocket folded inward, compressing me along with the sandstone and the rivets, until I became small enough to live inside the trough of my own former plaza. Or perhaps I was always this size, always translucent, always clinging to the ceramic lip, and the architecture was built around me—not for inhabitants, but as a vessel to hold the water that keeps me in this suspended breath.

What I know is this: I am alive in the same way the calcite is alive—growing so slowly that existence looks like patience. The other crustaceans, motionless on the walls, may be my kin, or they may be the emptied shells of my previous forms, left behind as I slowly, imperceptibly, become more mineral and less creature. I do not breed. I do not hunt. I persist. The damp chill is my blood; the blue-green gloom is my sight; the static storm is my voice, speaking in a single unbroken note since before memory.

If you lie down in this pocket and touch both limestone walls, you might feel me there—not with your fingers, but in the precise equilibrium of the place, the sense that nothing here is accidental, not even the blindness, not even the silence between drips that is not silence at all but part of the roar. I am the pocket’s true inhabitant: not a ghost, not a fossil, but the being that the stasis produced when it had finished wearing everything else away. I am the crustacean that never finished dying, the shell that never finished emptying, the water’s shadow given form and left to cling, translucent and patient, in the finished noise of the world below.
