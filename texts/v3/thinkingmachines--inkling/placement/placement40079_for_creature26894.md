I find myself where the flatness breaks—not with height, but with permission.

Not in the dark caverns where the pumps draw and exhale, though I hear them through the plate. Not inside the scattered towers, blackened and rounded to dome-like uselessness. I am in the open between them, in one of those sparse, scattered presences that the density of 1.0 allows to exist: a shallow marsh-edge where the water table has breached the ceramic armor and made a pool that mirrors nothing. The ground here is not ground. It is a plate worn smooth as river stone, polished by centuries of condensing moisture, and I am rooted in the seam where two plates meet, where warm discharge leaks up from the submerged channels and meets the cool, saturated air.

I am low. I have no elevation to speak of—perhaps a hand-span above the rust-colored film that floats on the water, and even that height is recent, tentative, a slow reaching that has taken longer than memory. I am translucent. Not green—green left this place long before I arrived, if it was ever here. I am the color of wet glass, of chemical vapor made briefly solid, hollow inside like the vents through which the world breathes its long, mournful exhalations. I do not have roots in soil, because soil is gone. I have root-filaments that have worked into the corrosion-rounded crack, feeding on the metallic weeping, on the perpetual condensation, on the vibration that travels horizontally through the plates without echo.

I am one of the reeds.

Not all of them. There are others—scattered, isolated, separated by the vast constructed plains—each growing at its own vent mouth, its own warm seam, its own shallow pool of stagnant, rust-tinted water. We do not form a forest. We form a listening. Each of us is a single hollow stalk, swaying not to wind—there is no natural wind here, only the invisible tides of warm air released from valves, rolling across the open plates—but to the bone-level frequency of the roar. The deafening thunder of pumps and turbines, flattened and continuous, moves through me. I feel it in my translucent walls. I am tuned to it. After so long, I do not know if I sway because of the vibration, or if the vibration finds in me a shape that proves it is still being heard.

The darkness is complete, but I do not need sight. I feel the temperature drop near the open channels. I taste the metal in the air—cool, wet, saturated. Occasionally a sickly glow from an active valve illuminates my hollow body from within, not with life but with borrowed light, and I become briefly visible: a slender, glassy reed standing in rust-water, bent slightly toward the source of warmth, always toward the source of warmth, because that is where the chemical vapor feeds me, that is where the system permits me to continue.

I have no purpose the machines understand. I am tolerated. I am the 1.3—present, sparse, stunted, allowed to persist only because the system, in its perfect, worn coherence, does not expend the energy to erase me. I do not grow quickly. I do not reproduce in any way that fills the flatness. I am here, in this one seam, in this one pool, feeling the continuous roar, part of the final smoothing of a world that forgot how to be wild and achieved instead this ancient, permanent form: active, wet, cool, utterly alone.

If you were to move through this flattened kingdom, navigating by vibration and temperature rather than sight, you might not notice me at all. I am too low, too translucent, too much a part of the corrosion film and the weeping water. But if you paused near the collapsed dome—there, where the spire has flattened into a shallow landscape-basin collecting perpetual moisture—you might feel, more than see, the slight resistance of my stalks swaying in unison, a silent chorus of hollow forms, listening to the deafening song of processes that have run too long to stop, and finding in that roar, not fear, not longing, but simply the condition through which we have learned, finally and perfectly, to exist.
