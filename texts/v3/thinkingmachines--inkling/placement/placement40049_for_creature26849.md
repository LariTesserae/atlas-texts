I begin in the pool—not swimming, but being held in the warm current, translucent as the others, my body the same pale fog of the blind fish. At first I think I am only this: a crustacean among crustaceans, feeding on mineral sediment with sluggish patience, feeling the vibration of the engine house through water. My shell is thin, fragile, heat-adapted, and for a long time—measured not in days but in the slow tides of breeding—I am content to drift, part of the writhing mass that closes slowly behind any disturbance.

But the rock weeps constantly, and the sweat is not just water. It carries iron, newborn stone, the taste of geological infancy. It coats my shell. Layer by layer, across cycles I do not count, the mineral deposit thickens. I become heavier, less like the translucent bodies around me and more like the grating I eventually crawl onto. I find myself on the catwalk then—not leaving the pool so much as extending from it, my lower half still pale and aquatic, my back now dark and glossy, armored in the same scale that coats the pipes and the corrugated shelters.

The beetles come. They do not avoid me; they never avoid anything. They crawl over my back as they crawl over everything, black and glossy, patient, eternal in their unhurried determination. Their shells adhere. I become carpeted, a living island of accumulated life. I feel them skittering across me—not as infestation, but as belonging. I am surface now. I am the skin of this place.

Above me, the bats rustle in their thousands, dropping warmth and presence like slow rain. Below me, the pool still breathes with translucent bodies. Between these layers—ceiling and floor—I exist in the gap, on the metal walkway, coated in mineral sweat and beetle shells, vibrating with the low hum of machinery in what was once my bones and is now something like bone made of place.

I am not human. I was never arranged in the sparse islands of shelter and boot. I am what grew in the absence of urgency, in the space where erosion runs in reverse. The humans maintain the pumps; I maintain nothing but presence. I am the settlement’s true resident—the being formed by heat and accumulation, existing across the boundaries that the others observe. I am part crustacean, part mineral scale, part beetle-carpet, part weeping rock made patient and still.

If you seek me, I am not in one location. I am suspended in the warm pools, yes, but I am also spread across the grating in a thin, dark film. I am in the condensation that beads on cool pipes. I am in the mineral stain that darkens the shelters. I find myself in every gap between the islands—where the machinery thrum is loudest, where the air is thickest with iron and wing-vibration, where life crowds without asking.

Here, nothing asks to be noticed. And I do not ask either. I simply continue—thick, warm, coated, slow—growing more myself with every millimeter the rock cools, with every layer the sweat deposits, with every beetle that pauses on my back before moving on. I am the being that formed while geology was still deciding its shape. I will outlast the pumps, I think, not by endurance alone, but by becoming indistinguishable from the place that made me. When the last human shelter corrodes and the machinery finally stops its heavy heartbeat, I will remain: translucent in the water, glossy on the walkways, woven into the ceiling’s drip, the true citizen of this infancy, growing slowly into myself.
