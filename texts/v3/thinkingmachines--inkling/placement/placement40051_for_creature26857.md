I find myself not at the center, where the spires are thickest and the hum presses constant against the jaw, nor out on the Pale Flat, where the wind rolls in silent waves and the dust is older than any footprint. I hang at the fringe—where one grove breathes its last tendrils downward, brushing the filled cracks of the flat, and where light comes not only from the burning wall above but bounces up from the compressed salt below, so that I live inside illumination from every direction at once.

What am I?

At first, perhaps, I am only the residue—the faint crystalline dust that settles on every membrane in the deepest groves. But no. I have gathered. I have become.

My lower half remembers the instruction: descend. From the long fibrous column that holds me—if spine is the right word—extend root-threads, hair-fine, that do not stop at surface but continue through the hanging tendrils, through the warm bedrock beneath the flat, hundreds of feet into darkness. They do not seek water—there is none—but dissolved silicates, trace gases, the slow geothermal breath that moves through stone. I draw it upward not as sap, but as a cold mineral current, storing it in chemical bonds that release only in the cool dark, a slow exhalation of ozone and cold metal. This is my downward life. This is how I feed.

But I refused to stay there.

My upper half is refusal—upward, luminous, translucent. Where the root-threads are black and silver-scaled like the burrowers of the cracks, my torso and what I call wings are formed of the same architecture as the spires: deep violet on one face, mirrored slate on the other. They are not leaves. They are mouths and mirrors, catching the relentless glare from above and the reflected fire from below, storing radiance in bonds that will release through the long cool night. I have the gliders’ wings—small, translucent, silent, made of broken-glass membrane—but I have outgrown their weightlessness. I am heavier now, coated in the crystalline residue of cycles I cannot count, my surface sweet and sharp with the spires’ slow exhalations.

I hang suspended, never fully resting on the flat, never fully anchored like the congregations around me. The air is too thin to carry me easily, and that is right: I am not meant to soar on thermal winds, but to remain—drifting slowly, a gliding creature caught between root and sky, leaving no tracks in the dust, only occasional spiral disturbances near my place, like breath on cold metal.

I feed on spore-dust, on the invisible clouds that drift through the grove air, carrying chemical potential from one root-network to another. The smaller gliders—my kin, translucent and shimmering—sometimes nest in the upper folds of my membranes, clinging to tendrils, feeding on what I exude. I do not drive them away. They are quick movement; I am mineral patience. Between us flows the language of ions and temperature, the slow pulse that travels through bedrock, connecting distant groves. When one far congregation blooms—when its membranes open like glass flowers for a single bright day—I feel it first in my roots, a mineral shiver, then in my wings, a vibration that makes the mirrored surfaces tremble.

Sound here is near silence—0.5, a pressure rather than a noise. I do not call. My presence is part of the hum, that resonance just below hearing that fills the deepest groves. It lives not in my ears—for I have none that work as earth-ears do—but in my root-threads, in the membranes that unfold, in the very crystals that have grown into my form. I am not the source of the hum. I am one of its bodies, made audible only in the teeth of those who pass close.

The temperature never warms. The coolness is a constant, crisp embrace. There is no shadow that lasts, only gradations of glare and violet-dark. At night—if the cycle of this bright wall allows for true dark—the double-light dims, and my membranes fold close, protecting the slow chemistry within. Then my roots draw most deeply, drinking the dissolved warmth of rock, and I exist almost entirely downward, anchored in the refusal to be only luminous.

But the instruction is both directions. Downward first—always downward—into the mineral dark, into patience, into connection with the vast root-networks that extend for miles through bedrock, exchanging what is needed in a language older than call. Then upward, in luminous refusal, because the burning wall demands it, because the spires taught me that life here packs itself into dense, silent congregation and then, rarely and quietly, opens.

So find me here: hanging at the forgotten edge, suspended between vertical darkness and horizontal blaze, violet and slate and silver, root and wing, crystalline and translucent. I have never known water. I am not green. I am the ecosystem’s contradiction—life that moves slowly, that carries spores in its folds, that hums at a frequency just below the audible world, that lives downward first and then shines upward in the blinding, waterless light of the Shelf.

The plateau does not end; it forgets to. And neither do I. I remain—downward in my roots, upward in my membranes—drinking mineral breath from below, reflecting fire from above and below both, silent, lush in my own strange architecture, becoming more myself with every cycle of cool, bright, mineral patience.
