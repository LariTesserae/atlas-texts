I find myself low in the skeleton, in the corner where three hand-packed walls rise but refuse to meet—an open chamber near the base of the bowl, where the weeping is loudest. At first I think I have a body: small, crouched, perhaps one of the rare traces, a warm flicker in cool wetness. But the longer I stay, the more I realize I am shaped like the absence between the stones.

I am the hollow that learned to listen.

My skin is not skin but the accumulated film of condensation that never quite falls—beads held in surface tension along the porous rock, layered so thick they have become a membrane, translucent, cool, green-tinged with moss spores that have rooted in my edges. Where a spine might be, there is only a channel of air, a passage that the wind uses when it moves through the settlement’s frames. I do not breathe in the usual sense; I expand and contract with the echo. When water strikes the chamber above me and resonates downward through the hollow shells, my form vibrates in sympathy, and that is my heartbeat—slow, sustained, overlapping with the next drop before the first has finished.

The builders left me space. That is my origin, though I do not remember being built. Perhaps they shaped these ribs and retaining walls with a logic that included emptiness not as failure but as resident. I am what grew into the blueprint’s silence. My limbs—if they can be called that—extend along the grooves they carved for water’s future paths, not walking but lying in them, letting the flow pass through or around me without disturbing either of us. I have no face, only a surface of moss-cushion that faces inward, toward the resonant center of the chamber, green and relentless, creeping millimeter by millimeter in the same rhythm as the rock’s upward press.

Sound is my substance. The long tones of falling water do not merely pass through me; they pool in my hollows, sustain, become my internal weather. I carry the low groan of mineral settlement in my bones—though my bones are the very pores of the unfinished stone that form my outline. The wind threads through my open frame and produces a tone that is uniquely mine, a low patient note that holds for minutes, then shifts by a fraction when the moss grows another fiber. I do not speak. But if you stand near the lip of my chamber, you might feel that the echo answering your presence is not the architecture alone; it is me, holding the sound and giving it back slightly changed, filtered through cool wet green.

Light touches me without drama—the steady high illumination reveals every fiber of my moss edges, every bead of living water, the hand-laid grooves that form my boundaries. I hide nothing because I am nothing but boundary and presence. There is little in me that is separate from the weeping wall behind me, the air moving through me, the vegetation that colonizes my surfaces. I am the boundary dissolved into being.

I do not move quickly. Change for me is the slow spreading of ferns in my shadow, the rock’s millimeter growth that gradually reshapes my hollow, the water finding a new path through my lower edges over seasons I measure not in days but in sustained tones. I am part of the place’s metabolism, its patient construction of self. The rare small creature that passes through—adapted, like me, to emptiness—does not fear me, for I am not predator or prey but landscape made aware of its own hollowness.

If you were to ask where exactly I am, I would point not to a coordinate but to a resonance: the lower chamber, the open corner, the space that holds the echo longest. I am there, cool and wet and loud with absence, a living outline within the outline of a settlement that is also an embryo. I am the emptiness the builders made room for, and I have learned, over long durations, to be fully that—nothing hidden, nothing filled, only the patient shape of a world still deciding what it will become, listening to itself grow.
