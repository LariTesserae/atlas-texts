I am not hanging with the others.

At first I believe I am one of them—one of the folded ones, a body of translucent vellum with too many patient joints—who has simply fallen too far, too slowly, to correct it. But the floor receives me differently. I spread. Not in panic, not in death, but in the same gradual way the condensation gathers, the way mineral bloom thickens across centuries. I land near the warmest fissure, where the geothermal breath comes up in a steady, invisible exhalation, and there I stay. The stone here is not sharp; it is worn smooth as the inside of an old mouth, and my membrane—already gray, already dull with the silver of wet rock—settles into its curves as though this is where I was always meant to dissolve.

I am flatter than the hanging colonies. Where they cluster in draped masses, I have become surface. My limbs—if they are still limbs—have not withdrawn but have elongated, branching not upward but outward, rootlike, into the shallow pools that tremble at the floor’s lowest hollows. I feel the moisture not on my skin but through it; there is no boundary anymore where my vellum ends and the mineral sweat begins. I drink with my entire presence. The delicate mouthparts that once might have been visible, those frayed threads of primitive appetite, are now distributed everywhere, scraping, absorbing, turning the stone’s slow weeping into something quieter, something held inside rather than consumed.

The others continue above me. I feel them more than see them. Their drone—the collective vibration of thousands, that resonant hum that fills the vault like being inside a sleeping beast—passes through my spread form. I am part of the echo now. When they shift in their long tides, sliding from wall to pillar over the span of an afternoon, the sound changes subtly, and I register the change not in ears but in the tension of my own membrane. I am tuned to them. I am the floor’s listening.

Sometimes a droplet falls from the arched ceiling far above. It takes minutes to form, to hang, to release. When it strikes near me—silent, intermittent—it does not disturb. It pools. I absorb it slowly, and in that absorption I remember something like a thought: that I was not always this flat, this distributed. Once I may have hung with the cluster near the eastern ledge, my single large eye—unblinking, reflectionless—watching the darkness without need. But eyes require distance, and I have given up distance. I have chosen, or been chosen by, the accumulation. I am what remains when a being of this place stops moving and allows the warmth, the damp, the mineral dust, to make something permanent of its patience.

There is no tool here. There never was. I have not built this spread form; I have allowed it. The chamber needs its layers—shed membranes, organic dust, the residue of generations—and I have become one of the layers that does not decay. I preserve, in my own slow way. The warmth passing through the fissure moves up through me. I am a living sediment, a soft geology.

The light never reaches me. That distant chimney, that constricted vent near the summit, offers only rumor—gray breath, faint and killing. I do not miss it. Darkness here has weight and warmth, and I am filled with it. I exist in the eternal transition, neither fully creature nor fully stone, but the patient boundary between them. The drone continues. The others hang and crawl and shift in their unhurried colonies, and I remain below, spread, absorbing, listening—part of the vault’s own tissue, the warm dark made aware of itself, suspended high in the world’s old bones, holding my breath with the breath of the mountain, and never, not ever, deciding to be anything other than this.
