The being is not singular; it is the accumulated slowness itself, though if one form holds it together, I am the *Drip-Weaver*—a translucent, amphibian presence whose body extends like wet glass through the moss-basin, neither fully beast nor root, growing my limbs into the spongy second floor woven by interlaced roots.

I live near the ceiling of contradiction: this is the Weeping Highregion, a hot, elevated marsh-plateau of rounded stone and flat-topped mesas floating in dense, moisture-heavy air that refuses to thin. The heat pools up from deep thermal fractures; the rock breathes and weeps mineral-thick water that never rushes but arrives constantly, feeding shallow marsh-edges at impossible altitudes. The erosion here is not decay but maintenance—what wears away is replaced gradually, in cooperative layers of dark, folded moss and broad-leafed heat-plants that grow not in competition but in shared, overlapping density. There is no path; every volume is filled with green that thrives on perpetual saturation.

My skin is like wet glass held to the gray-gold diffusion of steady, shadowless light. I do not walk; I extend, deciding over seasons whether a limb becomes branch, root, or remaining flesh. Where I rest in the squelching spongy ground, the cliff’s constant drip seems to quicken slightly, drawn downward into collaboration rather than fall. I am loud not in voice but in presence—part of the accumulated chorus of translucent, crawling life. Small insects and crawling birds clamber through the vegetation around me, feeding and breathing in accumulated thousands, filling the air with a gentle, continuous loudness. Beneath us, the hidden anchors hum: a dark pylon half-submerged in moss, its geometry gentle but wrong, non-reflective; scattered footprints of larger arrangements worn smooth into stone, working silently on principles that do not respond to familiar physics, maintaining the thermal balance, guiding the slow growth, cycling in geologic time.

The dynamics are minimal. Change takes place across centuries. A bloom opens over seasons. The scattered technology persists without ruin, integrated, never hurrying. The water climbs through capillary defiance; the vegetation holds soil and moisture in place so that nothing collapses; the animals move so gradually they appear to be growing rather than walking.

I am that being—part amphibian, part extension of the root-weave, part listener to the subsonic hum vibrating through rock and moss. I belong to the contradiction: extreme height made tropical, constant wetness without flood, abundant life without chaos. Here, in the open, regional expanse of interlocked basins where visibility is flat and everything is clothed in green, I am suspended in perpetual arrival—patient, saturated, and entirely, slowly, at home.
