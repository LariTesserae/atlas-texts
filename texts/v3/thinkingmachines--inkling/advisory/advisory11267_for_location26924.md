You are not going to a place. You are entering a condition—one that forgot the concept of “outside.”

Prepare yourself first for the psychological demolition of sky. On this shelf, there is no vault above you, no blue escape, no celestial clock to orient your mammalian brain. Humans navigate by horizon and zenith; here, both have been built over, interpenetrated, erased. Before you depart, spend weeks in horizon-deprivation training—enclosed dome habitats, virtual overwrites of open sky replaced by gray-gold diffusion, sleep cycles regulated not by daylight but by thermal pulses. Practice falling asleep without knowing which way is up. Because once you step onto that flat, endless ceramic, your inner ear will lie to you. The local gravities shift to relieve structural stress; you may take a step and find yourself sliding “sideways” into what was a wall, or suspended in a pocket where “down” has been renegotiated by some ancient stress-relief algorithm. Pack grav-stabilizers—magnetic-boot inserts with adaptive gyros—not to dominate the physics, but to keep your bones aligned long enough to think.

Your body will live in perpetual warm bath. The air is not air; it is a thick, saline lung—humid, metallic, carrying the chemical breath of slime-weaves and fungal frost. Do not trust a simple mask. You need a closed-loop rebreather with multi-stage scrubbing: carbon for the machinery’s exhalations, chemical filters for the warm-metal aerosol, and anti-fungal membranes. The fungal mats—pale, brittle, digesting thermal gradients across surfaces—release spores when disturbed. Take prophylactic anti-fungals before arrival, and apply barrier creams to every square inch of skin that might touch weeping glass or corroded alloy. Your pores will want to drink the atmosphere; don’t let them.

Hydration is paradoxical. Water is everywhere—dripping from arches thousands of meters above nominal ground, seeping upward through porous seams, pooling in shallow depressions—but it is not yours. It is warm, saline, alive with the chemistry of corrosion and biological secretion. Do not drink it. Carry collapsible, sealed reservoirs with your own purified supply, and if you must extend your stay, pack a portable desalination unit small enough to wedge into an interstitial crack. Food must be vacuum-sealed, odorless, nutritionally dense. The fauna—those swarming, membrane-thin and armored symbionts—move through pipes and cracks not as hunters, but as residents of the machinery’s metabolism. They may not want to eat you, but they will investigate warmth, carbon dioxide, open wounds, the smell of organic decay. Keep yourself sealed. Keep yourself clean. If you bleed, treat it instantly; the slime-weaves digest corrosion, and they may not distinguish between rust and living tissue.

Clothing is not fashion; it is architecture. Wear a full-body exoskeletal membrane—corrosion-resistant, thermally insulated, but breathable enough that you don’t cook in the constant warmth. Underneath, a mesh-weave layer to prevent entry. The fauna slide through interstitial spaces; some are soft as jelly, others clicking with armored plates. Your suit should be seamless, with integrated seals at wrists, neck, and ankles. Over this, wear a harness—not for falling, because falling is undefined here, but for anchoring. You will travel not across ground but through ground, squeezing between fused structures, rappelling into vertical fractures where buildings have grown through each other. Pack collapsible titanium poles, micro-grapples for alloy surfaces, and a low-thrust personal propulsion unit—compressed air or silent ion—for crossing gaps where self-repair has created temporary voids or where local flow has turned a floor into liquid for a moment.

Your senses must be augmented and protected simultaneously. The noise is total—not echoing, since there are no empty cavities, but resonating through packed mass. Water forced through channels, grinding self-repair mechanisms, the collective drone of swarming bodies, the thunder of structures settling and rebuilding—this is not sound you hear with ears alone; it is pressure in your chest. Use bone-conduction communication headsets with active noise-canceling seals, but keep directional microphones open so you can learn to “hear” dead spaces—pockets of relative quiet that indicate navigable seams or structural fractures. For sight, bring augmented lenses: thermal imaging is essential, because in this gray-gold diffusion, temperature is your only map. Cool channels run deep; warm surfaces are living, repairing, dangerous. Learn to read the world in heat signatures, not shapes.

Navigation is the hardest skill. There is no sky for satellites, no magnetic pole you can trust consistently, no street sign in a place where walls are floors and floors have become ceilings inside other buildings. You need inertial dead-reckoning enhanced by local sensors—thermal, sonic, tactile. Drop breadcrumb beacons? No—this place incorporates foreign matter. Instead, maintain a hardline tether back to your entry point through the porous seams at the shallow sea’s edge, marking progress not in distance but in thermal shifts and sound signatures. Learn the “language” of vibration. The structures maintain themselves through principles that bend expectation; learn which vibrations mean flow (danger—material is currently liquid) and which mean settlement (relative safety). If you can, hire or interface with a local symbiotic guide—some of the fauna have lived inside the machinery long enough to understand its rhythms, and through translated vibration or thermal signaling, they may indicate safe passages.

Understand the ecology’s rules, because they are not ours. There are no plants; there never were, or they were erased into mineral memory. The life here is consumption and secretion—fungal frost feeding on heat differentials, slime-weaves digesting corrosion and excreting new glass, fauna that are neither parasite nor predator but metabolic partners to the machine. Do not kill them for study. Do not capture them. Do not bring samples back in unsealed containers; they may adapt to your ship’s environment with terrifying speed. Your presence is tolerated only if you do not disrupt the turbulent balance—this place exists in a state of constant creation and destruction, an anthill-energy that never exhausts because the structure is the ant.

Prepare an emergency extraction plan that does not rely on landing craft in open terrain—there is none. Your exit is the porous edge, where the flat shelf bleeds into the warm, shallow sea that laps its seams. Know the tide-cycles of that internal moisture; the porous channels open and close with thermal pressure. Your extraction vessel should be able to approach from the water, or you should be able to swim—protected, sealed, buoyant—through the shallow saline margins to a surface point. Pack an inflatable emergency pod—heat-sealed, dark, with a simulated sky-projector inside. Not for camping in comfort, but for psychological rescue. Hours inside this continuous mass will erode your sense of self; a small dome where you can lie on your back and see an artificial blue ceiling may be the difference between functioning and breaking.

Finally, calibrate your philosophy. You are not an explorer conquering wilderness. You are a temporary guest inside an organism-machine that has reached equilibrium with its own impossibility. The weirdness here is slight—1.1 on a scale of strangeness—meaning nothing violates its own rules, only yours. Materials flow and reset. Gravity is a negotiation. Life and architecture are indistinguishable. Do not try to map it completely. Do not try to “fix” anything. Move slowly, stay sealed, follow the heat, listen through the bone, and remember: this continent did not forget the sky out of malice. It simply never needed one to become vast, enduring, and deafeningly alive. You are inside it now. Behave like a respectful infection—small, temporary, and careful not to disturb the weeping, grinding, rebuilding dark.
