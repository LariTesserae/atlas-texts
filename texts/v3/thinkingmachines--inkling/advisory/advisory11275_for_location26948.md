Prepare as if you are not entering a place, but being *swallowed by one*—slowly, politely, with your own chemistry kept strictly apart from its.

**First, wait outside the boundary and listen.** Do not push through the dark masonry or the wall of growth immediately. The boundary is unclear because the growth *is* the boundary; there is no gate. Spend at least one full rotation of the central machine—perhaps an hour, perhaps a day—just outside in the stagnant air pocket, feeling how the temperature does not change and learning the rhythm of the deep groan. You need to hear the wet thunderous rush that follows the grinding turn, and then the long, patient drone that settles in after. Time your entry for the stillness that follows the rush, never during the movement itself. The root-mat transmits vibration; when that pump turns, the floor yields differently, and hidden pipes may shift. Learn the hum in your bones before you let your feet touch the mat.

**Dress for immersion, not for weather.** The air is permanently post-rain and warm—there is no chill, no sun to burn you, only a gentle, unvarying heat that feels like being inside a mouth. Wear a single layer of tight-weave synthetic, quick-dry and sealed at wrist and ankle, but not insulated. You want something that will not absorb the constant condensation dripping from every vine and membrane, something that will not rot on you in the humid green. A light, waxed canvas outer shell—breathable but water-resistant—will keep the droplets from soaking through without trapping your own heat. Your boots should be the strangest part of your kit: not heavy, but *broad*. The ground is not ground. It is a spongy, interwoven root-mat several feet thick, floating or resting in shallow warm water, yielding like a living tongue. Heavy boots will punch through; you need flat, flexible soles with wide, snowshoe-like frames—perhaps collapsible flotation plates strapped over reinforced shoes—so your weight spreads across the root-carpet instead of sinking into hidden pools. The soles must also be tough; rusted iron spines run hidden through vine-trunks, and ceramic pipe-mouths have been widened by slow pressure into sharp, flowering orifices.

Cover your hands in thick, grippy rubber or treated leather gloves, not for cold, but for pushing. You will not walk here; you will part curtains of warm, thick vine, shoulder through hanging biomass, and brace against bulbous, leafless bodies that cluster in corners like organs. Do not wear loose sleeves that can be grabbed—though nothing here has intent, the vines hang in ropes and may wrap by simple physics if you struggle against them.

**Protect your lungs and eyes with gentle, constant care.** The air is not toxic in any dramatic sense, but it is heavy—suspended moisture in visible layers, carrying spores from the bulbous bodies, the mineral breath of rust, and the faint metallic haze of water that has passed through the machine’s heart too many times. Wear a low-profile respirator with organic-vapor and particulate filters; not a sealed gas mask (you need to smell the place, to know if the air has changed), but something that keeps the spore-load and the metallic mist out of your throat over hours of slow wading. For your eyes: the light is subdued, aquatic green—enough to see, but tinted as if through shallow water. Do not wear dark glasses; instead, wear clear goggles with side shields, because droplets fall in rhythmic percussion and vines brush the face constantly. If you must bring artificial light, make it amber or warm red—a soft lantern, not a beam. Harsh white or blue light will feel like an insult to the geometry of the place, and may startle nothing (there is nothing to startle) but will destroy your own ability to see the true colors hidden in the green filter.

**Hear selectively.** The sound is loud—constant, loud, patient. The grinding turn, the wet rush, the drone of clogged ancient lines. After an hour inside, your ears will ring with sympathy. Use filtered earplugs—active noise-dampening that lowers the volume without erasing direction—so you can still hear the shift in the hum that warns of an impending turn. You must hear the root-creak that accompanies the machinery; it is the only warning you will get.

**Carry no cutting tool.** This is essential. The vegetation is not present; it is the substrate. To cut is to wound an organism that has already incorporated every ruin into its flesh. The pipes run through hollow stems like bones; the rust has become mineral skin shared between metal and root. If you need to move through, use a slender staff—a carbon-fiber pole, light and strong—to gently part the hanging curtains. Wear gloves and push with your shoulder. The mat will yield; step onto it knowing it is alive.

**Bring your own water, sealed and cold.** The pools that reflect the green-filtered light are part of the cycle: drawn up by the slow pump, condensed in the air, dripping back. They carry a faint metallic taste, not unpleasant, but they have passed through the machine’s heart and through roots that never sleep. They are not for you. Carry at least twice what you think you need, in flexible bladders that can be compressed when not in use.

**Navigate as if you are underwater in a cathedral.** The space is only forty paces across—intimate, complete—but density makes it feel infinite. There are no paths. You will become disoriented in the curtains of stem and the green-filtered shadow. Before entering, tie a thin, brightly colored line—not to the plants, which you must not damage, but to the rusted iron rail that serves as a vine’s spine near the entry point, or to the ceramic pipe whose mouth you can feel without harming it. Let the line trail behind you, floating on the root-mat, a tactile guide back to the boundary. Do not rely on a compass; the submerged metal of the pump, half-buried in the root-floor like a metal island in a green sea, may pull a needle into nonsense. Use the line. Use the sound: the pump is the center. In a space this small, you can always orient yourself by the direction of the deep groan.

**Study the alignment before you move.** The plants do not grow randomly. They run parallel to pipes; they coil in the same direction the gears turn; bulbs cluster where pressure accumulates. The place has rules, though they are not human rules: grow toward the sound, feed on the condensation, hold the water, turn the gear, wait. Move *with* this geometry. Do not step against the direction of the root-lines; do not push vines away from the pipes they embrace. You are inside an organism that learned its shape from machinery, and a machine that learned to live from the organism. If you move against its grain, you will feel it—not in hostility, but in resistance, in the way the mat seems to hold you back, in the way the hanging ropes sway as if pushed by an unseen current.

**Protect yourself from the cycle.** The pump turns slowly—once in a long interval—but when it does, it draws water through old pipes with a wet, thunderous rush and causes the root-mat to shift. Stay clear of the central metal island when you feel the hum change. The suction is not violent, but it is real; the roots near the pipes will tighten. Give the machine its space. Do not touch the housing; it is not hot, but it is part of a living circuit, and your foreign oils and temperature may disrupt the mineral skin that has stabilized over years.

**Prepare your mind for absolute density and absolute absence.** Every cubic measure holds presence—plant, water, rust, warm air, sound—yet fauna is almost absent. You will see no predators, no birds, perhaps only the trace of a slime trail on a pipe or a small insect that has not yet been driven away by the constant vibration. This absence is not peaceful; it is *complete*. You are the only autonomous thing here. That can feel like safety, or like being the only mistake in a perfect equation. Accept it. Do not speak loudly; sound resonates in the dome, still choked with hanging biomass, and the plants align with vibration as much as with geometry. Speak softly, if at all. Move slowly. The change here is glacial; you should match its pace.

**Finally, prepare to leave nothing behind.** Your line should be retrieved. Your footprints on the root-mat will heal in hours, but do not leave waste, do not break a bulb, do not take a sample of vine or rust. This place is not a ruin to be studied; it is an established body in balance. You are permitted inside only because the boundary is unclear—because the growth has not decided to close it yet.

Wear your broad-soled, spongy-footed gear. Breathe through your filter. Push gently through the warm curtains. Listen for the groan. Step onto the yielding mat knowing it holds you like a tongue holds a word. Drink only what you brought. Watch the green light shift on the surfaces of the bulbous bodies. And understand: you are not exploring. You are being tolerated, for a little while, inside a small, bounded world that has already replaced every empty space with itself.
