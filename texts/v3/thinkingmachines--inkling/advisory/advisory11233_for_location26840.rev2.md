You are preparing to enter a room that has never known air, never known shadow for long, and never known anything that was not deliberately placed by hands that left thumbprints in the glaze. It is not a cave. It is not a ruin. It is a breath-sized machinery of water and fired earth, hidden beneath the skin of a larger, warmer body of clear water, and you must treat it not like a landscape but like a living instrument—delicate, loud, sharp, and utterly without forgiveness for the careless.

Here is how an ordinary multiverse traveler should prepare.

### Understand the Scale First
This chamber is *small*—barely wider than a single held breath. Before you ever put on gear, train your body and mind for extreme confinement. Practice sitting in spaces the size of a bathtub, then smaller. Learn to curl without panic, to turn slowly, to extend only one limb at a time. You will not be swimming through an open pool; you will be occupying a pocket. Claustrophobia is your first enemy, long before the water.

If you can, meditate in tight, dark, warm enclosures so that the sudden absence of horizon does not trigger a reflex to thrash. Thrashing here means cutting yourself on fresh mortar joints, breaking a clay pipe, or dislodging the woven fiber nets that hold the cultivated plants. The room is young—days or weeks old. Its surfaces are unrounded, its colors unmuted, its joints white and brittle. It will not absorb your mistakes; it will show them.

### Breathing: No Air Exists Here
The chamber is filled completely. There is no ceiling air pocket, no hidden dome of breath waiting above the water line. The flat ceramic ceiling is an arm’s reach overhead only in theory—if the water ever left, which it will not. The blown-glass aperture in that ceiling pours light downward, but it is set with woven fiber gaskets into glazed ceramic; it is not an escape hatch, and it is likely submerged within the larger body of water that hides this place. Do not plan to surface inside.

You need a **slim-profile, closed-circuit rebreather**—not bulky twin tanks, which would be impossible to maneuver in this space. The unit should be shallow-water rated, warm-water tolerant, and silent (or near-silent), because the chamber already roars with its own mechanics. Test your mouthpiece for chafe; you may be holding it for the duration of a careful, slow visit. Carry no bailout bottle that is wider than your shoulder, or you will not fit through the entry.

If your multiverse physiology allows for magical or psionic water-breathing, still prepare a mechanical backup. The current is constant; if your innate ability falters, you will need to follow the pull of the drain out immediately.

### Armor Against Newness
Every wall is hand-laid tile, fired elsewhere and set with crushed-shell mortar. The edges are sharp—not worn smooth by years of flow, but fresh, like paper cuts made of ceramic. The floor is a single concave dish with a central depression where artificial dust from fresh mortar gathers in slow spirals. The plants hang in woven fiber nets anchored in small fired pots.

Your suit should not be a thick thermal wetsuit—the water is engineered warm, held by ceramic retention and enclosed circulation. Instead, wear a **thin, flexible dive skin or a custom-fitted abrasion layer** that covers you from ankle to wrist to neck. Over this, add:

- **Heavy-duty, flexible gloves**—not thick winter gloves, but cut-resistant dive gloves that still let you feel the wooden lever and the texture of tile.
- **Knee and elbow pads** with hard ceramic-facing shells. You will brace against walls.
- **Soft-soled but puncture-resistant booties**—the concave floor is smooth ceramic, but the sediment spirals can hide grit, and you must feel the gentle tug of current at your ankles without losing balance.

Do not wear anything with metal buckles, zippers, or hooks that could snag the woven fiber nets or scratch the fresh glaze. The thumbprints and cord-marks in the tile are still raised like scars; treat them as art you are not permitted to damage.

### Eyes for Blinding Light
The illumination is total and clinical. A column of white-gold strikes through the bubbled, warped blown glass and penetrates the water, scattering in sharp dancing fragments on the concave floor. There is nowhere for shadow to accumulate. For a human eye accustomed to dim underwater green or multiverse twilight, this is assaultive.

Prepare with **polarized, anti-glare goggles with transition capability**—clear for the dark approach through the larger body of water, but able to filter sudden radiance. Do not stare directly up at the aperture when you first enter; angle your gaze downward and let your pupils contract slowly. The glass is primitive—bubbled, warped—so light will refract in unpredictable patterns. Expect visual disorientation. A small, subdued waterproof flashlight can help you inspect details without competing with the sun-column, but you may not need it; the chamber reveals everything.

### Ears for Mechanical Roar
The sound is loud, constant, and built of no machine—just three clay pipes pouring thin, forceful streams through primitive nozzles, striking the far wall, then draining through a wider lower channel in continuous circulation. You will hear the hiss, the splash, the pull.

Bring **high-quality foam earplugs designed for pressure equalization**—not to block sound entirely (you need awareness of pipe failure or current shifts), but to reduce the roar to a manageable hum. If you use electronic noise-canceling earpieces, ensure they are sealed against warm water and do not interfere with your rebreather’s audio cues. Learn to communicate by tapping: one sharp knock on ceramic for “stop,” two for “exit,” a sustained palm-slide for “all clear.” Your voice will not carry well through a mouthpiece in this noise.

### Navigating the Entry and the Current
The chamber is hidden, sunk below the surface of a larger warm clear body. You will swim downward through that open water—clear, warm, alive with its own gentle motion—until you find the hidden mouth. The only connections are the three high inflow pipes and the wider, lower drain.

The drain is your likely portal: approach it from the outside, swimming upstream against the outflow. The current pulls gently but persistently at your ankles—this is the “tug of departure.” Do not fight it with panic. Brace one foot (carefully, softly) in the central floor depression and hold.

**Crucially, learn the wooden lever before you enter.** It sits carved—not sawn—in a ceramic cradle near the drain gate. Its purpose is to open or close the outflow by hand. Understand its mechanics: does pulling it seal the gate, stopping the suction? Or does it release? If you need to exit quickly, or if you need to reduce the current to stabilize yourself, this lever is your only control. Do not pull it experimentally inside; the dynamics never stop, but altering them suddenly in a space this small could shift sediment, dislodge plants, or create unexpected pressure. If you must use it, do so with one gloved hand while bracing the other.

Enter slowly. Let the current guide you rather than resist it. Once inside, settle into the concave floor. Do not stand—there is no “standing” in a space barely larger than your held breath. Sit or float in a crouch.

### Interacting with the Contents
Inside, there is almost nothing: the pipes, the lever, and a few sparse aquatic plants cultivated rather than wild, hanging in woven fiber nets attached to the north wall, anchored in fired pots of crushed shell and sand. They sway gently—the only soft things in a hard space.

- **Do not touch the plants.** They are anchored delicately. Their nets are woven fiber; your gloves could snag them. They are part of the chamber’s function—perhaps filtration, perhaps offering—leave them undisturbed.
- **Do not block the pipes.** The three eastern inflow streams are thin and directed. Even a brief obstruction could alter pressure on fresh mortar joints.
- **Do not stir the sediment.** The central depression holds fine artificial dust from the fresh mortar. It moves in slow spirals. If you disturb it, visibility will drop instantly in a space where visibility is your only safety.

If you take nothing, break nothing, and leave the lever exactly as you found it, you have done well.

### Psychological and Sensory Protocol
You will be in a place of pure intention and no history. It has no animals, no fish, no insects—only the rushing water. This absence can feel louder than the pipes themselves. The warmth is engineered, not natural; it holds you like a hand. The sharpness of every edge reminds you that you are inside something that was *made for a purpose* you do not yet understand—perhaps a bath, perhaps a filter-chamber for some larger constructed system, now submerged and functioning alone.

Prepare for a kind of reverence that has nothing to do with religion and everything to do with scale. You are smaller than the intention that built this room. Move slowly. Breathe slowly through your rebreather. Let the light column pass over you without staring into it. Listen to the water not as noise but as language—entry, circulation, exit.

If you feel panic rising, do not claw at the walls. The mortar is fresh; your gloves will leave marks. Instead, feel the gentle tug at your ankles, orient yourself toward the drain—the direction of departure—and exhale slowly. The current knows the way out.

### Exit and After
When you leave—swimming back through the drain against the flow, or perhaps allowing the gentle pull to guide you if you have opened the gate—do not rush. The transition from this blinding, loud, sharp, warm pocket back into the larger clear body of water may feel like stepping out of a loud workshop into open sky.

Once outside, do not bring the water with you in your mind as mere “pool.” It was moving, alive with current though containing no life. Rinse your gear, check for cuts (the sharp edges may have found a gap in your protection), and record exactly the position of the lever, the state of the nets, and the pattern of the sediment spiral—because this place is still being made, and your presence is now part of its very brief history.

Go gently. The room forgot to be on land, and it has not yet learned what it is. You are only a visitor in its first breath.
