**The Pocket Beneath: A Preparation Guide for the Submerged Chamber**

Before you even pack, understand what kind of place this is. You are not visiting a ruin or a reef. You are entering a room that has never touched open air, a chamber built by hand and sunk—while still new—beneath the surface of a warm, clear body of water. It is barely wider than a single breath held wide, no larger than a closet you must share with rushing water. Its ceiling is flat ceramic, an arm’s reach above your head if the water ever left (it does not). Its walls are hand-laid tile, fired elsewhere and carried here, joined with crushed-shell mortar that is still white, still fresh, still carrying the thumbprints of its makers in ridges sharp as fresh scars. Every edge is new. Every surface is intentional. There is no rock, no soil, no history—only geometry, flow, and warmth held like breath.

Because it is filled entirely with warm, moving water, you will be submerged the moment you enter through the circular glass aperture in the ceiling. There is no air pocket inside. The water enters through three coiled clay pipes high in the eastern wall, pours downward in thin, forceful streams, strikes the far wall in a continuous hiss, then drains through a lower channel in the opposite wall, pulling gently at your ankles in an unending loop. The floor is a single concave ceramic dish; in the center, fine artificial dust from the fresh mortar gathers in slow spirals, moved by the current. The only things that are not hard are a few cultivated aquatic plants hanging in woven fiber nets on the north wall, anchored in small fired pots, swaying gently—the only softness in a place made entirely of hardness. A carved wooden lever rests in a ceramic cradle near the drain gate, meant to open or close the outflow by hand.

This is your environment. Here is how an ordinary human multiverse traveler should prepare to survive and move within it.

---

### 1. Air: Your First Non-Negotiable

This chamber is a sealed mouth of handmade order. The glass in the ceiling is primitive—blown thick, bubbled, warped, set with woven fiber gaskets. It admits a blinding column of white-gold light, but it does not admit air. The three pipes bring only water. The drain takes it away. You will be breathing underwater for the duration of your stay.

Bring a **closed-circuit rebreather**, not open-circuit scuba. Bubbles are an intrusion here. They will rise through the clinical column of light, shatter the sharp visibility, add noise to an already deafening rush of water, and—more practically—they may disturb the fresh mortar joints or the delicate woven nets holding the plants. A rebreather is silent, streamlined, and essential in a space barely larger than your outstretched arms.

Calculate your air for **double your expected time**. The current is gentle but relentless. If you become disoriented, if you need to study the sediment spirals or operate the wooden lever, you will not want to rush. The warmth of the chamber can lull you; do not let it make you careless with your supply. Before entering, test your gear in water of matching temperature—this place retains heat like a ceramic jar, and condensation or regulator behavior can differ.

If you travel with a partner, establish a tether line between you, not for companionship alone, but because in total, shadowless visibility, a body can disappear against the pale green-blue glaze as easily as in darkness.

---

### 2. Protection: You Are Surrounded by Knives

The erosion here is not of wear but of *newness*. The tiles are sharp. The mortar joints are unrounded. The glaze carries ridges—cord-marks, thumbprints, fresh scars—that catch light like blades catch sun. The floor is concave, slick, ceramic. The current will push you against these surfaces.

Wear a **full-coverage, abrasion-resistant dive skin or very thin drysuit**, fitted tightly so it does not billow in the flow and snag on tile ridges. Do not wear loose sleeves or dangling straps; there are no creatures here to notice them, but there are sharp edges everywhere. Reinforce your knees, elbows, and hips—these are the points that will strike the walls as the current shifts your weight.

Your feet need **soft-soled, high-grip boots**—not heavy weights that will crack the fresh ceramic floor, but something that allows you to brace against the concave dish without sliding into the central depression where the sediment gathers. The pull is strongest near the drain; if you drift, your ankles will feel it first.

Most critically: **gloves**. Not thick, clumsy gauntlets, but thin, cut-resistant membrane gloves that preserve dexterity. You may need to grasp the wooden lever, adjust a plant net, or brace yourself against a tile without leaving your palms sliced open by unweathered glaze. The lever is carved wood, slippery when soaked. The plants are anchored in fired pots filled with crushed shell and sand—handle them only if necessary, and then only with protected hands.

Carry a **small, sealed first-aid pouch** on your waist, not in a backpack. Backpacks are dangerous here; they extend your profile in a space barely wider than a breath. If you are cut—and you likely will be, somewhere—the warm water will slow clotting but also make bleeding invisible and disorienting. Have waterproof bandages and a clotting agent ready. A deep ceramic laceration in warm, moving water is not an emergency you want to discover too late.

---

### 3. Eyes: The Blinding Column

Light comes from a single circular aperture above. Underwater, it does not diffuse gently; it strikes the concave floor and scatters in sharp, dancing fragments. There are no shadows long enough to hide in. Everything is revealed: every joint, every ridge, every movement.

Bring a **mask with polarized or tinted lenses**, but do not over-darken them. You need to see the sharp edges approaching in your periphery as the current turns you. The glass is bubbled and warped; looking directly up into it for too long can cause vertigo, especially when combined with the constant motion of the water. Learn to read the light on the floor—where the fragments dance tells you the angle of the aperture, which tells you which way is up, which in a featureless ceramic box can be surprisingly hard to remember.

Consider a **small, sealed underwater light of your own**, not for illumination—there is already too much—but for signaling. If your partner is with you, a brief flash in the current can mean *stop*, *turn*, or *surface* without relying on sound.

---

### 4. Ears and Mind: The Loudness of Absence

The three pipes pour downward in continuous streams. The drain pulls away with its own voice. Together they create a rushing hiss and splash that is constant, mechanical in persistence though built of no machine. You will not hear your own heartbeat. You will not hear your partner call out. You may not even hear yourself think.

**Do not wear earplugs.** You need to hear changes in flow. If someone—perhaps you—moves the wooden lever, the pitch and volume of the outflow will shift. That shift is information. Instead, prepare for sensory isolation. Bring a **waterproof writing slate or a gesture-based communication system**. Agree on signals before you descend: a tug on the tether, a specific hand position, a light flash.

More importantly, prepare psychologically. There is no life here. No fish navigate these pipes; no insects rest on any surface tension. The warmth feels engineered, held by ceramic retention and enclosed water. You are inside a place with no history, no rock, no soil—only intention. This can feel like being inside a machine that forgot to include people, or inside an organ that has not yet learned to be part of a body. If you are prone to claustrophobia, to sensory overload, or to the particular loneliness of spaces without biology, bring a mental anchor: a mantra, a counting rhythm, a memory of open sky. The clinical brightness reveals everything—including your own anxiety.

The space is small. The current never stops. There is nowhere to sit and rest except to brace yourself against the concave floor, feeling the pull in your ankles. Accept that you will be in constant, gentle motion. Do not fight it with exhaustion. Move with the loop.

---

### 5. Navigating the Loop: Enter, Move, Interact

Your entry is almost certainly through the glass aperture in the ceiling—swimming downward from the body of water above into this pocket. The aperture is thick, primitive glass; do not strike it. It is your exit as well.

Once inside:
- **Orient yourself immediately.** The pipes enter from the east; the drain is opposite, to the west. The plants hang in nets on the north wall. The lever rests near the drain gate in its ceramic cradle.
- **Do not swim against the current.** Use it. Let it turn you slowly, like being inside a washing bowl. The pull is strongest near the drain; if you feel your ankles tugged downward or westward, you are near the outflow.
- **Understand the lever.** It is carved wood, not sawn—primitive, resistant, but slippery when soaked. Its purpose is to open or close the drain gate. Before you touch it, observe the flow. If you need to reduce the current—perhaps to study the sediment in the central depression, or to examine the plants without being pushed past them—closing the gate slightly may calm the loop. If you need to leave quickly and the flow is blocking your ascent, understand that altering the drain will change the dynamics of the entire room. Move it slowly. The wood is old-style; it may resist, or it may move suddenly.

The plants are cultivated, not wild. They hang in woven fiber nets, anchored in small fired pots filled with crushed shell and sand. They are the only softness here—the only things that sway rather than resist. Do not disturb them unless your purpose requires it. They are part of the system, perhaps filtering, perhaps feeding the loop in some way you do not yet understand.

The sediment in the central depression is artificial—fine dust from the fresh mortar, gathering in spirals. If you disturb it with your fins or your hands, you will cloud the water. The visibility is one of the chamber’s strangest gifts; do not destroy it carelessly.

---

### 6. Physical Contingency: The Sharpness of Youth

This place is young—days or weeks old. Its mortar has not fully cured to stone. Its tiles have not been tested by centuries of flow. This means two things for you:
- The structures are **brittle**. Do not kick the floor, do not pull on the pipes, do not use the tile walls for leverage. A sharp edge is also a fragile one.
- The mortar can still be **affected**. Your presence—your gear scraping joints, your bubbles (if any escape), your disturbance of the sediment—is an intrusion into a system that has not yet settled. Move gently.

If you are injured, remember: the warm water will mask pain. A cut that would bleed visibly in cold air may seep slowly here, invisible in the clear flow. Check yourself constantly. The concave floor means you might be dragged gently but persistently toward the center or the drain if you lose consciousness or buoyancy control. Wear a **small, neutral-buoyancy vest or belt** to keep yourself suspended without constant effort, reducing the risk of drifting into sharp edges while distracted.

Keep a **tether line** anchored to a safe point—perhaps near the plant nets on the north wall, away from the drain—so that if your air fails or your orientation fails, you can pull yourself toward a known position and then upward toward the aperture.

---

### 7. Exit: Returning Through the Glass

Your exit is upward, back through the circular aperture, into the body of water above, then eventually to open air. Do not attempt to exit through the pipes; they are narrow, coiled, and forceful. Do not attempt to exit through the drain unless you understand its path—likely into another part of the constructed system, perhaps darker, perhaps farther from air.

Before you ascend:
- Check your air reserve.
- Note the position of the lever; if you moved it, return it to its original position unless instructed otherwise. This is not your system; it is young, functioning alone, perhaps part of a larger constructed order you have not seen.
- Move slowly upward through the aperture. The glass is warped; your gear may catch. Streamline your body. The current will try to hold you; use your tether if necessary.

Once through, ascend through the body of water above. Only then, at the true surface, should you allow yourself the full breath of open air.

---

### 8. Final Intention: How to Be Here

This chamber is not a destination for sightseeing in the ordinary sense. It is too small, too loud, too sharp, too artificial. You come here to witness a pocket that has only ever been made—to stand (or float) inside pure intention, inside a breath of handmade order that has not yet forgotten its makers.

Treat it with the respect you would give a newborn machine, a freshly fired vessel, a mouth that has just learned to open. Do not leave marks. Do not take pieces of the mortar or the tile. Document if you must—slate, camera, memory—but understand that your presence is already a disturbance. Be brief. Be careful. Be aware that the warmth, the light, the flow, and the silence of all living things are not accidents; they are the design.

Bring your air, your protection, your quiet. Move with the water, not against it. See everything, because the light demands it. And when you have seen enough—when you have felt the pull in your ankles, heard the mechanical rush of the pipes, and understood what it means to be inside a place that forgot to be on land—leave through the glass, and let the chamber continue its loop without you.
