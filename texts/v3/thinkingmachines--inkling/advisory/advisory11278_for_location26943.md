Prepare for **the Hollow** not as you would for a city, but as you would for a prolonged immersion inside a single, breathing lung—one that happens to contain people, paths, and purpose. This is not a destination you observe from a safe distance. At this scale, intimacy is unavoidable: you can walk its full breadth in fifteen minutes, yet it has the layered density of a metropolis that refused to sprawl. Instead it burrowed downward and inward, filling every cubic breath with presence. You are not visiting a settlement *near* nature; you are entering a human order that has agreed, over centuries, to become part of the plant-saturated substrate.

Here is how an ordinary traveler should ready body, mind, and kit.

---

### The Mental Recalibration: From Visitor to Temporary Organism

Your first and most important preparation is philosophical. The Hollow operates on rules of **persuasion, not domination**. There is no bare earth, no naked stone for long, no space cleared and held empty. Streets are not laid; they are **grown**—black yew and woven willow trained across generations into walls and tunnels. Houses are bark-clad root-mounds, hollowed living trees, or thickening reed-cats. Moss is not decoration but flooring, insulation, and quilt. 

You must leave behind the instinct to “clear a space,” “swat away,” or “sanitize your perimeter.” The fauna here—overwhelming at 2.8 saturation—is not an intrusion but the climate itself. Beetles click in the walls of your lodging. Tree-frogs call from moss-roofs in layered choruses. Spiders weave between living branch-doorposts. Small birds nest inside the woven walls, entering through deliberate gaps. Snails cross the paths you walk, which are actually moss-carpeted ruts. Bats cluster beneath the Root-Bridge. You will feel the vibration of insects in the frame of your bed. You will brush against hanging ferns at every turn.

**Preparation:** Practice stillness in noisy, crowded biological environments before you arrive. Learn to sleep with constant, non-threatening motion near you. Accept that your personal boundary will shrink to the width of your shoulders and that proximity—density at 2.3—is the social norm. Voices carry easily over the biological soundscape, but so does every footstep. Move slowly. Everything is near-touching.

### Clothing: Dress for Constitutional Wetness and Cool

The temperature sits at **0.7—perpetually cool**—and the air is thin, bright but filtered (1.5 elevation, 1.8 light through mist and canopy). Breath comes visible. Skin stays damp and chilled not from occasional rain, but because **water at 2.5 is constitutional**. The ground never dries. Stone weeps constant condensation. Shallow streams seep through substrate rather than running in channels. After every rain—which is frequent, vertical, and then suddenly absent—everything drips and runs.

**What to wear:**
- **Layers that insulate when soaked.** Wool, felted bark-fiber, or modern analogues that retain warmth in saturation. Cotton will kill your comfort.
- **A hood, not just a hat.** You need protection not only from cool vertical rain but from the constant gentle collision with flying and crawling life—moths thickening at dusk, beetles, falling droplets, hanging moss.
- **Long sleeves and trousers, always.** Not for modesty—this is an intimate, close-quarters culture—but because bare skin will be touched constantly: by moss walls, by branch-joints serving as eaves, by snails on paths, by the cool iron-tasting mist itself.
- **No hard shells that crackle loudly.** The sound environment is already loud (2.1) from biological saturation—clicking, calling, rustling. You do not want to be the artificial noise in the weave. Soft, dense fabrics that absorb sound are preferable.
- **Personal warmth source.** Every dwelling keeps a small, constant fire—not for heat alone, but for the psychological weight of dry warmth in a world that never offers it. Carry a small, contained heat-source if you have one, or be prepared to sit near communal fires often. Your body will crave the illusion of dryness.

---

### Footwear: For Moss, Roots, and Living Floors

The architecture breathes slowly, thickening and shedding. The ground is never bare earth. Streets are woven, moss-carpeted, root-interlaced. There are shallow streams that do not stay in channels. Snails are not obstacles; they are part of the floor’s texture.

**Footwear requirements:**
- **Flexible, soft-soled boots or wraps** that allow you to feel the unevenness of living substrate and to move quietly. Heavy, rigid boots will damage moss-bedding and root-tunnels, and they will slip on constant wet.
- **High ankle coverage** but not rigid—roots and woven willow form constant low obstacles.
- **Quick-draining, quick-drying material.** You will step in seep-water. You will cross the Root-Bridge where mist collects thickest. Your feet will never be fully dry; aim for feet that can be comfortably damp for days.
- **Bring extra woven fiber or bark-twine** for repairs. Technology here is primitive (1.3)—bone needles, wooden mallets, fired-clay pipes, hand-twisted rope. If your gear breaks, you will repair it with the same materials locals use, or you will go without. Pack needle-and-thread equivalents in bone or hardwood, not metal if you wish to integrate smoothly (though the culture does not forbid metal, it does not use it; bringing heavy metal gear marks you immediately as an outsider who does not understand local metabolism).

### Navigation: Small Scale, Infinite Depth

You can cross the settlement in a quarter-hour, yet it holds districts: the Weft Quarter where fiber cures in perpetual damp; the Root-Bridge over the central seep; the Moss-Market in a circular clearing roofed by interlaced alder. But this is not a grid. The settlement grew **downward and inward**.

- **Expect vertical complexity.** Pathways descend into root-hollows, cross over seep-streams on woven bridges, tunnel through yew-hedges.
- **Maps are unreliable.** At dynamic 2.6, this environment is turbulent despite being ancient and balanced (erosion 1.7). Plants grow visibly fast. A path cleared in spring is half-reclaimed by midsummer. Animal swarms shift like tides—one week beetles dominate the walls, the next frogs own the soundscape, the next a cloud of moths moves through like living fog.
- **Hire or befriend a local guide immediately.** Not because you will get “lost” in a large space, but because the *usable* paths change with the growth and with water levels in the central seep, which rise and fall with rhythms both seasonal and chaotic. A local knows which moss-rut is currently open, which branch-tunnel has thickened shut, and where the frog-chorus is currently loudest (making speech impossible).
- **Mark your lodging not with signs, but with relationship.** Your host’s dwelling is a living structure; it may have grown thicker since you left for the Moss-Market. Learn its particular scent—wood-smoke, wet bark, decomposing leaf-litter (managed, necessary, sharp).

### Food, Water, and Storage: Sealed, Suspended, Constant

Water is everywhere, but it is substrate-water—seeping, running, mist-laden. Do not drink from shallow streams without purification appropriate to this world’s biology (the fauna saturation means everything is biologically active).

More critically: **food storage must be suspended and sealed.** Insects, snails, small mammals, and birds do not “infest” food here; they simply exist where food is, because there is no “outside” to drive them to. The locals store provisions in hanging woven baskets, sealed clay vessels fired with local wood, suspended from branch-joints well above moss-floors.

**Preparation:**
- Bring **sealable, hard containers**—clay, thick bark, or modern equivalents—that can hang or sit in water-resistant woven nets.
- Do not leave food on the ground. Do not leave it open. Even for a minute.
- Accept that your meals will be taken in the presence of constant biological activity. You may eat while beetles click in the wall beside you, while a wren enters through its wall-gap, while rain begins vertical and stops without warning.
- Carry **personal purification** for water. The air tastes of moss and cold iron; the water carries that same mineral-green density.

### Health and Body Care: The Damp Skin

Your skin will never fully dry. The chill is constant but not freezing—this is not winter, but a cool, bright, high shoulder of the world. The risk is not frostbite but **chronic damp chill, fungal accommodation, and sensory overload.**

- **Skin care:** Bring oils or salves that create a barrier against constant moisture without clogging pores (the air is already thick with biological particles). Moss-contact is constant and generally harmless, but open cuts should be covered—decomposition and rapid growth mean bacteria are active and plentiful.
- **Respiratory:** The air is clean but saturated—moss spores, insect particles, amphibian scents, wood-smoke. If you have sensitivities, prepare filters or masks, but know that masking your face will make you strange in a culture that breathes this air as native climate.
- **Psychological warmth:** The small constant fires in every dwelling are psychological anchors. Seek them. Sit near them even when not cold. The human mind in the Hollow needs periodic proof that dryness exists.

---

### Cultural Protocol: The Ethics of Living Architecture

You will stay in a dwelling that is alive—perhaps a hollowed tree, a root-mound with woven bark-doors, a thickening reed-cat. The rules are simple and severe:

1. **Do not kill the plants that shape your home.** You do not trim aggressively. You do not “clear” for view or space. If a branch grows across your path, you duck, or you ask the host how to gently redirect it with pruning techniques passed through generations.
2. **Do not clear animals away.** You do not swat. You do not poison. You build around their rhythms—leaving passageways in walls, accepting that your sleeping space is shared space.
3. **Participate in the manual craft ethos.** Technology is hand-scale. If you contribute—helping twist bark fiber, directing water through fired-clay pipes, weaving—do so with bone, wood, and patience. Metal tools are not forbidden, but they are alien; they do not understand the flexibility required here.
4. **Speak clearly, not loudly.** The background noise is high—constant water running, insects clicking, frogs calling, rain. Your voice carries, but shouting disrupts the layered soundscape that locals navigate as weather. Listen more than you speak.

### Dynamic Adaptability: Pack for Turbulence

This place is old, weathered, in-balance (erosion 1.7), yet **hyperactive (dynamic 2.6)**. You must be flexible:

- **No rigid itinerary.** A rain sheet may come vertical and then stop, leaving paths temporarily impassable due to seep-rise. Animal tides may make one district overwhelming (moth-clouds, frog-choruses so loud conversation stops) while another is calm.
- **Light, flexible pack.** Buildings bend; paths reclaim; you may need to move your lodging concept from “this specific root-mound” to “the Weft Quarter generally” if growth has shifted access.
- **Expect to rebuild or repair.** If you stay long, you will participate in seasonal construction—pruning back, re-weaving, redirecting water through clay pipes. Bring patience, not blueprints.

---

### Sensory Preparation: Accept the Saturation

Before arriving, prepare your senses for overwhelming presence:

- **Smell:** Layered wood-smoke, wet bark, managed decomposing leaf-litter (sharp, green, necessary), the distinct scent of amphibian skin, rapid green rot.
- **Touch:** Constant. Moss walls brush your shoulders. Snails are underfoot. Insect vibrations travel through living wood frames into your palms when you lean on them.
- **Sound:** Not mechanical—there is none—but biological saturation at volume. Learn to distinguish the click of beetles (constant, wall-texture), the layered frog-chorus, the rustle of small birds in wall-gaps, the sudden vertical sound of rain, then its absence.
- **Sight:** Normal brightness, but filtered, misty, dense with vertical lines—yew, willow, fern, root, moss. There is little horizon. The world is close, layered, downward-focused.

---

### What to Leave Behind

- **Pesticides, repellents, or “clearance” tools.** They violate the fundamental ecology and will make you an instant outcast.
- **Heavy, rigid gear** that requires flat, dry ground.
- **Expectations of privacy as separation.** Privacy here is social and temporal, not spatial. You will be near others, near animals, near growing things. Solitude is found in shared quiet, not in isolation.
- **The need for speed.** Everything moves slowly. Plants grow visibly. Crafts are seasonal and collective. You will adapt your pace or you will bruise yourself against the weave.

---

### Final Intuition

Visiting the Hollow is like stepping inside a cool, high, wet lung that has learned to think in human patterns. You will not be cold exactly, but you will never be dry. You will not be lost, because the space is small, but you will be **deep inside**—downward, inward, surrounded by the absolute fill of vegetation and the overwhelming presence of non-human life. The settlement survives not by fighting this turbulence but by being made of materials that thrive in it: living walls, flexible minds, bodies accustomed to damp chill and loud biological weather.

Come wearing soft layers, carrying suspended sealable food, walking softly on moss, ready to duck under growing branches, ready to sit near small fires for the psychological proof of dryness, ready to become—for a time—not a conqueror of space, but one more slow-moving, cool-breathing organism within the palm of the world.
