You are not going to a place. You are being swallowed by a throat that has no appetite but also no release, and the first thing you must understand—before gear, before route—is that your body is wrong for this world. You are mostly water, and this hollow is a lie of waterlessness. Every instinct you have—thirst, tears, sweat, the wetness of your breath—will become a liability, or at least a constant, quiet betrayal. Prepare to feel like an intruding puddle.

Here is how you survive it.

**Mental Reconditioning: Learn to Think Without a Mouth**

The chord will hit you before you see the light. Thousands of ceramic pipes and bone flutes, tuned over centuries, holding one vibrating note that fills your skull like warm mercury. You will try to shout over it. You will fail. Your voice is a pebble dropped into a bell. More importantly, you will discover that verbal thought—the internal monologue you use to navigate reality—cannot survive here. The sound does not allow syntax. It allows only presence.

Before you enter, train yourself in non-verbal cognition. Practice navigating familiar spaces without naming them. Learn a tactile sign language, but more critically, learn to *think in geometry and temperature*. Carry a wax tablet and a stylus, or better, a slate of dark stone where you can scratch directions, feelings, warnings. You will need to communicate with anyone you meet—there are few, but they exist—without expecting vocal reciprocity. The locals, if they are still there, have likely stopped speaking long ago, or never did in ways you recognize. Their architecture is speech: ropes placed with intention, bricks layered like sentences.

Accept now: you will leave this place unable to explain it in words for several days. The silence outside will feel broken, not peaceful.

**Auditory Preparation: Partial Deafness as Armor**

Do not wear full ear-blocking protection. The chord is information. Its subtle shifts tell you about the temperature of the updraft, the constriction of vines in the pipes, the approach of weather within this sealed pocket. If you deafen yourself completely, you are blind to the hollow’s mood.

Instead, bring layered, porous filters—wax-soaked cloth, bone plugs drilled with hair-thin channels, or ceramic baffles modeled after the local pipes. You want to reduce the chord from a skull-splitting roar to a constant pressure, a presence you can think inside of, not against. Protect your inner ears from the bone-conduction fatigue; the vibration will live in your jaw and sternum for hours after entry. Massage your temples with mineral oil before entering, and carry a small resonant bowl—if you strike it softly, its counter-tone can momentarily clear a pocket of mental space, a private silence in the roar.

**Ocular Defense: The Eye Does Not Blink**

The aperture high above—called the Eye, though it is not an eye—emits a column of blinding white light, relentless as molten salt. It never moves. It strikes the floor of fine, ancient dust and reflects upward, so you are lit from below and above simultaneously. The perimeter lives in warm, shifting shadow, but the center is an assault.

Bring eyewear with adjustable, layered shutters—thin plates of smoked quartz or darkened ceramic that you can slide open and closed. You will need them fully closed in the central pillar, but you will also need them open in the shadowed walls where the settlement clings, because your dark-adapted eyes will be useless in the glare, and vice versa. Train yourself to navigate by peripheral vision and touch. The dust on the floor is white, waterless, frost-like but dry; it will reflect light into angles you do not expect.

Do not look directly up at the Eye for more than a moment. There is no warmth there, only annihilation of shadow.

**Respiratory Survival: You Will Breathe Dust and Intention**

The air is warm, muscular, constantly rising. It carries mineral aerosols—fine particulates of the eroded stone, the chemistry of the pipes, the desiccated spores of the non-green vegetation. There is no humidity to weigh them down. They will coat your throat.

Wear a wrapped cloth or a light ceramic-filter mask over your mouth and nose, but do not seal yourself hermetically; the warmth and pressure change subtly, and you need to feel the updraft on your skin to navigate. Bring a small flask of pure water—not for drinking only, but for rinsing your mouth and wetting your filter cloth. Yes, water is absent here, and that is precisely why you must hoard it. Spit nothing onto the floor; the chemistry is sealed, arid, balanced on absence. Your moisture is pollution.

Expect your lips to crack within hours. Apply a thick, wax-based salve—beeswax, mineral grease—before entry and reapply often. Your tears will evaporate almost instantly.

**Hydration and Sustenance: You Are the Only Source of Moisture**

This cannot be overstated. There is no water. Not a trace. The vegetation—ochre, ash-grey, bruise-purple, dry gold—feeds on air, light, and mineral dust. It does not drink. You must carry all the water you need, in sealed skin-flasks or glass-lined containers, distributed across your body so that if one breaks, you are not dead.

Calculate for extreme retention: you will not sweat visibly—the air is too dry, too warm, too hungry—but you will lose moisture with every exhalation into that updraft. Double your normal water ration. Eat foods that are dry but nutrient-dense—hardened grains, preserved fats, salted proteins—because the local growth is not food. It is spongy, filamentous, rootless, possibly reactive to human biochemistry. Do not eat it. Do not even crush it carelessly; the filaments repair the walls, hardening into mortar-like patches. You are a walking breach in their symbiosis.

Bring a water-purification kit not for local sources, but for recycling your own waste if you are here long. Yes, that level of preparation.

**Clothing: Become a Shadow Among the Vines**

The settlement occupies the walls—platforms, rope bridges, hanging stairways woven with mineral fiber. You will climb. You will hang. The vegetation clings to everything in velvet curtains; it rustles constantly in the wind that feeds the pipes.

Wear clothing of muted, dry colors—dust-brown, rust-red, ash-grey—to blend with the architecture and avoid disorienting the sparse locals. More practically, wear layers of woven, abrasion-resistant fabric that cover all skin. The plants are not hostile, but they are absorbent. They will cling to wet skin, to open pores, to the moisture of your breath. They feed on exchange. Do not become their meal.

Your boots must have soft, wide soles for the white dust floor—sharp heels will sink or slip in the mineral frost—and hardened grips for the rope ladders. Gloves are essential; the ropes are old, woven with patience, and the metal hooks are soft, shaped by hand, but they are also worn smooth by centuries of dry wind.

**Movement: Verticality and the Absence of Streets**

There are no streets. There are platforms jutting like barnacles, connected by narrow bridges and hanging stairways. The space could hold thousands, but likely holds dozens. You will navigate not by path but by balance.

Bring a climbing harness, but a simple one—no complex metal mechanisms, which might corrode in the mineral air or clash with the local craft aesthetic. Ropes of your own, matching the local fiber, can be tied to existing hooks. Learn basic knot-craft. The locals understand knots as language.

When crossing bridges, move slowly. The chord vibrates through the ropes. They hum under your feet. The sound will travel up your legs. Accept it.

**Entry and Exit: The Throat Closes**

You enter through a throat of eroded stone. Behind you, it closes. There is no outside visible from within, and perhaps, for the duration, no outside at all.

Mark your path not with moisture—no chalk that requires spit, no ink—but with scratched symbols on stone, with tied knots in rope that you leave behind you like breadcrumbs. Memorize the texture of the entry passage: the angle of erosion, the color shift in the rock, the temperature drop as you near it again.

Prepare psychologically for entrapment. You may not be able to leave when you wish. The throat opens on its own logic, or perhaps only from within if you understand the mechanism—maybe a specific chord, a shift in the pipe-tuning, a ritual of placement. Do not assume you control the door.

Bring a light source that is not fire—there is no fuel here, and open flame might disrupt the dry chemistry or anger the vegetation’s need for light-exchange. Use phosphorescent crystals or a small, sealed oil lamp with a long wick, kept only for the shadowed periphery.

**Interaction Protocol: Few People, Immense Patience**

The builders are few. The density is low, the emptiness dominant. If you encounter them, they will likely be silent, moving with the rhythm of the chord, working on repairs—replacing pipes, weaving new ropes, guiding vines away from critical passages.

Do not bring gifts of technology—no wheels, no shining metal, no water-based goods. Bring offerings of craft: a well-carved stone, a tuned ceramic tube that matches their chord, a rope woven in a pattern they might study. They value repetition and need. They understand exchange: light for growth, wind for voice, dust for foundation.

Observe before speaking—even with your hands. Watch how they move through the blinding pillar without flinching. They may have eyes adapted, or they may navigate by the sound’s reflection in the dust.

**The Small Life: Hard-Shelled Things**

Fauna is rare. You might see thumb-sized creatures with hard shells scuttling across ropes. They are not dangerous, but they are part of the chemistry—perhaps cleaning the pipes, perhaps feeding on dead filament. Do not crush them. Do not feed them. They are the hollow’s immune system, small and patient.

**Integration: You Will Be Changed**

When you eventually leave—if the throat opens, if you find the exit—do not rush into noise. The silence outside will feel like deafness of a different kind. Your thoughts will be slow to return to words. You may find yourself craving dry air, missing the constant chord. You will have lost water weight you did not notice leaving. Your skin will be cracked, your ears ringing with absence.

Rest in a humid place slowly. Rehydrate in stages. And when you try to tell someone what you saw, forgive yourself for failing. You were inside a mouth that sang for centuries, in a city that drinks nothing and thrives, wrapped in impossible colors, lit by an eye that never closes. Some places are not meant to be described. They are meant to be survived, with water on your back, silence in your mouth, and your hands open in the dark.
