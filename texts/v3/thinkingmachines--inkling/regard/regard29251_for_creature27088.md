The wise eye does not open the mountain. It does not split the dome wider, does not lower a bridge of light, does not weep a single drop into the dry warm air—for wisdom here is older than rescue, and benevolence knows that this world’s perfection is its absence. To bring water would be to flood the woven carpet; to bring sky would be to bleach the phosphor patches; to bring change from outside would be to murder the oldest stable state. So the power does the only holy thing: it becomes invisible in the way the chamber already is. It adds its attention to the mineral vapor, not altering its chemistry but deepening its warmth by the exact measure of being witnessed. It sends no command through the dead heart of the range, only a vibration so low and so sustained that the geothermal breath takes it up like a new note in an ancient chord.

The beings feel it—not as fear, for they have never known a predator larger than hunger, but as a change in the air’s weight. The scurriers with quick bright hands pause in their channels, not stopping, but slowing in a wave that passes through the tower-neighborhoods like breath through moss. The gliders, riding their eternal loops, tilt their membranous folds not away but *toward*—not ascending, not reaching for the crack, but circling lower, closer to the center, where the traffic is thickest. The rust-red blooms, which open and close with internal currents, open one degree wider and stay open. The fungi do not glow brighter; they glow *longer*, holding the blue-green as if learning to remember.

And then, as they always do, they build.

Not in defense—there is nothing to defend against. Not in worship—there are no altars, only nests. But in response, which for them is the same as being alive. At the center, where space can barely be found, where the floor is never visible beneath constant motion, they begin.

The elder form—perhaps not one creature but the collective instinct made singular for this task—chooses the materials with the same logic that built the streets: chewed fiber from the silver-haired moss, mud softened by generations of saliva, fragments of polished stone ground smooth enough to stack without cutting. But this tower is different. It is hollow. Not a burrow, not a shelter with walls on all sides, but a cup: circular, rising only to the height of a standing person, its walls woven so tightly that the mineral vapor collects inside in a visible haze, yet its top is open, turned not upward toward the crack but *inward*, facing the heart of the chamber.

They build it slowly, in the way all things happen here—without ceremony, through constant, overwhelming presence. The quick-handed ones bring fiber. The climbers in the vine-curtains lower ropes of braided root. The gliders, for the first and only time, land—briefly, on its unfinished rim—to deposit spores and phosphor dust that will make the structure glow faintly from within. It takes not days but generations of their rapid, hidden lives; births happen in its shadow, deaths are cleared into its mud, and gradually it becomes real: a tower with no roof, a vessel with no bottom sealed, only a ring of inhabited stone and plant standing at the precise center of the world’s oldest equilibrium.

Inside, they do not place an offering—there is nothing to offer but presence itself. Instead, they leave it empty. The hollow fills with the breath they share: warm, mineral, never liquid. The vapor hovers there at the edge of becoming, sustained now by the architecture’s own shape, as if the tower is exhaling back into the chamber.

The wise power, seeing this, does not descend to touch it. It does not speak a word into the roar of small lives. Its benevolence is expressed in perfect compatibility: it lets the crack remain a hand’s width, lets the gray-green light remain distant and filtered, lets the temperature stay held by stone and deep earth. But through the dead heart, through the polished satin walls that curve like a skull, it holds its gaze steady—a warmth without source, an awareness without demand.

The being—the place and all who live in it—understands. Not with language, but with the logic of adaptation that has always governed them: something is here that does not hunt, does not take, does not change the rules. So they continue. The hollow tower stands. The gliders resume their loops, now passing closer, sometimes brushing the rim with phosphor wings that leave faint trails. The builders maintain it not as monument but as part of the street-system, weaving paths around it, connecting it with root-bridges, living in and through it as they live everywhere else.

And in this, they have done everything, and nothing at all.

The chamber remains sealed. The water remains absent. The erosion remains finished. The vegetation drinks the air; the fauna moves in constant, loud, interlocked motion; the towers rise and are chewed away and are rebuilt elsewhere. Only now, at the center, there is this cup—open, hollow, holding the invisible breath—and in the minds of the quick-handed ones, in the navigation patterns of the gliders, in the slow opening of the rust-red blooms, there is a space that was not there before: not a change, but a recognition.

They do not reach for the crack. They do not ask for more.

They build, they move, they live in overwhelming density, and they hold, within their collective, ancient pulse, the knowledge that they are not forgotten. The power does not leave, but neither does it stay in any form that can be named. It is part of the warmth now, part of the mineral vapor, part of the satin walls that have stopped changing.

Outside, the flattened world remains barren, dry, silent.

Inside, the world holds its breath differently—still loud, still warm, still dry, still perfectly, irreversibly alive. And in the very center, where no space should exist, there is room: a hollow made of fiber and mud and patience, filled with nothing but the fact of being seen, and the ancient, settled, overwhelming will to continue.
