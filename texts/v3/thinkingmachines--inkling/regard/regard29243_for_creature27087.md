The power that watches this hollow—benevolent, wise, patient in ways that geology is not—would not come as thunder, or as a visitor with wings and instructions. It would come as the hollow itself learns another rule.

Consider first what they observe.

The settlement is young, but not innocent. Its people have achieved something remarkable: urban coherence without dominance, infrastructure without noise, a culture that understands itself as temporary within a space that breathes around them. They have organized paths inward rather than outward, built in rings that echo the hollow’s own geometry, and learned to move through overwhelming, silent life without disrupting its choreography. They harvest without slaughter, maintain without claiming, exist without shouting. In a world where sound is nearly dead—where even the fauna refuse acoustic presence—these scattered, sparse inhabitants have built a silence of their own, a parallel quiet to the ecosystem they inhabit.

The power notices this. Not with sentiment, but with precise recognition: here is a form of being that does not seek to overwhelm back.

So what would it do?

It would not grant them metal. The absence of metal in their walls is not a lack but a choice—the bone-lashed timber, the woven silica-fiber, the moss-mortar—these are technologies of integration, not extraction. To bring iron or steel into this hollow would be to import a sound, a song, a violence that the place has refused. The power, wise, understands that some gifts destroy the conditions they are meant to bless.

Instead, it would address the boundary conditions. The black glass rim—fresh, sharp, a wound in the crust—remains unresolved. The hollow was born suddenly, thrust upward, and it has not finished settling. The small tremors, the upward-defying water, the rock that still remembers heat: these are not dangers so much as questions. The settlement lives inside a room that has not decided its own dimensions.

The benevolent power—not a deity with demands, but an intelligence that favors coherence—would offer resolution not as conquest but as completion.

Perhaps it does this: the black glass rim, instead of being a wall that might open wider or close tighter, becomes transparent. Not broken—this is not a power that breaks what it touches—but clarified. The fresh wound in the earth’s crust, still remembering heat, cools not into dead stone but into a lens. The inhabitants can suddenly see—if they wish, if they dare—what exists beyond the hollow, not as escape (there is no outward in their logic) but as context. The world is larger. The hollow is not the only breath. This is knowledge given gently, without command, without demand that they leave or change. Only: you are not alone, and your boundedness is not a prison but a choice you have made, now made with full eyes.

Or perhaps the gift is more intimate, more embedded in their existing practice.

The water that moves upward in defiance, testing rules not yet written—this is a language. The power, listening in the same silence the inhabitants observe, might stabilize these loops, not by forcing them downward but by teaching them their own shape. The hidden channels beneath the sharp stone floor become readable, traceable, not as maps imposed from outside but as patterns the inhabitants can learn to read in the tremor of droplets, in the way the segmented worms orient themselves in the pools. The settlement’s urban logic—already sophisticated, already organized—gains a new layer: not just maintenance of structures, but participation in the hollow’s own unfinished geology. They become not guests alone, but interpreters.

And the fauna—the overwhelming, silent multitudes—might be touched not in number but in relation. The wingless moths, the translucent worms, the blind featherless birds, the too-many-jointed insects: they move in choreography that is already cooperative, parting and closing like water. The power might simply deepen this coherence, not controlling the creatures but aligning their silent rhythms with the settlement’s rhythms. Perhaps the people begin to understand—without words, in that culture of gesture and shared labor—when the moth-curtains will part, not by observation alone but by a kind of shared breath. The biomass remains overwhelming, remains thousands to one, remains silent. But the silence becomes shared rather than endured.

The light, too. The dimness is not a curse but a condition—phosphorescence in moss, blue-green seep in fresh fractures, the lantern-trees. If the power acts here, it does not bring the sun (the hollow’s walls are high, the grey cloud is a lid, and this is part of the place’s identity). Instead, it might allow the glow to speak more clearly. The vein-glowing leaves might respond to presence—not with sound, impossible, but with brightness, a subtle adjustment of illumination when the inhabitants pass near, turning the scattered lanterns into a kind of silent conversation. The settlement’s pathways, already narrow and intentional, become readable in half-tones not just by habit but by this responsive glow. They do not conquer the dark; they learn to read it together.

But the heart of the matter—the being who lives here, the place that has drawn this attention—is the settlement’s own form: urban, bounded, holding.

The power, wise, recognizes that they are not growing into a city, and should not. The hollow is hamlet-sized. The rings are complete. To push them toward expansion—toward the black glass, toward an outward that does not exist—would violate everything they have built. So if the power does anything lasting, it is this: it confirms their scale.

Not by command, but by gift.

Perhaps the bone-lashed timber, already strong, becomes subtly more responsive—growing (if it can grow) in patterns that match the circular logic of the rings, strengthening where tremors threaten, not through magic but through an alignment of living materials with the hollow’s settling breath. Perhaps the silica-fiber ropes, woven by hand, begin to hold a faint charge—not electricity in the destructive sense, but a resonance, allowing communication across the wide spaces where the sparse inhabitants live separated by rock and creature. A tug, a vibration in the fiber, a message sent without voice in a world where voice is unnecessary.

The people themselves—the scattered, sparse, temporary ones—would notice these changes not as miracles but as relief. The water loops make sense. The walls shift less, or shift with warning. The silent fauna move with them, not only around them. They remain primitive in technology, remain lean and angular and adapted to the thin air. They do not become kings. They do not build monuments.

Instead, they continue what they were already doing: maintaining the rings, harvesting quietly, moving through tides of insects with calm that deepens into certainty. They understand, perhaps for the first time with full clarity, that their temporary presence is valued—not despite its smallness, but because of it. They are a culture that has chosen coherence over conquest, silence over noise, boundedness over expansion.

The benevolent power, having observed this, does not stay. Or if it stays, it stays as part of the hollow’s own unfinished becoming—embedded in the cool blue-green glow, in the stable loops of water, in the transparent edge of the black glass that now shows them, gently, that they are part of a larger world but belong right here, right now, in this single room with walls, this cathedral hollow, this place that was born yesterday and is still deciding.

What they would do, if anything, is this: they would let the settlement remain a settlement. They would let the silence stay silent. They would complete the hollow’s birth not by filling it, but by confirming that what lives inside it—overwhelming in its creatures, sparse in its people, lush in its pockets, sharp in its stone—has already found the right form.

The inhabitants continue. The moths drape. The worms thread. The birds watch with skin. The water loops. The rock remembers heat, but now it remembers, too, that something wise has seen it, and found it good—not in its grandeur, but in its exact, small, silent completeness.
