They do not wake, because they never slept. At 2,600 meters, in air so thin it bites with perpetual shade, wakefulness is not an event but a condition—like the cool damp itself, like the diffuse overcast that never lifts to reveal a sun. What lives here settles, instead. The spires settle into their double-vision. The root-plains settle into their slow rearrangement. The engine, loud and distant, settles into the valley’s chest as a pulse that refuses to become a memory.

This is their first doing: **they maintain the sound.**

Not for communication—there is no other to hear it, except perhaps the worn mountains, those smooth primordial knuckles who have finished rounding themselves into tenderness and now listen, if they listen at all, with the patience of stone. The spires, forty meters of accreted cellulose and resin, do not speak in words. They speak in the low thrum of pumps never turned off, now encased in woody growth, vibrating through compressed root-matting like a heartbeat made public. They speak in the hiss of pressure released through ducts that are stem-tissue, carrying not steam now but the idea of steam, the metabolized ghost of electricity. They speak in the creak of gears—bone-white knuckles of dense root turning on axles of petrified sap—rotating slowly, perpetually, processing something that is no longer visible: nutrients, yes, but also time, also the lingering architecture of an industrial past that has been digested rather than abandoned. The sound is loud because the emptiness demands it. Across miles of archipelago, across the grey-green ocean of root-plain, the noise travels unimpeded, echoing back from the mountain walls. It fills the necessary breath between organisms. To stop sounding would be to admit that the valley has finished its transition into absolute age. So they continue. The sound is their refusal to become ruins.

Their second doing: **they exist in superposition, and they tend to both faces simultaneously.**

Each spire is an elder—standing, blooming with thick cool-green moss, and at the same moment hollow, eroded, near-flattened into the same ancient posture as the mountains that ring the valley. They do not choose between these conditions. They do not switch. A wall of hardened bark unfolds with the precise, mechanized rhythm of an old assembly line, then hangs in biological stasis, dripping condensation in a rhythm that matches no clock but the valley’s own intermittent weather. They grow downward into worn bedrock even as they reach upward, anchoring not from need but from remembrance—remembering steel, remembering steam, translating both into wood and pressure. To be a spire is to be a record written in cellulose, each layer an accretion of decision made so slowly it resembles fate. They do not build. They continue. Continuation is the architecture.

The hollow vines—the cathedrals—do the connecting. Their cavities, once carrying electricity or water or air, now carry condensation and echo. They are the nervous tissue, threading between islands across the flat compressed matting that was once pavement and is now skin. Where they knot, there form the gears. Where they split, there form channels—streets rewritten as flows, leading nowhere particular, connecting nothing quickly, because speed has been metabolized out of the system. The vines hiss. The hiss is not waste. It is speech without language, pressure made audible, the valley exhaling its internal weather. When the brief rain comes—for minutes, never more—the vines fill. Their sound changes pitch. This is the closest they come to ceremony: the rain arrives, they drink through moss and hollow stem, the gears turn a fraction faster, then the rain stops, and they enter the long condensation, the waiting that is not passive but dense with presence. They are always processing the moisture that never becomes abundance, always translating scarcity into structure.

The root-plain itself is the body they share. It is soil and pavement, membrane and ground, shifting subtly over years in dreams that take decades. It reclaims flat spaces not aggressively but inevitably, like a thought returning. You stand upon it, and you are permitted—because the emptiness between structures is not dead space but architecture, the necessary breath that allows the loud sound to travel, that allows the spires to be islands and not a forest. The root-plain thinks, if thinking is the slow rearrangement of channels, the opening and closing of flows like synapses firing in geological time. It remembers the urban plan—not as a map, but as a pressure pattern, a distribution of weight and absence.

Then there is the piston—made not of metal but of petrified sap—pulsing in a hollow stalk somewhere in the archipelago. It tends to nothing visible. Perhaps it maintains the cycle. Perhaps it is the cycle aware that it continues. Around it, a leaf unfolds with industrial precision, then hangs, existing in superposition with its own decay—green and grey, present and already part of the root-matting’s future compost. The rare beetle moves inside, carrying something unreadable—spore, sound-fragment, a residue of steel-memory—from one spire to another. It is the only mobile fauna, and even it moves not as predator or prey but as messenger in a system that has banned quickness except for this one permitted exception. The beetle lives briefly, dies inside a hollow stalk, and is translated—like everything here—into substrate.

But the central doing, the action that underlies all others, belongs to the engine in the distance.

It runs. Always. Its sound is carried through a pipe that is also a vine, vibrating through root-knuckles that are also gears. It does not drive wheels or lift water or generate light—those functions were digested long ago, translated into biological rhythms that need no electricity. It runs because without it, the valley might complete its settlement into the mountains’ posture—might become fully flat, fully old, fully finished. The engine is the heartbeat that holds the place in its oldest-stable-form, which is not dead but perpetually, strangely alive. It may be operated by no one, or it may be that the valley itself has become the operator—distributed across spires and vines, present in every hissing duct and turning knuckle, loud and clear and impossible, running not out of necessity but out of an intuition older than purpose: that to stop is to forget, and to forget is to finally become the mountain.

So what do they do?

They listen—to the echo, to the hiss, to the brief rain.

They translate—industry into moss, electricity into pressure, steel into sap, citizens into substrate.

They hold—holding themselves in contradiction, blooming and hollow, growing downward and upward, being machine and plant and neither.

They wait—not for an end, but for the next minute of rain, the next rotation of the root-gear, the next passage of the beetle.

They remember—by continuing. By never turning off. By filling the emptiness with sound so that the valley knows it is still a city, still a settlement, still a being that has not finished becoming itself.

And in the cool, biting air, under the diffuse light that reveals everything clearly while guaranteeing nothing can be fully understood, they do the final, beautiful thing: **they remain strange.** They do not resolve into one condition. They do not choose between reality and its breakdown. They are the spires seen blooming and eroding at once. They are the root-plains that are ground and skin. They are the loud, pervasive sound that travels for miles through pipes that are vines, saying nothing and everything—saying that the valley is old enough to have forgotten its birth, but not so old that it has forgotten how to live in its own impossible form.
