What they do begins before consciousness, in the rock’s exhalation. The being—small, darkened, built for vertical patience—wakes not to a sun but to a change in pressure, a deep thermal breath rising through the basalt floor. The warmth is body-close, constant, not given as gift but as geology. They do not greet it. They orient themselves by it, feeling which wall is breathing more strongly, which path will be easier on the joints that have never known flat ground.

Then they move. Not quickly. The rubbery mats—burnt copper, blackened bronze, that violet so dark it denies color—hang in layers, heavy as wet leather though they contain no moisture. To pass through is an act of negotiation. The being parts them with palms callused from rope and stone, feeling the waxy resistance, the slow drag. They do not cut unless they must. They guide: tying new growth into the fiber ladders, repairing the honeycomb cells where the vegetation has grown too thick, weaving fallen black fur into patches. This is not gardening. It is conversation in slow motion, measured in centimeters per year.

Sound comes next, inevitable. The dark-feathered birds—small, quick, never beautiful, only present—begin their stitching. The being listens not for meaning but for location, for the echo that reveals which ledge holds the nest, which rafter needs reinforcement. Their own voice is rarely raised. Speech here is low, infrequent, shaped by smoke that hangs rather than rises. They navigate by the birds’ repetition, by the click of hard-shelled insects in visible streams across the warm stone.

They gather. Not with nets flung in urgency, but with carved bone and patient hands. The insects feed on fallen matter; the being harvests them, and in turn offers crushed shells and dropped fiber back to the mats, maintaining the chemical exchange that replaces thirst. There is no drinking. No ritual of water. Only this: taking in moisture from the plants, from the insects, from the air itself—exchange without urgency, feeding without possession.

In the lower paths, they sit. The slow-moving creatures—heavy-bodied, short black hair, neither wild nor tame—come not because they are called, but because the space is shared. The being sits beside one for an hour, both warming in the geothermal breath, neither needing the other, both present. Sometimes the creature’s fur is woven into a garment; sometimes it is left to fall and feed the insects. There is no hierarchy of use.

The fire is tended last, or always. Compressed plant matter, mineral oil, burning with a low, patient flame. The smoke stains them generation by generation, darkening skin that never saw direct sun. They do not clear the smoke. They live inside it, breathing the chemical richness, letting it hang in slow currents that never resolve. The clay vessels are fired, the metal pins shaped from native ore—nothing quick, nothing made for distance, everything for this compressed vertical world.

If they have a center, it is not a temple but a point of stillness in the floor’s middle, where the echo is densest and the vegetation parts just enough to see the gray coin of sky. They stand there alone sometimes, feeling the finished work of ancient time—the erosion complete, no fractures fresh, no change impending—and understand that they are not building a future. The stone has settled. Their work is attendance. To maintain the weave. To listen. To exchange warmth with the rock that breathes beneath them.

Then, the attention.

Not a sound—there is already too much sound—but a shift in the quality of the warmth, as if the geothermal breath has become aware of being observed. The benevolent and wise power does not arrive with form. It recognizes the Hollow from above, through that narrow opening, seeing what has drawn it: not suffering, not need, not chaos requiring order, but completion. A world that has reached its final shape and continues, without water, without sun, without progress, in lush, dry abundance.

The being in the center feels it—not as fear, for there is nothing to fear in a finished world—but as a deepening of the silence between birdcalls. The power observes the vertical grove, the woven settlement, the exchange between plant, insect, creature, and being. It sees that nothing is lacking. The absence of water is not a wound but a condition that shaped this thriving. The darkness is not a prison but a cathedral’s interior. The slowness is not stagnation but alignment.

What would such a power do?

If anything, it does this: it adds nothing. No water falls—such a gift would drown the chemical balance, dissolve the rubbery mats, destroy the fire’s chemistry, end the exchange. No light is granted—the beings’ eyes are built for twilight, and sudden illumination would blind rather than bless. No speed is introduced, no technology accelerated, no path made easier.

Instead, if it acts at all, it confirms the warmth. Not stronger—too much heat would crack the finished stone, disturb the patient erosion—but steadier, more patient, a recognition made thermal. Perhaps it sends a single pulse of understanding through the rock’s breath, not changing the temperature but deepening its meaning. Or perhaps it does nothing visible, only remains present, a witness that does not demand worship or response.

The being, feeling this presence or absence of intervention, continues. They part the vegetation. They sit with the slow creature. They tend the fire that hangs its smoke in low, eternal currents. They understand, without language capable of expressing it, that they have been seen—not as a problem to be solved, but as a solution already achieved.

The wise power, for its part, learns. If it is benevolent, it practices the highest benevolence: restraint. It leaves the Hollow exactly as it is—lush, loud, warm, dry, finished. It takes away no being, transforms no vegetation, corrects no primitive tool. It only holds in its awareness the image of this vertical world, this settlement woven into living darkness, as proof that life does not require water, light, or change to be complete. That sometimes the most profound act of wisdom is to recognize when the stone has finished settling, and to let it be.

And so they continue together—the being and the presence—without touching, without altering. The birds call. The insects click across warm basalt. The mats grow in centimeters. The smoke hangs. The rock breathes. Nothing is done, and everything is maintained.
