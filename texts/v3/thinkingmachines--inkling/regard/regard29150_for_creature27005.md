They do not wake. They arrive—slowly, as condensation arrives, as the shadow of the gear shifts from one planting bed to the next. There is no urgency in their becoming. The being who lives here has no single form that can be separated from the substrate; they are distributed like the humidity, present in the moss-filled cracks of old gear-housings and in the hollow spaces of ventilation towers where the birds nest. But there is a center, a locus of attention: a figure, or a presence, moving through the lattice-tunnels at the pace of root growth, wearing the weathered warmth of the valley in their skin like the thermal cladding on the pylons.

What they do is attend.

They begin—if one can call it beginning—by placing both palms flat against the oxidized copper lattice of a retaining wall. Not to check for damage. The erosion here is established, not violent; nothing breaks suddenly. They touch the surface to confirm continuity, to feel the low-grade thermal exhalation rising through the ceramic composite plates, to remind themselves that they are part of the breath. The vines respond to this presence not with speed but with inclination: a tendril, thick and broad-leafed, loosens its grip on a porous wall for a moment, then re-anchors closer to the warmth of the hand. This is conversation. This is work.

They walk the riverine pockets—those narrow channels of captured runoff lined with algae-resistant tile—carrying nothing, fixing nothing. The water moves slowly, intermittently, and when they find a shallow trough where condensation has pooled too deeply, they do not drain it. They drink from it, or they let it remain, knowing the moisture network requires imbalance as much as flow. The insects drawn to the warmth gather on their shoulders, on the weathered edges of their clothing (which is not clothing but accumulated fabric: moss, vine fragments, the smooth abrasion of centuries of wind). They do not brush the insects away. They are part of the fauna now, or the fauna is part of them.

Midway through the long bright arc—when the light is intense, high-altitude, unfiltered except where the second-layer canopy of trees filters it to dappled gold—they sit in a circular aperture where a tree grows. Here, they watch. This is the central act of their existence. They watch the massive horizontal gears at the valley’s center turn so gradually that motion is imperceptible except as a slow migration of shadow across the planting beds. They watch for hours. Not out of duty. The machinery does not require witnesses to turn; it turns on seasonal schedules, on the long process of transformation that outlasts individual lives. They watch because watching is the form of love appropriate to this place. To observe without urgency is to honor the dynamic that rules here: slow, settled, enduring.

They hear everything. The loud, persistent industrial drone fills the valley like weather—the grinding of bearings, the rhythmic release of steam from valves opening and closing not on human hours but on the schedule of months. The sound is not noise to them; it is respiration. They learn the season by the pitch. When the bearings shift a half-tone lower, they know it is time to move to the upper decking, to check—no, to attend—the planting wells that have become mechanical access points for root and mammal alike. The small animals move through the lower lattice-tunnels, and the being does not block their path. The corridors never close; they were built with flow-routes, with niches, with the understanding that occupancy requires passage. The being steps aside, not in deference but in recognition: they are another body using the corridor, not its owner.

They eat rarely, if at all. Sustenance comes from the synthetic ecology itself: the humid green scent of the grasses, the warm air exhaled from the substrate, the algae that grows in the captured mist channels. They sleep in the hollow ventilation towers, cradled by the constant drone, waking not to an alarm but to a change in temperature, a shift in the condensation pattern on metal surfaces.

Sometimes they speak, though rarely, and never loudly. Their voice is low, blending with the mechanical hum, addressing no one and everyone: the vines, the gears, the birds nesting in hollow spaces, the slow-moving water in ceramic-lined troughs. They say things like *the shadow has moved* or *the moisture is high today* or *the bearings are singing winter*. These are not reports. They are offerings—marks that a consciousness is present within the system, dissolving the last boundary between observer and observed.

What they do, if anything, is maintain the dissolution. They are the proof that the distinction between the living and the built has not been lost but completed. They do not build, for everything is already built. They do not destroy, for erosion here is settled. They do not rush, for nothing rushes. They tend—not as a gardener tends a garden, not as an engineer tends a machine, but as the being who lives in it tends itself: by existing in perfect, patient alignment with the warmth, the brightness, the green saturation, the loud soft drone of a valley that breathes.

And when the occasional rain falls through the lattice-work above, collecting in shallow troughs, beading on vertical surfaces, they stand in it—not to seek shelter, for there is no need, but to feel the intermittent presence of water as it was meant to be felt here: not as a force that carves, but as a gift that seeps, that belongs, that returns to the channels, that keeps the vines thick and the moss filled and the gears turning slowly, slowly, into the next long duration.

They are not the ruler of this place. They are its answer. When asked—if anything is ever asked—whether anyone lives here, they are the response: a being made of copper warmth and root purchase and slow observation, walking through the interwoven construction, belonging to the density, breathing with the exhalation, turning with the gears, enduring.
