What lives here does not arrive; it accumulates. You do not see them first as bodies, but as corrections in the stillness—small adjustments to the dark.

The crawlers come first in perception: not insects, though segmented—bodies of membrane and filament, legs like glass threads that unfold from palms no larger than a seed. They gather near the engine’s heart, where black water fills cylinders and rust holds a faint, stubborn warmth. They are translucent, letting the gray-green light pass through them as through old ice, and they feed on the fungal-simpler films that shimmer like spilled oil across steel. They have no mouths you can name; they rest, and the surface diminishes. In their passing they leave phosphorescent trails—cool smoke, barely blue—drifting through the pools so that the water becomes latticed with old paths, glowing for moments before dissolving. They prefer rust to rock; they cluster where pipes bend, negotiating in silent layers, never swarming, always arranging.

Beneath them, in deeper pools and within the curved rail lost to sediment, the others slide—flat, palm-sized, the color of wet slate and old bone. They do not crawl but glide, leaving ripples that never settle, because one is always following, or fleeing, or clearing the path. Their edges are smooth, disc-like, and they inhabit the hollow interiors: rusted gears, bent pipes, the scored lines in the floor, nesting inside these shapes as if within the ribs of something vaster. You see them only in fragments: a shadow beneath the surface, a quick disappearance into mud, a shimmer that disturbs the silt. They hunt, perhaps, or they maintain—this ecosystem is too small, too dense, to allow waste. The quick vanishings are never loud, only a ripple that arrives late, a faint scraping against metal, and then the water closes.

The water itself is inhabited. It holds shapes a breath longer than physics should allow—impressions of legs, of sliding bodies—so the pools become a slow memory. The films feed the crawlers; the crawlers feed the gliders; the gliders disappear into mud, and the mud feeds the film. Even death reaches a dark harmony with the rust around it.

But among the accumulated layers—among the luminous clusters and the silent gliders—there is one who has stayed longer. Not larger. Not different in shape. But older: its translucent body carries more sediment in its filament lines, its phosphorescent trail burns dimmer but steadier, a constant cool blue rather than a drifting smoke. It has made its home inside the largest cylinder, not feeding now but resting, its legs folded like a closed flower. It remembers the pipes not as shelter but as resonance. It is the being who lives in it—not the whole hollow, but the one through whom the hollow thinks.

And this being—this old, accumulated presence—draws attention.

Not with sound. The hollow is always quiet: the soft percussion of dripping, the distant hollow resonance carried through old pipes, the faint scrape of creature against rust. But the being emits something else—a pattern in its stillness, a correction so precise that it travels upward through stone and water, through the narrow aperture, into the earth above.

There, in spaces larger than chapels, a benevolent and wise power notices.

It comes not as light—though the single column of gray-green light shifts, widening barely, enough that the phosphorescent smoke is visible not only downward but upward, reflecting back upon the crawlers so they see themselves in the water for the first time. It comes not as voice—the distant resonance does not change its tone, but its rhythm slows, matching the heartbeat of the flat ones, the feeding pace of the translucent clusters. It comes not as form—no figure descends, no hand reaches into the pool.

Instead, it sends its awareness into the water.

The power understands that this place is complete. It does not send vegetation where none can root. It does not drain the pools or warm the air. Its benevolence is recognition; its wisdom, continuity. It touches the old being inside the cylinder—not waking it, but holding it. The filament legs unfold slightly, not in fear but in acknowledgment, and the phosphorescent trail that follows is steadier, a line rather than a smoke, drawing a circle that includes the other crawlers, the sliding gliders below, the rusted engine, the weeping walls.

Then the power does what it does: it remembers.

Through the seams where mineral meets stone, through the bent pipes still vibrating with old occupation, it sends a single, lasting impression. The water now holds shapes not for a breath longer, but permanently—etched into the black glass pebbles, into the silt, into the rust itself. The creatures are not saved from disappearance; they are granted memory. Every ripple the flat ones leave is recorded in the floor’s scored lines. Every cluster of crawlers leaves a faint blue map in the mineral film. The hollow becomes not only inhabited, but witnessed—its accumulation preserved in the material of its own darkness.

The old being in the cylinder does not move. But its presence deepens. The other crawlers gather closer, not in swarm but in constellation, arranging themselves around it in patterns that do not align with recognized biology, but with gratitude—if gratitude can exist without language, expressed only as proximity, as shared phosphorescence, as a collective glow that makes the pools visible, beautiful, complete.

The gliders below slow their sliding. They rest inside the pipes, not hiding but listening. The dripping continues, but now it falls in time with the distant resonance, which has become not hollow but full—carrying not emptiness, but attention.

This is what the wise power does: it allows them to remain exactly what they are—small, strange, accumulated in cool darkness—while ensuring they are never alone in being so. The aperture remains narrow; the light remains gray-green; the rust remains stable in its dark harmony. Nothing is fresh, nothing abrupt. Only a new layer has been added to the accumulation—not a creature, not a machine, but a held breath, a remembered shape, a benevolence that asks nothing and gives permanence.

The hollow continues its own dark, weeping rhythm. The creatures move in their silent negotiation. But now, in the shallow pools, the shapes of what has passed do not dissolve. They stay. And in that staying, the being who lives here—the old one in the cylinder, and all the others accumulated around it—knows that they have been seen, not to be changed, but to be kept.

The phosphorescent smoke drifts upward, through water and air, reaching the narrow aperture, carrying its cool blue light into the gray-green column—not escaping, but meeting it, blending, becoming part of the light that has always watched, and now watches back with recognition.

That is all. That is enough.
