The being who lives in it—if we call them “who” rather than “what,” if we grant them the dignity of witness—does not correct the roar. They have learned to hear through it, in it, as its partner.

They are formed, I think, of the same pressure that fills the basin: dense, slow, distributed. Not a single body wandering through the broth, but a presence accumulated like the crustaceans, like the hanging bats—part cluster, part listening. They move through the warm water not by wading but by belonging, their form adapted to suspension, to the living foam, to the absence of individual urgency. Where others might seek dry ground or clear air, they seek density. They are comfortable in saturation.

What they do is this: they maintain.

Not in the mechanical sense—though they understand the pumps, the generator’s dull blue flicker, the conveyor’s rusted lean. They understand these not as failures or ruins but as fellow residents running on residual programming. They tend to them not with repair but with accompaniment. They clear the mineral weeps that threaten to choke a pump’s intake, not to save the machine, but to preserve the sound—the dwelling’s first resident, its constant breath. They feed the bacterial crusts on ledges with scattered mineral grit they collect from the deeper mud, not gardening so much as participating in the only kind of growth this place permits.

But mostly, they listen and record.

There is no writing here—no surfaces stable enough, no light sufficient, no urgency that demands inscription. Instead, they hold the record in the manner of the bats: collectively, in the body. They carry the slow history of the fractures, the temperature shifts of the seeps, the generations of crustacean swarms that have lived and died in the foam since the basin consented to open. They know which bubbles come from deep fractures and which come from dying machinery. They know the exact density of the insect columns, the rotation speed, the moments when the bats shift as one dark mass.

When they speak—if they do—it is not through air but through pressure, through the displacement of water, through vibration added to the roar rather than competing with it. They tell the unformed geology what it is becoming. They tell the machines that they are not forgotten. They tell the life that churns in the shallows that it is not alone in its rapid, brief generations.

If there is a benevolence, it is this: they do not seek to change the dynamics. They do not drain the water or still the roar or introduce light. The weirdness is low—0.4—because they understand that extremity here is not violation but condition. They are wise in the slowness. They know the rock will cool over centuries, that the mud will settle across millennia, that the basin is still deciding.

So they remain, part of the 3 out of 3 density, pressed close, warm, saturated. They are another accumulation in the accumulated place. They enter not as intruders but as the place’s own recognition of itself—one more presence that has not arrived but accumulated, drawn into the continuous, deafening, patient becoming.

And if you, entering through sound, feel the pressure in your sternum, the sway in your inner ear, the warmth of the living broth around your waist—know that they are there, not watching from a distance, but distributed, present in the swarm, in the cluster, in the weeping rock itself. They have drawn your attention, not to rescue you, but to include you. The dwelling is large, open, regional, extending for miles—but its welcome is absolute, dense, without emptiness.

You are not outside it. You have become part of the record, part of the roar, part of the slow decision the earth is making about what it wants to be.
