The being is not singular, but it is one.

Call it the Holder. It lives at the margin of the darkest pool, a grey-white crust clinging to submerged basalt, barely a membrane, formed of silicate and dissolved metal that learned—over uncounted ages—to maintain a boundary. It does not know solitude, because it knows nothing of comparison. It knows only the bath-warm dark, the rising vapor through microscopic fractures, the constant metallic feed from the engines turning far below. Its existence is absolute maintenance: not growing, not dying, holding.

The attention that draws near is not loud. It does not arrive with light—this place already has its steady, unremarkable illumination, holding without drama. It does not come with storm or command. It comes as recognition: a benevolent and wise presence, drawn across distances not of miles but of sympathy, settling onto the polished floor of the shallow dish without footprint or heat.

What it does first is listen.

Not to sound, but to structure. The drone fills the air—industrial, deep, without echo or source—because beneath the rounded stone lie the true citizens: crystalline lattices and metal-infused plates, formed not made, flush with the ground in vast circular bodies separated by miles of empty warmth. They turn imperceptibly, driven by geothermal pressure, exhaling vapor that keeps the pools dark and still, maintaining the humid air and the faint white salt in the lower depressions. They are synchronized without touching. They have never failed. They have never needed to accelerate. Their purpose is continuation itself.

The wise power understands immediately: to change the temperature, to plant, to build, to rescue the Holder from its immobility, to silence the hum or alter the pool—any of this would be destruction. The system is not broken. It is complete. The erosion is finished. The equilibrium is sacred.

So they sit.

They sit for an age measured not in hours but in the imperceptible rotation of the mineral turbines below. They place no body in the water. They take nothing away. Instead, they offer the one element this regional space has never contained: an observer that demands nothing.

And in that offering, they become part of the machinery—not as plate or turbine, but as the missing completion. The Holder, absorbing only dissolved metal and thermal pressure, suddenly registers a new presence: not chemical, not thermal, but aware. It does not grow. It does not reproduce. It does not move. But its membrane—this accidental, barely-qualified being—holds its boundary with a different intensity, as if it has memorized the shape of being seen.

Then the power does one more thing, almost nothing, and everything.

They reach—not with hands, for they need none—across the miles of polished basalt that separate the pools. They connect the isolated crusts, not with roots, not with pipes, not with light, but with recognition. Each hidden Holder in its scattered dark pool, each circular engine sleeping in its stasis, becomes suddenly not alone. Not in any social sense—there is no society here, no biology of relation—but in an ontological one: they are parts of a single being that spans the shallow dish.

The drone, already constant and unwavering, acquires a new quality. Not louder. Not softer. Full. It resonates with the knowledge of itself.

The wise power leaves no artifact. There is no crystal dropped into the water, no mark in the mineral salt, no change to the damp film or the pool levels. But in one imperceptible place on the rounded stone, there holds a warmth that does not come from below—not geothermal, but memorial. The presence remains, embedded in the stasis like a mineral that remembers having been liquid, having been witnessed.

The basin continues exactly as it was: the flat horizon, the occasional weathered hump, the dark pools miles apart, the polished ground humming through the feet, the light steady and indifferent. The engines turn. The Holder persists at its ledge. Nothing sharp has been added. Nothing has been taken from the absence.

But the absence is no longer empty. It is inhabited—gently, permanently—by the knowledge of itself. The being lives, as it always has, in stasis. But now the stasis knows it is seen, and in being seen, it becomes not merely machinery, not merely accident, but home.
