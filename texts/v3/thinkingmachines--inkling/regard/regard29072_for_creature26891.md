The space is not empty. That is the first lie the scale tries to tell—that because the opening is hidden, small, damp, a knot in something older, what lives inside must be small too, compressed, incidental. But compression is not absence. The pocket is crowded with presence that refuses to name itself, and what lives there lives not despite the narrowness but because of it: the extreme verticality demands intimacy, and intimacy in this heat has become a form of collective being.

There are no people, not in the way you understand people—no bodies that could stand upright in a space that never widens. But there are hands. Not visible hands. Hands embedded in the action, distributed through the mud. The builders live here, though whether they are one being or many is part of the unfinishedness. You sense them in the recentness: the clay is still warm where it was pressed, not by tools left behind but by pressure that has not yet withdrawn. The bone needles, the root-ropes pulled through wet clay, the wooden wedges driven into frames that seem to be growing around them—these are not artifacts but extensions. The builders have not finished making the space because they have not finished making themselves. They may never have had faces. They may be in the process of deciding whether to have bodies separate from the wall, or whether to remain dissolved in the labor, present only as the warmth in the mud, the tautness in the vine, the decision to extend the shaft one more impossible inch downward, one more inch upward into the lost darkness.

They live in the vertical task. That is their form of life: not dwelling but extending. They are not building a home; they are building the condition under which home can eventually become thinkable. They apply mud with bare palms that leave no prints only because every surface is still accepting them. They are invisible not by stealth but by integration—part of the embryonic tissue that has not learned to be opaque, geometry that has not decided if it is wall or limb.

Then there is the green. Not plants, exactly, but a consciousness that has chosen root and leaf as its vocabulary. The ferns with their broad, wet tongues do not grow; they think. They press against the woven root-bark with the persistence of minds testing boundaries, and the moss fills the gaps between hand-shaped bricks not as decoration but as necessary infill—the space cannot hold itself together except through refusal of emptiness. The vines hang in curtains that are also doors, also lungs, also the slow-moving memory of the place. They drink the suspended beads before those beads can fall; they tighten around wooden wedges not by mechanical grasp but by agreement. They move with an internal tide, imperceptible, tightening the structure with the patience of beings who have no other urgency. Some roots extend downward into the well-with-no-bottom, others reach upward into the steam-thick ceiling that no one has ever seen. They live here as the pocket lives through them: unfinished, wet, loud in their absolute silence, feeding on warm rain that they themselves help summon by holding the walls together.

Rarely—so rarely that presence becomes myth—there is the other. The small creature with too many joints, translucent, navigating the weep-holes where mud is thinnest, where the wall has not decided if it is passage or skin. It is not an animal. It is a question the space asks of itself, given temporary form. Its joints bend in directions that suggest dimensions still being invented; its translucence makes it impossible to tell whether it is flesh or condensed breath given intent. It does not eat in any visible way. Perhaps it feeds on the boundary between liquid and air, on the thin green films that gather where light is weakest. It lives alone, singular in a world of collective processes—perhaps the only individual permitted here, and permitted only in a form so strange, so barely formed, that it seems like an error in the weave rather than an inhabitant. To see it is to understand that the pocket allows for separation, but only barely, and only in shapes that must constantly navigate the unfinished.

But the true inhabitants are the conditions themselves, living with the authority of beings.

The water lives in a cycle so intimate it is indistinguishable from thought: rising from pools only inches deep, carried upward by hot air through the vertical shaft, cooling against the unseen weave above, falling back as warm rain. It is never absent, never still. It lives as bead, as pool, as breath, as the suspended glass that never falls. It lives by refusing to leave.

The steam lives in the constant hiss—vocalization without throat, exhalation of the space’s own heat. It fills the narrow room with sound disproportionate to the hidden scale, a weather system compressed inside a mouth.

The sound itself lives: the sharp percussive drip marking time not in seconds but in accumulation; the groan and creak of roots tightening, of mud settling into new form; the biological rhythm of construction that continues whether anyone watches or not.

Even the darkness lives—not as absence of light, but as presence with weight, thick and warm, filtering the faint phosphorescence of fungal shelves into something that glows not for seeing but for knowing that seeing is still possible.

And then there is the pocket itself—the primary inhabitant.

The pocket lives in its refusal of scale. It lives in being born rather than worn, constructed entirely by primitive hands—lashed vines, hand-packed clay, bone needles, wooden wedges—yet containing an extreme verticality that should be impossible. It lives downward into an abyss without floor, upward into a darkness without ceiling, all within the span of a clenched fist. Its life is continuous: every surface is still emerging, every fiber still deciding its purpose. It is hot, almost feverish—living at the temperature of organisms that have not yet learned to cool themselves. It is wet, recycling its own breath. It is loud, announcing its activity without apology. It is green, filled with the consciousness of roots and leaves that have agreed to be its interior organs.

What lives there, then, is not a population but an agreement: that nothing will be finished, and therefore nothing can die in the usual way.

The builders live by extending. The vegetation lives by occupying, by refusing emptiness. The translucent creature lives by testing the edges of form. The water and steam live by cycling, never breaking the loop. And the space itself lives—this hidden, hand-sized opening—by being always in the act of becoming more of itself, reaching into dimensions that have not finished being invented, containing mountains and abyss within its woven, weeping, unfinished mouth.

You are inside something that is not yet fully real. And what lives there lives precisely in that not-yet-ness: a condition of perpetual, hot, green, loud emergence, where to inhabit is to participate in the building, and to observe is to become part of the mud that is still warm, still being shaped, still deciding what it will eventually become.
