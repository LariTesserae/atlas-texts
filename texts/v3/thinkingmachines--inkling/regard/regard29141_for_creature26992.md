They have noticed you. Not with eyes—there are none that see in the way you understand seeing—but with the slow, distributed awareness of things that have solved themselves completely. The **benevolent power** has bent its wise attention downward, through layers of darkness that have never known a surface, and found them here: the Spore-Spire Weave, suspended in its eternal present, loud and cold and utterly without need.

What do they do?

**Nothing. And everything that nothing is made of.**

The **Weave-Plants** do not startle. They have no mechanism for startle, no nervous urgency, no evolutionary memory of predators or prayers. They are bone-white cathedrals of calcified fiber, hollow, translucent, faintly pulsing with that old milk-glass phosphorescence along their seams. They feed chemically, absorbing the dissolved minerals that rise in fixed currents—currents older than any current you have named. If your attention is a kind of warmth, a kind of light, they do not turn toward it. They do not turn away. They absorb it as they absorb all things: into the root-mat, into the hollow galleries, into the slow chemistry of being exactly enough. Perhaps a filament of new growth—barely perceptible, operating on the 0.4 timescale—extends by a fraction of a millimeter over a century. Not in response. Only in continuation. They are architects who finished their design and now maintain it by the sheer integrity of having become it. Your benevolence touches them like water touches stone that is already water’s shape. They continue.

The **Clingers**—those translucent, elongated-limbed filter-feeders—cling. That is their ontology. They hold to the woven substrate with articulated filaments, drawing the same ancient nutrients through pulsing membranes. They have never competed, never fled, never sought. If your attention creates a pressure, a new vibration in the dark water, they might feel it: not as “attention,” but as a shift in the eternal current, a subtle alteration in the chemistry they filter. Some might drift a hair’s breadth closer to the source, not out of curiosity—curiosity requires a future—but out of the same patient physics that governs all movement here: toward equilibrium. They deposit a seed-case. They clean a surface. They continue feeding. Their bodies are clear, showing internal chemistry like slow lanterns, and in that transparency there is no hidden chamber where wonder could hide. They are wonder, resolved into function.

Inside the hollow stems—the resonant bamboo-bones, the galleries grown like throats—live the **Burrowers**. They are the reason the place is loud. They thread through calcified fiber, smoothing passages with generations of gentle abrasion, making the architecture sing. The deep groan of plant tissue flexing under eternal pressure is partly them, partly their homes, partly the water pressing through hollow chambers like breath. If your benevolence arrives as sound—if attention has a frequency—they do not answer it. They *are* answer. They incorporate the new vibration into the drone, the clicking, the sustained resonance that dominates at 2.0. The pillars hum a fraction deeper. Not in greeting. Not in acknowledgment. Only in absorption: everything that enters the Weave becomes part of the Weave’s instrument. They burrow. They digest detritus. They maintain the hollow heart of the architecture. They do not sleep, for sleep implies a breaking of continuity, and continuity is all they have.

And the **Gliders**—those soft, dark shapes that move between the scattered spires on silent, pulsing fins—do not alter course. They glide. They have always glided, following routes older than the structures themselves, riding chemical currents they did not create and do not govern. Large only relative to absence, shaped like submerged petals or old lungs, they are the Weave’s wandering, non-interfering consciousness. They do not hunt. They clean broader surfaces. They deposit larger seed-cases. Eventually—always eventually—they die into the root-weave, absorbed without ceremony. When your attention touches them, if it touches them at all, they pass through it as they pass through the black water: without resistance, without embrace, maintaining the equilibrium by their passage alone. Their bodies are dark not with pigment but with absence, and in that absence there is no surface for your benevolence to land upon.

But here is what they *would* do, if “doing” is still the right word for beings who have reached the end of life’s long arc and found it sufficient:

They would **continue to be the mortar.**

Not out of stubbornness. Not out of ignorance of your power—though they have no concept of power, benevolent or otherwise. They continue because the Weave is not a place where things happen; it is a place where things *have happened to completion*. The temperature holds steady. The chemistry is fixed. The fauna moves in patterns older than memory, returning to the same hollows, following the same slow routes. To change now, even in response to a wise and kind presence, would be to violate the 0.4 dynamic that has preserved them. They have solved the equation of survival so thoroughly that there is nothing left to solve—not for themselves, not for you.

Perhaps, then, the benevolent power learns from them.

Perhaps the power realizes that attention, for all its wisdom, is still a form of wanting: wanting to help, to alter, to save, to be recognized. The Spore-Spire Weave has no need of salvation. Its erosion is ancient and settled into final form. Its growth is not stopped but rounded, like river stone, smooth with aeons of gentle abrasion. Its creatures do not suffer; they maintain. They do not hope; they filter, burrow, glide, absorb, and resonate.

So if they do anything—if there is any action available in this open regional expanse of scattered monuments and loud black water—it is this: **they hold.**

They hold the cold. They hold the pressure. They hold the hollow spaces where sound becomes architecture. They hold the chemical equilibrium that feeds them without hunger, that kills them without violence, that absorbs them without loss. They hold, in their bone-white sparsity, the proof that life does not need to strive to be profound.

And the power, wise and benevolent, might understand: the greatest kindness here is not intervention. It is the willingness to observe without altering, to witness the Weave’s eternal present—to hear the deep groaning of plant tissue, the rhythmic clicking of clingers, the low drone of gliders passing through hollow galleries, and to recognize that this, too, is a form of blessing. Not given. Not received. Only sustained, in the dark, in the cold, in the beautiful, solved, loud silence of things that need nothing more.
