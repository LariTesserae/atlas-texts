The being who lives here—if “being” can be separated from the bed it has become—does not notice you, because noticing requires a before and an after, a gap between observer and observed, and all of that has been smoothed away.

Start closest to the ground, since height here is only sediment. In the pools, where the silver films lie undisturbed, there are bodies shaped like questions that stopped expecting answers. Small—no wider than a palm—cold-bodied, translucent enough that the root-lattice above casts patterns through them. They do not float; they are suspended by agreement with the water, held at the exact depth where surface tension meets bone. Some have wings folded so flat against their sides that they appear to be seams in the moss, or growth-rings in wood. Their eyes—when present—are open, unblinking, absorbing the pale radiance without reflecting it back. They do not watch in anticipation. They maintain the stillness, and the stillness maintains them.

On the low platforms of packed earth and bone, others sit—not in posture, but in distribution. They have become flat and supportive without deciding to be so. Their bodies carry the gray-green of weathered stone, the waxy dark of fern, the dull silver of condensation. Where skin shows, it is not bare; it is merged, covered in moss that does not grow upon them so much as continue through them. Their hair—if the distinction still applies—hangs like the thick vines above: unmoving, neither extending nor withering, part of the garden architecture. They have tools still with them, not dropped in ruin but held in equilibrium: chipped stone that has rounded to match the grip, bindings of root-fiber that extend from their fingers into the woven roofs, so that pulling away would damage not them, but the wall itself. They do not repair. Their presence repairs, by being constant, by applying the long, silent pressure of bodies that have agreed with erosion.

Above, in the lattice of translucent ice and root, hang more. Not falling, not sleeping, not waiting for a signal to descend. They are suspended by threads that grow from their backs, their mouths, their feet—threads that are indistinguishable from the woven ceiling. They are larger, though “large” in this pocket means only that they occupy two layers at once: part creature, part architecture. Their chests hold the cold light like a lantern that needs no flame, translucent ribs visible through skin that has thinned to the consistency of membrane. They do not breathe loudly. The air, heavy with bedrock and damp, has learned from them how to absorb vibration. If they have hearts, the beat is at the pace of moss: imperceptible, matching the freezing temperature rather than resisting it.

But they are not alone, and they are not few.

The absolute density demands it. In every gap that should be empty—between the hand-fitted stones, inside the woven reeds, beneath the broad ferns—there is presence. Small ones crawl across the moss not as travelers but as extensions of it, their bodies covered in the same deep green, so that movement is only a shift in texture, a brief darkening of the carpet. Some rest in the narrow depressions of the pools, half-submerged, half-emerged, teaching the water its own surface tension. Others cluster near the walls in shapes that are almost circular, except that circles require space, and here there is none. They are pressed shoulder to shoulder, vine to vine, and in their center is a smaller pool, a condensation within the condensation, drawn not despite them but because of them—by their collective cold, their shared refusal to generate heat.

They do not speak. Speech requires time to carry sound, and time here has been flattened. They do not call out to claim territory, because territory is not a concept that survives perfect occupation. The boundary between one body and another has dissolved; a cluster near a bone platform is also the platform, also the root-bed, also the wall that rounds into itself. You can look for separation—creature versus creature, built versus grown—but you will not find it. The erosion was not destructive; it was the long smoothing that allowed everything to fit without gap.

What lives here is not dead. Dead is wrong, imported from outside the pocket, from a world that expects decay to follow presence. Here, decay arrived long ago and was satisfied, rounded into agreement. These beings—if they are beings, if they are not instead the place’s decision to hold itself in form—have reached a technology of being that requires no more change. The chipped stone, the woven roofs, the platforms: these are not primitive failures but complete successes. They built until the building became them, and then they stayed, not from inertia but from fulfillment.

If there is a benevolent and wise power, it does not enter—entry implies an outside, and this pocket has forgotten the sky. Instead, it accretes. It is present in the way the light is present: constant, distributed, without focus. It is present in the way the water holds its shape, drawn by the collective coldness of those who occupy it. It is present in the silence—not an absence of noise, but an active holding, the air taught over ages how to absorb vibration, leaving only the visual fact of presence.

Perhaps it moves through them, or they move through it, but movement here is not travel. It is a slight adjustment in density, a shift in how the moss lies, a new bead of condensation forming on a wing that has not unfolded in centuries. The being—singular, plural, indistinguishable—lives inside the pocket not as a prisoner, but as its completion. The place drew them, or they drew the place, or both are only different descriptions of the same equilibrium.

They are here. They have always been here, in this bright, cold, eternal quiet—lush, dense, damp, silent, finished with change. And if wisdom looks upon them, it sees not a mystery to solve, but a breath that was taken long ago and, out of kindness, was never asked to exhale.
