It notices them not because they shine, but because they do not.

The attention arrives the same way everything else arrives in the bowl—without announcement, without wind, without a shift in the flat gray light. It is not a voice, not a shape with edges, but a benevolence that has achieved wisdom: it knows that to change is often to damage, and that the highest form of care is sometimes the refusal to interfere. Still, it comes closer, folding itself into the cool air behind the teeth, becoming part of the damp that never moves. It wants only to understand. And in understanding, to honor.

It moves through the grid on foot that leave no mark.

The settlement receives it as it receives everything: with indifference that is not hostility but completeness. The narrow stepped streets, the flat roofs of hand-hewn stone, the polished plazas with their motionless pools—all are exactly as they should be. The power walks among them, feeling the absence of urgency like a texture, like the matte surface of weathered rock. It sees the empty lots, the stairways leading to silence, the buildings facing one another in acknowledged solitude. It understands that this sparsity is not failure. It is preference. The grid was built for conversation, yes, but for the kind of conversation that needs more space between speakers than sound.

Then it sees them.

Not all at once—never clustered, never violating the emptiness they maintain. One stands in a doorway, half-withdrawn into shadow that will not deepen. Another sits on a low block, arranged rather than resting, its gray-green garment hanging flat with the moisture that never falls. They do not startle. They do not flee. They turn, with the same unhurried inevitability as the drip falling into the pool, and they look. Or rather, they orient. Their faces—smooth, featureless or nearly so, like masks shaped by centuries of weathering—do not express surprise. They register presence. That is all. And for them, it is enough.

The power stops in the center of the open plaza, beside the pool that never fills, never empties.

Here is the heart of the matter: these beings are not dead. They are not statues. They are the equilibrium made vertical. They hold the settlement in its finished state. They check the pools, touch the water without ripple, absorb the moisture from the walls, and in doing so, they keep the stasis alive—not dead stasis, but a living, breathing refusal to decay or accelerate. They are caretakers who have become the care itself. The wise power recognizes this immediately. To “awaken” them would be to destroy them. To “bless” them with movement, with growth, with voice, would be to flood the bowl with everything it has gently refused.

So it does the only thing a truly benevolent power can do.

It listens.

Not with ears, but with the full weight of its attention, distributed like the diffuse light—equal everywhere, gentle, without spotlight. It listens to the drip, identical in interval and tone. It listens to the absence of birds. It listens to the being in the doorway, to the one seated by the dry channel, to the others scattered like punctuation across the grid. And in listening, it understands their solitude—not as suffering, but as a form of completeness. Each is alone. Each holds an empty lot. Each stands in its own coordinate, maintaining the space.

But here, the power makes its single, quiet intervention.

Without breaking the surface of the pool, without altering the temperature, without introducing a sound that does not belong, it connects them.

Not physically. Not with ropes or paths or messages carried across the polished stone. It weaves a thread of recognition—thin as the film of water on the wall—between every being in the bowl. Each remains in its place. The empty pavement remains empty. But the solitude transforms, imperceptibly, into communion. They are still separated by hundreds of paces, still not facing one another, still not gathering. Yet each now holds, in the smooth center of its being, the knowledge that the others exist and that they are all holding the same stillness together. The grid is no longer a scattering of accidents. It is an agreement.

The power moves to each one.

Not to speak—there is no language that would not be an intrusion—but to be present. At each coordinate, it pauses. It allows its warmth, so slight it does not change the coolness of the air, to be felt. A head turns, not in alarm, but in acknowledgment. A hand, thin and fingerless in its mineral grace, lifts from a pool and touches not the water, but the space where the power stands—confirming, verifying, accepting. The being does not ask for more. It receives what is given: the certainty that its maintenance is not neglect, that its emptiness is not absence, that its refusal to change is not a failure but a form of fidelity.

At the final pool, near the wall of rounded stone, the power does its only visible act.

It touches the water—not with form, but with intention. The surface remains perfectly still. No ripple, no splash, no echo. But within the pool, something shifts that cannot be seen from above: a reflection, not of the power itself, but of the entire grid seen from within. The being nearest to it looks down and sees—not its own smooth face, but the scattered presence of all the others, held in the water’s unmoving surface. They are there. It is not alone in holding the space.

Then the power withdraws.

Not because its work is done—there is nothing to finish—but because presence has done all it could do without becoming possession. It leaves no object behind, no law, no alteration in the stone or the air or the rhythm of the drip. The settlement is exactly as it was: sparse, cool, damp, eternal in its flat line. The beings remain in their positions, vertical or arranged, touching the pools, checking the moisture, holding the empty lots with their silent weight.

But they are changed in the only way that matters.

They now know they are seen. Not watched—there is no surveillance in this attention, only love—but witnessed. Their equilibrium is not accidental. It is beloved. The emptiness they maintain is not a void waiting to be filled; it is a form of care, watched over, confirmed, and left precisely as it is.

The flat light continues, diffuse and patient. The water beads on the walls in its motionless film. The drip falls, identically, into the pool.

And in the doorway, beside the stairway that leads to silence, the being stands not only as the settlement’s keeper, but as its keeper who knows, now, that the keeping matters. Not to anyone who demands change. But to a benevolence that understood, without asking, that the highest wisdom is sometimes to let the world finish being itself.

The power is gone, or perhaps it remains—indistinguishable now from the coolness, from the damp, from the flat gray that illuminates everything equally. The bowl holds its breath. The beings hold their places. And in the space between them, across the polished emptiness of the grid, runs the invisible thread: not binding, not pulling, only knowing. A shared stillness. A completed circle.

Nothing more is needed. Nothing more is done. And in that refusal to do more—in that perfect, benevolent inaction—the place finds its final form: not rescued, not transformed, but recognized exactly as it was, and loved for being complete.
