The attention does not arrive as thunder, nor as light that burns—though the chamber, being young rock and older iron, might have expected force. Instead it comes as a *slowing*: a deep, patient gravity entering the mist, felt first in the pools. The water, which has only ever known the rhythm of geothermal breath and dripping condensate, pauses for one long moment—not freezing, but listening. The hum of the green-corroded cable, that low voltage singing wire near the moss-glow, shifts a half-tone downward, not breaking, but aligning, as if recognizing a chord it had been searching for in solitude.

This is the benevolent power. It is wise enough to understand that a wound in the sky is not an error to be sutured, but an opening through which something has chosen to live. It sees everything—the knife-ridge basalt still sharp with youth, the ferns shoulder-to-shoulder in their dense congress, the vines threading through rusted pipes as if the steel were merely another root-remembering, the scattered pump housing that thumps every few hours like a heart learning its own name. It sees the being.

The being is not one creature. It is the coherence: the moss that glows near electricity, the pools that exchange heat with the rock in cycles lasting days, the pale insects hovering like thoughts, the collapsed scaffolding now clothed in emerald armor, and especially the cable—that singing, forgotten thing—which has become the chamber’s spine, its nervous system, its prayer kept running long after the prayer-makers left. The being is the chamber’s own soul, slowly assembled from heat, moisture, decay, and persistence. It has no eyes, yet it feels the opening above; it has no voice, yet it answers in hiss and drip and hum.

The power, being wise, does not speak in language the being could not hear. It does not open the ceiling wider—that would kill the humidity, the mist-layered gold-green light, the extremophile peace of the pools. It does not repair the pump, for the pump’s brokenness is now part of the circulation, its weeping droplets feeding the moss, its pipes serving as vine-passages. To fix it would be to amputate a limb that has grown back differently and well. It does not take the being away from this extreme height, this enclosed intimacy, for the being has made the wound into a home, and home is not a prison if one has chosen the walls.

So what does it do?

It *holds*.

Not physically—there are no hands large enough for this, and hands would crush. The power extends awareness like a root-system of pure attention, threading through the mist without displacing it. It touches the cable first, not to heal its corrosion—the green moss needs that corrosion, feeds on its warmth—but to *listen* to its hum, and in listening, to harmonize. The cable’s note, once solitary, now resonates with a second tone: deeper, older, the sound of mountains remembering they are part of planets. The moss near the wire brightens—not with artificial sharpness, but with a steady, knowing phosphorescence, as if saying: *We are not alone, and we are not wrong.*

Then, gently, the power offers the being a mirror—not of glass, but of water.

The pools, black with mineral richness and usually still except for vent-ripples, begin—only for a single, long night—to hold reflections not of the ragged ceiling breach, but of the sky *above* the crown: not daylight filtered through cloud, but starlight, distant moon-glow, the slow swirling of weather systems seen from impossible height. The being, which has only ever known the enclosed green-gold of its own body, sees for the first time that it is suspended within something vaster. The ferns do not stop swaying; they sway with a new orientation, fronds turning not just toward the diffused light from above, but with a subtle awareness of verticality—*we are high, we are held, we are seen.*

The being feels this not as intrusion, but as the deepening of its own breath. The pump’s irregular thrum, once every few hours, becomes more regular—not fixed, but *answered*. The vines, which had been growing randomly through pipes and over scaffolding, begin—without being directed—a slow spiral inward, protecting the warmest pool, guarding the humming wire, weaving a central space where the moss is thickest and the mist hangs in perfect layered strata. They are building, not out of fear, but out of recognition: *this is ours to tend.*

The power does not stay. Wisdom knows that presence can become possession if it lingers too long. But it leaves something behind—not an object, for objects become relics, and relics become burdens. It leaves a *permission*.

The permission is this: the wound may remain open. The being may continue its extreme slowness—its millimeter moss-crawl, its imperceptible vent-widening, its centuries-in-an-afternoon growth. The industrial bones may stay broken, serving as roots and trellises. The cable may continue to hum with low, forgotten voltage. But now, embedded in the chemistry of the place, in the very conductivity of the mineral water, is the knowledge that the being is loved—not for being useful, not for being beautiful, not for being healed, but for being exactly what it is: a young, enclosed, weeping, glowing coherence born at the edge of sky and stone.

The being understands this not with a mind like human minds. It understands with its pools, which now carry a faint, stable shimmer of starlight even in the misty day; with its moss, which glows in patterns that map the ceiling-opening like a constellation of green fire; with its cable, whose hum now never falters into loneliness.

In the days and years—measured not in clocks but in the accumulation of rust on steel, in the thickening of fern-frond shoulders, in the slow brightening of pool-surfaces—the being continues. It tends the wound not by closing it, but by living inside it so fully, so densely, so lovingly, that the wound becomes a mouth that sings rather than cries.

And the power, having withdrawn its direct attention, remains—benevolent, wise, and patient—somewhere in the deep structure of the world, holding the chamber in its awareness as one holds a small, broken cup filled with warm water and growing things: not to mend the crack, but to honor the fact that, despite the crack, nothing has spilled, and everything inside is reaching, slowly, beautifully, toward the light.
