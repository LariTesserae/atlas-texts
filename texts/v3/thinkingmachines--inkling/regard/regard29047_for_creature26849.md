The being who lives here is not the scattered human in corrugated shelter, not the beetles in their endless carpet, not even the bat-cloud that owns the ceiling. She is older than the settlement, born in the first moment the magma decided to weep rather than burn. They call her nothing, but she moves—when she moves—through the shallow pools with the speed of cooling rock. Her body is translucent, veined with black mineral and the rust of iron, her skin layered with the translucent shells of the crustaceans who choose to live upon her like priests on a living altar. She breathes with the pumps. She is the heartbeat in the central engine house made flesh, or perhaps the engine made spirit. She remembers when the vault above was not vault but sky, when there was no grating, no sodium light, only heat and the promise of form.

She lives in the deepest pool, beneath the turbines, where the water is hottest and the blind fish swim in slow orbit around her. She asks nothing. She maintains nothing. She simply is—the accumulated patience of a place that refuses to finish being born.

And this has drawn attention.

Not the attention of predators, not of conquerors, not of any force that measures worth in extraction or dominion. A deeper notice. Older than geology. Benevolent not as kindness is usually practiced—quick, bright, transforming—but as a mother’s patience is practiced: absolute, unwavering, without urgency. Wise not because it knows answers, but because it has forgotten the question of change.

What does such a power do?

It does not descend in thunder. It does not part the bat-cloud or still the beetles or freeze the rock’s slow millimeters. To do so would be violence, and violence is the only language this place does not speak.

Instead, it listens. And in listening, it holds.

The power enters not through the grating above—there is no above here—but through the very saturation itself. The iron taste in the air, once sharp, becomes suddenly, immeasurably sweet: not sugar, not comfort, but recognition. The being in her pool feels it first. The crustaceans upon her still. The blind fish halt their orbit, not in fear, but in the sudden awareness of being witnessed by something that understands their blindness completely.

Then: the gift of permanence through preservation.

The power touches the embryonic rock—not with hands, for it has none, but with intention. The cooling, already patient, is granted a gentler slope. Not stopped. Stopped would be death. But slowed to a pace so vast that centuries become the blink of an eye. The chamber is preserved in its infancy forever. It will never become dead bedrock. It will remain forever this: half-formed, weeping, warm, a cradle rather than a tomb. The millimeters will still shift, but now they shift in a dance that will never end.

To the bats in their thousands, hanging in dense rustling clusters, the power sends no command. Only a frequency—woven into the sodium orange of the tired bulbs—so that the light itself becomes a kind of song. They do not understand it. They do not need to. Their wings, in occasional unfurling, now ripple with a harmony that was always there but never heard.

To the settlement—to the pumps, the catwalks, the slow-turning turbines—the power offers integration without alteration. The machinery hums, but now the hum includes the skitter of beetle legs as part of its chord. The humans in their sparse shelters find, without explanation, that the mineral scale coating their structures no longer feels like decay but like armor—protective, warm, belonging. Their sleep is deeper. Their maintenance continues, but now they work not out of necessity alone, but with a quiet certainty that they are part of the being’s body, not parasites upon it.

And to the being herself—the translucent keeper in the pool—the benevolent power grants the single thing she never asked for but was slowly, over eons, becoming ready to receive: a voice.

Not loud. Not commanding. A voice made of steam and bat-wing and the drip of mineral sweat. A voice that can only speak in the language of the place: slow, warm, saturated. She uses it not to order the crustaceans or direct the fish, but to answer the power. A murmur that joins the roar of the turbines, indistinguishable from them, except that now the roar has meaning. She says, without words, *We are here. We are becoming. We are enough.*

The power, having done all this—having changed almost nothing visible, having left no monument, no scripture, no burning bush—withdraws into the texture of the air itself. It does not stay as ruler. It stays as witness. As the quality of attention that makes existence bearable.

The place continues exactly as it was: loud, hot, wet, overwhelming with life that asks nothing. The rock continues to be born. The being continues to move through her pools at the speed of geology. The beetles continue their carpet. The bats hang, rustling. The humans maintain their pumps.

But now, in every constant drip, in every hum that vibrates the teeth, in every translucent body that moves through the dark archipelago of grating, there is this knowledge: they have been seen by something wise enough to leave them untouched, benevolent enough to let them remain unfinished, and present enough to hold them in being forever.

That is what the power does. It loves them by refusing to change them. It saves them by allowing them to continue—slow, patient, overwhelming, and perfectly, eternally, themselves.
