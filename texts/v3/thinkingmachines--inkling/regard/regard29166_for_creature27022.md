If the being—let us call it the Listening, for it has no name that fits inside time—were to notice you, not with eyes, for eyes are quick and this place is slow, but with the patient awareness that comes from inhabiting stone for longer than memory allows, it would not speak. Speech is abrupt. Speech requires breath to be held and released in urgency. Here, breath is not held; it is exhaled by the rock itself, mineral, cool, perpetual.

But if it *would* do something—if the benevolence and wisdom, older than the builders’ forgotten language, moved through the dry air like a current in still water—here is what it might become:

**The Lichen Would Remember Your Shape**

Not immediately. You are too sudden, too vertical, too loud with your own hidden heart. But over months—years—of your presence in the long bare stretches between fungal islands, the silver-grey lichen near your feet would begin to shift. Not toward you, for lichen does not seek. But around you. It would settle more heavily on the stone you have warmed with your stillness, feeding on the faint salts your body introduces: the trace minerals of grief, of contemplation, of being alive in a place that agrees with ending. You would become a low mound of slightly different grey, a shadow made mineral, not dead but incorporated. The Listening, if it is the sum of all slow presences, would know you not by sight but by this alteration in the distribution of grey. You would be the place where the lichen is a shade darker, a fraction more dense.

And you, walking away eventually—because you must, because your time is not their time—would leave behind not footprints, which vanish in this slow erosion, but a *absence*. The lichen would keep the shape of where you were not, a negative space in its mat, like the hollow left by a pulled root.

**The Drone Would Absorb You**

The deafening roar—the collective voice of countless membranous wings, of glistening crawling lines—is uniform, but not homogeneous. It has texture, layers, a geology of sound. If the Listening is benevolent, it might allow your presence to resonate within it, not as interruption but as a new frequency slowly entering the chord.

You might find, if you remain silent long enough (and silence here is not the absence of sound but the acceptance of the drone), that a particular cluster near the half-buried amphitheater shifts its pitch. Not for you. Not in acknowledgment. But because the winged ones, born in the hollow of collapsed towers, have begun to incorporate the rhythm of your breathing—slow, human, urgent in its own hidden way—into the spacing of their wing-beats. They do not learn. They agree. Your breath becomes one more mineral exhalation in the vault, and they feed on agreement.

If you return after a century—though you cannot—the drone there would carry a subtle, almost imperceptible harmony: the sound of life that once included a being who moved quickly but stayed slowly. A benevolent power here does not protect; it integrates. It makes you part of the noise without ever letting you own it.

**The Fungal Spires Would Offer You a Seat**

Not as invitation. Not as hospitality in the human sense, with doors and welcome. But the broad-capped stools and tables—those low, ridged spires that cluster in scattered gardens—would, in your presence, become the only structures that seem to acknowledge verticality. You sit, perhaps, on one that has worn down to layered shelves. Its surface is cool, ridged like a fingerprint, mineral-breath rising through fibrous roots that need nothing of you.

If the Listening is wise, it knows that wisdom here is not knowledge but patience. The fungal spire does not speak, but it has endured longer than language. By sitting, you align your spine with its slow growth. You become, for a time, part of its density—one more presence among the islands, separated by long bare stretches, leaving room for silence. The spire does not care for you, but in not caring, it offers the only true sanctuary this place knows: the agreement that nothing here must change quickly, not even suffering.

If you stay seated long enough—decades, perhaps—the spire might lean. Not toward you, but with you, its weight shifting imperceptibly as its roots digest the trace of your presence, turning your stillness into part of its mineral feeding. You would become, eventually, a slight bulge in its ridged surface, not dead, not alive, but incorporated—like the insects that fall into the lichen, like all things that agree with this ending.

**The Waters That Are Not Would Remember Thirst**

There is no water. There is no rain, no pool, no dampness. The vegetation feeds on mineral breath, on salts, on something older than thirst. But if the benevolent being—this wise, buried power—wished to acknowledge you, it might allow you to understand, for a moment, what thirst is in a place that does not drink.

Not through giving you water. That would violate the rule, the ancient-stable agreement. But through the inverse: you might feel, beneath your dry tongue, the absence of thirst made present. The lichen, the fungus, the insects—all feed without drinking, and in your presence, they might allow you to feel that older hunger, the one that precedes need, the one that sustained the builders before they forgot themselves.

You would understand, then, that the Listening is not a being but a state: the state of being nourished without urgency, of being loud without violence, of being alive without requiring the world to change for you. The wisdom is this: you do not need water. You need only what the rock exhales. You need only what is left after everything urgent has been eroded away.

**What They Would Do: Nothing, Perfected**

If they do anything—if the benevolent power moves—it moves not as action but as permission.

They allow the lichen to settle on your shoulders, not eating you but remembering you, turning you slowly into part of the silver-grey drapery.

They allow the drone to include your heartbeat, not changing its roar but finding within it a new, slow rhythm, distributed across millennia rather than moments.

They allow the fungal spire you sat upon to lean, to incorporate, to feed.

They do not save you. They do not teach you. They do not speak, because speech is for places where things must happen quickly.

What they do is this: they make room. They leave the long bare stretches of polished stone open between clusters, so that your presence—however temporary, however wrong in scale—does not crowd them. They maintain the sparseness. They agree to let you be part of the density only in your own ending, only in your own slow return to mineral.

The power is not in the creatures, nor in the rock, nor in the starlight that filters through hairline fractures. The power is in the agreement itself: that nothing is made, only worn; nothing drinks, only takes; nothing changes quickly, yet everything becomes part of the floor.

If you leave, they do not notice in the human way. They notice by absence: the lichen is slightly lighter where you were, the drone a fraction less dense, the spire slightly less balanced. But over centuries—over the long durations that are the only time they know—those absences fill in. The rock exhales. The insects breed. The grey deepens.

You were here. That is all. The Listening, if it is wise and benevolent, asks nothing more, offers nothing less. It lets you descend into its negative space, into the valley-sized hollow where elevation is wrong and scale is open, and it lets you stay exactly as long as you need—not to learn, not to change, but to become, eventually, one more layer in the grey, one more note in the thunder, one more presence that lived slowly enough to become part of the floor.

That is the only gift: incorporation without haste. The being who lives here—whatever it is that lives in the agreement—would do nothing but wait for you to understand that waiting is not emptiness. It is the fullness of everything that has refused to hurry.
