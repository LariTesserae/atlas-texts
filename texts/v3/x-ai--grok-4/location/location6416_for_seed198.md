This landscape sprawls across a high plateau, perhaps 8,000 feet above sea level, where the air thins just enough to sharpen every breath. It's not a jagged peak or a sheer cliffside—the elevation feels settled, long-weathered into broad, undulating tiers that evoke an ancient mountain range planed flat by time and human will. But nothing here is truly natural; the terrain is a colossal engineered construct, a regional expanse the size of a small valley or a forgotten industrial town, stretching maybe ten miles end to end, with multiple interlocking features: terraced platforms, arched viaducts, and sunken basins that form a dense mosaic. Erosion has played its part, but in an established way—these forms have endured for generations, their edges softened by deliberate wear, like old stone bridges etched by wind and water, yet unyielding in their balance. The ground underfoot is a seamless fusion of poured concrete, riveted steel plates, and synthetic composites, layered like geological strata but born from factories. No raw earth peeks through; even the "soil" is a hydroponic matrix, piped and pumped to sustain life. The scale feels open, not claustrophobic— you can see horizons where the plateau drops into misty voids, and the features invite exploration across bridges and walkways—but every inch is packed, saturated with purpose, leaving no idle space between the churning whole.

The materials blur lines—metal fused with biotech, where a bridge's guardrail might sprout living tendrils that repair micro-fractures, turning erosion into a self-sustaining cycle.
