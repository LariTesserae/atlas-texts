Imagine descending into a world that has forgotten the surface, a vast submerged expanse where water is not merely present but absolute, enveloping everything in a cold, unyielding embrace. This is no fleeting flood or storm-swept shore; it is a primordial ocean-realm, scaled to the breadth of continents, where the liquid medium stretches endlessly in all directions—upward to a distant, unseen ceiling of pressure, downward into abyssal trenches that could swallow worlds, and outward across horizons that curve with the subtle arc of planetary girth. The water here is thick with age, its currents sluggish and deliberate, carrying the chill of forgotten glaciers melted eons ago. Temperatures hover in a perpetual near-freeze, just above the point where ice would claim dominion, turning every movement into a labored push against the dense, viscous cold. There's no sun-warmed shallows or bubbling vents to temper it; this is a domain of uniform frigidity, where heat is a myth whispered by the rare, faint upwellings from deeper fissures.

The terrain is low-lying, a flattened expanse worn smooth by the inexorable patience of erosion over geological epochs. What might once have been jagged peaks or rugged valleys has been gentled into rolling undulations, broad plateaus, and shallow basins, all settled into a final, ancient form—like the bones of a long-extinct leviathan, polished by time until only the faintest contours remain. But this is no natural seabed; everything here is built, a total fabrication wrought by hands long vanished. The "ground" is an interlocking mosaic of enormous stone slabs, hewn from quarries lost to memory and fitted together with primitive precision—mortarless joints sealed by the weight of water and the slow creep of mineral deposits. Walls rise in staggered tiers, not as ruins but as enduring monoliths: vast barriers of roughly chiseled granite and basalt, stacked and wedged with wooden beams that have petrified into something like fossilized veins. Towers spiral upward in helical forms, their surfaces etched with simple carvings—geometric patterns made by flint tools or early bronze chisels, depicting cycles of flood and submersion that feel less like art and more like warnings etched into the world itself.

These structures extend across the vast scale of the place, forming a constructed continent adrift in its own watery void. It's large enough to birth its own weather: slow-swirling eddies that mimic storms, condensing mists that drift like fog banks over artificial ridges, and pressure gradients that create languid "rains" of denser water falling from higher layers. The density is palpable—every cubic span is layered with purpose, structures nestled close, their edges nearly touching in a labyrinthine weave. Narrow channels wind between them, not empty but filled with suspended lattices of woven reeds and cordage, primitive aqueducts that once channeled flows but now hang in silent suspension. There's no untouched earth here, no raw rock or silt; even the "sediment" is a fine particulate of eroded masonry, ground down by the water's eternal friction into a soft, drifting haze that settles in crevices like aged dust.

Yet amid this total artifice, vegetation thrives in lush profusion, a verdant counterpoint to the cold stone. It's as if the built world invited life to colonize it, and the plants obliged with relentless, slow-growing abundance. Vast forests of kelp-like fronds, adapted to the chill and dark, cloak the structures in swaying mantles—thick blades of deep green and bruised purple, rooted not in soil but in the porous joints of the stonework, drawing nutrients from the water's dissolved minerals. Fern-analogues unfurl in dense clusters, their feathery leaves forming underwater thickets that brush against one another in the faint currents, creating pockets of even greater density where tendrils entwine like living scaffolding. Bulbous, rootless pods float in tethered clusters, anchored by fibrous vines to the artificial substrate, blooming sporadically into pale, luminescent flowers that provide the only sparse light—faint bioluminescent glows that flicker like dying embers in the otherwise profound darkness. The vegetation is ancient too, its forms echoing the erosion of the stones: gnarled, oversized growths that have layered upon themselves over centuries, forming canopies that span entire districts of the submerged cityscape.

There's no animal life to stir this place, no darting schools or lurking predators; the ecology is a silent ballet of plant and water alone, sustained by the slow diffusion of nutrients from decaying fronds and the faint chemical exchanges in the depths. Fungi-like mats creep across the stone surfaces, breaking down the primitive constructions at a glacial pace, weaving mycorrhizal networks that bind plant to plant in a dense, interconnected web. The atmosphere—such as it is in this all-liquid realm—is one of hushed endurance, the water heavy with dissolved gases that lend a faint, metallic tang, like the memory of forged tools long cooled.

Sounds are mostly absent, swallowed by the depths: a profound quiet broken only by the occasional, distant groan of stone shifting under pressure, or the soft susurrus of fronds rubbing in a rare current. No echoes rebound sharply; everything is muffled, diffused into the vastness. The dynamic here is slow, unfolding across durations that mock human haste—cycles of growth and decay that span decades, where a frond might extend a fraction over a year, and erosion carves a new groove in a century. The rules of this world seem etched into its very fabric: gravity pulls downward with a patient insistence, but buoyancy counters it, suspending debris and vines in eternal limbo. Time is the true architect, refining the primitive builds into something eternal, where change is not disruption but a gentle, inevitable settling. Light is a scarcity, the darkness near-total save for those sporadic vegetal glows, forcing any wanderer to navigate by touch and intuition, feeling the dense press of structures and foliage like a blind explorer in a forgotten library.

As you drift deeper, the place asserts itself more fully—not as a relic, but as a living archive of submersion. The vastness begins to feel intimate in its density, each layered nook revealing another facet: a chamber where stone arches form artificial grottos overgrown with luminous moss, or a broad plaza where the water's slow whirlpools mimic the wind over fields that never were. It's a world that has aged into equilibrium, cold and quiet, where the built and the grown entwine in a primordial pact, enduring in the dark heart of the flood.
