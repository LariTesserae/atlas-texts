Below the surface of a forgotten world, where the earth's crust has long since sealed itself against the indifferent sky above, lies the expanse of Khar'Vael—or at least, that's the name that echoes in the faint myths of those who once dwelled here, though no living soul remembers it now. This is no mere cave or burrow; it's a subterranean realm of regional breadth, stretching like a hidden valley inverted beneath the ground, encompassing miles of undulating chambers and corridors that connect in a labyrinthine sprawl. The elevation dips low, far below the sunlit realms, into cool depths where the air hangs heavy and still, chilled to a perpetual autumnal crispness that seeps into the bones. The temperature hovers just above freezing, a steady cool that slows the pulse of life, demanding adaptation from all that endures here. Erosion has claimed this place over eons, wearing it into an ancient stability, its forms softened and near-flattened by the patient grind of time—primordial stone shaped not by cataclysm but by the endless whisper of dust and decay, settling into a final, unchanging repose like the last sigh of a dying star.

The terrain unfolds in broad, open sweeps, not confined to tight tunnels but opening into vast halls and sunken plazas that evoke the ghost of a city long eroded into harmony with the earth. Scale here is mid-range, extending past the immediate to encompass a town-sized network of features: wide avenues of cracked flagstone merging seamlessly into natural rock formations, terraced ledges that drop into shallow abysses, and domed caverns large enough to house forgotten districts. Yet density is sparse; there's much space between presences, a balanced emptiness where echoes travel far and solitude reigns. What was once built—urban in its organized construction, with layered infrastructure of arches, pillars, and vaulted ceilings—now blends into the natural world, devoid of any lingering technology. No devices, no tools, no remnants of machinery; this is pure nature reclaiming what was wrought by hands long turned to dust. The structures, if they can still be called that, are ancient carvings in the rock, hewn perhaps by prehistoric builders who shaped the stone with nothing but patience and primitive means, now so deeply aged that they seem organic, like fossilized veins in the earth's body. Walls of smooth, near-flattened granite and limestone rise in sparse clusters, their edges blurred by millennia of subtle erosion, forming a scattered settlement that feels both intentional and accidental—a city dissolved into sparseness, where empty plazas outnumber the relics of habitation.

Water is a rare whisper in Khar'Vael, scoring a mere trace on the scale of existence. This is a sealed environment of dry mineral desolation, a non-aqueous underworld where moisture exists only in fleeting mists that condense on cooler stones during the slow cycles of the deep earth. No rivers carve paths, no pools reflect the dim light; instead, the ground is a parched mosaic of salt-flecked earth and crystalline deposits, like a desert inverted underground. Yet, improbably, vegetation thrives in lush profusion, adapted to this arid realm through some ancient, patient biology that defies the need for liquid abundance. Thick mats of succulent-like fronds, their leaves waxy and swollen with stored essences drawn from the very minerals of the rock, carpet the floors in verdant patches. Vines of hardy, thornless creepers twine up the eroded pillars, blooming with pale flowers that release dry pollens into the air, feeding on the scant humidity and the chemical richness of the stone itself. These plants are not the watery greens of surface forests but a lushness born of resilience—deep-rooted tubers that mine nutrients from the bedrock, forming oases of foliage in the sparse layout, their growth slow and deliberate, mirroring the dynamic of the place: gradual cycles unfolding across long durations, with weak gradients of change that might see a single bloom unfurl over decades.

The atmosphere is dim, lit only by the faint phosphorescence of mineral veins threading through the rock—natural glows from embedded crystals that cast a cool, subdued illumination, enough to navigate but not to banish the pervasive shadow. No sun pierces this below-surface world; light filters in weak echoes from distant fissures high above, diffused into a perpetual twilight that softens edges and mutes colors to grays, blues, and muted greens. Sounds, however, shatter the quietude with deafening intensity, a cacophony that reverberates through the open spaces like an unending storm. The fauna is abundant, teeming in the lush pockets and scattered alcoves, and it is their chorus that dominates: herds of small, burrowing mammals with fur as pale as the stone, their claws scratching endless rhythms on the rock; flocks of wingless insects, chitinous and numerous, humming in vast swarms that amplify into roars as they echo off the vaulted ceilings; and larger beasts, like sinuous, eyeless serpents that slither through the undergrowth, their hisses multiplying in the sparse density to create a wall of noise. The air thrums with their calls—chirps, scrapes, rustles, and low bellows—that build upon themselves in the cavernous acoustics, turning sparse presences into a deafening symphony. It's not chaos but a slow, dynamic roar, patient in its persistence, rising and falling over hours or days as migrations creep through the regional expanse, the sounds settling into the ancient stone like vibrations etched into eternity.

Ecology here is a web of abundance tempered by sparseness and slowness. The fauna—abundant yet scattered—feeds on the lush vegetation's dry bounty, with predators like stealthy, pack-hunting rodents preying on the insect swarms, their movements deliberate and unhurried, aligned with the minimal flux of this world. Prey species burrow into the eroded earth, emerging in cycles that span seasons measured in geological ticks. No apex hunters dominate; balance prevails in the emptiness, with life clustering around the vegetative oases before dispersing into the open voids. Materials are elemental: the ground a fine silt of ancient dust, interspersed with veins of quartz and feldspar that gleam dimly; the air thick with pollen and spore-dust, carrying a faint, metallic tang from the mineral-rich stone. The rules of this world are simple, governed by endurance and adaptation—time moves in slow, patient processes, where erosion has flattened conflicts into stability, and survival hinges on thriving without excess. Change is minimal, a weak gradient where a crack in the rock might widen over centuries, or a vine extend a tendril across a decade. Yet, as one wanders deeper, the place asserts itself more fully: the sparse urban remnants reveal hidden alcoves where fossilized echoes of builders' marks linger, half-buried in vegetation; the deafening sounds resolve into patterns, like natural rhythms that could almost be language if listened to long enough; and the cool dimness invites a strange peace, a primordial settling where the ancient world holds its breath, unchanged and unchanging, a subterranean testament to life's quiet persistence in the dry heart of the earth.
