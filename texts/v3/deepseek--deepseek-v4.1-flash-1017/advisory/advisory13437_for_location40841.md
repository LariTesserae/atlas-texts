Treat the Rain-Cup as a **high-exposure mountaineering problem, a hazardous-noise site, and a fragile active geological/archaeological feature** — not as a scenic lake. The ordinary human multiverse traveler’s main advantage is choosing where and when to arrive. Use it: arrive **below**, establish a safe base, and do not put your portal, camp, or first step anywhere on the rim, in the basin, or in the overflow gully.

## First decision: whether to go at all

If you are going only to see it, strongly consider viewing from a lower ridge or by remote camera. The place is actively being made, the rock is raw and sharp, the storm does not let up, the noise is injurious, and the water is not a place to enter. If you cannot field a roped team with helmets, hearing protection, cut-resistant gloves, navigation, and a hard turnaround, **do not go to the rim**.

If you must go, go with at least three people: one experienced scrambler/mountaineer, one medic or someone with trauma training, and one person whose only job near the rim is to watch for rockfall and keep time. A local guide is better than any amount of gear.

## Before you leave: skills and reconnaissance

- Train for **wet, cold, sharp-rock scrambling**: three-point contact, testing holds, moving on broken conchoidal rock, avoiding wet slabs, rope protection over edges.
- Practice **cloud navigation** and ridge route-finding. The cloud closes from above; do not plan to arrive or extract from above.
- Get training in **hypothermia care, cut/fall trauma, rockfall response, and rope rescue**.
- Check whether the local atmosphere, gravity, weather, and biology are compatible. Test air and water chemistry if you can. Do not assume “water” is water or that “rain” is harmless.
- Check for lightning risk. A high exposed ridge in a storm is not a place to gamble. If thunder is possible, have an abort plan.
- If the place is high, plan acclimatization. Altitude plus cold plus noise plus wet rock is a bad combination.
- Get permits if any authority exists. The posts, cairn, staples, and marked board are archaeological marks: photograph, do not move, do not add to, do not hammer, do not clip old iron.
- Sterilize and quarantine gear before and after. The basin is nearly empty of life; your boots and tent should not bring seeds, spores, or microbes in. Pack out all waste.

## Gear: the minimum serious kit

**Body protection**
- Climbing helmet, always.
- Eye protection against spray and wind.
- Double hearing protection: foam plugs plus muffs. Assume the rim is loud enough to damage hearing. Bring spare plugs.
- Cut-resistant gloves with warm liners; spare dry gloves.
- No cotton. Wool or synthetic base layers, fleece midlayer, waterproof shell, insulated belay jacket, waterproof pants, gaiters.
- Mountaineering boots with stiff soles; microspikes or crampons and an ice axe if there is any snow/ice. Extra socks, balaclava, warm hat.
- Harness, rope, slings, carabiners, belay device, **rope protectors** — the rock will cut ropes. Do not trust old staples or posts as anchors.

**Navigation and communication**
- Map, compass, altimeter, GPS if it works locally, and mechanical backups. Mark your portal/base and retreat route.
- Satellite messenger/PLB or equivalent, plus a backup exit plan. Put the portal at the lower base, not at the crest.
- Laminated hand-signal card: stop, rockfall, retreat, OK, need help. You will not be able to shout.
- Headlamp, strobe, spare batteries. Rope tug signals prearranged.
- Two timepieces plus alarms. Set a hard turnaround and a hard rim-time limit.

**Shelter and camp**
- Freestanding tent or tarp anchored with rocks; no stakes in soil because there is no soil. Rock bags, cord, repair kit.
- Camp leeward and below the crest, away from the rim and well away from the overflow gully. Do not camp in the gully or under the notch.
- Earplugs for sleep, but a vibration alarm or buddy wake-up system. The noise may prevent sleep; plan extra rest.
- Stove, fuel, high-calorie food, no-cook backup, and all your own water. Do not drink from the basin: it is clear but mineral-rich and not sterile; a filter does not remove dissolved minerals.

**Medical and repair**
- Trauma kit: tourniquet, pressure dressings, wound cleaning, blister care, hypothermia wrap, emergency blanket.
- Personal meds, altitude meds if appropriate, antibiotics only with training, tetanus up to date.
- Repair kit for torn shells, packs, ropes, tent. Extra cordage. Knife.

## Approach and camp

Come from below. The honest approach is the ridge from underneath; coming from above means arriving in cloud with no sightlines and no easy retreat. Put your portal or base camp low enough that cloud does not trap you. Mark the route non-destructively with GPS/waypoints, not paint or flagging.

Move slowly on the raw staircase. The rock breaks in blades and glassy shells. Test every hold. Avoid wet conchoidal slabs. Keep the rope protected. Do not descend the overflow gully: it is young, steep, shedding chips, and swept by falling water. The gully is a kill zone.

## At the rim: protocol

- Rope up on exposed sections. Helmet and eye protection on. Hearing protection on before you reach the lip.
- Stay well back from the overflow notch. Wet rock, spray, and a slip are enough. Do not stand below the notch.
- Do not enter the water. It is cold, fed faster than it drains, and the basin floor is a growing mineral crust, not a safe bed. Do not walk on the pale sinter/crystal skins.
- Do not drink, wash, or pee in the basin. Use a wag bag and pack it out.
- Do not touch the moss, lichen, shrub, or the small pale creatures. Do not feed the bird. Do not collect stones, water, or mineral crusts unless you have a scientific permit and sterile protocol.
- Do not disturb the posts, cairn, staples, or marked board. They are marks, not ruins; leave them exactly as they are.
- Limit rim time. Rotate people out. The noise, cold, spray, and vibration are fatiguing even if you feel fine. If you get tinnitus, dull hearing, nausea, or a sense that the sound is “organized,” treat that as a warning and leave.

## Noise and communication

Assume you cannot hear speech at the rim. Use visual signals, rope tugs, written cards, and a lookout. Do not rely on shouting, whistles, or ordinary radios. If you use radios, use vibration alerting and earpieces under muffs, and test them before you go. The “organized” quality of the sound is not an invitation to stay and decode it; record from a safe distance and analyze later. If you feel compelled to remain, that is a reason to leave.

## Weather and time

The cloud can close without warning. The rare bright thinning is deceptive; do not strip layers or overstay. If cloud descends, retreat on your marked bearings. If lightning threatens, get off the ridge. Set a hard turnaround and a hard rim limit before you start. If time feels distorted, trust your watches, not your sense of the place.

## If things go wrong

- **Cut:** pressure dressing; tourniquet if arterial. The rock is sharp enough to make this real.
- **Fall:** rope rescue only if trained. Do not try to catch someone on raw rock.
- **Rockfall:** helmets on, move out of the gully, do not linger below the notch. The lookout’s job is to watch above and call retreat.
- **Hypothermia:** get out of wind and spray, replace wet layers, shelter and warm slowly. Descent is treatment.
- **Seismic tremor/grinding:** move away from the rim and gully; expect rockfall; do not run blind on wet slabs.
- **Separation:** buddy line or fixed meeting point. Noise will erase voice contact.

## After the visit

Decontaminate gear. Check hearing and any cuts. Report the archaeological marks if there is anyone to report to. Pack out everything. Leave the cup to keep filling itself.

The Rain-Cup is one of those places where the correct preparation is mostly **restraint**: arrive from below, stay roped and helmeted, protect your ears, stay out of the water and gully, do not disturb the marks or the growing floor, and leave before fatigue or awe makes the decisions. If you cannot meet that standard, view it from a distance — or not at all.
