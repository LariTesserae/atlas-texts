## How an ordinary human should prepare for the Soke

**First posture:** You are a soft, dry-land animal entering a wet, vertical, loud, living machine that is continuously being edited by its inhabitants. The Soke will not adapt to you. You adapt to it: pack light, stay dry where it matters, hire a guide, ask before touching, and never try to make anything permanent.

### Before you go

- **Arrange a local guide before arrival**, ideally through the Cut or a guesthouse. The town’s map is re-argued every spring and is partly oral. A guide is not a luxury; it is navigation, etiquette, and safety.
- **Learn the basic civic law:** no structure outlives its pruning. Do not build, nail, tie permanently, carve, paint, or leave markers. Do not “improve” anything. Do not bring nails, screws, permanent adhesives, or permanent ink.
- **Prepare for constant wet and cool:** no cotton next to skin; quick-dry layers; wool or fleece midlayer; a shell that can wet out and still keep wind off; spare dry clothes in a dry bag. Hypothermia is possible in cool, windy, wet conditions even if it never freezes.
- **Get physically ready for verticality:** ladders, grafted stairs, dished wet steps, low doorways, narrow walkways, no rails. If you have bad vertigo, poor balance, or cannot carry a small pack hands-free, reconsider.
- **Check your biological baggage:** sterilize boots and gear as much as possible; bring no seeds, soil, live plants, pets, or uncontained foodstuffs. On return, quarantine and clean gear. The Soke is a crowd; do not add an invasive member.
- **Leave an itinerary with your multiverse authority:** route in, expected stay, exit point. Assume no reliable outside communication inside the shelf.

### Kit that actually matters

- **Footwear:** sticky rubber soles, drainage, ankle support, and a second pair. Smooth soles on wet travertine are a liability. Bring wool socks, foot powder, blister kit, and gaiters for leeches and brush.
- **Hands and head:** work gloves for wet wood, splinters, and grip; brimmed hat for drips and bird droppings; eye protection if you are sensitive; earplugs for sleep and for the Spill. Keep a whistle.
- **Dry storage:** two dry bags—one for sleep clothes and one for electronics and paper. Assume humidity kills electronics; bring silica, spare batteries, and do not rely on them. A pencil and waterproof notebook beat a phone.
- **Water:** filter or purifier and a bottle. The spring is cold and steady, but the channels are open to animals and people. Do not drink untreated channel or cistern water.
- **Health:** antifungal cream, antiseptic, blister care, antihistamine, oral rehydration, anti-diarrheal, personal meds, insect repellent, head net, sleeping net, leech-removal supplies. If you have allergies or respiratory sensitivity to mold, take it seriously.
- **Light:** headlamp with red mode, spare batteries in a dry bag. Avoid strong white light at night and during the Spill; it attracts moths and disturbs birds and people.
- **Sleep:** silk liner or light sleeping bag in a dry bag, earplugs, eye mask. Lodging will be damp; keep your dry layer dry.
- **Do not bring:** drones, loudspeakers, disposable plastics you cannot pack out, open flames, pets, live plants, seeds, nails, permanent markers, or a conquering attitude.

### Arrival

You will hear the Soke before you see it. Stop at the edge of the shelf. Do not rush in. Find your guide or the Cut. Ask where you may stay and which ways are safe **today**—not which ways were safe last year. Choose lodging by exit route and pruning schedule, not by view. Keep your pack small; you will be climbing and squeezing.

Ask specifically:
- “Which steps and ladders are safe this season?”
- “When is the Spill?”
- “Has the seep-line moved?”
- “When is the next cut over this way?”
- “Where may I put waste?”
- “May I pass under the cut?”

Accept that the answer may be an argument between two confident people who are both right in different decades.

### Daily rhythm and conduct

- **Mornings:** cloud, drip, condensation. Wear the hat. The Cut works in the cool; do not stand under pruning, do not grab falling branches, do not interrupt a cutter unless it is an emergency.
- **Afternoons:** storms come up the Throat. Stay off exposed ladders and terrace edges during wind and rain. After it clears, surfaces are extra slick and steam comes off the moss.
- **Dusk:** the Spill. When it comes, stop. Stand still, keep your mouth closed, protect your eyes if needed, and let it happen. Do not wave, shout, photograph with flash, or push through the crowd of birds. If you must move, move slowly along a wall.
- **Nights:** headlamp on red, watch the dished steps for frogs and salamanders, keep food covered, do not walk the channels on the eel-run night. Ask when that is.
- **Water:** ask before bathing, washing, or dumping anything. Do not contaminate cisterns or channels. Use designated waste arrangements; if none, pack out.
- **Fire:** the smouldering heaps are managed. Do not add trash, do not start fires, do not smoke casually near cut piles.
- **Animals:** do not feed or handle bats, rats, cats, otters, or birds. The moths are food; the children sweeping them are working. Leeches: check after walking, remove properly, keep bites clean.
- **Food:** eat cooked food; try the fried moths if offered, but do not refuse hospitality rudely. Fungi only with a guide. Wash hands.

### The real hazards

1. **Slips and falls.** Wet travertine, moss, dished steps, grafted ladders, no rails. Use three points of contact. Do not carry heavy loads. Do not walk drunk.
2. **Structural surprise.** Living walls close; bearers push; terraces slump; the seep-line at the foot of the wall is not to be built on or camped on. Stay on maintained routes and ask which ones are maintained this month.
3. **Water and storm.** Flash rise in the Throat; cold rain; slick everything. Do not camp low or in the gorge. Know your storm shelter before you need it.
4. **Insects and disease.** Leeches, biting flies, mold, unknown water and food microbes. You are immunologically naive; treat water, cook food, cover skin, and keep cuts clean.
5. **Noise and sensory overload.** The Spill is a pressure on the chest. Earplugs are not weakness. The town is never quiet; plan for that.
6. **Getting lost in a changing maze.** Use a guide, set a rendezvous, carry a whistle, and do not cut your own path.

### Social and ethical preparation

- **The Cut is the authority on cutting and grafting.** Ask permission to observe; never take a knife to a living wall, floor-bearer, or graft without invitation and training.
- **Do not try to preserve the Soke.** It eats its own past on purpose. Do not mourn the cut, do not “rescue” old structures, do not call the town primitive or ruined.
- **Barter and ask.** Copper is treasured; good blades, salt, dry cloth, and lowland goods may be welcome—but offer through your guide, and never give live plants, seeds, or pets.
- **Photography:** ask people first; no flash during the Spill; no drones. Do not photograph cutters while they are working without permission.
- **Waste:** pack out all synthetics, batteries, wet wipes, and broken gear. Ask before adding anything organic to a heap. Do not leave cairns, ribbons, or gear.

### Emergency plan

Know the route back to the valley and the nearest safe shelter. In storm, get off exposed ladders and edges; in injury, use your whistle and stay put; in the Spill, stand still; in the dark, red light and slow feet. If you fall into the Throat, that is the emergency you prepared not to have.

**In one sentence:** go as a temporary, dry-bagged, well-guided guest who carries little, cuts nothing, packs out everything, stands still for the Spill, and accepts that the Soke is not a place you visit so much as a place you are briefly tolerated by.
