# Field Brief: The Flue

## Before anything else: understand what kind of place this is

The Flue is not a cave. Caves are places where water has been at work for a long time — they are rounded, they are cool, they have a hydrology, they have a way out that water made. The Flue is the opposite of all of that. It is a fracture that opened and then stalled, a room halfway through being born, and the thing that runs it is not water but heat and chemistry. Every hazard inside comes from that one fact. There is no smoothing agent, no wetting agent, no biological decay to soften the edges. Nothing here has been rounded because nothing here has had the chance.

Practically, that means your preparation is not caving preparation. It's more like preparing to spend an hour inside a sealed industrial vessel that operates itself — a foundry with no operators, no gauges, and no shutdown, which has been exhaling poison into a small closed room for decades and will go on doing it whether you are in there or not.

Plan accordingly, and plan to be a guest, not an explorer.

---

## The first decision: go in at the trough

The room breathes. You are told the convection turns over every few hours, and the vent's glow pulses over hours — brighter, dimmer, a hand's width of light swelling and shrinking. Those are the same signal. When the vent is bright, it is working hardest: more heat, more vapor, more gas, faster turnover. When it dims, it is at its ebb.

**Your entry window is the trough.** This is the single most important piece of judgment in the whole trip, and you cannot learn it from a distance in ten minutes. Arrive a day early if you can. Sit outside the crack, at a respectful distance, and watch the glow and feel the exhalation for a full cycle. Time the dim phase. Then go back down, sleep, and come up to enter on the next trough — not the one you just watched, the next one, so you enter fresh and rested and not at the end of a day's climb.

Everything else in this brief is gear. This is the one thing that is not gear, and it is worth more than all the gear combined. The room will tell you when it is least angry. Learn to read it before you squeeze through.

---

## The gas is what will kill you, and it will not announce itself

A vent exhaling heat through sulfur-yellowed seams and salt crusts is exhaling a chemistry set: sulfur dioxide, hydrogen sulfide, carbon dioxide, probably carbon monoxide, acid aerosols, water vapor. In a low-ceilinged chamber the heavier fractions gather low, along the floor, in the hollows between the fallen slabs — the same low ground your ankles and your face will occupy when you crouch. The convection stirs it, but stirring is not clearing.

Three properties of that gas matter more than the rest:

**It dulls your nose.** Hydrogen sulfide at dangerous concentrations stops smelling like rotten eggs and starts smelling like nothing. If you are relying on your nose as a detector, you are relying on a detector that fails silently at exactly the wrong moment.

**Your eyes are wet.** Sulfur dioxide in contact with moisture makes acid. Your eyes are the wettest thing you have. Sealed goggles are not optional and not a comfort item.

**The animals are not your canary.** You will see the swarms clustering thickest around the hot fissure and think *well, they're fine there, so it must be fine*. No. Their physiology is not yours, their respiratory chemistry is not yours, and they have had a very long time to adapt to a room that has only just met you. Use them to read the mineral map, never the air.

So: **carry a personal multi-gas monitor** — oxygen, carbon dioxide, carbon monoxide, hydrogen sulfide, sulfur dioxide — with audible and vibrating alarms, clipped where you can see it, checked and calibrated before you leave. Set the alarms conservatively. When it chirps, you do not negotiate; you leave, at a walk, along your marked route, and you do not stop to look at anything on the way out. If you can afford only one serious piece of equipment for this trip, it is this one, and it is not close.

Back it up with a **full-face respirator** on acid-gas cartridges with particulate filtration, fitting-tested, plus a spare set of cartridges sealed in a bag. Full-face rather than half-face, for the eyes. Note that the air is so dry that fogging is not your problem — the opposite is; your breath will desiccate your own mouth and throat behind the mask, so plan drinking breaks and plan them in the cool corner, not near the vent.

And understand the geometry of the squeeze before you trust it: the crack is breathing *out*. When you stand at the mouth, you are standing in the exhaust. Do not arrive at the throat winded from the climb. Do not wrestle your pack through the crack while breathing hard in the outflow. Kit off, pack pushed through ahead of you, calm breaths, steady pace, get through and get to the cool side of the room before you do anything else.

## You will not notice the heat until it has you

Dry heat is a liar. In humid heat you feel yourself sweat and you know the score. Here, sweat leaves your skin so fast it never accumulates, so you never feel wet, so you never feel that you are losing water — and you are losing it at a rate that would frighten you if you could see it. The room is a desiccator with a hot corner. The dryness presses on the skin "like a hand," and it is pulling moisture out of you the entire time.

So:

**Drink on a schedule, not on thirst.** Thirst lags in this environment. Sips through a hydration line under the mask if you can rig it; if not, planned stops in the cool corner, mask off, drink, mask on. Water and electrolytes, both. Carry more than the arithmetic says, because the arithmetic will assume a humidity that does not exist here.

**Wear long sleeves and long trousers** — not for warmth, for the dryness and the rock and the animals. But layer so you can strip as you cross from the cold wall to the warm side; the two ends of this room may differ by tens of degrees, and the shoulder-wide entrance means you start cold outside and finish roasting near the fissure.

**Do not go to the warm spot to get comfortable.** The warmest place in the room is the most toxic place in the room. That is the trap. The vent is pleasant for about ninety seconds and then it is a poisoning.

**Never touch the hot rock.** The fissure's lips and flanges are hot enough to shimmer, and the crusts there will be hot enough to burn through a glove. You will want to put a hand out for balance in the dark. This is why every handhold must be tested — tap, hover, then commit — and why your route must keep you away from that corner entirely.

---

## The floor is the second-most-dangerous thing in the room

The floor is not a floor. It is a jam of fresh slabs and scree, none of it settled, edges still glassy and sharp enough to open a glove. You will be crossing it in near-darkness, in a respirator that narrows your field of view, over a surface that shifts. This is where a competent traveler gets hurt — not by the gas, by a fall onto a blade of new rock.

**Stiff-soled boots**, nothing that flexes onto an edge; nothing soft enough for a slab to cut through the sole. **Cut-resistant gloves** you can still work in. **Ankle support**, because the scree will roll.

Move like you're on talus, because you are: weight over the feet, low, one deliberate step at a time, back foot loaded before the front foot commits. Test each slab. Never step onto a glassy plate at an angle. Keep to the wall line where the floor meets the wall if the slope allows, because that's usually the least mobile ground — but check it, because in a fresh fracture the wall line may be undercut and worse than the middle.

And listen for the low boom of a slab shifting. Except — you can't, because of the noise, which is the next section. Which is why:

**Rig a handline.** Not for climbing. For orientation, for steadiness on the scree, and above all to make sure you *cannot* wander into the hot corner in the dark. One fixed line from the squeeze, along the cool side, ending well short of the vent, with reflective tabs every few paces. You will not believe you need it in a room you can cross in forty paces. You will need it the moment your light is pointing the wrong way and the hiss is filling your head.

And **do not linger under anything overhanging.** The room is paused mid-collapse. It has been paused for decades and it may unpause while you are in it, and the volume of sound a slab makes when it lands in that chamber will tell you how much rock is thinking about moving.

---

## The noise is a fatigue weapon

The vent's hiss fills the room completely and the hard walls compound it. It is loud enough to be a hearing hazard over a long visit, so bring musician's plugs — enough attenuation to take the edge off, not so much that you're deaf. But the deeper problem is not your ears. It's that the noise **masks everything else.** You will not hear the slab shift until it has shifted. You will not hear your companion's voice. You will not hear the change in the vent's pitch that might have told you the cycle was turning.

So:

**Communication is by rope tug and light flash.** Agree the signals before you go in. Whistle if you like, but don't count on it carrying over that hiss.

**Do not rely on hearing for safety.** Rely on the gas monitor, on your eyes, on the handline, and on not being under things.

**Set a hard time limit, and set an alarm.** The noise and the density and the dry heat will wear you down faster than you expect, and worn-down people make bad calls in small rooms. Forty minutes of working time is generous. The alarm is not a suggestion.

## The animals: a courtesy, not a threat — but a real one to your equipment

Thousands of blind, shelled, salt-grazing things on every surface; hand-long black segmented things moving among them; a knocking in the walls; a floor of husks that will not rot because the air is too dry and too sterile for rot. You will be walking on the dead.

They are almost certainly not dangerous to you — blind, small, mineral-fed, uninterested in a warm body that is mostly water and noise. But three practical consequences:

**You cannot touch anything without killing something.** Every surface is a moving rind. A hand laid flat on a slab will crush dozens. Accept that you will crush some — you cannot cross the room otherwise — but minimize it: work from the edges inward, brush surfaces with a soft brush rather than a palm, and never lean. It costs you nothing and it keeps the record intact for whoever comes after you.

**The dust is not inert.** Desiccated husk and salt crust and mineral fines will be in the air you stir up. Your particulate filter handles it. Do not take the mask off in the chamber except for a planned drink in the cleanest corner you can find, and then keep your mouth closed and your face downwind.

**Do not let anything into a wound.** If you cut yourself on the rock — and you might — cover it immediately, glove back on, and clean it properly on exit. A hot, dry, salt-and-sulfur-coated, invertebrate-dense chamber is not a place to have an open abrasion.

## Light, and what to do with it

The room gives you almost nothing: dull red at the fissure, a faint sheen on shells, otherwise dark. Your eyes will never adapt because there isn't enough to adapt to. That means:

**Two independent headlamps, a hand torch, and spare cells**, all sealed against the salt and acid vapor, which will get into everything and eat the contacts. Zip-bags. A red filter on one lamp — not for the animals (they're blind, light means nothing to them) but for you: red light preserves your dark adaptation better and it is gentler on a room that is supposed to be dark, and the first thing you want to do in there is *not* flood it.

**And the first thing you want to do is sit still.**

That is the instruction I would underline twice. Enter, get clear of the squeeze, get to the cool corner, sit down, kill every light, and do nothing for ten minutes. Let your ears and your skin learn the room before your eyes try to. Feel the breath cross your ankles and then your face. Learn which wall is warm. Listen until the hiss stops being a wall and starts being a sound with a shape. You have a hard time limit and a hard reason to be efficient — but ten still minutes bought at the start will make the rest of the visit safer and better, because you will have been *told* the room's rhythm instead of guessing at it.

Then turn on the red lamp, and work outward from where you sit.

## Outside the crack

The outside is not benign either, and it's where you'll be most of the time.

**High, dry, thin, cold at night.** The air at the shoulder of the massif stings the throat; exertion is harder than it should be. Give yourself days to acclimatize before you plan the entry, and treat the climb to the crack as a real climb.

**The crack is hard to find.** A seam that is "not quite a cave mouth" on a bare massif is a navigation problem. GPS point, photograph it from three angles on the approach, cairn the route, and *fix an anchor outside the mouth* — a bolt or a solid natural horn — with the handline running from it. You want the outside person to be able to find the throat in the dark and in weather.

**Cache dry layers at the entrance, sealed.** You will come out wet with sweat that you could not feel accumulating, into cold thin air, at night. That is a hypothermia recipe. Dry insulating layer in a dry bag, change outside, before you do anything else. Do not try to dry anything inside the chamber; nothing dries in there, it only crusts.

**Decontaminate on exit.** The salt and sulfur crusts on your gear and your skin plus your own sweat make a mild chemical burn. Rinse hands and face with plenty of water — carry rinse water specifically for this, separate from drinking water — before you eat, before you touch your eyes, and before you handle anything you care about. Your electronics will need cleaning too. The vapor gets everywhere.

**And never go in alone.** The room is small and self-contained, which sounds safe and is the opposite: there is no refuge inside, no cell of clean air, no place to sit out a problem. Your safety margin has to live outside the crack. The partner stays at the throat with their own full kit, a rope, and a watch, and they know your time limit and they come in if you're late. That is the whole rescue plan, and it only works if the plan is agreed before the squeeze and honored without argument.

---

## What it will actually be like, so it doesn't surprise you

You will be ready for the heat and the darkness and the gas. What will get you is the *density.*

Forty paces across. You could throw a stone to the far wall. And yet everything is touching something — the rock to the animals, the animals to each other, the living to the dead, the salt to the stone — and there is no clean surface anywhere, no place to put a palm, no unoccupied square of floor. The claustrophobia will not come from the size. It will come from the saturation, and from the noise filling the space so completely that your own thoughts feel crowded. Combined with a respirator narrowing your vision and a hiss you cannot escape and a dryness pulling at your throat, it will tire you in a way that an open cave of ten times the size would not.

Which is why everything above reduces to four habits: **know the cycle, know your gas, know your footing, know your way out.** The room is loud and patient and it has been making itself for a long time without anyone's help. It does not need you. Go in at the trough, sit still before you move, keep to the cool side on the line, watch the monitor more than you watch the sights, and leave on the alarm rather than on your curiosity.

The fissure will keep breathing out after you're gone. That is the correct relationship to have with it.
