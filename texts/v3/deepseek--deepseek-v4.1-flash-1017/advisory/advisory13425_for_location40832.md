Prepare for the Kettle as for a **short, self-supported expedition into an active geothermal works with no trail, no shelter, no rescue, no potable water, and unreliable visibility**—not as for a hike. You are a soft, wet, heat-sensitive bag of water visiting a live steam plant that does not notice you.

**Before you go**

- **Do not go alone, and do not go without someone who knows geothermal terrain** if you are an ordinary traveler. Minimum two people; three or four is better. If you cannot get a guide or experienced partner, treat the Kettle as a no-go.
- **Scout remotely first.** Use whatever remote sensing, drone, or prior-visit data you can get for route, vent locations, slope, and the portal’s exact coordinates. Assume any map is stale: the Kettle rearranges its streams and terraces.
- **Set a hard turnaround time and a return window.** The place erases marks and has no landmarks that survive weather. Your portal must have a precise coordinate, a bearing, and a backup beacon. Do not rely on “I’ll recognize it.”
- **Check your health and fitness.** Altitude, wet cold, gas exposure, and cut-risk rock are all real. People with asthma, heart/lung disease, or poor cold tolerance should not go.
- **Calibrate a multi-gas monitor** before arrival: at minimum oxygen, H₂S, and CO₂/SO₂ if available. Add an escape respirator and eye wash. Sulfur smell is not a safety system; H₂S deadens the nose.
- **Plan for quarantine and decontamination on return.** The Kettle has a dense, punctate biosphere around warm seams. Do not bring back mineral dust, mats, insects, or unknown microbes on your gear.

**What to wear and carry**

- **Helmet, impact goggles, cut-resistant gloves.** The rock is fresh, glassy, and sharp. Cuts will be contaminated with mineral water; avoid them.
- **Abrasion-resistant clothing, gaiters, and a helmet-compatible hood.** The place is wet, cold, and sharp. Cotton is a bad idea. Bring spare dry layers in dry bags.
- **Boots with aggressive rubber and ankle support**, plus spare socks. Wet slick stone is the normal surface; near vents the ground can be hot and undercut. Trekking poles help you probe before you step. Add microspikes only if ice is present.
- **Waterproof shell and warm insulation.** The summit is inside cloud for hours; wet cold causes hypothermia even when the ground is body-warm. Manage sweat—warm ground plus shell can overheat you.
- **Headnet, buff, and glasses.** The fly-sheets near warm seams are dense enough to enter eyes, mouth, and nose. Flies are not the main hazard, but they are a distraction and a contamination route.
- **Bright outer layers.** Grey light, no shadows, and whiteout make visibility poor. Orange or red helps your partner find you.
- **All your water.** Do not drink streams, condensate, or vent water. It carries sulfur, salts, iron, and unknown biology. Bring 3–4 liters per person for a short day, more if you linger. Food in sealed containers; eat away from fly-sheets.
- **Navigation redundancy:** GPS with trackback, compass, altimeter, paper bearings, spare batteries, whistle, headlamp, and a satellite messenger or radio if your multiverse setup allows. Use a temporary route marker only if you will retrieve it; the Kettle will erase it, but not necessarily before you need it.
- **Emergency kit:** trauma dressings, irrigation syringe, antiseptic, blister kit, emergency bivy, space blanket, and a small rope or sling for steep bits. No fire ring; no stove needed. Pack out all waste.

**Route and timing**

- **Keep the visit short and close to the portal.** The Kettle is enormous and empty between warm seams; an hour of walking can put you in a place with no distinguishing feature. Do not “just go a little farther.”
- **Use high, dry, wind-scoured ground for rest and route decisions.** Avoid low hollows, vent caves, and the bottoms of gullies: heavier gases and steam collect there.
- **Approach warm seams from upwind and from above** where possible. Do not stand in steam, even briefly. Steam can scald lungs and skin, and the vent cave is a gas trap.
- **Probe every step near sinter terraces, mineral crusts, and warm ground.** Thin crust can hide hot water or a void. Do not walk on orange/white microbial mats—they are slick, living, and often underlain by fragile sinter.
- **Watch above as well as below.** Fresh faces shed rock; cooling rock cracks at night. Wear the helmet, do not linger under overhangs, and move one at a time through steep sections.
- **If you feel tremor or hear a sharp rock report, move away from steep faces and gullies to open ground** and expect rockfall. Do not run downhill into a gully.

**On-site rules**

1. **The warmth is the only wealth; distance from it is life or death for everything here.** For you, it is also the hazard: vents, hot water, steam, gas, and unstable sinter cluster around warm seams.
2. **Nothing is finished.** Test ground, expect changes, and do not trust yesterday’s route. The mountain is arriving, not eroding.
3. **Nothing is kept.** Do not carve, cairn, cache, or camp on a warm seam. If you must mark a route for hours, use removable flags and retrieve them.
4. **Do not sample, eat, or handle the biology.** Photograph from a distance if you must. Keep gloves on, do not touch your face, and decontaminate afterward.
5. **Gas alarm means move upwind and uphill, not downhill.** Do not investigate the smell. If you feel headache, nausea, dizziness, or unusual breathlessness, leave the area immediately.
6. **Whiteout means stop and orient.** The grey light kills depth perception. Use your compass bearing, headlamp at an angle to cast shadows, and poles. If you cannot fix your position, shelter in place rather than wander.
7. **No rescue is coming.** Your plan must be self-rescue: buddy checks, hard turnaround, spare layers, and the ability to sit out a whiteout.

**Emergency priorities**

- **Cut:** stop bleeding, irrigate with clean water, antiseptic, cover. Do not use local water on a wound.
- **Gas:** upwind, uphill, fresh air, monitor. Do not enter a hollow to “help” without respiratory protection.
- **Whiteout/lost:** stop, insulate from the wet ground, shelter from wind, whistle, use beacon. Do not descend into unknown terrain.
- **Hypothermia:** get out of wind and wet, replace layers, warm slowly. The warm ground is a temptation but not a shelter; it is wet, gassy, and unstable.

**On return**

Decontaminate boots, poles, and outer layers before you leave the portal area. Bag and quarantine gear if your multiverse rules require it. Leave no trash, no food, no markers, and no biological material. The Kettle will not remember you; make sure it has nothing to remember.
