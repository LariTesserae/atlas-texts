## Preparing for Hart’s Fold and the Lichen Rivers  
### A practical guide for an ordinary human multiverse traveler

This is not a place that will kill you with monsters. It will kill you—or quietly unmake your sense of self—through cold, wet, scale, disorientation, and a reality that does not hold still. Prepare as if for a remote cold-wet hiking expedition, a cultural visit to a small and self-contained community, and a long exposure to mild but persistent perceptual instability. Do not prepare as a conqueror, explorer-hero, or anomaly collector. Prepare as a guest who needs a local guide, redundant simple gear, and the humility to follow other people’s common sense.

### 1. Before you go

Train for the physical baseline: long days walking on slippery moss and wet bedrock, carrying a pack, scrambling gently, camping in cold damp conditions, and navigating without trusting electronics. Practice cold-weather layering, hypothermia recognition, foot care, water purification, map-and-compass work, and basic low-tech repair. If you cannot spend a night comfortably wet-cold and then walk out the next day, you are not ready for the field outside Hart’s Fold.

Arrange a local contact or guide before arrival if at all possible. Hart’s Fold is one settlement in a vast empty landscape; a guide is not a luxury. Agree on an insertion point outside town, preferably on stable high ground with old, clearly weathered topography—not in a seep channel, not under a frost-split cliff, not inside a cavern, not in the middle of a rain squall. Build in a twenty-four-hour settling buffer after arrival. Do not arrive at night or in a hurry.

Prepare psychologically. The silence—no birds, no animals, no rustle of small creatures—is a presence. The pale diffuse light, the immense horizon, and the constant low-grade reality slippage can produce anxiety, derealization, and a compulsive urge to stare at anomalies. Rehearse grounding routines. Plan short exposures, regular returns to town, and a companion or guide. Do not use alcohol or psychoactive substances to manage the strangeness. Memorize your emergency plan; do not rely solely on written notes that may change in your pocket.

Sterilize and clean your gear. There are no animals here, but the plant mat and microbial world are fragile. Do not bring live animals, seeds, soil, or unapproved plants. Avoid introducing invasive microbes or plant matter on boots and equipment.

### 2. What to bring

Bring layers, redundancy, and repairable simplicity. Wool or synthetic base layers, fleece or wool midlayers, a waterproof breathable shell, warm hat, gloves, buff, gaiters, and broken-in waterproof boots with aggressive tread. Avoid cotton. Bring spare dry socks and a way to dry them. The cold clings; stay dry and change layers before you are chilled. Earth-toned clothing is respectful and practical, but carry one small bright emergency marker.

Shelter: a low-profile, freestanding, waterproof tent or bivy with a sealed floor, a good insulated sleeping pad, and a sleeping bag rated for cold wet conditions. The ground is a wet sponge. Camp high and stable, away from channels and below overhangs. A tarp and emergency bivy are useful. Bring dry bags for everything.

Food and water: bring several extra days of high-calorie, no-cook food. Local food exists, but do not plan to forage or live off the land without guidance. Bring water purification—filter plus chemical backup plus a boil option. Local seep water may look clean and may be biologically active; purify it. Bring electrolytes, vitamin D, and protein. If invited to eat, accept cooked food, ask about ingredients, and avoid raw plants unless your host says otherwise. Keep your own food sealed.

Medical: first aid, blister care, antifungal powder, personal medications, painkillers, antihistamines, lip balm, spare glasses, and a hypothermia kit—emergency blanket, dry layers, high-calorie food. Bring a light climbing helmet if you will walk under frost-split rock. Bring a wooden or metal staff for slippery moss; do not rely on one fancy trekking pole that may become something else.

Tools: one good fixed-blade knife, kept discreet. Metal is rare and valuable here; do not display a shiny metal kit or treat metal as casual trade goods. Bring cordage, repair tape, sewing kit, waterproof matches and a ferro rod, a small stove if you can carry fuel discreetly, a headlamp with red mode and spare batteries, a whistle, a small mirror, and a mechanical watch if you need one. Bring a simple compass and durable maps, but do not trust them blindly.

Reality kit: carry several small pieces of worked stone—shaped by human hands—as local custom advises. Ask a guide to validate or give you one. Keep one on your body, one in your pack, and one with a companion. These are anchors and reminders, not magic shields. Carry redundant simple tools so that if one changes, you are not stranded. Do not bring drones, loud electronics, flashing gadgets, firearms, large amounts of metal wealth, or anything you cannot afford to lose or see transformed. Bring small gifts—salt, tea, sugar, good cloth, cordage, sewing needles—but ask before offering metal items.

### 3. Reality-instability protocols

Treat the local rules as safety regulations, not superstition.

Do not stare too long at water. When you collect water, look at the bank or your container, not the reflective surface. Use brief glances and peripheral vision.

Always carry a piece of worked stone. Check your anchor stones daily. If one changes shape or texture, stop and re-establish your baseline with a companion or guide.

If the path ahead looks too new—sharp stone edges, fresh woodsmoke in the air, unweathered surfaces, a suddenly clean channel—wait. Give it time to settle back into age. If it does not settle, retreat and find another route. Do not cross a threshold while it is shifting: a ford, a narrow gully, a bridge, a cave mouth.

Verify critical gear by function, not appearance. Test the knife on scrap, test the fire starter, test the filter. If a tool becomes unfamiliar, stop. Do not use an unknown object as if it were the object you packed. If it changes mid-use, put it down and reassess.

If you notice a time slip—the stone path suddenly fresh, different smoke, a valley that looks glacially young—stop. Stay put. Speak calmly. Compare notes with your companion. Wait for the world to settle. Return along the last stable route. Do not run, and do not chase the anomaly.

Speech may hang in the air as visible colored shapes. Keep your voice low and your words plain. Do not speak secrets, insults, or promises you cannot keep in open places. Avoid sarcasm and complicated idioms. If your words become visible, do not panic and do not try to grab them; let them dissolve. Ask locals about speech customs.

Do not pick up polished stones that show images unless a guide tells you to. If one is given to you, accept it carefully and do not demand more. Do not try to use such things to see the future or “fix” the past.

### 4. Arrival

Arrive in daylight, outside Hart’s Fold, on stable old ground. Wait. Observe from a distance. Check your body, your gear, and your anchor stones. Mark two or three large, old landscape features in your mind—not small cairns that may change. If you have a companion, agree on a rendezvous and a waiting protocol before you move.

Approach town slowly with your guide or with hands visible and a calm greeting. Do not walk into a home or cavern uninvited. Ask for the person who speaks for travelers or for a guide. Offer a small gift. Explain that you are a traveler who wishes to learn and to pass through respectfully, not to trade metal or collect anomalies unless invited.

### 5. Etiquette in Hart’s Fold

Keep your voice low. Do not fill the silence with noise. Ask before photographing, recording, or drawing people and places. Do not touch the water channels, the lichen mats, or the stonework without permission. The channels are streets and lifelines; do not block, pollute, or bathe in them. Use designated latrines or dig well away from water. Pack out your waste.

Do not make promises you cannot keep; visible words make that socially costly. Do not criticize local beliefs or call their world “unstable” or “fake.” They know. Learn a few phrases. Offer labor or fair trade. Respect closed caverns and private homes. Remove muddy boots if invited inside. Eat what is offered if you can, and ask about ingredients. Do not display metal wealth. If you must trade metal, ask first.

### 6. In the field

Travel with a guide. Keep excursions short. Plan half the distance you think you can cover; the scale is deceptive and the ground is slow. Carry rain gear always; local squalls are visible as grey curtains and can catch you across a valley. If one approaches, shelter; do not try to outrun it over moss.

Avoid camping in seep channels, on soft moss slopes, or under overhangs that may shed frost-split rock. Listen for the crack of splitting stone. Use a staff on moss and wet bedrock. Cross rivers only at established fords, with a pole and a guide. Do not swim or wade into the Lichen Rivers; the water is cold, the bottoms uncertain, and the reflective surfaces are exactly what locals warn you not to stare into.

There are no animals to warn you, no birdsong to mark weather changes, no scavengers to clean up waste. Use wind, water sound, and visibility. Carry a whistle for emergencies only; loud noise is startling in this silence. If lost, stop, shelter, stay warm, and make yourself visible. Do not wander into terrain that looks new. Wait for it to settle, then return to the last stable landmark or town.

### 7. Health and mind

Watch for hypothermia and trench foot. Keep feet dry, change socks, air boots, and treat blisters early. The damp cold and fungal conditions are persistent. Drink and eat even when you do not feel thirsty; the diffuse light and silence dull appetite. Take vitamin D.

Watch your mind. The absence of animal sound, the grey light, the immense horizon, and the quiet reality shifts can produce derealization. Maintain routines: sleep, eat, dry your gear, check anchors, talk to your companion, return to town. If you feel detached, compulsive, or panicky, sit down, touch a worked stone, name three stable things—the feel of your pack, the sound of seep water, the weight of your boots—breathe, and return to Hart’s Fold with your guide. Do not push deeper to prove you are fine.

### 8. Emergency and extraction

Agree on check-in times, a rendezvous, and a signal. If separated, return to the last stable landmark or town and wait; do not search into new-looking terrain. If injured, shelter, warm yourself, splint, and send your companion for help. If alone, use your whistle or mirror sparingly and stay put.

If your gear transforms, stop. Do not use an unknown tool. If a path shifts, wait or retreat. If you fall into a lichen river, get out, get dry, get warm, and wash; assume the water is cold and contaminated. If you lose your anchor stone, re-establish with a guide and shorten your range.

Leave with a margin. Do not exit during a squall or at dusk. Allow an extra day for the world to settle. Thank your hosts, trade fairly, and do not promise to return unless you mean it.

### Bottom line

Prepare for Hart’s Fold as for a cold, wet, roadless upland visited with a local guide, plus a cultural stay in a quiet, integrated town, plus a persistent low-grade reality hazard. Bring layers, shelter, water purification, redundant simple tools, anchor stones, gifts, and humility. Keep trips short. Follow local rules. Do not stare at the water. Carry worked stone. Wait when the path looks too new. And remember: the land is not hostile—it is old, indifferent, and enormous, and you are a small warm creature who needs to stay dry.
