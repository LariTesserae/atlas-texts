Treat this as an abyssal expedition and first-contact problem, not as “a swim.” The default safe plan is: **do not enter the water in person unless you must**; observe from a sealed pod and use a drone. If you insist on going in, go as a sealed, dim, slow, sterile observer who can be extracted without ever needing to “surface.”

### 1. Before you arrive
- **Confirm the physics of arrival.** Find out the pressure, temperature, water chemistry, gravity, current speeds, and whether any surface actually exists. A portal that opens at depth without pressure equalization will kill you before you notice. Arrive inside a pressure-rated pod or hard suit, not in a soft diving suit, and not by holding your breath.
- **Pick a boring arrival point.** A stable open plaza or wide passage, away from dense dwellings, fungal crops, and fragile architecture. Anchor a return portal/pod there. Do not arrive inside a tunnel, under an arch, or in kelp.
- **Assume no rescue from above.** The “surface” is a myth. Your only reliable exit is the portal/pod you brought. Set an outside monitor, a fixed extraction time, and a lost-contact protocol before you go.
- **Train for the environment.** Practice in cold, dark, confined water; drysuit or hard-suit movement; rebreather drills; low-light navigation; and sensory deprivation. If you panic in darkness or confinement, do not go. This place will strip away horizon, sky, weather, and time cues.
- **Medical prep.** Clear your ears/sinuses and cardiovascular fitness. Plan for barotrauma, hypothermia, CO₂ buildup, and decompression only if you use an ambient-pressure suit. A hard atmospheric suit is better; it keeps you at one atmosphere and avoids decompression, but it must be rated for the external pressure.

### 2. What to bring
- **A pressure-rated hard suit or small submersible with an airlock.** Soft neoprene and ordinary scuba gear are not enough. You need redundant breathing gas, scrubbers, power, heating, buoyancy control, and a sealed waste system.
- **Closed-circuit breathing.** No bubbles. Bubbles rise toward a “ceiling” these people may regard as myth; they also announce you and disturb the water. Filter or contain your exhaust. Do not exhale into the environment if you can avoid it.
- **Active thermal protection.** The cold is absolute. Use a drysuit with active heating, insulated undergarments, good gloves, and redundant batteries. Monitor fingers, toes, and core temperature.
- **Dim, adjustable light.** Their world is lit by faint blue-green bioluminescence. Bright white light will blind, shock, and possibly damage their light cultures. Use wide, low-intensity beams, red/amber if it helps preserve your night vision, and keep spotlights for brief checks. Carry chemical backup lights. Remember: **light is precious here**.
- **Navigation that does not trust your body.** No horizon means vertigo. Use inertial navigation, sonar, a 3D map, and a tether to the pod/portal. Mark your route. Do not free-swim into the labyrinth. Keep clear of kelp fronds and narrow passages that could entangle or collapse.
- **Acoustic communication.** Radio will not work well underwater. Use low-power ultrasonic/acoustic comms, bone conduction, and a tether line with signal pulses. Keep sound low; their world is quiet.
- **Sealed food, water, and waste.** Do not eat or drink local material. Do not leave scraps. Sterilize your suit exterior before and after. You are a walking package of foreign microbes; the people and fungal crops have been isolated for a very long time.
- **Nonthreatening tools.** No loud thrusters, no flashing lights, no grappling hooks tearing at the coral-like stone. Use a small drone with a manipulator if you need close inspection. Do not collect souvenirs without quarantine.

### 3. How to move
- **Slowly.** The whole world is patient. Sudden movements, thruster wash, and bright lights will make you a monster or a disturbance. Move at the pace of the current.
- **Do not touch the architecture.** It is ancient, porous, softened, and possibly unstable. Do not climb it, anchor to it casually, or enter tunnels that look like mouths. Use neutral buoyancy and keep clearance.
- **Respect the currents.** They are slow but persistent and can carry you into the city’s maze. Plan drift, keep your tether, and do not fight a current until you know where it goes.
- **Avoid the crops and light bowls.** The fungal gardens and bioluminescent cultures are their food, light, and livelihood. Do not harvest, step on, or “improve” them. Do not bring a brighter alien light source as a casual gift; it could disrupt their economy and cosmology.

### 4. Meeting the people
- They are small, pallid, webbed, slow-moving, and numerous. You will be a large, sealed, warm-blooded object in their dark. Do not tower over them, crowd them, or approach with a bright helmet lamp.
- **Observe first.** Stay at a distance. Let them see you are slow and nonaggressive. Learn their gestures, tones, and light signals. Their speech is low murmurs; your acoustic gear should record rather than broadcast loudly.
- **Do not play missionary.** Their rule — “the world above is a story and the world below is forever” — is not a superstition to correct. You are a guest. If they ask where you came from, be gentle and do not claim divinity or promise rescue.
- **Follow their rules visibly:** conserve light, tend the crops, respect the currents. If you are offered food or a place to rest, decline politely unless you have verified it is safe for you; do not pretend to eat.
- **Do not introduce technology casually.** A durable lamp or metal tool could be a miracle or a catastrophe. If contact deepens, let them set the pace. Your first gift should be restraint.

### 5. Psychological preparation
- The absence of horizon, sun, weather, and animals will feel like sensory starvation. Keep a schedule: sleep, exercise, check-ins, voice logs, music or recordings, and a fixed “day.” Watch for vertigo, panic, obsessive thoughts, and the urge to swim upward.
- Accept that time here is slow. A visit that feels endless is normal. Do not rush the people or the place. If you feel disoriented, return to the tether and pod immediately.
- Have an abort rule: suit breach, lost tether, lost comms, rising CO₂, heater failure, or persistent vertigo means leave. Do not “push on.”

### 6. Exit and aftercare
- Exit at the pod/portal, not by swimming up. If you used an ambient-pressure suit, follow decompression plans. If you used a hard suit, check the seal and pressure before opening.
- Quarantine your suit, tools, and any samples. Assume you are contaminated and that you may have contaminated them. Medical check for hypothermia, barotrauma, and oxygen/CO₂ issues.
- Leave no waste, no light cultures, no tools, no microbes if you can help it.

Bottom line: you are a fragile air-breathing warm-blooded creature visiting a cold, high-pressure, lightless, inhabited ocean. Go sealed, dim, slow, tethered, sterile, and humble — or send a drone. If you cannot guarantee pressure protection, breathing redundancy, thermal control, navigation, and a reliable exit, do not go in person. The deep is not empty. It is a patient, ancient house, and you would be the loudest thing in it.
