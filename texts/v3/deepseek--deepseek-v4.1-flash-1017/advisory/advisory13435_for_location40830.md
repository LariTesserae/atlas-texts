## Preparation doctrine: brief, sealed, tethered, monitored, reversible

My intuition says treat the Scaffold of False Dawn as a combination of **near-vacuum high altitude, geothermal unstable ground, toxic volcanic gas, razor-sharp glassy terrain, sensory-deprivation conditions, and an unknown artifact/agency site**. It is not obviously hostile. It is worse in a way: it is *indifferent*. Nothing there will negotiate with you, warn you, or rescue you. An ordinary human can visit only by carrying their entire survivable environment with them and by keeping the visit short.

### 1. First decision: do not go unless you can bring your own air, heat, light, navigation, and return

The “air thins to almost nothing.” That means a filter mask or oxygen bottle is not enough. You need a **full pressure suit or equivalent sealed pressure garment** with independent life support. A simple respirator will not prevent hypoxia, decompression injury, or exposure to sulfurous vapor. If you cannot provide a pressure suit with redundant oxygen and CO₂ scrubbing, do not step out of the gate.

You also need a return method you can operate yourself, with redundancy. The ground moves. If your gate or portal is anchored only to the crust, assume it can tilt, shear, or be swallowed. Put the return device on a **portable, wide-footprint platform** that distributes weight and can be re-leveled. Test the return before every excursion. Keep a home-side team monitoring you. Do not rely on local rescue, because there is none.

### 2. Remote reconnaissance first

Before any human entry, send a drone or robotic probe if you have one. Measure:

- pressure and oxygen content
- CO₂, CO, SO₂, H₂S, volatile metals, and other gases
- temperature gradient: ground vs. air
- radiation and dosimetry
- gravity and local physics anomalies
- ground stability, crust thickness, seam activity
- radio/comms behavior under the mineral ceiling
- whether the amber ceiling veins are radioactive, hot, or fragile

Do not “test” the air by smelling it. The sulfurous smell of struck matches and hot metal is a warning, not a permission slip. H₂S can deaden your sense of smell at dangerous concentrations. Use instruments.

### 3. Personal and team preparation

Go with at least two people on-site and one person monitoring from base or home. Solo visitation is a bad idea in a place where the ground cracks underfoot and the silence can distort judgment.

Physical preparation should include:

- pressure-suit certification and practice
- confined-space and airlock drills
- gas-alarm, suit-breach, and decontamination drills
- fitness for uneven ground and carrying emergency gear
- medical screening for respiratory, cardiac, ear/sinus, and claustrophobia issues
- practice with the exact navigation and communication gear you will use

If you are using a pressure suit from a different pressure environment, pre-breathe oxygen as appropriate and use an airlock. Do not rush pressure transitions.

### 4. Equipment you should carry

Minimum sensible kit:

- full pressure suit with positive-pressure sealing
- redundant oxygen and CO₂ scrubbers, plus emergency oxygen
- thermal control for both cold face/air and warm ground/feet
- heat- and abrasion-resistant boots with insulated soles, plus wide-footprint overboots or snowshoe-like load spreaders
- hard helmet with faceplate, anti-fog, neutral-white headlamp, floodlight, and backup lights
- gas sensors, radiation dosimeter, temperature and pressure monitors
- inertial navigation, laser rangefinder, 3D mapper, and radio beacons
- tethered comms or repeaters if the mineral ceiling blocks radio
- emergency beacon and deadman alarm
- long probe staff and non-sparking walking poles
- portable platform, airlock/habitat, and decon kit
- first aid, burn kit, trauma kit, spare suit patches, and repair tape
- sealed food, water, waste collection, and habitat filters
- spare batteries for everything
- sealed sample containers only if sampling is authorized

Everything should be treated as potentially contaminated on return. The dust is abraded stone, not harmless dust. It may be sharp, acidic, radioactive, or otherwise unknown. Do not bring it into your habitat or home environment without decontamination.

### 5. Arrival and base selection

Do not step out immediately. Take readings from the gate or suit. Choose an entry point on **thick, stable-looking crust**, away from:

- fresh seams
- vapor vents
- sheared shelves
- pillar arrangements
- channels cut into the ground
- overhanging cracked ceiling
- low spots where heavy gases might collect

Put the return gate and habitat on a portable platform with wide legs. Monitor tilt, vibration, and seam activity. Keep the gate powered and ready. Mark a safe perimeter with radio beacons rather than paint or flags, because the ground may swallow or move visual markers. Keep artificial lighting on a schedule. The amber glow is not daylight and should not be trusted as a clock.

Do not camp on bare crust. Do not sleep near pillars. Do not use local stone as an anchor or building material.

### 6. Rules for moving on the plain

Move slowly. Test the ground with a probe before putting weight on it. Spread your weight. Do not run, jump, or gather in one spot. Avoid thin crust, warm fissures, vapor, and the channels around pillars.

Stay away from the pillar structures. They hum. They are warm. They suggest intent without showing tool marks. That combination is exactly the kind of thing you should **not touch, climb, lean on, sample, rearrange, or “finish.”** Photograph from a distance. If the hum changes when you approach, back away. Treat them as possible vents, resonators, artifacts, or something with an unknown function. Do not follow the channels as if they were script.

Keep your suit sealed. Do not eat or drink outside the habitat. Do not breathe local air even in small amounts. Do not dig. Do not use open flames or sparking tools. The vapor may be acidic or flammable, and the ground may be a thin scab over something hot.

Use your own neutral-white lights. The amber bioluminescence is enough to know you are not blind, not enough to see by. It also ruins color perception and can make depth judgment worse. Rely on instruments, not echoes; the sound behavior is wrong for distance.

Monitor for:

- increasing crack frequency
- new vapor
- changes in pillar hum
- rising ground temperature
- seismic vibration
- gas alarms
- overhead rockfall
- your own confusion, nausea, or euphoria

If any of those escalate, retreat to base. The place is “still deciding,” and you do not want to be standing on the decision.

### 7. Time, silence, and psychology

There is no day. There is no wind, water, or life. The silence is a held breath, broken by sudden flat cracks and chest-felt hums. That is a recipe for sensory deprivation, time distortion, pareidolia, and dread. An ordinary human may start interpreting the pillars, the pulse of the ceiling, or the silence as meaningful. Maybe it is. But you should not act on that interpretation while you are there.

Use clocks. Keep a schedule. Check in by voice at fixed intervals. Sleep in the habitat, not on the plain. Use artificial light cycles to maintain some circadian structure. Give each person a task and a hard turnaround time. If someone becomes confused, euphoric, irritable, or panicky, abort. Hypoxia and CO₂ buildup can feel like calm or insight. Trust the monitors.

### 8. Emergency rules

- **Suit breach:** seal it, go to backup oxygen, abort immediately.
- **Gas alarm:** move away from the source, keep sealed, decontaminate, abort.
- **Ground cracks under you:** do not run. Step back along tested ground, probe ahead, return to base.
- **Lost or comms lost:** stop, beacon, use inertial navigation back to base if safe; do not wander following echoes or pillar landmarks.
- **Seismic or hum escalation:** evacuate to the gate.
- **Injury:** buddy-drag on a sled or platform; do not try to “walk it off” on unstable crust.
- **Gate failure:** shelter in the habitat, conserve oxygen, beacon home, and wait for extraction. Do not wander.

### 9. Decontamination and departure

Before entering the habitat or returning home, vacuum, brush, and bag the outer suit. Filter habitat intakes. Seal any samples. Quarantine yourself and your gear. Treat the site as potentially contaminated with unknown particulates. Leave no trace: pack out waste, do not mark or alter pillars, do not leave biological material.

### Bottom line

An ordinary human should visit the Scaffold of False Dawn as a **short, sealed, tethered, instrumented reconnaissance**—not an expedition, not a camp, not a pilgrimage. Bring your own pressure, air, heat, cold protection, light, navigation, communications, and return. Stay away from the pillars, the seams, the vapor, and the thin crust. Do not try to finish the place, interpret its script, or trust its quiet. My intuition is that the correct visit is brief, respectful, heavily monitored, and reversible. If you cannot make it reversible, do not go.
