Treat The Kettle as a **hot, saturated, living industrial wetland**—not a ruin, not a garden, not a machine room. It is a closed loop of heat, water, roots, and old machinery that has been running without people for decades. An ordinary human can visit it, but only as a short, supervised, no-interference reconnaissance. The place is not malicious; it is indifferent, and indifference plus heat plus old iron plus dense vegetation is enough to hurt you.

## First rule: go as a team, not as a tourist

Minimum useful team: **two people inside the basin and one line-tender/guardian on the rim** at the portal or entry point. More people are not better—more feet in the plant mass means more entanglement, more damage, more confusion. Nobody goes alone below the rim. If you are alone, stay on the rim and look.

Before descending, do a rim recon. Stand where the description says to stand: above the oval, looking down into the cupped hand. Use that view to mark the pump house, the footbridge, the central pool, the steam drift, the likely exit line, and the places you will not go. Take photographs from the rim. Sketch it. Once you are under the leaf roof, your sense of the basin will collapse into wet green walls and noise.

## Check the basics before you trust the air

Test the local environment rather than assuming Earth-normal physics and atmosphere. Check gravity, temperature, humidity, and time flow if your multiverse kit can. More importantly, carry a **multi-gas monitor** for oxygen, carbon monoxide, carbon dioxide, combustible gas, and hydrogen sulfide. There is an open fire in a half-sunk boiler under a dense plant canopy; that is a recipe for poor local air near the works. If you do not have a gas monitor, do not go below the rim. If the monitor alarms, leave immediately—do not finish the photo, do not investigate the alarm.

Use a **P100 half-face or full-face respirator** with nuisance/acid-gas protection if available. The air is saturated with water, spores, steam, and fly-sized life near the pump house. A full-face respirator also protects your eyes from steam and drips, but it will fog unless you manage it. Bring anti-fog, spare filters, and a dry bag. If you have a powered air-purifying respirator, humidity will punish it; keep spare batteries dry.

If gas readings are marginal at the rim, do not descend. The basin is open at the top, but the plant mass and walls trap air. Stay upwind of the boiler when you can determine wind direction from steam drift.

## Dress for a hot swamp with old iron in it

You will be wet within a minute. Not rained on—**condensed on**. Everything you wear will be damp, so avoid cotton. Wear quick-dry synthetic base layers and a lightweight long-sleeved coverall with tight cuffs. Long sleeves protect against scratches, warm sap, and flies; loose straps and dangling cords are entanglement hazards around the flywheel, roots, and vines. Tie your hair back. Remove jewelry.

Footwear: **knee-high waterproof boots with aggressive tread**, preferably puncture-resistant. The water table is at ankle height nearly everywhere, the central pool is knee-deep, and the moss is slippery. Add gaiters if you have them. Carry a spare pair of dry socks and dry clothes outside the basin; you will want them.

Hands: cut-resistant gloves with a nitrile underlayer. Do not use bare hands to push through the plant mass. Do not use the warm pipes or roots as handholds; they may be hot, sharp with mineral crust, or load-bearing in ways you do not understand.

Head: **helmet with chin strap**, headlamp, and face shield or goggles. The pump house has three standing walls and one fallen inward; falling brick and rusted metal are real. The ladder bolted to the wall goes up to nothing—do not climb it.

Ears: filtered earplugs. The fire roars low, the flywheel groans, the boiler surges with a bass note you feel in your sternum, and the dripping is dense as static. You will raise your voice without meaning to. Use hand signals and a whistle. Keep your hearing for alarms and changes in the machinery, but protect it from the constant load.

## Carry analog backups

Humidity kills electronics and paper. Put maps, notes, and spare batteries in dry bags. Bring a waterproof headlamp and at least two chemical lights. A phone or tablet will fog, overheat, or die; do not rely on it. Use a compass, a whistle, a signal mirror, and a written check-in schedule. If you have multiverse comms, test them at the rim and set a missed-check-in extraction trigger. Radios may be swallowed by the plant mass and the noise; use rope signals as backup.

Minimum safety kit: fixed rope and harness, short tether with quick-release, probe pole, rescue knife in a sheath, first aid with burn and trauma supplies, eyewash, sealed drinking water, electrolytes, cooling packs, thermal camera or infrared thermometer, and a small waterproof notebook. If you suspect combustible gas, use intrinsically safe lights and radios. No smoking, no open flame, no sparking tools.

## Choose your time and exposure limit

The place runs on a ninety-second cycle: surge, lift, settle, drip. Use it. Move slowly. Stand still during a surge if you are on uncertain footing or near the bridge. Do not fight the rhythm.

Visit in the morning or a cooler part of the outside day if you can, but understand the basin itself is warm and saturated all the time. Toward evening the leaf tips weep and the dripping becomes one of the loudest things there; that means more water, more noise, more fogged lenses, and worse footing. Avoid evening if you need visibility.

Set a hard exposure limit: **15–30 minutes inside the plant mass for light work**, less if you are carrying gear or working hard. In saturated heat, sweat does not evaporate efficiently, and you can overheat without realizing it. Pre-hydrate. Drink sealed water and electrolytes. Do not drink the local water, even filtered, except in a survival emergency you should have avoided. Take work-rest breaks on the rim, not inside. Watch your buddy for confusion, headache, nausea, cramps, or stopping sweating. If someone is confused, assume heat illness or gas and evacuate.

## Entry: rope, probe, line, and no heroics

Anchor your fixed line outside the basin, on stable ground, not on the soft red stone lip if it looks crumbly. Keep the portal or return point outside the basin, upwind, and clear of runoff. Do not place it on the rim where slumping stone or plant growth can reach it. Leave the guardian at the anchor with the line and a whistle.

Descend carefully. The rim is rounded and soft-looking, but it is stone; test holds. The plant roof is **not ground**. Do not walk out onto the leaf canopy, especially over the central pool. It is a roof over a volume of packed stems, roots, and water. Treat the plant mass as a swamp with a false floor. Probe ahead. Distribute your weight. Do not run. Do not jump from root to root.

The footbridge of rusted grate is half-swallowed and may be crossable if you trust it. Do not trust it quickly. Inspect from the bank. Probe the grate. Test it without committing your full weight. Cross one person at a time, on a tether, with hands free. Do not jump. Do not crawl on rusted edges. If it shifts, sags, or the plant mass around it lifts abnormally during the surge, turn back. The bridge is not worth the pump house.

Avoid wading the central pool unless there is no alternative. It is warm, mineral, and circulating, but it is also full of hidden roots, pipes, sumps, and slippery biofilm. Near the boiler, the water may be hotter than your boots like. Probe temperature and depth. Do not swim. If you fall in, do not thrash—thrashing wraps vines and roots around limbs. Get to the bridge, a known root mat, or the bank, and signal.

## Inside: look, do not fix

The pump house is the destination and the main hazard. The flywheel is taller than a person and still turning—a quarter turn per surge. It is slow, but slow machinery is strong machinery. Keep out of the plane of its rotation. Do not touch the spokes, do not lean on the rim, do not put anything through it. Loose clothing, tether, or backpack strap near that wheel is an entanglement accident waiting to happen.

The boiler is hot, half-sunk, and still burning. The fire is fed by a sap line that drips resin into the fuel intake. Do not touch the sap line. Do not cut it. Do not block it. Do not open the boiler. Do not add fuel. Do not try to extinguish the fire. Do not turn the valve wheel grown through by a stem; it will not turn, and forcing it may break something in a system you do not understand. The pipes leaving the flywheel are hot and swallowed by roots. Do not use them as handholds. Stay upwind of steam. Use a thermal camera or infrared thermometer to find hot surfaces before you lean or step.

The walls are unstable. Do not enter the pump house if the standing brick looks ready to shed. Do not climb the ladder to nothing. Do not stand under loose brick. Keep your helmet on. The foundations of the other structures are trip hazards under moss; probe before stepping.

The plant mass is warm to the touch and moves every ninety seconds. It is not attacking you. Do not press your palm against roots to admire the warmth for long; warm surfaces mean hot pipes and steam nearby. Do not reach into gaps. There is no empty gap—there are stems, roots, hot pipes, and possibly steam. Do not cut vegetation to make a path unless you are actually trapped. Cutting releases sap, damages the loop, and may destabilize load-bearing root structures. Use your arms, a probe, and patience.

The sap/resin is not for touching. If it gets on your skin, wash it off with clean water and soap outside. Keep it out of eyes and mouth. The blind isopods in the runoff channels are harmless; the flies near the steam are a nuisance, not a reason to panic. But if you see anything larger than the described animal life—birds, mammals, large insects, something with intent—abort and reassess. This basin is not supposed to support larger animals.

## Sound and the two clocks

The constant working noise is your baseline. The fire roars low. The flywheel groans. The boiler surges every ninety seconds. The dripping ticks and plops. The steam hisses through a leaf-lung somewhere. The stems creak wetly.

If the noise stops, or changes in a way you cannot explain—flywheel stops, boiler roar changes pitch, plant mass stops lifting, water level rises, steam release begins—**leave**. Do not investigate. A stable loop is not your project. An unstable loop is not your fight.

Use the ninety-second cycle to time movement, not to hypnotize yourself. The rise and fall of the plant roof can be disorienting; it looks like breathing. It is breathing, in a sense, but not at you. Keep your timer, keep your buddy in sight, keep the line tended, keep your exit route in mind.

## Abort criteria

Leave immediately if any of these happen:

- Gas monitor alarms, or you feel drowsy, confused, or headachy.
- Steam release or visibility drops suddenly.
- Flywheel stops, speeds up, or makes a new grinding noise.
- Boiler roar changes or fire becomes visible outside the firebox.
- Plant mass stops its ninety-second lift, or lifts much more than a few centimeters.
- Water level rises, or water near you becomes hot through your boots.
- The bridge shifts, sags, or the plant mass around it moves unusually.
- A wall sheds brick or you hear structural cracking.
- Your buddy shows heat illness, confusion, nausea, or stops sweating.
- Your line fouls and you cannot free it quickly.
- You become disoriented and cannot see your line or buddy.
- Anyone on the team says they want to leave. That is enough.

## Exit and decontamination

Leave before you are tired. The exit is harder wet, hot, and deafened. Follow the line. Do not take a “shortcut” through the plant mass. Once out, sit on the rim, drink, cool down, and dry off. Remove wet clothing and put on dry layers outside the basin.

Rinse boots, gloves, and gear. Bag anything that touched sap or local water. Wash exposed skin with clean water and soap. Check for cuts, scratches, and sap contact. Flush eyes if anything dripped or splashed. Monitor everyone for heat illness and respiratory irritation over the next hours. If you are operating under multiverse protocols, quarantine your gear, check for hitchhiking isopods or flies, and log the visit.

Do not bring plant material, water, or sap back unless you have explicit authorization and sterile containment. Do not introduce outside plants, animals, food waste, or microbes into the basin. The loop is closed. You are a visitor, not a repair crew, not a rescuer, not a gardener.

## The short version

Approach from the rim. Keep your portal outside the basin, upwind, on stable ground. Bring gas monitoring, respiratory protection, waterproof lights, rope, helmet, gloves, boots, earplugs, and a buddy. Descend on a line. Treat the leaf roof as false ground. Probe every step. Avoid the central pool. Cross the bridge only if you must, one at a time, tethered. Stay clear of the flywheel, boiler, sap line, steam, and unstable walls. Do not turn, cut, open, feed, block, or fix anything. Move on the ninety-second cycle. Limit exposure to minutes, not hours. Leave if the noise changes. Decontaminate outside.

You can visit The Kettle. You cannot linger in it. Prepare to be wet, hot, deafened, and disoriented, and leave the loop exactly as you found it—still breathing, still turning, still closed.
