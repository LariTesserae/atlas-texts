Prepare for the Sunken Grove as if you were visiting a humid cave, a fragile greenhouse, and a shrine all at once. It is not a hike. It is a vertical descent into a warm, wet, enclosed living pocket where the main dangers are not monsters but slip, heat, stagnant water, spores, disorientation, and disrespect. The grove is patient and indifferent; your job is to be brief, quiet, redundant, and hard to notice.

## 1. Before you commit

**Get local intelligence first.** Ask guides, locals, or other multiverse travelers about:
- Whether entry is permitted, taboo, or watched.
- Recent rain and how the sump behaves.
- Air quality — sinkholes can trap gases or have low oxygen.
- The root-ladder: is it living root, rope, or both? Has anyone fallen?
- The three-fingered handprint and the stone arc: are they known? Sacred? Avoided?
- Local fauna in the sump: how large, how aggressive, how curious.

**Verify the basics of the world.** As an ordinary human, do not assume Earth-standard gravity, atmosphere, microbes, or time flow. Confirm breathable air, temperature, gravity, return-window stability, and whether local biology is hazardous. Establish a fixed return point and a check-in schedule.

**Go with at least one partner and a local guide if possible.** One person can stay at the rim with the rope and communication. Do not solo the descent.

**Train for the actual strain.** You need to be able to climb a wet rope or root-ladder with a pack, in heat, with poor grip, and then climb back out tired. If you are claustrophobic, heat-intolerant, or poor with slippery exposure, do not go.

**Choose timing carefully.** Daytime is safest: the light above is strongest, the waxy flowers open, and navigation is easiest. Avoid immediately after rain; the rock will be slick and the sump rising. Wait for a drier window if you can. A night visit is possible for the bioluminescence, but only with a guide, redundant lights, and a very conservative exit plan.

## 2. Gear that survives heat, wet, and enclosed space

Think lightweight, quick-drying, redundant, and waterproof.

**Clothing**
- Quick-dry long pants and long-sleeve shirt. Covering protects against insects, scratches, and moss contact, but must breathe.
- Permethrin-treated clothing if available; minimal skin repellent. Avoid strong scents.
- High socks, gaiters if you have them, spare dry socks in a dry bag.
- Light rain shell for above; inside it will mostly trap heat, so use sparingly.
- Spare dry layer sealed for the exit. You will be wet and cooled once you climb out.

**Footwear and hands**
- Sticky-rubber approach shoes or boots with ankle support and aggressive tread. Not smooth soles; not heavy boots that stay waterlogged.
- Thin grippy gloves for rope and rock, fingerless if you need dexterity. Spare dry gloves.
- Helmet with chin strap — for drops, roots, and accidental knocks in a tight shaft.

**Vertical safety**
- Climbing harness, helmet, static rope or fixed line, personal anchor, prusiks, carabiners, descender/ascender as appropriate.
- Pre-rig and inspect the descent line from above. Do not trust moss-covered roots, sagging vines, or wet ledges without testing. Use redundant anchors and rope protection. One person on the rope at a time.
- Pad anchors so you do not damage living roots. Leave no trace, but do not sacrifice safety to politeness.

**Light and navigation**
- Waterproof headlamp with red filter, spare batteries, and a second light. The fungi glow is not enough to see by.
- Watch, compass or local equivalent, whistle, small mirror. Do not rely on phone signal.
- Waterproof notebook and pencil; camera in a dry bag with desiccant. Condensation will be constant.
- Mark the rim anchor and your route. The sinkhole is small, but vertical spaces disorient.

**Health and water**
- Bring your own water and electrolytes. Do not drink the sump. Do not eat anything from the grove.
- First aid: antihistamines, blister care, antifungal powder, wound cleaning, antibiotic ointment, personal meds. If you have mold or spore sensitivity, bring a lightweight respirator or at least a buff; be aware humidity makes masks unpleasant.
- Small gas monitor if local conditions justify it; otherwise know the symptoms of bad air — headache, dizziness, unusual breathlessness — and leave immediately.
- Pack out all waste. No soap, urine, or food waste in the sump.

**Recording and respect**
- Red light, no flash unless permitted. Photograph the handprint, stone arc, and worn ledge from a distance. Do not touch, trace, rearrange, or collect.
- No drones, music, loud voices, or bright white light except emergency.
- Small group. Keep your impact to the established path and durable surfaces.

## 3. The descent

Treat the root-ladder as the crux. Inspect it from above. Use a fixed safety line and harness rather than free-climbing wet roots. Descend slowly, one at a time, with the person above spotting and the rope managed. Note the waterline marks on the wall as you go down; compare them with the sump. If the water has risen since the last known mark, or rain begins above, shorten the visit or leave.

At the bottom, stop and listen before you move. Let your eyes adjust. Keep your exit route clear and your rope untangled. Stay out of the sump and away from its edge; do not lean over it, splash it, or put your hands or feet in. If a smooth-backed shape surfaces, freeze, then withdraw slowly without shining a light directly into the water or cornering it. The grove notices; do not make yourself interesting.

## 4. Behavior inside

Move slowly and speak softly. The grove is loud only with small sounds; your voice will carry strangely. Use red light. Stay on the worn path or durable rock where possible, but do not widen it. Do not sit on the smoothed ledge, step on the stone arc, or touch the ochre handprint. Those are marks of use, not conveniences. If you feel watched, treat that as information, not an invitation. Keep your exit open and do not block the sump-to-fungus path.

Do not handle the beetles or frogs. Do not pick the flowers or fungi. Do not cut living roots or vines. Watch for slick moss, hidden holes, and loose rock under the green. The moss can sink to the second knuckle; test holds before trusting them. The heat will make you sweat without evaporative relief. Drink before you are thirsty, take electrolytes, and watch each other for heat exhaustion.

## 5. Emergency rules

Leave early if:
- The sump is rising or rain has started above.
- You feel dizzy, headachey, or short of breath beyond the heat.
- The rope or anchor looks compromised.
- Someone is overheating, panicking, or exhausted.
- You cannot keep the exit route clear.

Your ascent will be harder than the descent. Save energy for it. Do not wait until dark or fatigue to climb out. If someone cannot ascend, send for help rather than improvising a rescue in a wet shaft.

## 6. After you leave

Dry off, change into dry layers, and check everyone for cuts, bites, leeches, rash, and spore irritation. Clean and dry gear; quarantine or sterilize it according to multiverse biosecurity rules so you do not carry grove organisms elsewhere. Record what you saw, but respect local confidentiality. Debrief with your guide. If you touched the sump water or were scratched, clean the wound and monitor for infection.

## The short version

Go with a guide and a partner. Bring a helmet, harness, fixed line, red headlamp, dry bags, quick-dry covered clothing, sticky boots, water, electrolytes, first aid, and redundant lights. Choose a dry window, descend one at a time, note the waterline, stay out of the sump, do not touch the handprint or stones, do not collect anything, move slowly and quietly, and leave before you are tired or the water rises. Prepare to be a guest in a place that keeps its own time — and do not mistake patience for welcome.
