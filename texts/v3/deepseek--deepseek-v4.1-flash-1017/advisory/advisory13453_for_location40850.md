Treat this place as a high-altitude Antarctic plateau crossed with a wind tunnel, a desert, a cliff, and a swarm. The main dangers are not dramatic: they are wind, thin air, cold, sun, dehydration, falls, unstable rock, insects, noise, and the slow erosion of your judgment. You are a temporary, fragile anomaly there. Prepare as if no one will notice you are in trouble and no one is coming.

## First: scout before you commit

Do not step out on intuition alone. If you can, send a probe, drone, or tethered instrument package first. Measure or confirm:

- atmospheric composition, pressure, and oxygen partial pressure; whether the air is breathable and how “thin” it really is;
- sustained wind speed and gust behavior; assume the roar means damaging wind, not a breeze;
- temperature, wind chill, night condensation, and whether gear will freeze or stay wet;
- UV and radiation intensity; the “too-close sun” may be literal;
- gravity, day length, magnetic field, and whether compass/GPS assumptions hold;
- local water chemistry and biology; assume pothole water is contaminated;
- fauna behavior: swarms, stings, bites, nest aggression, whether silver-winged insects are merely dazzling or dangerous;
- rock stability: balanced stones, arches, fissures, and whether the plateau edge is a fall hazard.

Pick an arrival point in the lee of a stable rock mass, well away from edges, fissures, overhangs, and balanced stones. Mark it durably and photograph it. If your return method is fixed, treat that point as your only lifeline and cache supplies near it.

## Prepare as a small self-supported expedition

An ordinary human should not visit this as a day hike. Go with at least one competent partner, a written route plan, a turnaround time, and multiple exit contingencies. Leave a flight plan with whatever multiverse authority or home base you trust. Carry your own air, water, shelter, navigation, communications, and medical care. Do not assume local rescue, local water, local food, or local hospitality.

### Clothing and personal protection

- Full-body cover: wicking base layers, insulating midlayers, windproof/breathable shell, and a serious static insulation layer. Down is good only if kept dry; synthetic is safer in damp cold.
- Windproof overpants, gaiters, sturdy ankle-supporting boots, wool or synthetic socks, and spare dry socks.
- Helmet with a chin strap. Rockfall and wind-driven debris are real; the plateau is made of unstable sculpture.
- UV400 goggles with side shields, not just sunglasses. The pale rock reflects light; wind-driven dust and insects will find your eyes.
- Balaclava, neck gaiter, hat, windproof insulated mittens, liner gloves. No exposed skin. Tape or ointment for windburn and pressure points.
- Fitted earplugs or passive muffs. The wind is a permanent roar; it will fatigue you and can damage hearing. Use radio headsets or prearranged hand signals because shouting will be torn away.
- Respiratory buff or mask for dust and insect swarms.
- High-visibility outer layer, but nothing loose or flapping. Secure every strap, map, glove, and piece of gear. Use lanyards where sensible, but avoid snag hazards.

### Shelter and campcraft

- A tent rated for extreme wind, with strong poles and more anchor points than you think necessary. Bring cordage, rock/deadman anchors, repair patches, duct tape, cable ties, and spare buckles.
- A bivy sack and a sleeping bag rated well below expected night temperatures. The damp condenses at night; assume your bag will try to get wet. Use dry bags and consider a vapor barrier.
- Sleeping pad, earplugs, eye mask, headlamp with cold-resistant batteries.
- Pitch in natural lee if possible, but never under balanced stones, arches, or obvious rockfall. Test the ground. Avoid fissures and potholes. Orient the shelter into the wind and anchor it heavily. Secure everything inside; the wind will take anything loose.
- A small stove and windproof cooking shelter, or no-cook food. Open flame may be difficult or dangerous; plan accordingly.

### Water, food, and fuel

- Carry all your water in hard bottles, insulated against freezing. Assume there are no streams. The rare pothole water is an emergency source only: filter it, chemically treat it, and do not trust boiling if pressure is low and the boiling point is depressed.
- Carry electrolyte tablets and drink on a schedule, not by thirst. Wind and dry thin air dehydrate you quietly. Plan several liters per person per day if working, plus cache.
- High-calorie, easy-to-eat food that does not require much cooking. Seal it against insects. Do not forage or eat local life. Do not attract fauna.
- Fuel, lighters, matches in waterproof containers, and a repair kit for stove and bottles.

### Navigation and communication

- GPS if it works, plus paper maps, altimeter, compass, and dead-reckoning notes. Check whether local magnetism or positioning systems behave normally.
- Mark route with durable waypoints and natural landmarks; wind will erase cairns and flagging. Photograph landmarks.
- Satellite messenger or PLB, radio with wind muff, signal mirror, flares, strobe, and high-visibility panels. The wind makes sound unreliable; use visual signals and prearranged hand signals.
- Buddy system and check-ins. Never split up without a firm rendezvous and a turnaround time.

### Medical and survival

- First aid for cuts, sprains, blisters, stings, and eye injuries. Eye wash. Wound care. Splint and tape.
- Altitude illness plan: acclimatization schedule, pulse oximeter, supplemental oxygen if possible, and altitude medication only with medical advice/training. Know the symptoms: headache, nausea, poor sleep, breathlessness at rest, confusion, ataxia. Descent is the real treatment.
- Personal medications, insect-allergy kit/EpiPen if relevant, painkillers, antibiotics if trained, and a means to evacuate or shelter in place.
- Emergency bivy, extra insulation, extra water, extra food, and a way to signal. Assume self-rescue.

## On arrival and during the visit

Suit up completely before you open the door or step out: helmet, goggles, ear protection, windproof layers, gloves, boots. Tether yourself if the arrival point is near an edge. Spend the first period observing: wind direction and gusts, rockfall, insect clouds, bird colonies, visibility, and how your body reacts to the air.

Move slowly. Altitude and wind drain energy; budget half to two-thirds of what you would normally cover. Keep travel short, rest often, eat and drink before you feel hungry or thirsty. Avoid exposed edges; crouch or crawl on wind-scoured slabs. Rope up near fissures and steep rock. Do not shelter under balanced stones or arches. Do not reach into cracks. Shake out boots and check sleeping bags for ground fauna.

Avoid bird colonies and insect swarms. The silver-winged insects may be beautiful, but they may also clog eyes and mouth, sting, or swarm sweat and food. Use headnet, goggles, repellent, and sealed food. Keep your helmet on near nesting crags.

Protect your ears and sleep. The constant roar is psychologically exhausting. Use earplugs, keep a routine, log decisions, and watch your partner for altitude, cold, sunburn, and irritability. The place’s indifference is not hostile, but it is absolute; do not anthropomorphize it into mercy.

## Emergency rule

If someone is injured or altitude-sick, stop. Shelter, warm, hydrate, and signal. Do not wander in poor visibility or exhaustion. Conserve water and battery power. Use your beacon early, not late. If you cannot self-rescue, make yourself findable and stay put.

## Minimum go/no-go

Do not go if you lack measured breathable air or supplemental oxygen, a wind-rated shelter, carried water, reliable navigation, communications, a buddy, and a tested exit plan. Send a drone instead. The plateau will not negotiate, and the wind will not pause for you.

Bring all the hospitality you expect to receive. There will be none. Your preparation is the only shelter this place will give you.
