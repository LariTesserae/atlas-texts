Prepare for this place as if you are visiting a sauna, a cloud forest, and a piece of still-running ancient machinery all at once. You are an ordinary human: you do not evaporate efficiently in saturated air, you are mold-prone, your ankles are fragile on wet basalt, and your curiosity is the most disruptive thing you will bring. The goal is not to conquer the place or stay dry. The goal is to stay cool enough, warm enough, non-fungal, unlost, and minimally invasive.

**Before you go**
- **Verify the atmosphere first.** “Thick enough to drink” is poetic until it is CO₂ pooling in a lower amphitheater. Carry a gas monitor for CO₂, H₂S, and SO₂; treat low terraces and depressions as suspect until checked. Confirm oxygen, pressure, and gravity with a drone or sensor before you step through.
- **Train for wet heat, not dry heat.** Practice walking, resting, and sleeping in a humid sauna-like environment. Break in boots wet. Learn to manage chafing, foot rot, and heat exhaustion before you are in a place where everything drips.
- **Check your body’s weak points.** Dental work and pacemakers may object to the monolith hum. Photosensitive conditions may object to mirror-wasps. Asthma/allergies may object to spores. Bring meds, spares, and a buddy who knows your baseline.
- **Plan extraction.** Set a return time, a beacon, and a rendezvous at the portal/rim. Tell someone outside. Carry a satellite beacon or multiverse equivalent. Do not rely on GPS alone; carry compass, altimeter, paper route card, and pencil.
- **Quarantine your gear.** Sterilize boots, tent, and tools. Do not bring seeds, soil, or invasive microbes. You are the alien contamination event.

**Clothing and kit**
- **Dress for drainage and quick-dry, not waterproofing.** In 100% humidity, breathable fabrics do not dry and impermeable shells trap heat. Use light synthetic long sleeves and long pants, a highly vented rain shell for brief protection, and dry bags for critical layers. Avoid cotton. Bring a spare dry base layer sealed in a dry bag; even warm wet fatigue can chill you at night.
- **Footwear is critical.** Sticky-rubber, draining, ankle-supporting boots or canyoneering shoes. Synthetic or wool socks with liner socks; change and dry feet every few hours; carry antifungal powder, blister kit, and chafing salve.
- **Traction and balance.** Trekking poles with rubber tips, gloves for wet rock, short steps, low center of gravity. Moss and condensation make basalt treacherous; hex-stone paths are worn slick. Test every step before weight.
- **Respiratory and skin protection.** Water-resistant P100/N95 respirator for spore-heavy areas and sleep, spare filters, antihistamines, antifungal cream. Fine bug netting, long layers, no bare skin. Polarized sunglasses for mirror-wasp flicker; avoid reflective clothing and jewelry.
- **Shelter.** Hammock with integrated bug net and tarp, or a highly ventilated tent. Camp high and stable, away from channels, pools, drip lines, and monoliths. Do not drive stakes into ancient stone; use cord. No campfires. Expect condensation inside everything.
- **Water and food.** Do not drink the weeping water, pools, or condensation. Carry water or use a closed-loop purifier rated for unknown biology plus boil/UV/chemical backup; dissolved volcanic minerals may still be a problem. Electrolytes are essential. Sealed, high-calorie, no-cook food; eat under netting; pack out all waste. No soap into the water system.
- **Electronics.** Waterproof cases, desiccant, silica gel, spare batteries, red headlamp. Equalize temperature before opening sealed cases to avoid lens fog. Keep critical gear double-bagged.

**On arrival**
Pause at the rim. Do not descend immediately. Check gas, footing, visibility, and portal stability. Establish a high, dry-ish base at the edge. Do a short reconnaissance only. The uniform fog, lack of hard shadows, and constant drip destroy depth cues; move slowly and deliberately. Hydrate before you feel thirsty; in wet heat, thirst lags. Watch for confusion, headache, nausea, or stopping sweat—those are heat emergency signs.

**Daily operation**
- **Pace yourself.** Exertion in saturated heat is a heat event. Rest often, use a battery fan or phase-change cooling pack, drink electrolytes, and avoid the warmest part of the glow.
- **Manage feet and skin.** Dry feet, change socks, powder, check for fungal blooms and chafing. Treat every scrape.
- **Manage sound and vibration.** The constant water conversation is fatiguing; soft earplugs help sleep, but keep awareness. Keep distance from humming monoliths; low-frequency vibration can cause nausea, tooth sensitivity, and headache. Limit exposure.
- **Manage fauna.** Do not swat dirigible-beetles; they are fragile and may drift into you. Keep mouth closed around tendrils. Mirror-wasps are not aggressive, but their flashing is disorienting; use polarized lenses, no reflective gear, and no bright white lights at night. Red light only.
- **Respect the infrastructure.** The terraces, circular pool, hex path, and monoliths are not scenery; they are likely active watershed and sensing systems. Do not touch, climb, dam, divert, collect, or “fix” them. If the hum changes or water flow shifts, back away and leave.

**Emergency**
If lost, stop. Shelter, stay high, beacon, and signal. Do not follow water downhill into unknown terraces; the basin is uniform and fog hides drop-offs. If gas alarm sounds, move upslope immediately. If a monolith’s hum changes near you, increase distance. Keep a buddy system and a fixed extraction window.

**Afterward**
Decontaminate gear, quarantine clothing, and get a medical check for heat strain, fungal exposure, and spore irritation. Pack out everything. Leave the place as wet, humming, and patient as you found it. You are the fast, leaky, mold-prone visitor; the world is the ancient, slow resident. Prepare to move slowly.
