Prepare for the Vault of the Unfinished Rain as if you were combining a saturation dive, a cave/overhead-environment expedition, an industrial-site inspection, a first-contact ethnography, and a pilgrimage into someone else’s sacred workplace. The place is not merely underwater. It is airless, cold, enormous, loud, disorienting, inhabited, and partly unexplained. Your body is the wrong shape for it. Your first job is to accept that and build around it.

## 1. Understand what you are entering

- There is no sky and no breathable surface. “Up” leads to mist, condensation, and the Lid—not to air. Do not plan to surface for a breath.
- The water is the room. You will move through it as a diver, not a swimmer. There is no beach, no shoreline, no wave line.
- The environment is cold everywhere, with falling condensation like continuous snowmelt. Hypothermia is a constant background risk.
- The scale is continental: columns like mountains, halls a hundred miles across, no far wall found. Getting lost is easy; finding a dry chamber may be hard.
- The light comes from the Lid and breathes—dimming and returning. Plan around the dimming. Carry your own light.
- The sound is physical: fountain-roar, electrical hum, structural booms, engine heartbeat. It will mask danger and make speech useless.
- The fauna is real and balanced: schools, eels, grazers, blooms. Do not treat it as scenery. Do not treat it as a free-food aisle.
- The people are divers and householders, not “fish cousins.” They live in sealed dry chambers inside the columns. Their homes are not hotels.
- The Base is not a first-trip destination. It is a deep, dark, pressure-heavy edge with no clear account of what lies beyond. Treat it as a potentially one-way boundary.

## 2. Before you go: training and team

Do not go alone, and do not go as an untrained tourist.

You need, at minimum:

- Technical diving training: overhead environment, cave or wreck-style line discipline, lost-line procedures, gas planning, decompression, bailout.
- Closed-circuit rebreather training if possible. The Vault is huge, cold, and airless; duration and bubble discipline matter. Open circuit is possible, but you will be noisy, brief, and dependent on staged gas.
- Drysuit and cold-water experience, including active heating if available.
- Low-visibility and no-surface diving experience. You must be comfortable with the fact that ascending does not solve problems.
- Rescue and buddy-rescue training.
- Physical fitness for cold, heavy gear, and long periods of controlled breathing.
- Psychological screening for claustrophobia, isolation, sensory overload, and the “no sky” effect.

Assemble a team with roles:

- A local guide or liaison—ideally from a dive family or engine-tending lineage.
- A rigger/line handler.
- A medic or someone trained in diving medicine and hypothermia.
- A translator or gesture/slate communicator.
- A portal/transfer specialist if you are arriving by multiverse means.

Set hard limits before arrival:

- Maximum depth for the first visit.
- Maximum bottom time.
- Gas reserve and bailout duration.
- Nearest dry chamber and emergency decompression plan.
- Lost-diver procedure.
- Portal failure procedure.
- Absolute no-go zones: engine intakes, sealed hatches, private chambers, the Base.

## 3. Solve breathing, pressure, and thermal protection first

Breathing:

- Use a redundant closed-circuit rebreather or a carefully staged open-circuit system. Carry a bailout bottle large enough to reach the nearest known dry chamber, not merely to “get up.”
- Full-face mask with nose block for equalization, or a well-fitted mask and redundant gas.
- Carry spare CO2 scrubber material, oxygen sensors, batteries, and a gas analyzer.
- Do not assume Earth gas mixes are correct. Ask locals about pressure, gas, and depth. Test your equipment in the actual environment before committing.
- Remember: no air means no talking. Agree on hand signals, line pulls, slate writing, and touch signals before you enter.

Pressure:

- The Vault has no free surface. Decompression cannot be done by floating up. You must decompress in a dry chamber or a pressurized habitat, or not go deep enough to need it.
- Ask locals where the nearest pressurized dry chamber is, how it is supplied, and whether it can accept an ordinary human.
- Carry depth gauges, redundant timing, and written decompression tables. Do not trust a single computer.
- Treat the Base as extreme pressure and extreme cold. Do not attempt it without local deep divers, saturation equipment, and a clear rescue plan.

Thermal:

- Wear a drysuit with substantial insulation and, if possible, active heating.
- Carry redundant heat: chemical packs, spare batteries, thermal layers.
- Protect hands, head, and core. Cold will make you clumsy before you notice it.
- Limit dive duration. Plan warm dry-chamber recovery between excursions.
- Monitor for hypothermia: shivering, loss of fine motor control, confusion, apathy. In water, confusion kills.

## 4. Equipment to bring

Think redundancy, not minimalism.

- Primary and backup dive lights, dimmable and shielded. Locals have small eyes; do not blind them.
- Redundant rebreather or bailout gas.
- Drysuit repair kit, sealant, spare gloves, spare hood.
- Line reel, guide line, line arrows, cutting tools, entanglement knife.
- Compass, but do not trust it near metal. Use Lid seams, column patterns, current direction, light gradient, and the engine hum as navigation aids.
- Waterproof slates, pencils, pictogram cards, and a translation system.
- Acoustic beacon or pinger if locals permit; otherwise use line and light signals.
- Depth gauge, timer, gas analyzer, CO2 monitor if available.
- Emergency oxygen and first-aid kit for diving injuries.
- Food and water for yourself. Do not drink the Vault water. Do not eat unknown blooms or fauna.
- Spare batteries and power for heating and lights. Batteries are valuable; introduce them through your liaison, not as casual gifts.
- Gifts for hosts: good lights, cutting tools, waterproof notebooks, needles, line, or other practical items. Ask your liaison first. Avoid weapons, drugs, invasive organisms, and anything that could disrupt their economy or engines.
- Sealed containers for waste. Do not pollute the water or dry chambers.

## 5. Arrival and portal procedure

If you are arriving by multiverse portal:

- Do not open a portal directly into the flooded Vault unless you have tested pressure, water ingress, and buoyancy effects. A sudden air pocket in an airless flooded structure can be dangerous and disruptive.
- Arrive through a dry chamber, airlock, or locally approved transfer point if possible.
- Anchor your return portal or tether to a stable structure. Do not let it drift into a current, intake, or private space.
- Establish a fixed guide line from your arrival point to the first safe dry chamber.
- Confirm that your return method works before you go farther. Test it. Then test it again.
- Have a backup return plan that does not depend on a single device.

## 6. Movement in the Vault

- Move slowly. The locals move without hurry; hurry is how outsiders die.
- Maintain neutral buoyancy and keep your gear streamlined. Loose straps, hoses, and dangling tools entangle.
- Stay on the line in unfamiliar areas. Mark turns. Keep the line taut but not loaded.
- Avoid fountain bases, pump intakes, and engine spaces. Currents can pin you; suction can take you.
- Do not touch the architecture casually. The columns are load-bearing, inhabited, and old. Some surfaces are sharp, corroded, or covered in grazing fauna.
- Do not open hatches or sealed ports. They may be homes, pressure boundaries, or systems you do not understand.
- During the Lid’s dimming, use your lights but keep them shielded and low. Learn the local light etiquette.
- Use the engine heartbeat and the Lid’s hum as orientation, but do not go toward them unless invited. The engines are neighbors, not tourist attractions.

## 7. Hazards to plan for

- Cold and hypothermia.
- Pressure and decompression illness.
- CO2 buildup, oxygen toxicity, gas failure.
- Lost line, lost buddy, disorientation.
- Entanglement in nets, line, or fauna.
- Currents and suction.
- Electrical hazard near lighting and generators. Water conducts; do not assume insulation.
- Falling debris or structural movement. The Vault creaks and booms.
- Eels and other predators. Do not carry blood, shiny dangling objects, or wounded fish.
- Blooms that sting, poison, or irritate. Do not touch or collect without permission.
- Microbial contamination. Your microbes may harm them; theirs may harm you. Keep your suit sealed, disinfect gear, and quarantine afterward.
- Psychological stress: no sky, constant noise, vast dark, dependence on equipment, the sense of being small.

## 8. Meeting the people

- Ask permission before entering any dry chamber. These are homes, not shelters.
- Approach elders or engine-tenders first. They are the ones who know the light cycle, the safe hatches, and the stories.
- Learn basic signals and a few words. Do not assume your “OK” gesture means the same thing.
- Do not mock their stories about the Vault’s origin. The disagreement among elders is part of the culture. Listen.
- Do not call their home a ruin, a factory, or a mistake. It is where they live.
- Do not touch the engines without invitation. Speak to them respectfully if that is local custom. You do not want to be on bad terms with the heartbeat.
- Do not photograph or record without consent. Bright lights and devices may be intrusive.
- Offer useful gifts through your liaison. Do not create dependency or upset local exchange.
- Ask about taboos around the Base, the Lid, the blooms, and the dead. Follow them.
- Be honest about your limits. The locals will respect a diver who says “I cannot go deeper” more than one who pretends.

## 9. The Base

Do not make the Base your first trip. Do not make it your second. It is deep, dark, cold, pressure-heavy, and poorly accounted for. The divers who go there speak of the fountain-roar dying, the hum fading, the water getting heavier, and then the cut edge where the columns simply stop.

If you ever go:

- Go only with local deep divers who have been before.
- Use saturation or equivalent deep-diving support.
- Carry redundant gas, redundant lights, redundant line, and a long tether.
- Accept that the edge may be a boundary you should not cross.
- Have a return plan that assumes you will be cold, narcosed, and frightened.
- Be prepared for the possibility that the Base is not a destination but a limit.

## 10. After the visit

- Decompress properly in a dry chamber. Do not improvise.
- Warm up slowly. Watch for delayed hypothermia.
- Treat any cuts, abrasions, or suit leaks as contamination risks.
- Disinfect gear. Do not bring back local blooms, fauna, water, or microbes.
- Quarantine and medical-check yourself if multiverse contamination is possible.
- Debrief with your team and your local hosts. Write down what you learned. The Vault rewards patience and memory.

The short version: go as a humble, over-equipped, slow-moving guest. Bring redundant breathing, redundant heat, redundant light, and a local guide. Do not surface for air. Do not hurry. Do not touch the engines. Do not enter homes uninvited. Do not go to the Base until the people who live above it trust you enough to take you.
