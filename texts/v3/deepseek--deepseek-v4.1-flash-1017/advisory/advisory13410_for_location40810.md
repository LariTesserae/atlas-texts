Prepare for the Kettle as if you are going to a high, wet, cold, geothermally active braidplain with a seasonal village on its rim — not as if you are visiting a scenic basin. The central rule to internalize is: **everything moves and nothing builds.** Your gear, plans, and habits should assume that the ground you stand on may be water in an hour, that yesterday’s route is gone, and that the correct response to change is to get high first and assess second.

## 1. Before you go

- **Do not go alone if you can avoid it.** Go with at least one competent partner, preferably more. The Kettle rewards people who work together and punishes solitary cleverness. A group of three or four is better than two; a local guide is better than either.
- **Time your visit with the winter residents present if possible.** The people who winter there are your best source of surge behavior, safe shelves, and etiquette. Do not arrive expecting an inn, a rescue service, or a permanent settlement. The village is a shifting arrangement of shelters rebuilt yearly; it is not infrastructure.
- **Ask about conditions and permissions before entering.** Learn whether the melt is running high, whether ice is calving above, whether the vents are in a quiet or active phase, and where the current high-water lines are. Ask where you may camp. Offer labor or useful trade goods — rope, fuel, salt, dried food, dry socks, medicine, repair materials — but do not assume goods make you welcome. Respect comes from listening and helping.
- **Learn a handful of local words and signals.** At minimum: *high ground, dry shelf, surge, vent, deep pool, safe crossing, help, stop, go.* Sound does not carry well in the Kettle, so agree on hand signals and visual contact before you need them.
- **Acclimatize.** It is a high basin near a summit. If you are coming from low elevation, spend a night or two lower or on the rim before doing anything strenuous. Do not descend into the basin floor on arrival day.
- **Leave an itinerary and a multiverse beacon/emergency plan with someone outside.** The Kettle swallows sound; do not rely on shouting, whistles, or even ordinary radios carrying. Have a beacon, visual signals, and a prearranged extraction plan.
- **Train for wet-cold movement and simple rope work.** You should know how to cross a shallow braided stream with a pole or a partner, how to use a throw rope, how to treat hypothermia and scalds, and how to navigate with map, compass, altimeter, and watch when electronics fail.

## 2. What to pack

Pack for **wet cold, glare, slipping, scalding, flooding, and isolation**, with redundancy in the things that keep you alive.

**Clothing and footgear**
- Layered synthetic or wool clothing. No cotton next to skin. You will be damp; you need insulation that still works wet.
- Waterproof-breathable shell, warm midlayers, spare dry base layers, spare socks — more socks than you think.
- Sticky-rubber boots or approach shoes with excellent wet-rock grip. Bring microspikes or traction aids for icy sections and camp shoes that stay dry.
- Gloves that can get wet and still insulate; a dry backup pair. A hat, buff, and windproof hood.
- Gaiters and quick-dry trousers. Everything should be easy to strip off or add when you cross from scald-side to rime-side.
- Glacier-grade sunglasses or goggles with side shields and a retention strap. The light is doubled by every pool; snow blindness and glare headache are real hazards. Bring a backup pair.
- Sunscreen and lip balm. The light is enormous even when the air is cold.

**Shelter and sleep**
- A strong, freestanding four-season tent or equivalent. Rocky ground and wind make staking uncertain. Camp on the rim or a proven high shelf, never on the basin floor or a “dry” stone island that was dry yesterday.
- Synthetic sleeping bag, insulated pad, and dry bags for the bag and spare clothes. Down is a liability when everything is wet.
- Emergency bivy, thermal blanket, and a repair kit for tears. Check the high-water line before pitching; look for debris, wet moss, and scour marks above you.
- A small stove and fuel that work in cold and wet. Fire is unreliable here; treat it as backup, not plan. Ventilate any stove use.

**Water and food**
- Treat all water. Filter to at least 0.2 microns and boil or chemically treat as backup. Avoid still pools with strange light, scummy vent edges, and anything downstream of animal mats. Carry some water with you rather than depending on the basin.
- High-calorie food, no-cook backups, and hard-sided containers. The runners hoard seeds and chew; the birds are bold and numerous. Do not leave food in soft packs. Cache food high and heavy-lidded.
- Electrolytes and a way to make warm drinks. Wet cold drains you.

**Safety and navigation**
- Helmet if you will move on steep or icy rim rock; at minimum a good first-aid kit for burns, blisters, sprains, hypothermia, and intestinal illness.
- Trekking poles. A lightweight rope — 30 meters or so — plus slings, carabiners, and a throw bag if you will cross braids or work near pools. A lightweight PFD is not ridiculous if you must cross rising water; the basin can go from ankle-deep to waist-deep in a surge.
- Map, compass, altimeter, watch, waterproof notebook and pencil. Sketch the basin and channels daily; do not trust yesterday’s sketch. Use rim landmarks, not floor channels.
- Two headlamps, spare batteries, chemical lights, a signal mirror, flares, and a beacon/radio. Sound is unreliable; plan for visual signaling. Bright clothing for teammates to see you.
- A small thermometer and a way to note vent rhythms. A watch and logbook are more useful than memory here.

## 3. On arrival

- **Stop at the rim. Do not go straight down.** Find the residents, ask permission, ask where the safe shelves are, and ask what has changed this week. Watch the basin for a full day before you do anything ambitious.
- **Identify your high ground and escape routes first.** From every camp and every foray, know the nearest route to a shelf above the surge line. Mark it mentally with rim landmarks, not with floor cairns that will be rearranged.
- **Watch the water’s rhythm.** Note when the vents pulse, when the stream braids widen, where the warm green water runs, where the ice sits above, and which shelves stay dry through a surge. Listen for the low hum changing — it is felt more than heard. Watch the birds and runners: if they move up in numbers, you move up too.
- **Set group rules before you need them.** Everyone stays in visual contact. No one swims or wades a deep pool. No one follows the pale shapes in the standing water. No one camps on the floor. If the water changes, abandon gear and get high; gear can be recovered, you cannot.

## 4. Daily practice in the Kettle

- **Get up early and move before the melt surge.** The residents’ rule is the right one: do not stop the water, go with it, get up early, move fast, work together.
- **Stay high when it changes.** If the hum shifts, if a vent starts pulsing harder, if ice cracks above, if the birds go quiet or the runners scatter upward, get to high ground immediately. Assess afterward.
- **Cross only shallow braids, and cross deliberately.** Use poles, a partner, and a rope if needed. Face upstream. Keep your pack loose. Do not cross downstream of a vent; the water can be scalding. Do not stand on frozen pools that may crack or beneath ice shelves that may calve.
- **Avoid the vent edges and the crustacean mats.** The mats are living, slippery, and dense; they are not footing. The hot seeps can scald through a boot. Stay upstream or well away from vent outflow.
- **Keep moving, but do not exhaust yourself.** “Keep working” means stay active, useful, and warm — repair gear, help with drainage, haul water, check shelters — not march until you are hypothermic. Rest high and dry.
- **Manage wet and cold aggressively.** Change socks, wring and dry what you can, keep your sleeping bag dry, eat and drink regularly. The cold here moves; it comes off the ice and water and wind. It is a springhouse cold, not a dead still cold.
- **Protect your eyes and skin.** The glare can disorient you. Wear glacier glasses, use sunscreen, and take breaks in shade or in a shelter when the basin turns to all glare and water.
- **Keep a log.** Time, vent behavior, water level, route, wildlife movements, how you felt. The basin’s restlessness and the hum can make memory slippery. A written log and a buddy are your anchors.
- **Do not stare into the deep pools at night.** The strange light is not yours to interpret. Do not follow it, drink from it, or swim toward it. Treat the water as a host with intentions, not as a resource to be mastered.

## 5. Etiquette with the residents and the place

- Ask before entering shelters. Do not alter drainage channels, dams, or gutters without guidance. The architecture is about drainage and defensibility; it is layered repair, not decoration. If you can help repair, help. If you cannot, stay out of the way.
- Do not pollute the water, leave food scraps, or introduce seeds or animals. Pack out all waste. The Kettle is courteous enough to let you watch; do not give it reasons to notice you.
- Do not treat the Kettle as inert terrain. The people there may speak of the place’s own intention, and their practical rules — move fast, work together, get high when it changes — are the correct response whether you interpret that intention as metaphor, weather, or something else.
- Bring useful goods if you want to trade, but do not assume they buy you access. Patience, labor, and humility go further.

## 6. Emergencies

- **If a surge comes:** abandon gear, get high, and stay there until the water settles. Do not try to rescue equipment from rising water.
- **If you are in the water:** do not fight the current. Keep feet downstream, angle toward a shallow edge, use your PFD if you have one, and let your partner throw a rope. Do not enter a deep pool to rescue someone unless you are roped and trained; a second victim helps no one.
- **If someone is hypothermic:** get them high, out of wind, out of wet clothes, into dry insulation and a sleeping bag. Warm slowly, give warm sweet drinks if alert, and evacuate. Do not rub frostbitten skin.
- **If someone is scalded:** cool the burn with clean cool water — not stagnant pool water — cover it cleanly, and treat for shock. Vent scalds can be severe through clothing.
- **If separated:** go to the prearranged high rendezvous, stay visible, and use visual signals. Sound does not carry. Do not descend into the basin to search alone.
- **If lost:** climb to a high shelf, stay put, signal visually, and use your beacon. The floor is a maze that rearranges; the rim is your friend.

## 7. Leaving

Leave during a stable window, not in the middle of a change. Tell the residents your route and thank them properly. Pack out everything. Do not leave caches that could alter drainage or attract animals. Check the weather and the ice above before you climb out.

In short: prepare like a wet-cold mountaineer with flood sense and a good first-aid kit, and behave like a guest in a house where the floor is alive. The Kettle will not stop for you. It is thorough, not malicious — but it is very thorough. Keep moving, keep working, keep each other in sight, and get up high when it changes.
