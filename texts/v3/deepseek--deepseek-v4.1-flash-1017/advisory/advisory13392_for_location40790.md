Treat the Basin as **an overhead, warm-water, industrial confined-space penetration**—not a swim and not a cave. You are entering a flooded machine that is still running, with unknown atmosphere in any air pockets, unknown biology, sharp and hot infrastructure, and almost no sensory cues to orient you. The best preparation for an ordinary human is: **do not go in blind; send an ROV/drone first; if you must enter, enter as a trained overhead/cave diver with a surface team, a continuous line home, redundant breathing gas, and a hard abort plan.**

## 1. Before you step through: establish the portal as a lifeline

- **Probe first.** Send a tethered ROV or remote camera through the portal. Map the immediate arrival volume, confirm whether you arrive in water or air, check depth/pressure, temperature, visibility, obstructions, and whether anything is moving.
- **Fix the portal.** Do not rely on memory of multiverse coordinates. Anchor a physical guide line at the portal and, if possible, keep the portal remotely reopenable. Have a return beacon that works underwater and does not depend on ambient light.
- **Set a hard return window.** Decide maximum penetration distance, maximum depth, maximum time, and gas turn pressures before entering. In an overhead environment, use at least thirds for gas: one third in, one third out, one third reserve—and be more conservative than that because the place is disorienting and machinery may delay you.
- **Assume the water is not potable and the air is not breathable.** The water tastes of iron, salt, copper, and biological growth. Any air pocket may be stale, low-oxygen, high-CO2, or full of spores. Keep your regulator in and monitor gas.

## 2. Training and team minimum

You need, at minimum:

- **Overhead/cave diving training** or equivalent experience with continuous guide lines, zero-visibility drills, gas planning, and lost-line procedures.
- **A buddy and a surface/portal tender.** No solo entry. The tender stays at the portal with duplicate gear, spare gas, cutting tools, a first-aid/oxygen kit, and the ability to reopen or recover the portal.
- **Confined-space and industrial-hazard awareness.** Pipes, valves, ladders, copper lines, and moving machinery are not scenery. Assume hot surfaces, pinch points, sharp broken ceramic, unstable roots, and possible sudden releases of water or steam.
- **A decontamination plan.** Treat the moss, ferns, spores, and water as unknown biological material. Full skin coverage; no eating or drinking; wash and quarantine gear afterward; contain any samples.

## 3. Equipment: dress for a wet industrial machine

- **Exposure protection:** a thin wetsuit or drysuit with minimal undergarment, chosen for warm water but full coverage. The water is warm, but long immersion, hot copper lines, and sharp ceramic make bare skin a bad idea. Hood, gloves, and boots. Avoid overheating during exertion; a drysuit can become dangerously hot here.
- **Breathing system:** redundant gas supply, full-face mask or primary regulator plus backup, spare mask, analyzed gas, and a bailout plan. If you are not trained for the depth, stay shallow. Do not assume the amber light means breathable air above.
- **Buoyancy and trim:** BCD with redundant inflation, secure harness with no dangling straps. You will be floating through pipes and roots; snag hazards are everywhere. Short fins or no fins in tight sections—long fins will stir growth and catch lines.
- **Line and navigation:** primary reel tied at the portal, backup reel, spools, line arrows, cookies, and a cutting tool on your harness. Run a continuous line. Do not enter an unlined branch. Mark turns. Use depth gauge, time, and compass, but trust the line more than your sense of up. Exhaled bubbles can help orient you, but do not rely on them.
- **Lighting:** powerful primary light plus at least two backups. The amber haze fades with depth. Below, the light does not go. Lights will also help you see thin roots, copper lines, and broken ceramic before they catch you.
- **Communication:** hardwired tether comms to the portal if possible. If not, prearranged rope signals and strict timed check-ins. Sound is unreliable here—the machinery ticks from everywhere and the weir is nearly silent.
- **Tools:** line cutter, knife, small pry bar, trauma shears, waterproof notebook/camera. A gas monitor for any air pocket. Do not bring open flames or sparking tools.

## 4. Entry protocol

- Put on and test all gear **before** stepping through. Regulator in mouth, mask on, buoyancy set, line clipped, lights on.
- On arrival: stop. Equalize. Confirm up/down by line, bubbles, and depth gauge. Anchor your line immediately. Do a bubble check. Note the portal’s appearance and distance.
- Set a deliberate pace. The place is patient; you should be too. Fatigue and gas consumption are your real limits, not the silence.
- Keep your hands off the machinery unless necessary. Gloved hands only. Do not turn valves, lean on pipes, climb iron ladders without testing them, or grab copper lines—some are hot. Avoid the weir sheet and any intake/outflow; a quiet flow can still pin or pull.

## 5. Hazards to expect

- **Disorientation:** branching pipes, no natural landmarks, warm haze, no sound cues. The line is your only reliable route home.
- **Entanglement:** roots, cables, ferns, moss, drifting threads. Carry two cutters. Keep gear streamlined.
- **Sharp/broken structure:** shattered ceramic, corroded rungs, burst pipes. Full coverage and gloves.
- **Heat and burns:** warm water, hot copper, engines below. Test surfaces with the back of a gloved hand before gripping; better, don’t grip.
- **Machinery:** moving parts, valves, pumps, possible suction or sudden discharge. Do not put hands in gaps. Do not assume a valve has been open “forever” and is safe to touch.
- **Unknown atmosphere:** air pockets may be bad. Keep regulator in; monitor if you must surface in a pocket.
- **Biological irritation:** spores, sap, algae, unknown moss. Avoid touching your face; wash afterward; decontaminate gear.
- **Thermal and fatigue:** warm water can overheat you during exertion and dehydrate you over long immersion. Plan short penetrations, hydrate before, and do not push to the engines on a first visit.
- **Psychological load:** the silence, the “someone just left” feeling, and the endless green can make you rush or imagine threats. Use checklists and timed check-ins. Abort early if you feel anxious, cold, hot, or unsure.

## 6. What to do if things go wrong

- **Lost line:** stop, hold position, search by touch in a controlled sweep, use backup light, and follow lost-line procedure. Do not swim blind.
- **Lost buddy:** short search only, then exit on your own line. Agree on this beforehand.
- **Portal problem:** if the portal cannot be reopened, you are in a survival situation. This is why the portal must be anchored, monitored, and remotely reopenable before entry.
- **Entanglement:** stop, signal, cut only what you can see. Do not thrash.
- **Gas problem:** turn the dive immediately at your planned pressure, not when you feel short of breath.
- **Overheating or chilling:** abort. Warm water can still drain you over time; the gradient near the engines is not a comfort zone.

## 7. The right first visit

For an ordinary human multiverse traveler, the correct first visit is probably **unmanned**. Let a tethered ROV map the arrival chamber, follow the line of pipes, check the weir, look for air pockets, and confirm whether the machinery is stable. If you do go in person, go with a cave-diving buddy and a portal tender, run a line, stay shallow and near the entrance, and treat the Basin as a place to observe—not to explore to the engines on the first trip.

The Basin rewards patience and punishes casual curiosity. Prepare as if you are entering a flooded building whose lights are on, whose machines are still running, and whose occupants have just stepped out. Keep a line home, keep your gas reserve, keep your hands to yourself, and leave before the place has a chance to make you part of its quiet circulation.
