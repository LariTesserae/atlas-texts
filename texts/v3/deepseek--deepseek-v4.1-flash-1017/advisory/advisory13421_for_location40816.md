## First, understand what this place actually threatens

Nothing here is trying to kill you quickly. That's the trap. The Cup is warm, loud, busy, and welcoming, and its two real hazards are **time** and **edges**: it will build you into itself a mineral film at a time if you linger, and its soft rim and deceptive light will let you walk off something or into something before you notice. Prepare accordingly. Your job is not to survive an assault; it's to visit a place that is very good at making people stay, and to leave on schedule.

Everything below assumes you are an ordinary person: no resistance to heat, no immunity to altitude, no way to un-break a hair-thin thread.

---

## Before you climb: find the regulars

There are people who go up to this rim regularly, and they are your single most valuable resource. Find them first, below the mountain, and ask specific questions:

- **How fast is the accretion this season?** The stone is thicker every year; ask what a safe stay is measured in — hours, not days, I'd guess. Get a number, and halve it.
- **What is the lattice doing now?** The pulse pattern doesn't repeat. Ask what it looked like last time, and whether anyone has noticed a change.
- **When did the big thing last come?** Whatever eats the climbers is rare; the regulars will know its rhythm better than any rule I can give you.
- **Which instruments still hum?** Two were powered last anyone checked. Ask if the sound has changed.
- **What's the current safe line at the rim?** The rim is unfinished stone, soft in places, and it grows. What was solid last year may be a ledge now.

Also: the mountain has no name anyone agrees on, so don't navigate by maps. Get a guide or landmark directions. And tell someone below exactly when you'll be back and what to do if you're not. This matters more here than on any ordinary peak, because the failure mode is not injury — it's you deciding, pleasantly, to stay.

Before you go up, **pace off the cup at the rim** and note the landmarks. The light inside is amber and shadowless and it will lie to you about distance. Forty paces is your only honest ruler.

---

## The climb, and the transition

The outside of the mountain is cold, dry, and dark, and the climbers come up it at night. Climb in daylight if the route allows, and if it doesn't, climb with a guide who knows the outside. Treat the approach as a normal high-altitude ascent: ascend slowly, spend a night lower, and watch for headache and nausea — you do not want to confuse altitude sickness with the effects of the Cup, because the Cup will make you feel *wonderful*.

**Cache a full set of dry clothes and a dry bag at the outer rim, below the lip, before you go in.** This is the most important piece of logistics in the whole trip. Inside, you will be soaked and warm. The descent is cold and dry. Wet clothes plus a cold descent is how people die on the way home from a place like this. Change before you go down, every time, no exceptions.

Layer so you can strip to almost nothing inside and re-layer in under a minute at the rim. Wool or synthetic, never cotton. Accept now that you will be wet; your goal is warmth and cleanliness, not dryness.

---

## What you wear, and the "everything stays" principle

The stone is tacky and warm and it precipitates onto whatever touches it. Cuffs, laces, zippers, knee panels, fabric watch straps — all of these are accretion traps. Within a day they stiffen; within a season they belong to the mountain.

So: **bring nothing you are not willing to leave behind**, and dress for smoothness, not fashion.

- **Close-fitting, smooth outer layers.** No loose straps, dangling cords, hood drawstrings, scarves, or fringe. A snagged cord will pull a thread, and a broken thread is the one thing the regulars will actually be upset about.
- **Cover your skin.** The pale bloom on your palm after a minute of contact is the process in miniature. Gloves — thin, smooth, snug — and long sleeves. Barrier cream on your hands and face is a reasonable extra layer; wipe it off and reapply outside.
- **No jewelry. No rings.** They trap precipitate against the skin and they are perfect thread-catchers. Tie your hair back.
- **Boots with smooth, cleanable soles.** The mud apron at the pool is soft, warm, and settling. Aggressive treads will pack full of mineral mud and turn into skates. Test the mud with a short, smooth probe stick before you trust it — a hand's depth of water doesn't mean a hand's depth of mud.
- **Glasses, not contacts.** The air is saturated and full of fine precipitate; contacts will collect it. Your glasses will fog instantly and constantly. Bring a cloth and accept it.

**Pack list, minimal:** your own water and food from below (pack everything out), a low-output headlamp with a red setting, the short probe stick, a timer with a smooth band, a small first aid kit, a dry bag, a light buff for your mouth and nose, a spare cloth, and a written plan with a hard turnaround time. No drones. No sampling bottles. No fire. No tent inside the cup — you cannot dry anything, and a tent is a giant snag hazard.

## Inside: footing, hands, threads, pool

**Footing first.** Walk the rim deliberately and keep away from soft edges. The unfinished stone can be pressed-clay soft in places; probe before you weight. The mud apron is warm and moves underfoot — that's normal, but don't stand in one spot long enough to settle. Never kneel or sit on the stone. Your knees and seat will crust.

**Hands off the threads.** The lattice spans the cup and the threads are hair-thin. Keep clearance above your head and around your shoulders. Do not raise your arms under it. Do not lean on the rim anchors. If something snags, **freeze, then back out slowly** — never pull. If a thread breaks anyway, tell the regulars exactly where and what the pattern looked like before. It will regrow into a different pattern, and that is a real loss. Don't be the person who caused it.

**The pool is not for you.** Do not wade in it, do not drink from it, and above all **do not take water away in a vessel**. Bring your own water and carry it out. If you need to rinse your eyes or wash the bloom off your skin, use clean water from your own supply, not the pool.

**Time discipline is the whole game.** The accretion is slow and pleasant — that's what makes it dangerous. A minute of contact leaves a bloom. The tripod was grown over where it stood. Set a timer when you step in, check your seams and cuffs halfway through, and leave when it goes off. Keep moving; don't linger against stone. If you start thinking about how comfortable it is, that's your cue to go.

## The animals, and the silence

The place is loud, and the loudness is company until it isn't.

- **Mist-feeders** (thumb-sized, translucent, glowing amber when full) cling under the lattice. Fragile. Watch where you put your hands and your pack.
- **Grazers** on the mud are slow and hard-shelled. Don't step on them; their tracks fill in behind them within a day and they harm nothing.
- **Climbers** come up the outside in the dark, drink, and go down. Give them the pool. Their claws on stone sound like polite knocking — don't imitate it, and don't block their route.
- **The big thing** is rare. The cue is not a sound but the *absence* of one: everything goes quiet for a moment, then the noise resumes doubled. When the silence comes, **freeze, stay small, keep your light low, and stay near your exit line.** Do not run — the light is shadowless, distance is deceptive, and running at the rim is how you break a thread or a leg. Wait for the doubled noise, then move deliberately.

Don't be at the pool edge after dark. The climbers come then, and the thing that eats them follows.

## Senses, instruments, and the built things

**Sound** is dense and layered near the top of your hearing — a crackling like tiny dry sticks, chimes, a distant tuning-fork hum. You will get a headache. More importantly, you cannot rely on your ears for danger, so agree on hand signals with anyone with you before you go in, and set a fixed rendezvous point at the rim. Earplugs are for resting outside, not for traveling inside.

**Light** arrives late, leaves early, casts no shadows, and makes the cup look smaller or larger than it is. Walk it; don't eyeball it. Keep your headlamp off unless you need it — the place has its own glow, and your beam will wash it out and dazzle you.

**The instruments and the half-absorbed plates:** don't touch them, don't unbury the tripod, don't scrape the diagrams clean to read them. They are still watching, and two are still powered by something nobody understands. If the hum changes while you're there, note the time and tell the regulars. That's all you're qualified to do.

**Breathing and eyes:** wear the buff over your mouth and nose, and rinse your eyes with clean water if they start to sting. The air carries the same precipitate that blooms on your skin. Hours are fine. Days are not.

---

## Leaving: inspect, change, carry out

Before you cross the rim, **inspect yourself**. Look at boot laces, zipper pulls, cuffs, watch band, the seams of your pack. Anything pale and crusted gets wiped and bagged. Then change into the dry clothes you cached, bag the wet ones, and only then start down. The cold outside will find every soaked layer you kept on.

Carry out everything you brought — food, wrappers, waste, all of it. Take nothing back, especially not water, and I'd leave the mineral crust behind too. It's not a souvenir; it's the mountain, mid-sentence.

---

## The real briefing

The regulars at the rim don't think of being built into the place as a danger. They think of it as the reason to come. Understand that before you climb, because the Cup is warm and loud and busy and it will make a place for you, slowly, a little mineral at a time, and it will feel like belonging. Set a hard deadline with someone below. Keep it. Visit the small warm cave at the top of the sky, listen to it breathe, and then climb back down into the cold, dry, much larger world — which is where you actually live.
