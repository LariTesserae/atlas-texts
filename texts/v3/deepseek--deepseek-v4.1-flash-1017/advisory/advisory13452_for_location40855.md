Treat the Low Works as an engineered wetland vivarium crossed with a working industrial plant — not as wilderness, not as ruins, and not as somewhere you can “rough it.” Your preparation should assume three things: **you will be wet almost constantly, every surface is both machinery and habitat, and the place will not give you landmarks, seasons, or dry ground.** The two real dangers are the environment’s steady saturation and your own body’s slow failure in it.

## Before you go

Get a sector map if one exists, plus any maintenance access rules. Learn the difference between walkable catwalks, pipe banks, settling pans, cooling floors, and sumps. If you cannot get a map, make your first visit a short, escorted, daylight-scheduled probe to a marked platform — not a wander.

Check atmosphere and gravity before arrival. Assume the air is breathable but saturated, warm, and full of bioaerosol and tiny filter-feeders. Bring respiratory protection with a hydrophobic prefilter, eye protection, and a headnet. Do not rely on smell to tell you whether the air is safe; it smells of hot metal, mineral water, steam, and animal musk even when it is merely “normal.”

Medical prep: no open wounds, good tetanus status, routine vaccines, and a travel kit with waterproof dressings, antifungal cream, barrier cream, antihistamines, oral rehydration salts, sterile saline, and prescription antibiotics if your physician agrees. You are entering a warm, nutrient-rich, organism-dense environment; a small cut is a colonization site. Bring more foot-care supplies than you think you need.

Carry all water and food. Do not drink, cook with, or clean wounds with local water, even filtered — it is chemically saturated and biologically crowded. Do not forage; there are no plants, and the animals are part of the Works’ machinery.

## Gear priorities

- **Footwear:** grippy, closed, quick-draining or waterproof boots with gaiters; spare dry socks and camp shoes. Expect wet feet and manage them with scheduled drying and inspection.
- **Clothing:** synthetic quick-dry layers, long sleeves and trousers to reduce skin contact, sealed cuffs, a light waterproof shell that vents, and a change of dry sleep clothes kept in a dry bag.
- **Hands and eyes:** cut-resistant gloves for rails and ceramic edges; polarized glasses for glare and poor depth perception; goggles or a face shield where skimmers are thick.
- **Respiratory:** a vented respirator with spare hydrophobic prefilters; a headnet as backup.
- **Navigation:** waterproof map, compass or inertial unit, printed sector notes, a staff or blunt probe, temporary route markers, and a breadcrumb beacon system. Do not rely on footprints — the water closes them.
- **Communication:** waterproof radio, emergency beacon, spare batteries, desiccant, and a check-in schedule.
- **Shelter:** a suspended hammock or cot with insect mesh and rainfly, or a freestanding tent that can be pitched above waterline without blocking catwalks or touching hot pipes. Keep sleeping gear in a dry bag.
- **Waste:** sealed containers and wag bags. Pack everything out. Do not add soap, food scraps, or repellents to the Works; use physical barriers instead of biocides.

## Arrival protocol

Arrive on a marked, solid platform above the waterline — never in a sump, channel, or pipe bank. Anchor your exit point physically and electronically; record it. Bring a buddy. Stand still for at least fifteen minutes: watch the water mesh, check the air with your respirator on, test footing, confirm your beacon, and identify the first few meters of route. Do not immediately walk out across a pan because it looks shallow and flat.

Use a staff to probe depth, grating, and hidden sump lids. The soft shadows and reflective water ruin depth perception. Treat every “ground” surface as a lid over something deeper until proven otherwise.

## Moving through the Works

Walk slowly and deliberately. Do not run, splash, or kick through fauna. Use catwalks and marked service routes; pipe banks are not trails, and they may be hot. Keep three points of contact on wet ladders. Never step into a dark sump or reach into a pipe mouth or seam. The “hand-like” things work at seams; keep your fingers out.

Do not trust rails, flanges, or gratings to be secure just because they look crisp — the mineral skin keeps edges sharp and surfaces slick. Test before weighting. Avoid leaning on rails. Watch for thermal expansion ticks and low hums that change; they may indicate flow or pressure.

Navigation is the hard part: no hills, no vegetation, a gently curving horizon, and identical sectors. Use serial numbers, bolt patterns, and pipe labels as landmarks. Keep a written log. If you become uncertain, stop, mark your position, and return along your breadcrumbs rather than “cutting across.” In uniform terrain, wandering is how people get lost.

## Fauna and body discipline

Assume you are both a habitat and a food source. Seal cuffs and collars; inspect your body, clothing, boots, and pack every few hours. Shake out footwear before putting it on. Keep food sealed and eat in a sheltered spot. Do not swat or crush dense fauna unless necessary; brush them off. Do not collect specimens.

The quiet is deceptive: the animals do not call, so you cannot rely on sound to detect them. Use sight and touch checks. Keep one ear free at night; the low hum and water ticks are your alarm system.

Cover every cut immediately. Dry skin folds when you can, use barrier cream, and change socks at scheduled breaks. Warm saturated air promotes fungal and bacterial problems. Drink treated water and take electrolytes; humidity suppresses evaporation but not sweat loss.

## Sleep and psychology

There is no day/night cue. Set a clock and a light schedule. Sleep above waterline, behind mesh, with gear sealed. Keep your exit beacon on your person. The place can induce a strange monotony — a quiet of too many soft bodies, no sun, no weather change, no natural sound. That is not peace; it is equilibrium. Limit your first visit. Use a buddy and a routine. Do not go deep alone to “see what changes,” because nothing changes except you.

## Emergencies and exit

Plan for a fall into a sump: keep your head up, find a ladder or fixed pipe, use your buddy’s line, and do not swim far. If lost: stop, beacon, stay on a catwalk if possible, and wait rather than wander. If gear fails, return to your anchor. If injured, clean, cover, and leave; do not wait to see whether warm wet conditions make it worse, because they will.

Before you cross back, decontaminate both ways: inspect every seam, cuff, pocket, and boot; remove hitchhikers; bag and quarantine gear; clean yourself with sterile supplies. You are carrying a warm wet habitat’s organisms back with you, and you are carrying your own organisms in.

The single rule: **treat every surface as fragile infrastructure and living habitat at once.** Keep your exit close, move slow, stay as dry as you can where you sleep, check your body often, and leave no trace. The Low Works will not chase you or storm at you. It will simply be warm, wet, alive, and unchanging — and it will expect you to manage yourself.
