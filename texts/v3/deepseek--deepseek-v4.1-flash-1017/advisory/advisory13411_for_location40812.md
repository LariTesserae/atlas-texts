Prepare for **a flooded, active industrial confined space that may be slightly alive**—not a dungeon, not a ruin, not a haunted basement. The Underworks is closer to a working treatment plant crossed with a cave system and a machine that has learned to have weather. Your job as an ordinary human traveler is to be **dry, lit, tethered, informed, redundant, and boring**. Bravado is the main hazard.

## 1. Understand the visit before you enter

Treat the Underworks as having three overlapping hazard classes:

1. **Ordinary industrial hazards**: slippery grating, open water, ladders, steam, electricity, rotating machinery, bad air, cold, noise, contaminated water.
2. **Flood-system hazards**: pump cycles, sumps, rising water, currents, high-water marks, drainage logic.
3. **Anomalous-place hazards**: sound changes, lamp patterns, water running uphill, “doors” that are not doors, the sense that the place is inviting you to stay or become part of it.

Your preparation should assume the first two are certain and the third is real but should not be investigated alone, at depth, or on a first visit.

**First-visit rule:** stay in the lit central galleries and near the entry shaft. You do not need to reach the far chambers. The Underworks will still be there later.

## 2. Before you go

- **Get local knowledge if possible.** Ask about pump-failure cycles, lamp timing, high-water marks, sump locations, which side-chambers are sealed, which were “opened from the inside,” and whether the pale crabs have blocked routes before. If no one knows, treat every branch as unknown.
- **Do not go alone.** Minimum two people; three is better. One person should be a route-minder who does not explore, only tracks time, water, and the way out.
- **Plan entry and exit.** Identify the shaft you came down, the nearest alternate shaft, the nearest high/dry checkpoint, and a hard turnaround time. Write it down. Keep it.
- **Set a reality anchor.** Multiverse-traveler basics: record your home coordinates, leave a physical token or marker at the entry, and use a return phrase or ritual at entry and exit. Do not rely on it alone; use a watch, a rope, and a marked route.
- **Train or at least brief on:** confined-space movement, ladder safety, gas-monitor use, first aid, hypothermia, and rope signals. If you cannot use a gas monitor, do not enter sump rooms, sealed vaults, or low pockets.
- **Medical prep:** tetanus current, no open wounds, good footwear broken in, personal meds, and a plan for cold/wet exposure. If you have respiratory problems, reconsider.

## 3. Kit

### Clothing and PPE
- **No cotton next to skin.** Wool or synthetic base layers, fleece midlayer, waterproof shell, waterproof over-trousers.
- **Rubber or PVC boots with aggressive tread**, not steel-toed if cold is severe unless you have insulation. Gaiters help.
- **Helmet** with a mounted light. Tie back hair; no loose straps, scarves, or jewelry near machinery.
- **Gloves:** work gloves plus nitrile liners. Waterproof over-gloves for wet work.
- **Eye protection** and a **P100/N95 respirator** for mist, concrete dust, efflorescence, and pigeon galleries. A respirator is not gas protection.
- **Spare dry layers in a sealed dry bag.** Socks are critical. Bring more socks than you think.
- **Emergency blanket/bivvy bag** and chemical hand warmers.

### Light
- **Three independent waterproof lights:** headlamp, handheld, and chem lights. Lithium batteries; cold kills alkalines.
- Red mode is useful during dark cycles; white light for inspection. Do not depend on the orange emergency lamps—they run roughly six hours on, two off, and timers drift.
- Treat the bioluminescent panels as orientation only. Do not touch the trays, disturb the bacteria, or lean close enough to breathe aerosol near them.

### Air, water, and safety
- **4-gas monitor** if possible: O₂, LEL, H₂S, CO. Calibrate before entry.
- **Probe stick** for testing grating and depth.
- **Throw rope and short line reel.** A harness is useful for ladders and wet edges.
- **Whistle** and rope signals. Radios may fail in concrete and machinery.
- **First aid:** trauma dressings, blister care, wound wash, tape, analgesic, personal meds.
- **No flames.** Ozone, rust, possible gas, and unknown air mean no matches, lighters, or stoves.

### Navigation and documentation
- Waterproof notebook and pencil; watch; pace counter; waterproof camera if useful.
- Chalk or removable markers. Do not erase existing chalk high-water marks—they are data. Add your own marks in a distinct color or on your map.
- Compass is unreliable around rebar and machinery. Use the central axis, rope, pace count, and branch labels.

### Consumables and decontamination
- Sealed water and high-calorie food. Eat only at a dry, lit checkpoint. No crumbs, no food waste, no feeding crabs or rats.
- Dry bags, trash bags, disinfectant, spare clothes, and sealed sample containers if authorized. Pack everything out.
- Assume the water is contaminated with bacteria, metals, fuel, and unknown chemistry. Do not drink it, splash it on your face, or let it into wounds.

## 4. Entry protocol

At the shaft bottom:

1. **Stop and listen.** Establish the sound baseline: hum, tick-tick-tick of a dripping flange, hiss of live steam, gurgle of rising water, silence.
2. **Check air** before moving into any new chamber, especially low or sealed ones.
3. **Mark the entry.** Note time, lamp state, water level against chalk marks, and route.
4. **Identify retreat triggers.** For example: water over the grating at the entry checkpoint, water rising visibly in fifteen minutes, gas alarm, hum faltering, a new sound approaching, or lamp failure beyond spares.
5. **Rope up if visibility is poor or the route is complex.** The line-minder stays at the last known safe point.

The ladder rungs may be warm from steam pipes. Do not grab pipes. Test rungs. Use three-point contact. Keep your hands free enough to climb.

## 5. Movement rules inside

- **Stay on the central grated axis at first.** Branch chambers are optional; sealed rooms and rooms “opened from the inside” are not for casual entry.
- **Probe before you trust grating.** It can be slippery, corroded, or gapped. Do not run. Do not step where water flows over metal.
- **Treat all metal as hot, live, or wet.** Steam lines hiss; electrical panels smell of ozone; water plus electricity is a retreat condition. Use battery lights only; do not plug into local power.
- **Do not touch running machinery.** Flywheels, belts, and pistons do not care that you are a visitor. Keep loose clothing and straps tied down.
- **Walk slowly and stop periodically.** The soundscape is your map. A steady tick means a flange drip. A hiss means live steam. A gurgle means water is moving. A true silence means something has changed—move toward the lit axis and the exit.
- **Use earplugs only in loud pump rooms, and only with a buddy.** You still need to hear changes.
- **Light discipline:** use your own lamps; conserve batteries; plan to rest at a known dry checkpoint during dark cycles rather than explore.

## 6. Water and pumps

Water finds its level. Learn the sumps, channels, and pump rhythm. When pumps fail, water rises. Chalk marks show how high it has gone; some are very high.

- **Do not wade unless there is no alternative and you have rope, a buddy, a probe, and flotation.** Shallow-looking water can hide sumps, holes, and currents. The grating is safer—until it is not.
- Watch for **iridescent sheen**, discoloration, or a change in current. That may be a chemical leak. Leave.
- Set a hard water threshold and obey it. “It’s only ankle-deep” is how people get trapped.
- If you get wet: change socks, dry feet, decontaminate exposed skin, cover cuts. Trench foot and hypothermia are quiet and fast.

## 7. Cold, air, and health

The cold seeps; it does not bite. Wet cold is worse. Watch for shivering, clumsiness, confusion, and withdrawal—those are hypothermia signs. Retreat and warm up.

The air tastes of rust, ozone, and battery. Use the gas monitor at every new chamber. Low pockets, sump rooms, and sealed vaults are the most suspect. Concrete dust and efflorescence can irritate lungs; wear the respirator in mist and pigeon areas.

No eating or drinking outside a dry checkpoint. Wash or sanitize hands. Keep gloves on around water. Cover wounds before entry. If injured, exit—do not “finish the route first.”

Psychologically: the hum, monotony, and low light cause fatigue and pareidolia. Use a buddy, talk, keep a schedule, eat, and log time. Do not sleep in far chambers. Do not make decisions when cold, tired, or alone.

## 8. Ecology rules

Nothing lives here that was not brought. You are also something brought. Be a minimal invasive species.

- **Do not feed** rats, pigeons, or crabs. Seal food.
- **Do not handle the pale crabs.** They wait at the edge of light for reasons you do not need to learn. Give them space. If they mass on a route, retreat.
- Avoid pigeon galleries without respiratory protection.
- Do not touch or harvest the bioluminescent bacteria.
- Pack out all waste, food scraps, and biological material. Decontaminate gear on exit.

## 9. Anomaly protocol

The Underworks is active. The strange part is the accumulation, not any single event.

- **Water running uphill:** do not follow it. Mark the location, note the time, retreat.
- **Lamp flicker patterns:** do not interpret them as messages. Log them. Do not stand under failing lamps.
- **A door that is not a door:** do not enter. Observe from a distance, photograph if safe, and leave. “Opened from the inside” is a warning, not an invitation.
- **A new sound in the hum:** note direction and distance. If it approaches, repeats, or replaces the hum, move to the lit central axis and toward the exit.
- **True silence:** treat as an immediate change condition. Do not investigate alone. Move.
- **The sense that the place is inviting you to become more yourself:** acknowledge it, decline politely, keep your departure time. Keep a token from home and a fixed identity anchor. The Underworks does not need you to merge with it.

## 10. Turn-back criteria

Leave—without shame—if any of these happen:

- Gas monitor alarms or you cannot test air.
- Water rises past your threshold or rises visibly while you watch.
- The hum falters into true silence, or a new sound approaches.
- A lamp fails and you are down to one independent light.
- You are wet through, shivering, injured, or confused.
- You lose the line, the route, or your buddy.
- Crabs or other animals block the route in numbers.
- You find a “door” or opening that does not behave like architecture.
- You have exceeded your turnaround time. Time is a resource too.

## 11. Exit and decontamination

Retrace your marked route. Climb carefully; wet rungs and gloves are treacherous. At the surface:

- Bag wet gear separately.
- Wash or shower; check for cuts and water contact.
- Clean and dry boots and tools.
- Quarantine samples, if any, in sealed containers. Do not bring live bacteria, crabs, rats, or unknown water into your home ecology.
- Log anomalies, water levels, lamp states, sound changes, and route conditions. Report them to whoever comes next.

## The short version

Bring **warm waterproof layers, three lights, a gas monitor, a rope, a probe, a map, a buddy, and fixed turn-back rules**. Stay on the lit central axis. Do not wade, do not feed the crabs, do not open sealed rooms, do not follow water uphill, and do not investigate silence alone. Treat the Underworks as a working machine-place with weather and boundaries—and be the kind of guest who leaves dry, unchanged, and welcome to return.
