Treat the Aethelgard as a **saturation dive into a living building**, not as a scenic swim. You are an ordinary human with no gills, no pressure tolerance, no sonar, no cold adaptation, and no reliable sense of “up” in a three-dimensional water column. The place is beautiful, but it will kill you through small, boring failures long before anything dramatic happens.

### The core rule
Do not go unless you can arrange, at minimum: a closed-circuit rebreather with bailout, a heated drysuit, a fixed line and acoustic beacon back to your entry point, a local guide, a warm dry refuge, and a decompression plan that accounts for the fact that **there is no surface**. If you cannot measure the depth/pressure at the portal, do not enter. If you cannot stay for staged decompression, do not go deep. If you cannot bring a dry, heated habitat chamber, limit the visit to hours, not days.

### Before you go
Learn the place as a technical dive first. Establish:
- **Depth and pressure at the portal.** “Sun filters down” does not mean shallow. Treat it as a mixed-gas technical dive until proven otherwise.
- **Water chemistry and temperature.** Test salinity, density, oxygen, CO₂, toxins, and whether the water attacks your suit seals.
- **Currents and the plant’s movement cycle.** The trunk groans and flexes; that is weather. Learn what a “normal” groan sounds like before you trust it.
- **Fauna and local dangers.** Ask about the immense unseen caller, hunting fish, swarms, and anything that reacts to light, blood, food, or bubbles.
- **Local protocol.** Get an invitation or at least permission. The people are partners with the plant, not owners of a hotel. Ask where you may anchor, where you may not touch, and what counts as rude.
- **Emergency exit.** Mark the portal with redundant acoustic beacons and a physical line. Know whether it can close, move, or require a pressure change to use.

Train specifically for: neutral buoyancy in open water with no floor, dark-water line work, confined-space movement, rebreather bailout, lost-line procedures, cold-water hypothermia management, and slow, quiet movement.

### Gear priorities
You need gear that is quiet, sealed, redundant, and non-damaging.

- **Breathing:** Closed-circuit rebreather, because open-circuit bubbles are loud, wasteful, and disruptive in a sound-read world. Carry bailout gas and a CO₂ monitor. Use trimix/heliox as depth requires. Full-face mask with communications, but expect voice to be poor underwater; use acoustic modem, tactile signals, and symbol cards.
- **Thermal:** Drysuit with active electric heating, redundant batteries, heavy undergarments, dry gloves, hood, and booties. The cold is “gentle” only until you have been in it for an hour. Plan warm dry breaks. Monitor fingers, toes, and core temperature.
- **Buoyancy and trim:** Redundant buoyancy control, integrated weights you can dump, and a harness with no dangling straps. You will be suspended; buoyancy failure means you drift into living structure or into the dark.
- **Navigation:** Fixed line reel, line arrows, acoustic beacon, dive computer, depth gauge, compass/inertial reference, and a backup timing device. Do not rely on sight. Do not rely on “up” or “down” by feel.
- **Light:** Dim red and white LEDs, sealed and pressure-rated. Bright light will attract fauna and disturb inhabitants. Do not carry flares, pyrotechnics, or anything that makes open flame or sparks.
- **Tools:** Polymer line cutter, soft wedges, suction or padded anchors that do not pierce living tissue. Avoid exposed metal where possible; saltwater corrodes it and the culture has no metal. Do not leave metal, plastic, or line behind.
- **Life support for rest:** An inflatable or rigid habitat bell with air, heat, CO₂ scrubbing, food, water, waste collection, and a gas analyzer. If you use an existing air pocket in a chamber, test it first; do not assume it is breathable or low-CO₂.
- **Medical:** Hypothermia kit, oxygen, decompression illness reference, first-aid supplies, and a plan for evacuation through the portal. Carry your own food and water; do not eat unknown local life.

### Arrival
Enter slowly and under control. If the portal is underwater, you may arrive already under pressure; equalize ears and sinuses immediately and check gas and suit integrity before moving. Do not gasp, kick hard, or shine a bright light. Establish your line to the portal and your acoustic beacon first. Then find a chamber where you can hover safely, check your rebreather, and listen. The plant’s pulse and the fauna’s chatter are the local traffic report; give yourself time to hear them.

### Base camp
Set up a warm, dry refuge as close to the portal as safety allows, anchored without damaging the plant. Use padded contact points and existing lattice, not driven stakes. Keep your habitat quiet and dim. Maintain a schedule: check gas, scrubber, batteries, suit seals, and body temperature. Do not sleep in the water unless you have a saturation system and a guide who understands it. Keep waste sealed and carry it out. Sterilize gear and avoid open skin where possible; this ecosystem is ancient and you are a contamination risk.

### Moving through the city
Move slowly, in company, on a line. The inhabitants move with deliberate grace; match that. Keep your limbs close, your gear streamlined, and your lights low. Do not swim freely through the vertical volume unless you have a guide and a clear route. Chambers can be empty for long stretches; emptiness is not permission to wander. If a passage looks soft, new, or narrow, do not force it. The plant grows; your equipment can be engulfed by patience.

Use sound carefully. You are nearly deaf to the local low-frequency language, so carry a hydrophone and record. Learn the difference between the plant’s normal groan, a current shift, and a warning. Do not use loud thrusters, pinging sonar, or splashing fins unless necessary. If you get lost: stop, hold your line, listen, and signal. Do not swim blindly looking for light.

### Social protocol
Be quiet, slow, and humble. Ask before touching fungi, chambers, tools, or inhabitants. Do not offer fire, sparks, or metal as gifts. Heat is precious and metal is absent; your technology may be strange or disturbing. Offer non-corrosive, non-metal items only after asking what is acceptable — strong cord, sealed light, simple tools of polymer or glass, perhaps. Explain what your gear does rather than hiding it. Do not treat the people as specimens or servants. The plant is the law; the people are its partners. If they say a chamber is resting, leave it alone.

### Hazards to expect
- **Hypothermia and heat loss** — the primary everyday killer.
- **Pressure injury and decompression sickness** — especially if the portal is deep and you forget there is no surface.
- **Buoyancy failure** — drifting into the plant, the dark, or a current.
- **Entanglement** — living lattice, line, straps, growth.
- **Fauna** — swarms, hunters, the immense unseen caller; avoid attracting them.
- **Sound disorientation** — the world’s heartbeat can mask warnings and your own comms.
- **Psychological strain** — no floor, no surface, dim light, constant vibration, isolation.
- **Contamination** — you bringing Earth microbes, oils, food, or waste into a settled ancient system.

### Departure
Leave no trace. Recover every line, beacon, wedge, and wrapper. Do not prune, sample, or “improve” anything. If you anchored, check the contact points for damage. Return through the portal only after your decompression obligations are met. If you cannot meet them, do not go.

### Go/no-go checklist
Go only if you have: measured depth and pressure; a local guide and permission; closed-circuit breathing with bailout; heated drysuit with redundancy; fixed line and acoustic beacons; a warm dry refuge; a decompression plan; a contamination plan; and the discipline to move slowly and leave everything as you found it. Otherwise, admire the Aethelgard from the portal. It is a world unto itself, and it does not need you in it.
