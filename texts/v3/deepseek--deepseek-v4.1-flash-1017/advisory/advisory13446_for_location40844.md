Treat the Held Room as an **overhead cave dive into cold, dark, confined water** that is also a **sensory and cognitive hazard**. The room is not a treasure to be solved. It is a held breath, a fresh wound in stone, and you are a disturbance. Your preparation should make you small, slow, quiet, line-bound, and temporary.

## 1. First: do not go casually

If you are not already trained in overhead/cave diving, drysuit diving, zero-visibility line following, and redundant gas planning, the correct preparation is **not to enter**. “It is only ten paces” is exactly the kind of thought that kills people in small overhead environments. Get cave-level training or send someone who has it.

You also need to be medically and psychologically fit for:

- cold water and long immersion,
- confined spaces and zero visibility,
- delayed/altered sound, time distortion, and sensory deprivation,
- the possibility of dissociation or “boundary loosening,”
- the discipline to leave when the room starts feeling meaningful.

If you have a history of claustrophobia, depersonalization, panic in darkness, or difficulty stopping an investigation, do not go. The room is polite. It does not need to be hostile to keep you.

## 2. Go with support, not alone

Minimum: a trained buddy and a surface/outside tender. Ideally:

- one diver enters,
- one trained person remains at the seam/threshold as line tender and backup,
- one surface watcher holds the external clock and extraction plan.

Pre-brief tactile and line signals, because speech is unreliable here. For example: one pull = stop, two = OK, three = abort, four = emergency. Use them exactly. Do not improvise.

Set hard limits before entry:

- maximum gas use,
- maximum external time,
- minimum reserve,
- abort criteria,
- who pulls the line and when.

Because time is thick inside, **gas supply is your real clock**, not your sense of an hour. Use the rule of thirds or stricter: one third in, one third out, one third reserve/emergency. In a small room it is tempting to be loose. Don’t.

## 3. Equipment: streamlined, redundant, quiet

The seam is no wider than your shoulders. Nothing should dangle.

**Preferred breathing system:** closed-circuit rebreather if you are trained. It avoids bubbles, noise, and the problem of exhaled gas collecting at the ceiling of a sealed water-filled room. If you use open circuit, use a cave/sidemount configuration with redundant gas and small cylinders that fit the seam. Do not bring a bulky backmount rig unless you have verified it passes.

Bring:

- drysuit with adequate insulation, hood, and dry gloves;
- thin-but-protective gloves — enough to feel, enough to keep sharp fresh rock off your skin;
- spare gloves and a drysuit repair patch;
- continuous line reel anchored outside, threaded through the seam if possible, plus a backup spool;
- tactile knots or marks on your line every metre;
- line cutter and small knife for entanglement only — not for the room;
- two or three lights, red/low-output, hand-held; primary and backup;
- redundant timing: dive computer, bottom timer, surface watcher;
- depth gauge and gas gauges you can read with a brief red light;
- decontamination bags, rinse kit, and quarantine plan for gear afterward.

Do **not** bring: bright floodlights, voice comms, sampling gear, unnecessary electronics, or anything that makes you want to mark, cut, collect, or “fix” the room. Disable full-face-mask communications if you have them. You will not be speaking inside.

## 4. Arrival: calm, consistent, unexpectant

The room resolves differently depending on how you arrive. So arrive sober, rested, unhurried, and without greed. Do not carry a fixed map from a previous visit. Do not arrive angry, rushed, high, or desperate to prove something. A simple intention is best: **observe, do not take, do not fix, leave.**

The entry is a fall. Plan for it:

- anchor a line outside and through the seam before you commit;
- enter feet-first, streamlined, one hand on the line, one on regulator/mask;
- equalize early and often;
- do not fight the threshold. It meets you like a hand. Let it.
- pause at the boundary between moving water and held water. Feel the exact line where the two waters refuse to mix. Confirm your line. Then cross slowly.

Expect the closing sound behind you — “a sentence ending.” Do not panic. The line is your sentence. Keep it.

## 5. Inside: the rules of survival

**No speech.** Use hand signals or tactile squeezes. If the room returns your voice with a word missing and a word added, treat the added word as room noise. Do not answer it. Do not obey it. Do not repeat it.

**Trust the line over the room.** The room is stable within a visit and not across visits. You may note landmarks for this one visit, but do not expect the ledge to stay east, the lattice to stay overhead, or the marks to stay where you left them. The line is not negotiable.

**Keep neutral buoyancy and move slowly.** Frog-kick. Do not fin near walls. Part the growth gently with gloved hands; do not pull, break, or cut it. Do not stir the white down more than you must. You are a heat source and a disturbance; be a small one.

**Use almost no light.** Let your hands and the cold see. Use a red low-output light only for gauges or a brief orientation check. Do not illuminate the far end. Do not shine light at the breathing thing.

**Do not touch the marks.** The squared ledge, the drilled holes, the upright-then-laid-down stone, the cut counting-strokes: leave them. Do not move them. Do not add to them. Do not try to count what cannot be counted. The made things are older than the walls, and the room is absorbing them. You are not part of that process.

**Keep away from the far end.** The deepest tuft has something breathing not-water. Do not approach it. Do not wait to see what it is. Do not shine a light at it. If its rhythm changes when you move, leave. The shell that opens and closes: do not test it. The pale ribbon: do not follow it. This is the rule.

**Monitor yourself.** The room softens edges that are not stone. If you feel your skin’s boundary loosen, if a root is briefly your wrist, if you lose a word, if time feels wrong, if you feel the polite crowd of growth pulling at you — do not panic, but do not explore it. Withdraw calmly. Keep your gloves on. Keep the line in hand. Leave. The room will still be there. You do not need to understand the effect.

If you become entangled: stop. Do not thrash. Back out along the line. Use the cutter only if you must. If you lose the line: stop, slow your breathing, feel for the water boundary at the seam — moving water on one side, held water on the other — deploy your backup spool, and move along the wall. But the real preparation is to never lose the line.

## 6. Abort early and without argument

Leave immediately if:

- gas reaches your reserve;
- the line is compromised or lost;
- you are cold, shivering, or your hands are numb;
- the breathing thing changes rhythm or acknowledges you;
- the shell/ribbon/animal responds to your presence;
- you feel boundary loosening, dissociation, or the urge to stay;
- the room’s layout shifts in a way that distresses you;
- you hear an added word and feel compelled to answer;
- your timer and gas disagree with your sense of time;
- the seam closure panics you;
- anything in you says “just a little longer.”

“Just a little longer” is the room talking.

## 7. Exit and aftercare

Leave on reserve. Cross the boundary at the seam slowly. Feel the two waters again. Surface with a normal ascent and safety stop if depth requires it. Do not rush because the hill is a hill again and the crack is a crack again.

Afterward:

- rinse and bag all gear;
- shower and check skin for cuts, irritation, or unusual sensation;
- quarantine gear if you have any reason to think the water or growth is biologically unknown;
- log actual gas, time, symptoms, and any words missing/added;
- rest, warm up, eat, and ground yourself in ordinary objects and ordinary conversation;
- do not re-enter the same day;
- if dissociation, missing words, skin-boundary sensations, or time confusion persist, seek help.

Do not bring back samples, stones, growth, or water. Do not try to “fix” the room. Do not try to map it across visits. The room’s one occupation is filling itself. Let it.

## The short intuition

Prepare as if you are entering a cold, dark, sharp, living cave that is also mildly psychedelic and very old in a way that makes no sense. Be streamlined. Be line-bound. Be quiet. Be warm. Be redundant. Be willing to leave early. Do not solve the room. Do not take from it. Do not answer it. Visit, observe, and go. The room is a held breath; be a smaller held breath passing through it.
