Prepare as if you are mounting a small, self-sufficient, high-altitude freshwater diving expedition into an unmapped volcanic lake that happens to be full of a drowned, flowering forest — with no beach, no paths, no shelter, no rescue, and no animals to warn you. The place is not hostile in a predatory way. It is indifferent: warm, quiet, sharp, dense, and easy to misread. Your main enemies are entanglement, laceration, bad gas, heat/cold stress, disorientation, and the assumption that a beautiful warm sea must be benign.

### 1. First, understand what you are visiting

You are going to a highland basin filled to the brim with warm water. The surface is a humid lid, often clouded, sometimes exchanging air and water in the top meters. Below is a continuous sea over young volcanic rock: knife-edged ridges, fresh scarps, gullies, fractures, pumice with glassy edges, dark cooling seams, mineral crusts. There is almost no sand and no easy footing. The water is warm through most of its depth, fresh at depth, with only a thin saline lens near the surface. Light is green-gold and reaches far, but the water is crowded with plant matter: ribbon leaves, creeping mats, floating fronds, gas-bladders like pale glass, drifting pollen and spores. It is a dense living volume, not clear blue water.

That means you should prepare for three environments at once: a humid surface camp on open water, a shallow saline/breathing/weather layer, and a warm freshwater underwater forest with sharp rock beneath it. Treat the plant canopy as an overhead environment even though it is not a cave. Direct ascent may be blocked or tangled. Open holes in the canopy are your lifelines.

### 2. Plan the expedition before you arrive

Do not arrive in the canopy. Use your multiverse advantage to insert onto a stable floating platform on open water, with your return anchor fixed to that platform or another safe surface point. Have an independent extraction plan that does not depend on local rescue, because there is no local rescue.

Minimum team: two divers plus a surface watch. A solo visit under the canopy is a bad idea. Your buddy should be trained for altitude diving, entanglement, and small-boat/raft support. If you are not already a competent diver, do not go below the surface here. If you are, still keep dives short and conservative.

Before arrival, determine as much as you can remotely: altitude, weather patterns, water temperature at surface and depth, salinity profile, pH, dissolved gases, depth soundings, location of cold seams and possible volcanic seeps, day length, and the shape of the highland rim. Choose an insertion point away from dense canopy and away from steep scarps. Plan a conservative dive profile. If the highland is genuinely high, acclimatize and use an altitude-setting dive computer; low atmospheric pressure changes decompression planning. Do not treat this as sea-level tropical diving.

### 3. Gear for the human body

You are a soft, air-breathing animal in a warm, abrasive, entangling environment. Dress for full coverage and streamline everything.

- Thin neoprene or a dive skin with abrasion panels: warm water means you do not need heavy exposure protection, but pumice and glassy rock will cut and abrade. A 1–3 mm suit or reinforced dive skin, hood, cut-resistant gloves, and hard-soled booties are appropriate. Carry a thin thermal layer for the cold seams between ridges.
- Mask and backup mask, anti-fog, and a surface respirator or at least a high-quality particulate mask for pollen/spore clouds while on the raft. The air lid is humid and may be full of drifting pollen; do not assume it is clean or comfortable.
- Scuba or rebreather? Open-circuit scuba is simpler and more robust in a spore-rich environment. Carry redundant gas and a spare regulator. Keep first stages covered when not in use; pollen and plant fragments can clog gear. A rebreather is quieter and less bubbly but adds failure modes; only use one if you are trained and have redundant systems.
- BCD, dive computer with altitude setting, compass, depth gauge, timer, and redundant lights. Light reaches far here, but the green-gold light is low-contrast and pollen causes backscatter. Use a wide beam, not a narrow piercing light.
- Two cutting tools: one wrist-mounted line cutter, one accessible dive knife. Ribbon leaves and creeping weed will find loose straps, hoses, and fins.
- Surface marker buoy and reel, but only if you are trained to use a line without making the entanglement worse. In dense canopy, a line can be a hazard. The safer rule is: stay near open holes, navigate by terrain and compass, and deploy an SMB for ascent only when you have clear water above.
- Tucked gauges, taped fin straps, no dangling hoses, no loose mesh bags, no wrist lanyards that can snag. Streamline as if for cave diving, because the canopy is a soft ceiling.
- Waterproof dry bags, desiccant, spare batteries, and sealed electronics. The humid lid and warm rain will condense on everything.
- Inflatable raft or floating platform with a sea anchor/drogue, not a rock anchor. Do not anchor to plants or sharp rock. Bring shade and rain protection, but keep it low-profile. You will need a dry place to sit, eat, check gear, and treat injuries.
- All food and water. Do not forage. The water is fresh at depth but full of spores, pollen, and unknown biology; the surface lens is saline. Bring your own water, or carry a serious filter/UV purifier and test the water first. Warm water grows things.

### 4. Diving protocol

Treat the drowned forest as an overhead environment. Plan entry and exit through open canopy holes. Keep a mental map of the nearest surface opening. Do not swim deep into dense stands without a clear return path. Do not penetrate fractures, gullies, or cold seams. The terrain is young and sharp; avoid touching it, sitting on it, or using it for purchase.

Check buoyancy carefully. Fresh warm water at depth gives less buoyancy than the saline surface lens; you may float more near the surface and sink more below. Weight yourself for the fresh layer, then use your BCD. Do not over-weight.

Keep dives short and conservative. Warm water can cause hyperthermia if it is near or above body temperature, especially in a suit; cold seams can cause hypothermia if you descend into them. Monitor your core temperature, hydration, and exhaustion. The water is quiet and slow, which makes it easy to overstay. Use a timer and pre-agreed turn pressure/time.

Do not puncture gas-bladders. They may pop, startle you, release gas, or affect buoyancy. Do not grab plants to pull yourself along. Use fins and careful body position. If entangled, stop, back out, and cut only what you must. Keep your buddy in sight; the green room folds closed behind you.

### 5. Gas, chemistry, and volcanic hazards

Young warm rock means possible volcanic gases. Carry a multi-gas detector for oxygen, CO2, H2S, SO2, CO, and methane if available. Avoid bottom hollows, bubble streams, and cold seams where heavier gases may collect. If the detector alarms, ascend immediately. Do not use open flame. Do not assume the surface air is safe; test it. The humid lid can trap gases and make the air feel close.

Test water pH, salinity, temperature, and conductivity. Mineral crusts and warm volcanic water can be acidic or alkaline. Avoid skin contact with crusts and seeps. If the water is chemically aggressive, a full-face mask and dry gloves may be warranted. Rinse gear and skin after exposure.

### 6. Navigation and surface operations

There are no paths, markers, or structures. Use GPS on the surface, sonar/depth sounder to read the drowned ridges and saddles, compass and depth gauge underwater, and a waterproof slate for waypoints. Mark open-water ascent holes by GPS and visible surface features. Do not rely on memory in a uniform green forest.

Currents are slow but persistent. A drogue on the raft is better than an anchor. Check your drift periodically. The whole water body breathes over long intervals; the canopy may lift and settle by a meter. That is not an emergency, but it can change your depth and visibility.

Surface weather can close in as low cloud. Keep navigation independent of sight. Have a fixed return bearing and a timed check-in with your surface watch. If visibility in the air lid drops, stay on the raft and wait rather than diving blindly.

### 7. Health and emergency preparation

Carry a serious first-aid kit: irrigation for pumice wounds, antiseptic, closure strips, dressings, cut-resistant gloves, eye rinse, allergy medication, pain relief, and a comprehensive diving medical kit. Pumice cuts embed grit; irrigate thoroughly. Carry oxygen for diving emergencies and know how to use it. Have a plan for decompression illness: oxygen, hydration, warmth, and immediate extraction. Do not wait for symptoms to worsen.

Carry emergency shelter, extra water, high-calorie no-cook food, electrolyte powder, and two independent signaling/communication devices. The silence and uniformity can cause disorientation and time distortion; use a buddy, check-ins, and short tasks. If you feel dreamy, lost, or strangely reluctant to surface, surface anyway.

Abort criteria should be agreed in advance: gas alarm, lost buddy, entanglement you cannot quickly clear, equipment failure, visibility collapse, approaching cold seam, symptoms of heat/cold stress, or canopy closure over your ascent route. There is no shame in a short visit.

### 8. Leave no trace and biological isolation

The place is untouched. Keep it that way. Sterilize and dry your gear before arrival to avoid introducing microbes or propagules; decontaminate on return to protect your home environment. Do not collect plants, water, rock, or animals. Do not feed anything. Do not leave markers, line, food, or waste. Pack out all human waste. Use no soap or chemicals in the water. Do not light fires. Do not anchor to or cut plants except to save a life. If you must mark a route, use temporary GPS waypoints rather than physical tags.

### The short version

Go as a cautious, self-contained diver on a floating platform, not as a beachcomber. Bring full-coverage abrasion protection, redundant air, cutting tools, streamlined gear, altitude-aware dive planning, gas monitoring, water and food, first aid and oxygen, navigation independent of visibility, and an independent extraction plan. Stay near open holes in the canopy. Avoid cold seams, volcanic seeps, and bottom hollows. Do not touch the rock or grab the plants. Keep dives short. Expect a warm, green, quiet, densely folded room with sharp stone underneath and almost no animals — and treat that quiet as a reason for discipline, not reassurance.
