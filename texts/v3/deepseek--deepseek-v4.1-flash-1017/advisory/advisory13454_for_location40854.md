Prepare for Sallow Bottom as if you are visiting a cold, wet, noisy industrial site built on a lakebed that has not finished settling — because that is what it is. The town’s oddness is not magical; it is hydrological and administrative. Your real enemies are wet cold, slips, unstable banks, outdated maps, machinery noise, and the quiet authority of the Drainage Board. An ordinary human traveler should plan for mundane discomfort and mundane safety, not monsters.

**Before you go**

- Get current local information from the Drainage Board or a landlord, not from an old map. Streets are renumbered when channels shift, braided creeks appear and vanish, and sheds are winched onto new piles. Ask which routes are open, where water gates and winch paths are, and whether any building you plan to use has been jacked recently.
- Arrange raised lodging if possible: a house or room on screw piles, not a ground-level shed, not anything near a foundation pit or reed bed. Ask whether the structure is on rails and when it might be moved. Confirm that your room is above the wet line and that your belongings can stay off the floor.
- Update tetanus and routine vaccinations, bring personal medications, and pack a first-aid kit for cuts, blisters, nettle stings, insect bites, and chafing. Assume there may be no convenient clinic.
- Carry cash in small denominations, waterproofed, plus ID and any letters of introduction. Do not rely on cards, cell service, or standard electrical outlets. Bring a physical notebook and pencil; you will need to write down current routes and local names.
- If you have a return beacon or portal device, make it waterproof and keep it accessible. Do not test it near water, wet ground, or the low-voltage lines.

**What to pack**

Think “wet industrial walking town,” not “wilderness expedition.”

- Rubber boots with good grip and ankle support; not thin sneakers. The clay, greased rails, concrete aprons, and boardwalks are slippery.
- Wool or fleece layers, quick-dry trousers, a waterproof shell, hat, gloves, gaiters, and at least two spare pairs of socks in a dry bag. Morning breath fogs; metal is cold; rain comes in brief, deliberate passes.
- Hearing protection and earplugs. Sallow Bottom is loud for its size: mill flywheels and stampers, steam vents, pump-house clanks, water-gate thuds, rain on corrugated roofs, frogs and birds. If you are noise-sensitive, this matters for sleep.
- A headlamp with spare batteries, a whistle, a small multitool, a walking stick for muddy ground, and a waterproof map case.
- Dry bags for electronics and clothes. Condensation beads on cold rails and windows; nothing stays dry by accident.
- Water treatment: filter or purification tablets. Do not assume cistern water or standing water is potable.
- Insect repellent, antihistamine, and long sleeves/trousers for nettles and insects around lamps. Bring gloves if you might handle woodpiles; mice and shrews live there.
- Snacks and a water bottle for the first day. Small town or not, do not arrive hungry and assume everything is open.
- Eye protection if you are invited into the foundry or mill. Do not enter industrial buildings without permission and local PPE.

**On arrival**

If you can choose your arrival point, come in on the alder-and-willow rim rather than into the basin floor. Mark your exit route. Change into boots before you start walking through wet meadow or boardwalk. If your arrival drops you in the basin, move promptly to high ground and orient yourself by the big landmarks: paper mill, felt works, pump house, foundry.

Your first stops should be your lodging and, if possible, the Drainage Board or a local who knows current conditions. Get the current street numbering, channel status, winch and gate schedules, and any closed routes. Do not wander the alleys at night without a headlamp.

**Moving around**

- The town is small enough to cross on foot in fifteen minutes, but the route may not be the one you planned. The creek has right-of-way over every street. Use boardwalks, bridges, and established crossings; do not wade braided channels or reed beds. Reeds hide holes, and channels can deepen or shift.
- Stay back from the young ravine. Raw clay banks with sharp undercuts are not stable. Do not climb the banks, stand on fresh gravel islands, or camp near the outlet.
- Watch for greased rails, pipes along boardwalks, retaining walls, low-voltage lines, and winching paths. Do not stand under or beside a shed being moved. Do not lean on wet poles or wires.
- Avoid foundation pits and puddles that look shallow. They may be old cellars or settling holes. No one builds cellars here for a reason.
- Do not enter the mill, foundry, pump house, or felt works casually. Machinery, steam, fumes, and moving parts are real hazards. Ask permission and follow local safety rules.

**Daily conduct**

Respect the Drainage Board’s survey marks as if they were sacred. Do not move stakes, block gutters, interfere with water gates, or “improve” drainage. Report blocked channels rather than fixing them yourself. Ask before photographing workers or industrial sites. Sallow Bottom is small and gossip travels; be polite, pay your debts, and do not call the place a swamp or tell locals how to manage their water.

Keep your food sealed against mice and shrews. Do not feed foxes. Do not handle bats, especially at dusk when they pour out from under the mill roof. Do not stand under insect-clouded lamps at night without repellent. Wash cuts promptly; wet industrial ground is not clean.

Treat all standing water and cistern water as suspect. Ask locals what they drink and how they treat it. Keep your clothes and boots dry when you can; hypothermia is a real risk in cool, wet conditions even when the weather is not dramatic. Dry your gear at your lodging, not near foundry heat or open machinery.

**Emergencies**

Know the high-ground rim and your route to it. If water rises or a channel shifts, do not try to cross the young ravine or braided channels. Stay clear of downed lines and wet electrical poles. If you are injured on clay or in a reed bed, stop, use your whistle, and wait for local help rather than thrashing toward a road. Tell someone your itinerary each day.

The best preparation for Sallow Bottom is to be dry, warm, grippy-footed, hearing-protected, map-skeptical, and polite to the Drainage Board. The town is not hostile; it is simply always, slowly, rearranging itself around water. Prepare to move at its pace.
