## Intuition first

Treat this place as **a bog crossed with a very slow, very large machine room**—not a haunted marsh, not a god, not a trap with intent. It is a membrane over water, computing drainage by growing routes, and you are a warm, heavy, wet interruption in its work. The correct preparation is not to “understand” it on site. It is to be **brief, boring, externally anchored, and hard to notice**: light, timed, tethered if possible, silent, sober, and ready to leave early.

The basin is not hostile. It is also not safe. Most visitors will get hurt by ordinary things first—cold, wet, bad footing, gas, disorientation—and by anomaly second.

---

## 1. Decide the shape of the visit before you go

Plan for a **short reconnaissance, not an expedition**. First visit: 15–30 minutes inside the basin, one hour absolute maximum, with a strict turn-around time set before entry. Do not plan to camp, sit, eat, meditate, sample extensively, or “wait for the mist to lift.” The mist is the weather.

Have an **outside watcher** if at all possible. They stay on firm ground at the entry, hold the other end of a line or at least a duplicate timer, and do not enter after you unless properly roped and accompanied. If you are solo, leave a written itinerary and a hard return time with someone outside the basin—and still keep the visit shorter.

Choose an entry point on the firmest edge you can find. Note a tall external landmark—something outside the basin, not a hummock inside it. Take a bearing. Set a timer. Decide now: “When the timer sounds, I leave, even if nothing interesting has happened.”

---

## 2. Gear: light, sealed, broad-footed, no loose parts

You want to minimize weight, snagging, and the number of things that can be set down. The mat grows toward weight and is patient. Carry everything on your body in a close-fitting pack or waist pouch.

**Clothing**
- Quick-dry trousers, not cotton. Wool or fleece midlayer. Light waterproof shell.
- Breathable chest or hip waders only if you are trained and the water is confirmed shallow; otherwise waterproof boots with gaiters. Chest waders can fill and drown you in a sump, so do not wear them casually.
- Broad-soled, soft-footed boots—something that spreads weight and does not punch through the mat. No aggressive crampon-like tread. No high heels of any kind.
- Fitted cuffs, no trailing straps, no long scarf, no loose pack straps. Everything should be close to the body.
- Warm hat and gloves. It is cool, wet, and you will not dry out.
- Bright outer color for external rescuers, but nothing that dangles.

**Carry**
- Two independent timekeepers: a mechanical watch and a sealed digital backup. Set both. Do not rely on the sky.
- A lightweight blunt-tipped probe pole. Use it to test depth and mat firmness; do not stab or pry. A sharp tip punctures the membrane.
- A small sealed dry bag with one complete dry warm layer and an emergency blanket for the exit. You will be wet; change as soon as you are out.
- Water in a sealed bottle. Do not drink basin water.
- A sealed headlamp with red mode. Use sparingly. No open flame, no smoking, no gas stove, no sparking tools. Methane is present in quantity.
- A cheap multigas monitor if you have one—CH₄, H₂S, O₂—sealed and clipped high. If not, rely on smell, symptoms, and prompt retreat.
- A light line, 30–50 m, brightly colored, anchored outside, clipped to a harness with a quick-release. Use it if you go beyond sight of the entry. Do not tie it to your waist without a quick release. Keep it tended; a dragging line is a snag hazard.
- A small “grounding token”—a smooth stone, a coin, anything neutral—to squeeze when the sound starts resolving into shape.
- Whistle for emergency only. Not for casual signaling.

**Do not bring**
- Drones, loud electronics, radios played aloud, music. The basin is loud enough; you do not need to add to it.
- Dogs or other animals.
- Heavy packs, camp chairs, tents, sleeping gear.
- Sampling kits unless you have a specific, sealed, minimal plan. Do not dig, core, or take mat.
- Anything requiring you to set it down and stand still.

## 3. Entry protocol

1. **Toilet before entry.** Do not leave waste in the basin. Do not urinate there; it adds warmth and nitrogen to a membrane that notices weight and chemistry.
2. **Set timers.** Mark the turn-around time. Mark a hard abort time.
3. **Anchor the line** if using one. Clip in. Test the quick release.
4. **Take a bearing** and note the external landmark. Do not use hummocks as landmarks.
5. **Probe the first ten meters.** Step slowly, weight over the whole foot, not the heel or toe. If the mat gives more than a hand’s depth and does not hold, back out.
6. **Stay within sight of the entry on the first visit.** If the grey closes behind you so you cannot see the entry, you have gone too far.

---

## 4. Moving inside

Walk slowly and steadily. Do not run, jump, or hop from hummock to hummock. Broad, flat footfalls. Keep moving often enough that you are not standing in one place long enough for the mat to “notice,” but do not thrash. Avoid the edges of hummocks and the lips of channels. Probe before trusting a patch of moss. If a bubble dome the size of a plate rises, step away from it; do not pop it.

Do not deliberately walk the arcs of hummocks as if they were a path. They are the lattice of the membrane. Cross them; do not follow them.

If the water is above your knee, turn back. If you cannot see the bottom through tea-colored water, probe first. If you feel the mat sucking at your boots, do not struggle; distribute your weight, pull slowly with the pole or line, and retreat.

---

## 5. The sound and the attention

The basin’s loudness is water, gas, and peat. Under it are runs of bubbles. Most are ordinary. A long unbroken run is different.

**When the bubbles come in a long unbroken run:**
- Freeze. Do not speak. Do not move your feet.
- Look at your watch. Breathe slowly.
- Do not answer, hum, tap, or count back.
- Wait until the run ends and add thirty seconds.
- Then leave. Do not resume the visit as if nothing happened. An attention event is an abort criterion for an ordinary traveler.

**If the sound resolves into a phrase or shape:**
- Do not follow it. Do not turn toward it. Do not try to remember it by repeating it.
- Squeeze your grounding token. Check your watch. Say internally, “Not mine. I am leaving.”
- Retreat along your line or bearing. Do not investigate.

The basin is not speaking to you. It is speaking near you. Your job is to remain a stranger who did not reply.

---

## 6. Water, gas, and the rising hour

The water rises by about a hand’s width in the last hour before the light fails, everywhere, and falls by morning. You cannot trust the diffuse light to tell you when that hour is. Use your timer and leave early. If you notice the water beginning to rise—anywhere—leave immediately. Do not wait to confirm the pattern.

Methane is not the only gas. Sulfur pockets are present. If you smell sulfur strongly, or if the smell fades while you feel headache, dizziness, or shortness of breath, leave. H₂S can deaden the sense of smell. Do not lean over bubbles. Do not linger in low hollows. Move to open ground and retreat.

Do not drink the water. The clear channels are not clean; the tea-colored sheets carry iron skin and sulfide. Keep your hands away from your face.

---

## 7. Displacement and navigation failure

If you turn around and the hummock you stood on is on the other side of the water, do not try to correct it. Do not cross back to prove anything. That is the basin reworking its rules, not your memory failing in a small way.

If you have a line, follow it back under tension. If you do not, use your bearing and timer and retreat immediately. Do not follow the channels, even if they seem to run toward the entry. Do not follow the arcs. Do not follow the sound.

If you become lost: stop, check time, check bearing, check line. If no line and no bearing confidence, move in one consistent direction for a fixed short interval, then reassess. Do not wander toward anything that seems familiar. Familiarity is not reliable here.

---

## 8. Companions

Two people inside are better than one only if they are disciplined. Stay within arm’s reach. Use hand signals, not speech. Agree before entry: if either person hears a resolving phrase, sees a long bubble run, feels dizzy, or notices displacement, **both leave immediately**. No debate.

A third person outside is better. They hold the line, keep the duplicate timer, and do not enter casually. If you do not return by the hard abort time, they should not run in alone. They should rope up, take a second person, and enter on the line.

---

## 9. What lives there

The tall grey bird and the single dusk fish are not food, not guides, and not pets. Observe briefly if you must, but do not approach. Do not photograph them for long; standing still is the thing you are trying to avoid. The basin is occupied and busy with something that is not ordinary ecology. Do not add yourself to its diet of interesting events.

---

## 10. Abort criteria—leave immediately if any occur

- The bubbles run long and unbroken.
- The sound resolves into a phrase, shape, or meaning.
- You feel compelled to follow, count, decode, or answer.
- The water begins to rise.
- You notice displacement or lose confidence in your route.
- You smell strong sulfur, or dizziness/headache begins.
- Your feet sink beyond a hand’s depth and do not hold.
- Your timer reaches turn-around.
- You feel cold, wet, tired, or oddly calm.
- You realize you have been standing still.

Do not negotiate with yourself. The basin has had a great many seasons to get good at what it does. You have had one afternoon.

---

## 11. Exit and aftercare

Leave on your line or bearing. Do not stop at the edge to look back and listen. Once outside, change out of wet clothes immediately, drink clean water, eat something, and warm up. Write down what you saw and heard **outside** the basin, not inside. Do not try to reproduce the rhythm of the bubbles by tapping or humming. If you feel the phrase coming back, do something mundane and physical—walk, count objects in the room, wash your hands—until it fades.

Do not return soon. Let the basin rework itself around your absence. If you must return, go shorter, lighter, and with a better line.

---

## The one-sentence preparation

Prepare like a careful bog-walker entering a vast, patient, indifferent machine: **light, roped, timed, silent, broad-footed, no flame, no sitting, no answering—and leave before it has any reason to notice you.**
