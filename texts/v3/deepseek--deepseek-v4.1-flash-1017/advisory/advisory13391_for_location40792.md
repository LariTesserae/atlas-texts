Treat the Hesk as a cold, bright, damp, vertical village with an exact water budget, no road, and a silence that will defeat most of your normal habits. You are not preparing for a hike into a picturesque sinkhole; you are preparing to enter a finished, closed equilibrium as a soft, noisy, water-hungry mammal. The main risks are cold/wet, glare, slips and ladder falls, communication failure, and—most importantly—being a disruption.

## 1. Before you leave

- **Get an invitation or at least a contact.** The Hesk is not a place with visitor infrastructure. The empty room is for the next person who belongs there, not for you. Arrange a guide, a host, and permission to use the rope/notch hauling system before you arrive.
- **Plan a short visit.** The town is exactly the size its seep allows. Bring your own food and water as far as practical, and do not settle in. A day or two is a visit; a month is a strain on an exact system.
- **Confirm your arrival point is on the plateau above the bowl, not inside it.** Mark it. Know your exit route and have a backup plan. The only way out is up.
- **Do not go alone.** You need a partner for the rope, ladders, and the silence. Someone at the rim to belay and haul is worth more than any gadget.
- **Practice the physical skills.** Fixed-rope descent and ascent, ladder work with a pack, moving on mossy stone, and carrying things without using your hands. If you cannot do these safely, hire or accept local help and do not improvise.
- **Get fit for cold and damp, not just distance.** The bowl is small across but vertical and slippery. Train ankles, grip, and shoulder strength for hauling.
- **Prepare medically.** First aid for cuts, sprains, blisters, hypothermia, and insect bites; personal meds; antihistamines; water purification; hand sanitizer. Assume you cannot get outside medical help quickly.
- **Clean your gear before you go.** No seeds, soil, moss, insects, or fresh produce. The Hesk’s ecology is exact and damp; an outside microbe or seed is a bad gift. On return, clean your gear again so you do not carry Hesk moss or microbes to the next world.

## 2. Entry from above

The rim is pale stone, thin grass, cold wind, and a slumped mossy lip. It will look easy and then be slippery. Stay back from the edge until you are roped.

- Wear a harness and helmet for the descent. Use the local notch and hauling system only with permission and guidance. If the rope looks old, do not “improve” it with outside hardware unless your host agrees.
- Lower packs separately on a line. Keep your hands free on ladders and ropes.
- Establish rope signals before you descend: for example, one tug = stop, two = okay, three = attention/help. Voices will not carry. Do not rely on shouting.
- Do the descent before the short, fast dusk. The cold comes on quickly, and ladders in the dark are a bad idea.
- Keep your rim gear dry and anchored. The plateau wind will find loose things.

## 3. Clothing and personal kit

Dress for cold glare and constant damp, not for a sunny walk.

- **Layers:** wicking base layer, warm mid layer, windproof shell. Wool and synthetics; avoid cotton. The sun is bright but does not warm you, and damp wool gets heavy.
- **Spare dry clothes in dry bags.** Everything will feel damp by afternoon; condensation beads on tools and roofs. Dry socks are a luxury and a safety item.
- **Windproof hat, gloves, buff.** Gloves for rope and ladders; spares if possible.
- **Footwear:** lugged soles for mossy stone and ladders, with ankle support. Bring blister kit and spare socks. Gaiters can keep moss and damp off your trousers.
- **Eye protection:** glacier-style sunglasses or goggles with side shields. The bowl is lit twice—direct sun and bounce off pale stone. Treat it like snow. Sunscreen, zinc, lip balm.
- **Quiet clothing.** Avoid crinkly fabrics, dangling jewelry, and loud Velcro. In the Hesk, a zipper can sound like an event. Soft fabrics, secured straps.
- **Small pack.** Low doors, hatchways, and ladders punish bulky luggage. Keep it close to your body and do not carry things in your hands.

## 4. Gear that actually matters

- Headlamp and spare batteries. Interiors and the north-wall shadow will be darker than the glare suggests.
- Dry bags for clothes, electronics, meds, and notebook.
- Waterproof notebook and pencil. Paper will go soft; use it for notes, names, and simple drawings. Learn a few hand signals and the local knock: two knocks, pause, one.
- Signal mirror and a light for emergency signaling. A whistle may be swallowed by the moss; do not depend on it.
- Rope, harness, slings, carabiners, and gloves if you are competent. If not, rely on your host’s system and do not pretend.
- First aid, personal meds, insect head net, long sleeves, and repellent used carefully—nothing that will wash into the tanks.
- Water filter/purifier and collapsible bottles. Bring your own water for the visit if you can. If you must use local water, ask first and take only what is offered; do not drink from the tanks directly or wash in them.
- High-calorie sealed food. No fresh fruit, vegetables, or seeds. No strong-smelling food. Pack out every scrap.
- Wag bags or equivalent. Pack out all human waste and greywater. Do not soap the seep, tanks, or moss.
- Multitool, spare cord, emergency bivvy. Use them on your own gear only; do not “fix” the Hesk.
- No drones, no music, no radios played aloud, no open flame in the bowl. Ask before photographs or recordings. A silent camera is better than a loud one.

## 5. Behavior in a silent place

The silence is the thing you will underestimate. Hundreds of birds, and nothing arrives. Footsteps do not echo. Your voice comes out smaller than you meant. You will whisper, then discover whispering is no quieter than speaking.

- **Do not shout.** Speak low and close. Face people when you speak. Use gestures and written notes.
- **Approach in sight.** Do not come up behind someone and speak. Make visual contact first.
- **Use touch carefully.** People here touch to get attention. Do it gently and after a gesture, not as a grab. A light touch on the forearm or shoulder, then step back.
- **Knock, don’t call.** Two knocks, pause, one. Use knuckles on stone or wood. Wait. Do not hammer.
- **Keep close in conversation.** Voices die after a few paces. Do not expect to talk across a room or up a ladder.
- **Learn the local hand signals** for yes/no, water, wait, help, and exit. Agree on rope tugs before any climb.
- **Move quietly and slowly.** Do not run on mossy stone. Do not clatter gear. Tether small items so a dropped tool does not vanish down a hatchway.

## 6. The town and its rules

The Hesk is terraced into the bowl. There is no street—only a narrow switchback and many ladders. Roofs are shallow and weighted with stone; doors are low and rounded; people hand things up and down rather than carry them along.

- Duck for doors. Watch your head. Ask before entering. Do not climb on roofs or open hatchways unless invited.
- Use the hauling system as instructed. Do not haul heavy loads over people.
- Do not move stones, channels, tank rims, or pruned trees. Do not pick moss, ferns, watercress, or the pale frilled plant. Do not feed the birds or fish. Do not step on the flat grey moth if you can avoid it.
- Do not introduce outside goods casually. The Hesk has no export and no import; a steel tool or outside trinket may be more disruptive than useful. If you bring a gift, ask first. Do not offer to “improve” anything.
- Accept hospitality modestly. If offered water or food, take little. Reciprocate only in ways your host approves. Do not assume outside currency means anything.
- Keep your visit short and your water use minimal. You are an extra mouth in a system that has been exact for a very long time.

## 7. Health and safety

- **Hypothermia is real even in sun.** The light is cold, the wind bites at the rim, and damp steals heat. Watch for shivering, clumsiness, and confusion. Change into dry layers early.
- **Feet:** damp socks and mossy boots cause trench foot and blisters. Dry socks, air feet when safe, monitor hot spots.
- **Eyes and skin:** snow-blindness-style glare, sunburn, chapped lips. Use goggles and sunscreen.
- **Insects and mites:** midges over the tanks; mites in the moss. Long sleeves, head net, do not sit or lie in moss, do not touch your face after handling it.
- **Falls:** the ladders, hatchways, and mossy stone are the main physical hazard. Three points of contact. Do not carry loads in your hands. Do not solo the rope.
- **Emergency plan:** because sound dies, search and rescue by voice is nearly useless. Carry a light and mirror, agree on check-in times, and use rope tugs or visual signals. If you are hurt, stay put and signal; do not assume you will be heard.
- **Psychological preparation:** the constant glare, the damp, the silence, and the sameness can be disorienting. Bring a watch, keep a routine, sleep mask if needed. Do not try to fill the silence with noise. Leave if you feel the place getting into your head.

## 8. The intuition to carry with you

The Hesk is not hostile. It is finished. It holds its people the way the tanks hold water: exactly, and without spilling. Your job is to visit without making it spill.

Come roped, layered, quiet, and small-packed. Bring your own water and food as far as you can, pack out everything, ask before touching, learn the knock, keep your voice low, and do not stay long. Look at the moss, the ladders, the low doors, the flat grey moth, the birds opening their beaks in silence—and then leave the equilibrium as exact as you found it.
