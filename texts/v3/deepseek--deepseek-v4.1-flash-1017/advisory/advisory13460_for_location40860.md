Treat this visit as preparation for a cold, wet, low-tech mill town built on top of a living sponge. The central fact is not dramatic danger; it is pervasive dampness, slipperiness, mold, rust, and a surface that is sometimes walkable and sometimes not. Dryness is a technology here, and you must bring your own.

## The basic posture

You are an ordinary human in a place where nothing is dry, nothing heavy belongs off stone, and the ground itself is a crop, a water supply, and an industrial material. You should arrive slow, lightly loaded, waterproofed, and willing to ask permission. The town is small and quiet; the walking is the interesting part; the nap is not a lawn and not a floor. It is a tended plant that makes the town’s weather and its felt. Behave as if you are visiting a working farm, a waterworks, and a very damp old factory at the same time.

## Before you go

- Confirm entry and exit logistics. The basin is reached by the dry uplands and the east mule road. Arrange mule transport or join a caravan. Do not plan to walk down the slope onto the nap; approach via stone and deck.
- If you arrive by portal or other multiverse means, set your arrival on the dry uplands or a stone island, not on the nap. If you do appear on the mat, do not run or stand still; move steadily toward the nearest deck or stone and call for help.
- Check language, currency, local law, and biological customs. Learn at least: “May I walk on the nap?”, “Where is the guild hall?”, “Is this deck safe?”, “Where is the rust line?”, “Is the seep channel off-limits?”, and “May I take a guide?”
- Get routine vaccinations and a tetanus booster. If you have severe mold allergy, asthma, poor balance, or mobility problems, reconsider or plan heavily around guides and stone buildings.
- Bring trade goods the town actually wants: coffee, small iron goods such as needles, knives, hinges, screws, or tools. Iron is heavy and rusts, so oil it and keep it sealed. Coffee is lighter and welcome. Ask before assuming barter rates.
- Do not bring invasive biological material. Clean boots, gear, and clothing. No soil, seeds, pets, or foreign plants. Declare biologicals. The nap is one plant and the town’s water supply; contamination is a serious matter.
- Pack for the uplands too: hard light, dry air, sun protection. The basin itself is cool, soft-aired, grey, and wet.

## Clothing and footwear

- No cotton next to skin. Use wool or synthetic base layers, fleece or wool midlayers, and a waterproof breathable shell with a hood. The cloth roofs drip; a hood is better than an umbrella in cramped deck spaces.
- Bring quick-dry trousers and gaiters. Avoid long loose coats near belts, turbines, and presses.
- Footwear is critical: waterproof boots with sticky rubber soles and enough tread for wet plank, but not deep hobnails that slip on slick wood. Break them in. Bring spare laces.
- Bring at least two pairs of spare socks and change them before you think you need to. Wet warmth breeds fungus. Foot powder and blister kit are not optional.
- Waterproof work gloves and a warm hat. Stone and iron are wet and rounded; handrails and tools will be slick.
- Bring clear glasses or light eye protection if you dislike constant drips from roofs and gutters.
- A local felt coat may be worth buying once you are there: dense, grey, waterproof, warm. But do not rely on finding one immediately, and remember it is heavy when wet.

## Gear

- Use dry bags, not just a pack cover. Double-bag documents, medication, electronics, and sleeping insulation. Desiccant helps but will saturate; bring spare sealed bags.
- A waterproof headlamp is essential. Light is flat and grey, under cloth roofs it is dusk, and the deck has platforms that lead nowhere. Bring spare batteries in a dry bag. Do not rely on solar charging.
- Carry a whistle, a small stainless multitool, cordage, and a short rope if you plan to walk the nap with a guide. Keep hands free. A backpack is better than rolling luggage; wheels are useless and heavy bags should not be left on deck.
- Bring a watch. Shadows are soft and few; the grey light will not tell you the time.
- Bring a water filter or purification tablets and a bottle. The town catches water from air and ironwork; the seep is cold and constant, but you are an ordinary human traveler and should treat local water until you know its handling. Do not drink from open troughs or cisterns.
- Bring high-energy dry food. The town imports coffee and iron; do not assume a wide food market. Pack out your waste and do not dump soap, food, or foreign water into gutters or cisterns.
- Sleeping kit: synthetic bag or quilt, not down; a liner; a closed-cell pad or cot; earplugs. Ask for a room on stone, off the floor, with ventilation and a stove if possible. Do not sleep on the nap or on a deck platform. Down is useless here.
- First aid: blister care, antifungal powder/cream, antibiotic ointment, waterproof tape, painkillers, antihistamines, rehydration salts, personal medication, and a small supply of any prescription antibiotics your doctor has approved for travel. Include eye drops and lip balm for the dry uplands.
- Documents in a waterproof case: permits, map, phrasebook, money, emergency contacts, and your return plan.

## Arrival and first hours

You will see the basin as a grey-green dish in pale scrub, ringed by a low stone lip. Do not cut across the mat to reach town. Follow stone, deck, or the east road. The deck is the street plan: wide, black, slick, generous, and partly leading nowhere. Ask where to lodge and where the guild hall is. Keep your heavy pack on stone. If you must set a bag down, hang it from a rail or put it on stone; a heavy object left overnight on deck may be found lower in the morning.

Walk slowly. The deck is wet plank and slab, the stone is rounded and wet, and there are knee-high iron troughs, gutters, cables, low beams, and cloth roofs. Watch your head. Do not run. Use handrails. At night, use a headlamp and do not explore empty platforms alone.

## Living in town

- Keep yourself dry by heat or motion. Change wet base layers. Use drying sheds or stoves only with permission and never drape synthetic fabric near a hot stove.
- Expect mold, rust, and condensation. Wipe tools, oil metal, keep electronics sealed, and accept that paper will soften.
- The town is quiet. The stilling trickle is the loudest thing; presses clack only some days. Do not shout, play loud music, or fly drones. Ask before photographing people or work areas.
- The town is small and gossip travels. Greet people, ask permission, pay guides fairly, and do not claim ownership of the nap or try to “improve” the drainage. The mat is tended under an old rotation; follow it.
- Mules have the right of way on plank. Do not stand behind them; keep clear of loads.
- Fire is a real hazard in the drying sheds, where hanks of fiber are hot and dry. No smoking, no open flame, no camp stove except where permitted.
- Keep clear of the turbine, belts, and presses. Loose clothing and hands do not belong near moving machinery.
- Do not disturb the moths in the hanks, the gutter bird or its nest, or the smooth trails in the nap. Do not dig for the soft-bodied thing. Step over trails without comment.

## Walking on the nap

Only walk out onto the mat if you have a guide, daylight, and a reason. Go steadily. The nap takes your foot with a slight yield, then stiffens under a steady sole. Do not run. Do not stand still. Do not cross with a heavy pack; leave heavy gear on stone or deck and carry only a daypack. Avoid cut lanes unless told otherwise; step over the soft trails; never cut, pull, or dig.

If you sink, do not thrash. Call for help, spread your arms and legs, lean back, and crawl or be pulled toward the nearest deck or stone. The mat closes behind you. If someone else sinks, approach steadily with rope or a plank; do not run and do not stand still beside them.

Do not cut below the rust line. The brown depth is the part that makes the water. Do not take samples of the nap without explicit permission from the guild. Do not export felt or water without local permission.

## Industry and work areas

The harvest is a haircut, not a stripping. Stay out of crews’ lines unless invited. The drying sheds are hot, wet-fiber-smelling, and flammable; ask before entering. The presses and turbine are old and powerful; keep hands and loose clothing clear. The finished felt is dense, grey, waterproof, and slightly warm; buying a piece is reasonable, but do not treat it as a souvenir to cut from the mat.

## Emergency and exit

Carry a whistle. Stay on deck if lost; do not step off into the mat in the dark. Follow gutters and deck toward the cistern house or town, but do not enter open cisterns or the seep channel. If you fall into cold water, get warm and dry immediately; hypothermia is a wet-cold risk, not a dramatic one.

Know the mule-train schedule before you settle in. The east road is your exit. Leave no trace: pack out waste, do not dump foreign water or biological material, and clean your gear before you leave. If you have been on the nap or in the sheds, treat your boots and clothing as contaminated with local biological material until cleaned.

## The short version

Bring waterproof layers, synthetic insulation, spare socks, sticky-soled boots, dry bags, a headlamp, water treatment, foot powder, tetanus cover, coffee and small oiled iron goods as trade, and a guide. Keep heavy things on stone. Walk slowly on wet deck. Do not run or stand still on the nap. Do not cut below the rust line. Do not dig, sample, or introduce anything biological. Respect the quiet, the guild rotation, the mules, the stoves, and the fact that the ground is the one thing here you cannot build on, cannot trust, and cannot do without.
