Prepare as if you are visiting a damp, low-ceiling cave that is also a fragile biological nursery, a slow geological workshop, and a place with unreliable geometry and time. The Bright Pocket is not a place to conquer or map. It is a place to enter briefly, predictably, and with as little disturbance as possible. Your main risks are not monsters; they are disorientation, time loss, slips, soft-rock smears, contamination, and the very human tendency to stop noticing how strange things are.

**Before you go**

- **Do not go alone.** You need at least one person outside at the entrance, holding a line, watching an outside clock, and empowered to call you out. A second person inside is useful but not a substitute for an outside attendant.
- **Treat it as a permitted site.** Ask whoever has authority over the hillside. If you cannot get permission, do not enter. Do not enlarge the seam, dig it out, or “improve” access.
- **Check your body.** Tetanus up to date; no open cuts; asthma inhaler if you need one; antihistamine and any prescribed epinephrine; good knees and hips for stooping. If you are claustrophobic or prone to vertigo, do not enter. The brightness may help, but the space still edits.
- **Plan a short first visit.** Threshold-only. Stand just inside, breathe, look, leave. Only after that consider ten or fifteen minutes outside-clock time. Do not plan to “see the whole room” on the first trip.
- **Agree signals and a hard exit time** with your outside attendant. Example: one pull on the line = okay; two = come out now; three = emergency. A whistle can work if sound behaves, but the line and voice are primary. The outside clock is the boss, not your sense of minutes.
- **Test your return method** if you have one. Keep it on your body, dry, and accessible, not buried in a pack. Do not let it become an excuse to stay longer.

**What to bring**

Dress for a cool, wet cellar and a hospital nursery at the same time.

- Dedicated coveralls or a smooth-shell jacket and trousers that can be bagged on exit. Tuck cuffs, tie hair back, remove dangling jewelry and loose straps.
- Bump cap or hard hat with chin strap; kneepads; waterproof boots with aggressive tread; spare dry socks left outside.
- Close-fitting work gloves over nitrile gloves. You do not want bare skin on the soft mineral, the water film, or the fauna.
- Eye protection and a well-fitting P100 or N95 mask. The air may be fine, but you do not want to inhale spores, mineral dust, or tiny animals. Keep your mouth closed around drips.
- A small gas monitor for oxygen and CO2 if you can get one. The pocket breathes, but it is still an enclosed space. No open flame, no candles, no lighters.
- A high-visibility tether line anchored outside, smooth enough not to snag, plus a whistle. Keep the line tended by your outside attendant.
- Two independent timepieces: one mechanical, one electronic, plus the outside attendant’s clock. Time pools here; redundancy is not paranoia.
- Waterproof notebook and pencil, not ink. A small backup headlamp. Your main light is the room, but you want a fallback that does not depend on the room’s mood.
- First aid: saline, bandages, antiseptic for after exit, antihistamine, epinephrine if prescribed. Trash bags and sealed bags for contaminated outer layers.
- No food, no drink, no insect repellent, no strong chemicals, no chalk or paint, no collection jars, no pets, no drone, no large group.

**At the entrance**

Stand outside and watch the seam breathe. Do not pull on the crumbly rock. Do not widen the opening. Put on your mask and gloves before you enter. Let your eyes adjust at the threshold. Run your gas monitor. If you feel dizzy, headachy, or wrong before you are fully inside, leave.

Enter slowly, sideways if needed, with your arms free and the line clear. Keep the entrance behind you. Your first landmark is not a lobe of stone or a shelf; it is the line and the outside attendant. The room will edit its landmarks. Do not build a mental map you will mourn.

**Inside**

- Move slowly. Stoop where the ceiling asks you to. Do not lean on walls. Do not test the soft “cheese” mineral with a finger; it will hold the dent and slowly fill it, and you will have left a mark.
- Keep the tether on. If the line goes taut unexpectedly, stop. If it goes slack, check it. If you lose it, stop where you are and call. Do not wander looking for the entrance; the room is small, but its distances are not reliable.
- Do not touch the root curtain, the retracting moss-like patches, the stacked stones, the scratched marks, or the sitting hollow. Do not sit in the hollow. Do not drink the water film or the drips. Do not stir the water.
- Let the fauna alone. If something lands on you, brush it off gently; do not slap. Keep your bag closed. Do not feed anything. Do not “rescue” a larval creature or a creature with an extra segment.
- Ignore the light’s leftness and the shadows’ disagreements. Notice them, then stop staring. If the light pulses or the shadows lag and you feel a headache coming, exit.
- Use the outside clock. Check in at agreed intervals. If you feel you have been watching isopods for a few minutes and the attendant says it has been forty, leave. If you feel you have been in for an hour and the attendant says five minutes, leave anyway if that was the plan. Time is not a friend here.
- If a lobe of stone has moved or a shelf has grown, do not try to fix it or orient by it. Exit if the entrance is not obvious. The room is not hostile, but it is not obliged to keep your landmarks still.
- Do not take samples, scratch marks, or stack stones. If you are there for research, that is a different, permitted, sterile-tool protocol; an ordinary visitor should leave no trace.

**Getting out**

Leave before your hard exit time, not at it. On exit, step onto a clean mat outside. Bag your outer coverall and boots before you get into a vehicle. Inspect yourself and your gear for hitchhikers: mites, pale isopods, thread-like animals, damp mineral smear. Brush them off outside, away from the entrance, not inside. Shower and wash hair. Launder dedicated clothing hot or quarantine it. Clean any scratches with saline and antiseptic. Watch for allergic reactions. Debrief with your outside attendant while it is fresh, and write down the outside-clock times, not your remembered ones.

The Bright Pocket rewards boring, careful visitors. Pack for a wet cave, behave as if you are in a fragile archive and a nursery at once, keep an outside clock and an outside human in charge, and leave before the room convinces you that you have only just arrived.
