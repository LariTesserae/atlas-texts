## Preparation Brief: The Sinter Bowl

**Short answer:** prepare for a high-altitude geothermal site, a biologically active floor, a fragile archaeological site, and a place where the safest and most respectful visit is probably a short one from the rim. Do not plan to camp in the bowl, sleep in it, walk across the packed mass, build anything, feed anything, water anything, or “commune” with anything. Your job is to arrive warm, sealed, brief, and reversible.

Treat the Sinter Bowl as four hazards at once: thin air, dry heat from below, mineral dust and possible volcanic gases, and an unknown living mass that communicates by warmth and contact. Treat it also as a hearth that belongs to something else.

---

### 1. Before you go: decide what kind of visit this is

The ordinary human traveler should first decide whether they need to enter the bowl at all. They almost certainly do not.

A rim visit gives you the light, the pulse, the packed field, the old wall, the cairns, the hollows, and the sense of the place. Entering the bowl means stepping on living bodies, disturbing a heat-driven congregation, and putting yourself in the warmest, densest, most gas-prone part of the site. Unless your mission has a very specific and defensible reason, plan to stay outside the low wall and on the rim.

If you must go in, plan for minutes, not hours. Go with at least three people: one stays outside the bowl with communications, oxygen, and the exit route; two go no farther than necessary. Never visit alone. Never make this the last stop of a long day. Never go in tired, altitude-sick, dehydrated, or already cold.

Before departure, verify the local physics and biology as far as you can without disturbing the site: atmosphere composition, oxygen partial pressure, gravity, temperature gradient, radiation, gas seepage, microbial or protein hazards, and whether your return anchor works from the mountain. Do not assume the air is breathable because you can smell it. Do not assume the “warmth” is harmless infrared until you have measured it. Do not assume the creatures are mineral until you have seen them pulse.

Get medically cleared for altitude and exertion if you can. Be fit enough to climb slowly and descend carefully. Do not go with a respiratory infection, heart or lung problems, pregnancy, or anything that makes hypoxia dangerous. Carry altitude medication only with proper medical advice, and know your own early symptoms of altitude illness.

---

### 2. Acclimatize outside the bowl, not inside it

The bowl is high, the air is thin, and the work of breathing is real. You will hear your own blood. That is not mystical; that is hypoxia and exertion.

Stage your approach. Camp lower. Spend a night or two at intermediate altitude if the mountain allows it. Check oxygen saturation and resting breathing before you go up. Move slowly. Speak less than you want to. Stop often. The place already swallows sound; do not make your body compensate by shouting.

Set a hard turnaround time before you leave camp. Altitude and darkness both erode judgment. Use a written checklist, a timer, and a buddy who has permission to call the visit off. If anyone has a headache that will not quit, nausea, dizziness, confusion, loss of balance, or breathlessness at rest, descend. Do not “push through” to see the pulse.

Carry supplemental oxygen. Even if you do not need it at the rim, it buys you margin. Carry a pulse oximeter and know how to use it. Carry a portable shelter and insulation for the outside camp, but do not plan to sleep in the bowl. A warm human body lying down in that place is exactly the kind of heat source the creatures cluster toward.

---

### 3. Dress for dry cold, warm ground, and unknown dust

The bowl is warm from below, but the mountain air is thin and probably cold. You need layers you can open and close, because you may be cold standing on the rim and hot standing over the seam.

Wear synthetic or tightly woven layers rather than loose down or frayed wool. You do not want to shed fibers into a place that is already a slow accumulation of mineral crust and dry powder. Wear a windproof outer shell. Cover your skin. Gloves should be worn at all times, with a nitrile or thin liner under an insulating glove so that if you touch something accidentally, the contact layer can be bagged later.

Footwear is a compromise. You want insulated soles, good traction on greasy sinter, and enough protection from warm rock that you can stand safely. But you do not want aggressive crampons or sharp treads that will punch through the crust and crush the creatures. Wide, soft-ish soles are better than spikes. Gaiters will keep the pale dust out of your boots. Do not walk barefoot, in sandals, or in thin running shoes. Do not kneel or sit on the sinter; use your IR thermometer, not your hand, to check ground temperature.

Carry a full-face or well-sealed respirator with particulate filtration, and fit-test it before the trip. The sinter is a fine mineral powder, and the air smells of hot flint and burnt hair; that is a warning, not atmosphere. If gas monitoring shows carbon dioxide, sulfur compounds, carbon monoxide, methane, or oxygen deficiency, a particulate mask is not enough. You need supplied air or you leave. A filter mask does not create oxygen.

Sealed goggles are better than glasses. No contact lenses. The air is dry enough to chap eyes and nose; carry preservative-free eye drops, lip balm, and a skin barrier. But do not open water or gel containers in the bowl if you can avoid it. Keep your water sealed, drink through a closed bottle or bite valve, and do not spill. There is no water here, and the place does not forgive a spill.

---

### 4. Bring instruments, not just courage

Minimum useful instruments:

- **Oxygen and gas monitor** for O₂, CO₂, CO, H₂S, and combustible gas. Add radon if you have it and the rock warrants it.
- **Infrared thermometer** for ground and seam temperature. Do not test heat with your hand or boot.
- **Pulse oximeter** and a watch or timer.
- **Red headlamp** with spare batteries, plus a dim white backup kept pointed down. No flash photography, no floodlights, no drone lights.
- **Radio and locator beacon.** Shouts die in that air. Whistles may not carry. Use radio, tactile signals, and a buddy tether if you must move near the rim.
- **Compass, map, and a return anchor.** Do not rely on the sky. There is barely a sky. Do not rely on local GPS unless you have verified it in this reality.
- **Decontamination bags** for outer layers, gloves, and mask. A dry brush and sealed wipes. You will not have enough water to wash everything there; plan to bag and clean later.
- **First aid:** burn dressing, trauma kit, antihistamine, painkiller, altitude medication if prescribed, and emergency oxygen.
- **Food** that does not crumb. Eat outside the bowl. Do not leave scraps.

Do not bring a fire. Do not bring a stove into the bowl. Do not bring bright lights, music, drones, or offerings. Do not bring samples out unless you have a containment protocol and a very good reason. The pale efflorescence at the center is beautiful and not comforting; leave it alone.

---

### 5. Approach the rim slowly and survey before entering

Stop well below the rim. Use instruments first. Watch the light. Listen to the susurrus. Watch the pulse cycle. The mass contracts toward the center about every ninety minutes, then thins and opens. Time your approach to the quiet interval between pulses, and be off the floor before the next contraction.

Approach through the gap in the low wall, not over it. The wall never kept them in; do not add to it, repair it, or climb it. Do not touch the cairns. Do not put your hand into the hand-sized hollows. Those are marks of making, and they are older than your visit. Treat them as you would a shrine, a grave marker, or an archaeological surface: look, do not handle.

Keep one person outside the bowl with a clear exit route. Keep radio contact. Keep your speech low and brief, not because the place is magical, but because the air is thin and sound dies and there is no reason to fill the quiet.

Use red light. Let your eyes adapt. The dull red glow from the seam is enough to tell pale crust from dark rock, but not enough to walk by. A dim white light pointed at your feet is acceptable if you need it; a bright beam across the bowl is not. The creatures have no eyes, but the place is not yours to illuminate.

---

### 6. If you enter the bowl, enter as little as possible

The floor is packed with living bodies. They look like cobbles until they pulse. Do not step on them if you can avoid it. The description says that to walk you would have to step on them; that is the argument for not walking.

If you must go in:

- Stay near the rim. Do not go to the center. The center is the warmest, densest, most active, and most gas-prone part of the bowl.
- Do not stand over the glowing seam. Do not lean over it. Do not probe it.
- Step only on bare sinter, and only where you can see it. If there is no bare sinter, stop.
- Use slow, wide, soft footfalls. No trekking poles inside the bowl; they will puncture or lever against bodies. Leave poles outside.
- Do not sit, kneel, or lie down. Do not set your pack on the floor. Do not linger in one warm spot.
- Keep gloves on. Do not pick up a creature, turn one over, or inspect its underside. The mouth is a wet-dark slit against the sinter; that is close enough.
- If a creature touches you, stand still and let it move. Do not kick it, slap it, or pull it off. Retreat slowly. If one attaches or presses against you, use a gloved hand to lever gently if you must, then leave.
- If the pulse begins, leave. If the mass starts pressing inward, leave. If you feel heat through your soles, leave. If your gas monitor alarms, leave uphill and upwind of the bowl, not down into the low ground.
- If you feel the urge to lie down in the warmth, treat that as a danger signal. It may be fatigue, hypoxia, cold, or heat; none of them are safe here.

Do not try to communicate with the thin swallowed click. Do not answer the pulse. Do not offer warmth, food, water, or light. You are a warm leak in a place that knows how to use warmth. Be still, be brief, and be gone.

---

### 7. Watch the clock and the body

Plan a short exposure. A rim visit of twenty to thirty minutes may be enough. A floor visit should be shorter unless you have full support and a specific reason. Altitude, darkness, silence, and the pulsing mass all make time feel strange. Use a timer.

Turn back early if:

- Your oxygen saturation drops below your predetermined threshold.
- You have a new headache, nausea, dizziness, or confusion.
- Your breathing is hard at rest.
- Your hands or feet are cold and numb, or your boots feel hot.
- The gas monitor alarms.
- The pulse cycle begins and you are not already leaving.
- Anyone on the team wants to stay longer “just to see.” That is the moment to go.

Descend carefully. The descent is where tired people fall. Keep your red light, keep your buddy close, and do not cut the route short in the dark.

---

### 8. Leave no trace, and decontaminate afterward

The bowl is a hearth, a congregation, and an old marked place. Do not build cairns. Do not stack stones. Do not carve. Do not take “souvenirs.” Do not leave food, water, markers, or offerings. Do not try to “help” a creature that has left the bowl. Do not bring outside organisms in.

Outside the bowl, bag your outer layer and gloves. Do not brush dust off near your face. Inspect your skin. If you have had contact with the powder or a creature, note it and clean at base with the appropriate protocol. Quarantine your gear until you know whether the dust or biota is hazardous. Get a medical check if you have any reaction, breathing trouble, rash, eye irritation, or fever.

If you accidentally carry out a sample, contain it and do not open it. The efflorescence is not a souvenir; it is a question the place does not answer.

---

### 9. The mental preparation

Expect silence. Expect darkness. Expect your own blood in your ears. Expect the mass to look like stones until it breathes. Expect the creatures to ignore you until your warmth becomes useful to them. That is not welcome; it is physics.

Do not romanticize the place into a temple, and do not treat it as a monster. It is a hearth with no visible fire, a congregation with no god, a quiet that goes on and on. You are an ordinary human with water in your body and heat in your blood. That makes you interesting to the wrong things and vulnerable to the wrong conditions.

The best visit is a rim visit: acclimatized, masked, gloved, red-lit, brief, silent, and reversible. Bring nothing in. Take nothing out. Do not add your warmth to the center. Leave before the pulse tells you to.
