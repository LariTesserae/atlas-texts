# Preparing for the Kettle: A Field Guide for the Ordinary Traveler

## Before you decide to go

**Go in the dry season.** This is not optional. In the wet season the pool rises, the marsh floods, the entrance slope turns into a sluice, and you will either be trapped inside with forty people and a foot of tea-colored water in the low chamber, or trapped outside with nowhere to sleep. Ask locally when the water is lowest; arrive with margin on both sides.

**Do your reading first.** Learn what a marsh subsistence economy means: that "barely enough" is a real technical condition, not modesty, and that a guest who eats and does not give back is a genuine problem. Learn the basics of heat, humidity, and hygiene in close quarters. Learn to bend.

**Learn at least some of the language — or bring someone who has it.** Forty people in a hundred and fifty paces is not a place where you can bluff with gestures for a week. The Kettle is loud, so you will be shouting; the acoustics are strange, so you will be mishearing. A few dozen words and the drip-song's rhythm go further here than a phrasebook.

**Prepare to be the only outsider.** You will be watched continuously, not because they're hostile — they seem to be a warm, argumentative, hospitable people — but because there is nowhere for anyone to *not* watch. Do not go if you have a low tolerance for being looked at.

---

## Your body is the main tool

**Get used to bending and crouching.** You will spend almost all your time stooped. Practice it for weeks beforehand: squats, deep lunges, time spent under a low table, moving on your knees. Strengthen your back, quads, and neck. A tall person who cannot fold themselves will spend their visit concussed and unwelcome.

**Acclimatize to heat and damp.** It is warmer inside than out, always, and the air is the density of a wet mouth. If you have asthma, mold sensitivity, or bad sinuses, think hard — the air is full of gnats, dust, spores, and steam you can see. Bring antihistamines, and a plain cloth mask you can sleep in without looking like a lunatic.

**Assume vectors.** Marsh means mosquitoes and worse. Bring repellent (discreet, in a plain bottle), antimalarials and vaccines if your world's medicine works across the boundary, and accept that you will probably get some version of the runs adapting to the water. Oral rehydration salts, anti-diarrheals, and a course of broad-spectrum antibiotics are non-negotiable. A personal water filter straw is worth carrying — but understand that the Kettle drinks what it has, and you cannot filter for forty people.

**Train your gut and your sleep.** If you can sleep in a hammock or sling for a week, practice. If you have never drunk unfiltered water from a place you did not grow up in, consider gut-training and accept discomfort as part of the fare.

**Take your ears into account.** The place is *loud*: pigs, birds ricocheting off the vaults, frogs all night, beetle-rustle in every wall, and people who argue. Bring soft earplugs for sleeping, but keep them out by day — this is a place where you need to hear.

## What to wear

- **Nothing that needs standing up in.** Short, soft, quick-drying layers. Wool or modern synthetics; not cotton, which will be damp for your whole visit and stay that way. Colors that sit with reeds and clay: green, brown, undyed.
- **No metal on your person.** No buckles, zippers, grommets, or snap buttons. Metal here is the most precious material in the world — tiny, imported, and sacred in its scarcity. Wearing it flaunts wealth in a place that has almost none, and the single iron nail kept in a gourd is a communal treasure. Fasten with cord, bone, or wood.
- **Soft, thin soles — or bare feet.** The floor is warped brick laid crooked, the passages are polished and slightly too narrow, and the entrance is a slope of algae-slick rammed earth. Stiff boots are a hazard, not protection. Follow the Kettle's own practice on footwear at the threshold; be ready to go barefoot inside.
- **A soft cap or head-wrap.** The ceilings sag and ribs of drifted timber cross at head-height. Hats also help you sleep under the drips.
- **A belt pouch that closes.** Things fall into the pool. Keep your valuables tied on.

---

## What to carry

- **Dry bags, and a good towel, and then another towel.** Everything is damp. A small silk sleeping-bag liner that dries fast and packs to nothing is a kindness to yourself and invisible to your hosts.
- **A water-resistant notebook and pencil** (pencil, not ink — everything here is wet). If they read by glowing mushroom light, someone there may appreciate your notes and your words.
- **Gifts and trade goods, chosen thoughtfully.** Good salt. Good cordage — they use root-cord and gut-thread and yours will be perceived as finer. Cloth. Needles. Fish-hooks. A small, excellent knife. **And a little metal** — a handful of nails, a blade, a fish-hook or two. This is exactly the medium of their wealth; bringing it is consistent with their world, not a rupture. But bring *little*, give it *quietly*, and expect it to matter enormously. Think hard before you hand a community of forty the means to destabilize itself.
- **Medicines, for yourself and a modest surplus to offer.** Do not play god; do offer.
- **Sweets or a small treat for the children**, who are everywhere, and who will have invented games about you before you finish descending the slope.
- **A small flute or a song.** They are a singing people; the drips are tuned and the children learn the drip-song before they walk. Come ready to learn theirs, and to offer one of yours.
- **Do not bring a wheel, a large stock of metal tools, a rifle, or anything you cannot imagine forty people in a hollow mound storing.** No fires. No modern light you can hide — and if you have one, keep it for emergencies and explain it honestly if asked.

---

## Arrival

Approach through the reeds and past the green top of the mound, and **call down the slope before you go down it.** Do not simply appear in a household's throat. The slope is slick with algae — take it slowly, on your seat if you must, with a rope and a companion if that is possible. If you can arrive with a local guide from a neighboring marsh, do; it changes everything.

Bring **food to share** on arrival. Food is tight here. A guest-gift of fish, dried meat, or something they cannot catch is not a formality; it is the difference between a welcome and a strain. Do not present it as charity.

---

## Inside: manners, water, noise, and the dead

**Bend. Always bend.** Move deliberately and slowly. Watch the vaults and shafts. Do not grab the ceiling ribs to steady yourself — they are sistered, lashed, sagging, and holding up a woven roof; they are not handholds. Do not run. Do not try to stand.

**Respect the walls.** The dead are in the niches, wrapped in reed and sealed with daub and repainted with a handprint each generation. Never scrape, lean hard against, or point at a sealed niche. Never touch the sediment of handprints. Ask before you sketch them, and before you sketch people.

**Water is cycled and precious.** Everything runs to the low chamber and is lifted out in gourd-buckets and poured on the terraces. Do not splash, do not pour away, do not soap or wash in the pool. Ask where ablutions are done. Do not contaminate what they drink.

**Learn the drip-song.** The pots under the leaks are tuned, more or less, and the falling water keeps a loose, wandering time the children learn before they can walk. Join the keeping of time if you're invited. It is a social glue, and it will be one of the things you remember.

**Eat what is offered, finish it, and do not ask for more.** The terraces are sparse — mushrooms in the dark shelves, cress and mint at the pool, one stubborn light-starved vine — and everyone knows it is barely enough. Compliment the mushrooms and the cress. Do not complain about the smell of yeast, wet clay, and the animal pens; you will stop noticing it by the third day and miss it by the fourth.

**Accept the lack of privacy.** Forty people in a hundred and fifty paces; you are always observed. Do not skulk or seek hiding places — it reads as guilt. The kind privacy here is the privacy of a turned shoulder and a lowered voice, and you will be given it in the same measure you give it.

**The pigs are bad-tempered.** Stay out of their way; do not make them a joke. The eel-tanks are cut into the floor — do not step where you cannot see. The birds come and go through the shafts all day and have made the place their own; do not shoo them, they are part of the architecture now. The frogs will sing all night. Do not complain about the frogs.

**Offer labor, and ask first.** Carry reed, haul water, help mend daub. A wall here is not finished — it is *kept* — and a guest who helps keep it is a guest. A guest who "improves" it is a problem. Do not introduce the wheel, the nail, or the new method. If you bring metal, bring it as a gift and let them decide how to keep it.

**Do not lecture them about the marsh.** The Kettle is growing, the marsh is silting, the light-shafts have been raised twice in living memory, and it will not last forever. Everyone knows. It is the great argument of the place — two hundred years old, loud, unresolved. Do not join it uninvited, and do not offer the outsider's solution. Sit with the knowledge. It is one of the reasons to come.

## The hazards, plainly

Head injuries from low ceilings and sagging ribs; falls on the entrance slope; structural give in anything you swing from or load; cuts from the packed shell and sherd band at the base of the walls; respiratory trouble from mold and spores; vector-borne and water-borne illness; a fire risk in a structure of woven cane, dry reed, and fish oil (which they mitigate by never lighting what they don't need — you do the same). And the quieter hazard of forgetting that a place this beautiful and this warm is also a place of real, grinding scarcity. Do not romanticize it. Do not stay so long that your welcome becomes a burden.

---

## Leave before the water rises

A few days to a week is generous here, not stingy. Learn all forty names. Give what you can, take what you are given, help keep the wall, learn the drip-song, and go while you are still loved. Take the slope slowly on the way out. Look back once at the green top of the mound above the reeds, and listen to the din fade behind you into the marsh — and know that somewhere in those walls a new handprint will dry, and the drips will still be falling into their tuned pots, keeping their loose, argumentative, wandering time.
