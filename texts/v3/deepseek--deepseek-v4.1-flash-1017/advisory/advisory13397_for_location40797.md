Treat this as a visit to a warm, saturated, low-light, vertical wetland city with no dry ground, primitive hand-craft technology, and a people who do not think of their world as “drowned.” You are the clumsy alien: heavy, impatient, metal-rich, and prone to mildew. Prepare to be slow, dependent on guides, and constantly damp.

**First, verify the basics before you commit.** The description reads as breathable but hot, mineral-thick, buoyant air—not literal liquid water. If it is actually water you must move through and breathe, an ordinary human cannot visit without full aquatic support and local life-support cooperation. Do not assume. Check: oxygen, pressure, humidity, mineral aerosols, spore load, temperature, buoyancy, tide range, and whether the Aethel can tolerate your presence.

**Before departure**
- Arrange a local guide and a fixed arrival time at slack tide. Do not arrive unannounced in a vertical coral city.
- Learn the tide cycle and basic Aethel etiquette. Their schedule is tidal, not clock-based.
- Get physical: swimming/wading, breath control in humid air, balance on slick surfaces, upper-body strength for handlines, and tolerance for heat and constant wetness.
- Address asthma, sinus problems, skin conditions, mold allergies, and immune compromise. This is a bad place for untreated respiratory or fungal risk.
- Pack light, sealed, and non-invasive. Sterilize gear; no seeds, soil, insects, or uncontained microbes.
- Bring a small amount of metal as trade—needles, fishhooks, a small knife—but do not flaunt it. Metal is scarce and treasured. Do not bring weapons or advanced tech as a display.

**Gear**
- Tough, quick-dry, snug clothing; avoid loose straps and jewelry that snag on coral or vines. Hood, gloves, goggles, soft grippy boots.
- Low-lumen red/amber headlamp and spare sealed batteries. Avoid bright white light; bioluminescence is the local illumination and should not be blasted.
- Respirator or high-quality spore mask, saline nasal spray, eye drops, ear drops. Humidity will make filters miserable; bring spares.
- Waterproof bags, desiccant, silicone grease, cordage, netting, plastic zip ties, patch kit. Metal gear will corrode and may mark you as wealthy.
- Suspended sleeping kit: hammock with net, tarp, dry-ish sleeping clothes, microfiber towel. There is no dry ground to camp on.
- Water filter plus chemical purification; electrolyte tablets; high-calorie sealed rations. Do not drink ambient water untreated.
- Medical kit: antifungal powder/cream, antibiotic ointment, antihistamines, epinephrine if prescribed, blister care, wound closure, antidiarrheal, probiotics, vitamin D, personal meds.
- Compact buoyancy aid and a light weighted belt if local buoyancy is strong. Practice neutral movement; thick water/air makes you float and fight resistance.
- Whistle, signal mirror, waterproof notebook, tide chart, guide contact. GPS and compass may be useless; navigate by vertical landmarks, currents, and tide levels.

**What it will feel like**
You will move through warm, mineral-heavy air that feels like a wet blanket. Sounds are damped. Light is dim and diffuse. Everything is slick, curved, and encrusted. The city creaks and groans; living vine bridges flex; coral platforms may be layered and uneven. You will be constantly wet, mildly buoyant, and slower than everyone around you. Sleep will be strange under perpetual twilight; keep a routine and use a sleep mask.

**Safety rules**
- Never climb the chalky mountains. They crumble; the silt is slick and the dust is abrasive. Goggles and mask on if you must pass near.
- Test every platform and handline. Ask before grabbing living vines or coral. Do not cut or damage grown structures.
- Avoid dense kelp alone; it is predatory and easy to tangle in. Stay with your guide.
- Do not touch or eat bioluminescent fungi, algal mats, or local creatures without permission and preparation. Allergies and toxins are unknown.
- Watch tides. Routes may close or become dangerous at high or low water. Plan by tide, not by distance.
- Keep tethered near currents and drop-offs. Do not jump between levels.
- No open flames, no loud music, no drones. Pack out all waste. Minimize contamination.

**Social and cultural preparation**
- Be patient. The Aethel are described as lithe and patient; rushing will read as rude and clumsy.
- Do not mock their sky-as-story or dry-land-as-myth. Do not preach about the surface. Ask questions respectfully.
- Ask before photographing, touching gardens, or handling tools. Barter slowly. Small metal goods are valuable; too much metal may cause disruption or envy.
- Respect the bioluminescent gardens and snail pens. They are working infrastructure, not scenery.
- Accept dependency. You will need a guide, tide advice, and help with local food and shelter. Do not pretend to be self-sufficient.

**Arrival routine**
Arrive with your guide at slack tide. Declare trade goods. Get a local orientation: tide schedule, safe routes, emergency rendezvous, food/water rules, and what not to touch. Establish a signal plan—whistle, low light, tether. Keep your mask and goggles accessible. Set a return beacon or exit plan before you go deeper into the city.

**Emergency and exit**
Assume medical evacuation is difficult. Carry your own critical meds and a way to signal. If you lose your guide, stop, tether, and signal rather than wandering. On return, quarantine and decontaminate gear; treat yourself for fungal and respiratory exposure. Leave no biological material behind.

If you cannot tolerate heat, humidity, dimness, enclosure, constant wetness, and being visibly dependent on others, do not go. Aethelburg is beautiful, but it is not a hiking destination. It is a slow, patient, living place—and you will be the guest who drips.
