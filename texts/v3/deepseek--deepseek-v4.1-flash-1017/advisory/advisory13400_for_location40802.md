Prepare for a place that is less a wilderness than a **held museum with a working town inside it**. The central fact is simple and total: **nothing wears except wood**. That changes how you move, what you carry, what you say, and what you leave behind. Your main job is to be a low-impact guest, not an explorer who tests the rules. The basin does not heal and does not erase; anything you disturb may remain exactly as you left it.

## 1. Before you go: adopt the right mindset

- **Treat every disturbance as permanent.** Do not move stones, carve initials, stack cairns, cut plants, redirect water, pick flowers, collect insects, or “improve” a campsite. In a normal landscape, damage is forgotten by weather. Here it is not.
- **Do not test the rules.** The clean socket under a picked-up stone is a trap. The fixed rain column is not a shower. The held flies are not a swarm to swat. Watch, ask, and leave things alone.
- **Respect the locals’ view.** They do not experience the held world as a curse. It is the floor under their feet. Do not call the town stagnant, frozen, backward, or “cursed.” Do not suggest they need progress, relocation, or iron.
- **Assume your gear may or may not obey local rules.** Wood clearly ages. Stone breaks but does not weather. Your synthetic clothing, batteries, steel, and body are unknown quantities. Bring durable, repairable gear, but do not rely on the basin to preserve you.
- **Arrange a return anchor and outside contact.** Multiverse travel aside, this is a high, cold, foggy basin with fixed weather and unfamiliar infrastructure. Leave an itinerary, carry an emergency beacon or equivalent, and know how you will leave.

## 2. Physical preparation and clothing

The basin is high, cool, wet, and frequently fog-bound. The grey lid never really opens. The one wind comes from the same quarter at the same strength. Hypothermia is a real risk even when the place feels lush and green.

- **Layers:** wool or synthetic base layers, warm midlayer, waterproof shell, warm hat, gloves, spare dry socks. Wool is good; local sheep exist, but do not assume you can buy or borrow clothing easily.
- **Footwear:** grippy soft-soled boots. The stone is wet, mossy, and often sharp-edged. Avoid metal studs, crampons, or hobnails—they will scar held stone and are unnecessary. Bring gaiters for wet meadows.
- **Gloves:** useful for the raw scree pass and any accidental contact with slate. The edges stay fresh.
- **Trekking poles:** rubber tips, not metal spikes. Use them for balance on wet stair-streets and moss-filled joints.
- **Shelter:** prefer local lodging or an existing shelter. If you must camp, use a freestanding tent that needs no stakes, or a hammock only if you have explicit permission. Do not drive stakes into held ground; do not move stones to weigh guylines. Do not camp in orchards, wet meadows, animal rounds, or the rain column.
- **Navigation:** map, compass, watch, headlamp, spare batteries. Fog fills the basin floor in the morning and returns in the evening. Plan travel for mid-morning when it lifts. Use the fixed wind, the rain column, the stair-streets, and the ridgelines as landmarks—but do not walk through the rain column casually.
- **Emergency kit:** first aid for cuts and sprains, antiseptic, blister care, emergency bivy, whistle, cordage, repair kit. The stone is sharp; falls are the main hazard.

## 3. Water, food, and sanitation

The water is clear, cold, and apparently clean—rivers run over clean stone with no sand, pools do not silt. That does **not** mean it is safe for an ordinary human.

- **Filter or treat all water.** Use your own filter or chemical treatment. If you boil, use your own stove and fuel. Do not burn local wood; wood is the town’s economy, clock, and status marker.
- **Do not contaminate the water infrastructure.** The cisterns, condensation channels, weirs, drains, and covered aqueduct are working public works. Do not wash in them, bathe in them, drop anything into them, or step on the aqueduct slabs. Ask where visitors may draw water and where they may wash.
- **Do not forage without permission.** The orchards are heavy with fruit and the meadows are lush, but the ecology is held. Harvest may be regulated, and outside seeds or microbes are a serious risk.
- **Carry your own food.** Bring nonperishable food that does not contain viable seeds or invasive organisms. If offered local food, accept politely unless you have allergies; ask what is in it.
- **Pack out all waste.** Do not bury it. In a world where nothing wears, burial may not decompose as you expect. Use designated latrines if they exist; otherwise use wag bags and carry them out. Do not urinate near water sources.
- **Clean your gear before entry.** Boots, tent, clothing, pockets: remove seeds, mud, and biological hitchhikers. An invasive species in a held ecology could be catastrophic—or could simply persist forever. Either way, do not be the vector.

## 4. The pass and the terrain

The approach over the ridge is the first test. The scree and shattered slate look like they should slide, but they do not. Stones lie exactly as they fell, and every other stone holds its place.

- **Stay on established paths.** Do not scramble the raw scree. Do not kick stones to hear them fall. Do not pick one up “just to see.” If you accidentally dislodge a stone, put it back exactly in its socket and move on.
- **Watch your feet.** Fresh-edged slate cuts. Wet moss on stone is slick. The gullies are freshly incised and steep. Cliff faces are planar and bright, not softened; edges are sharp.
- **Do not assume “no erosion” means “no hazard.”** Stone can still break, slip, or fall if you disturb it. Water is cold. Pools are deep enough to be dangerous. Falls do not undercut their lips, but you can still fall over one.
- **Use the stair-streets and lanes.** The town is a wide, low weave of stone clusters with orchards and pastures between. Lanes are paved with fitted stone. Walk them. Do not climb dry-laid walls, roofs, or the covered aqueduct.

## 5. The town: etiquette and economy

The settlement is not a dense city. It is a town-sized scatter across the basin floor and terraces, with a minute between doorways, sheep on roofs, children in drains, and walls around every meadow. It is a city that has decided to be a landscape.

- **Ask before crossing walls or entering orchards.** The green spaces are built and owned. Empty-looking lanes may still be private approaches.
- **Do not touch lintels or comment on their age.** Wood is the only thing that ages. A household’s status can be read off how new its lintel is. Wood is the local clock, currency, and measure of time. Do not whittle, carve, burn, or casually gift wooden objects. If you need wood for anything, ask and barter.
- **Keep steel discreet.** There is copper—hinges, gutters, tools, bright buttons—and no iron. Your steel knife, buckles, zippers, and boot hardware may be conspicuous or disruptive. Do not display iron as a wonder or offer it as trade without asking. Use copper, bronze, ceramic, or stone tools where you can. Keep your steel sheathed and explain it as a personal tool if asked.
- **Bring modest gifts, but ask first.** Good bronze or copper needles, fishhooks, buttons, salt, spices, or high-quality cloth may be welcome. Do not bring outside plants, animals, seeds, or food that could invade. Do not bring a wooden gift without advice; a new wooden object starts a clock.
- **Respect the animals.** The small dark deer move on fixed rounds through the orchard terraces. Marmots sit on walls. Cranes wade in lines. Herons stand like furniture. The chough with the copper bell is treated as a civic official—do not catch it, feed it, shoo it, or laugh at it. Do not block animal routes. Do not bring pets or drones. Do not hunt or fish unless explicitly invited.
- **Do not disturb the held flies.** They hang in little clouds, part around your hand, and close again. Do not swat, spray, or collect them. Use a physical head net if midges bother you. Do not release collected insects elsewhere.
- **Keep the quiet.** The place is quiet in a way that is a substance: moss, turf, and fog swallow sound. Speak low. Do not play music, shout, or test echoes. Footsteps on the stair-streets are dull; let them stay dull.
- **Do not pity the town or preach change.** They demolish and rebuild in the same footprint because there is no reason not to. The plan does not change. That is not failure; it is their floor.

## 6. Water infrastructure and the fixed weather

- **The rain column:** a fixed ten-pace-wide band of drizzle falls on the same strip of scree all day. You can walk around it. Do not camp in it, dam it, divert it, or use it as a shower without permission. It is already walled, flumed, and led into the town’s works.
- **Condensation channels:** long shallow gutters of dressed rock collect morning fog. Do not step in them, block them, or wash in them. They are drinking-water infrastructure.
- **Cisterns, weirs, drains, aqueduct:** all are working, lichened, and maintenance-free because nothing needs maintenance. Do not pollute them. Do not walk on the covered aqueduct of dry-laid slabs. Do not let children’s play in the drains become your excuse to block or litter them.
- **Fog:** morning fog fills the floor to knee height, then chest height, and lifts by mid-morning into a grey lid. It returns in the evening. Navigation is easy to lose. Wait it out rather than scrambling blind.

## 7. Health and safety

- **Cold and wet:** the main risk. Carry spare dry layers, emergency shelter, and a way to make warm drink with your own stove.
- **Cuts and falls:** the stone is sharp and slippery. Carry gloves, antiseptic, bandages, and blister care. Do not run on wet stair-streets.
- **Water:** cold, clear, and attractive. Filter or treat it. Do not swim alone. Do not drink from pools used by herons and cranes without treatment.
- **Insects:** the held flies may or may not bite. Use a head net and keep your mouth closed in dense clouds. Avoid chemical sprays that could affect the held ecology; use physical barriers.
- **Animals:** no large predators are described, but do not corner deer, marmots, cranes, or herons. Give the chough room. Do not feed anything.
- **Psychological:** the held quiet and fixed rounds can be uncanny. The same wind, same rain column, same frog choruses, same lean of the trees, same held chord of sound. Keep a routine, keep a journal, take breaks, and leave if you feel derealization or creeping urge to “test” the world. Do not become a permanent feature by accident.

## 8. What not to do—short list

- Do not move stones, pick up scree, carve, stack cairns, or build anything permanent.
- Do not dam, divert, pollute, or bathe in the water works.
- Do not camp in the rain column, on animal rounds, in orchards, or in wet meadows.
- Do not burn, cut, carve, or casually gift wood.
- Do not display, trade, or introduce iron without asking.
- Do not forage, hunt, fish, or collect insects without permission.
- Do not feed, catch, block, or harass animals—especially the bell-chough.
- Do not swat or spray the held flies.
- Do not litter, bury waste, or leave any trace; pack everything out.
- Do not bring outside seeds, plants, animals, or microbes.
- Do not call the town stagnant, cursed, or backward.
- Do not play loud music, shout, or test echoes.
- Do not assume the basin is safe because it is static. Sharp stone, cold water, fog, and falls are real.

## 9. A practical packing summary

Warm layers, waterproof shell, spare socks, grippy soft-soled boots, gaiters, gloves, rubber-tipped poles, freestanding shelter or lodging arranged, cool-damp sleeping bag, headlamp, map/compass/watch, water filter, own stove and fuel, nonperishable food, mess kit, wag bags, trash bags, first aid, antiseptic, blister kit, emergency bivy, whistle, repair kit, head net, modest non-invasive gifts, notebook, return beacon.

Go in expecting a place where the land looks young and the city looks old at once, where the moss holds its breath and the smoke always goes the same way over the raw bright ridge. Be slow, ask permission, keep your hands to yourself, and leave the held chord exactly as you found it.
