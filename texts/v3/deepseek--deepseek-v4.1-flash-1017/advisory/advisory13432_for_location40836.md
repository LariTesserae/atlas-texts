To prepare for a visit to the Basin of the Warm Shallows, an ordinary human traveler from the broader multiverse must think less like a tourist and more like an engineer preparing to enter a slow, warm, and patient machine. The basin does not hate you, but it is profoundly indifferent to your comfort and will quietly disassemble your equipment and your expectations.

**I. Physical Preparation: The Body as a Machine**

Your primary adversary is not danger, but decay. The environment is designed for the slow transformation of materials, and your body is a material.

*   **Clothing:** Do not bring anything you are not willing to destroy. The salt and soda in the air and water will stiffen, stain, and eat through fabric, especially cotton and leather. Bring durable synthetics or simple, tough wool that you can rinse and wring out. More importantly, bring a change of clothes for when you leave; the ones you wear will never be truly dry and will carry the basin's smell of wet chalk and hot iron for months. Bring a wide-brimmed waterproof hat. Bring two pairs of tough, waterproof boots. One pair will be perpetually damp from the constant condensation; the other will be your dry pair for sleeping. Your feet are your most critical piece of equipment. Keep them dry, or they will rot in a way this basin, for all its stillness, will not.

*   **Skin and Eyes:** The entire basin is a mild but relentless chemical irritant. You will need a supply of fresh water and a mild, non-alkaline soap for washing skin and eyes. Goggles or sealed glasses are essential, as the brine spray from the terraces and the fine mineral dust from the crust will irritate your eyes. Your skin will dry out and crack; bring a thick, occlusive ointment, not a watery lotion.

*   **On Your Person:** Carry a walking stick, long and sturdy, to test the ground ahead of you. The crust is thin and the water is just below. A stick can mean the difference between a wet foot and a broken ankle. Carry your own supply of drinking water. The water is a trap: it is warm, inviting, and will dehydrate you as surely as seawater. Do not carry complex electronic devices. The warm, damp, alkaline air is death to fine circuitry. A simple, mechanical watch is your best tool for tracking the spring's cycles. Bring a small, waterproof bag for your most essential items—identification, currency, a pen and paper. Everything else is consumable.

**II. Mental Preparation: The Psychology of Stillness**

The most difficult adjustment will be to the basin's tempo. It is not a place of action, but of process. Your human instinct will be to find the "event," the thing to *do*. There isn't one. You are between the ticks of a very slow clock.

- **Embrace the Quiet:** Prepare for the sound of the shrimp. What is described as a sift and a pattering will become the dominant sound of your experience. It is a constant, low-grade, unceasing noise. It will be the last thing you hear before sleep and the first thing you hear upon waking. It can be maddening if you fight it; let it become a "white noise" that lulls you. The true silence of the place is in the birds, which are shockingly still.
- **Learn the Rhythm:** The springs breathe on a cycle of hours. Your first day, just sit and watch the water levels. Watch when the sluices are opened and closed. Observe the wall-dressers and the rakers. Their work is timed to this breath. Align your own day with it. Don't try to fight the cycle. Do your exploring when the water is low. Rest when it is high. You are a guest in the lung of the world.
- **Abandon Schedules:** A clock is a tool here, not a master. Time is measured in the fill and drain of the pans, in the slow growth of stone. If a path is blocked by a flooding terrace, you wait. If the fog is too thick to navigate safely, you settle in. You are on the basin's time now.

**III. The Visit Itself: Practical Wisdom for the Basin**

- **Finding Your Feet:** As you come down the ramp, stop. Test every step with your stick. The ringing sound of your boots on the crust is your constant guide. High, clear rings are good. A dull thud means you are over a void or a soft spot. Never trust a patch of crust that looks new and smooth; it is likely the thinnest.
- **Navigating the Town:** The town is a lattice of low walls that are slowly breathing in and out of existence. Follow the established paths between the channels and pans. Be wary of doorways; what fit a century ago may not fit now. If you are given lodging, check the door. You may have to lift it, or shave a layer from the stone yourself. The people here will understand. They are wall-dressers, all of them, in their own way.
- **Interacting with Locals:** The people are not unfriendly, just occupied. They are working to a rhythm you don't yet understand. Be respectful, be quiet, and observe before you speak. They are the only ones who can tell you which crust is safe, which pans are shallow, and which building is about to become a pool. The line about the town and the birds "being paid by the same warm water" is the key to their worldview. They are not fighting the basin; they are collaborating with it. You should do the same.
- **Food and Drink:** Eat what the town provides, which will likely be salted and preserved. Fresh food will not keep. Drink only what they give you. The local water is for the shrimp and the pans, not for your body.
- **The Dangers:** The dangers are slow and quiet. The main risk is falling through the crust into a warm, hidden pool. This might not seem dangerous, but the water is deep enough and the crust is fragile enough that the hole will close over within a few minutes. Do not go near the birds' nesting grounds; the birds may be silent, but the crust they nest on is often entirely hollow. The second danger is your own equipment. Your gear will fail. A strap will rot. A buckle will seize. Treat your equipment like a living thing and check it daily. A piece of gear that is fine one day will be useless the next.

**IV. Departure: Leaving the Warm**

The basin will try to keep you. Not with any active malevolence, but through inertia. Your clothes will be heavy with damp. Your muscles will ache from the constant, cautious walking. Your mind will be filled with the sifting sound of the shrimp. The greatest danger of your visit may be the temptation to just... stay. To sit by a warm pool and let the world's slow process carry you along.

When you are ready to leave, do so with purpose. Climb the ramp. When you reach the rim, take a deep breath. Feel the wind. It will be cool and dry and sharp. It will be the first truly dry thing you have felt in days. Take your "dry" boots and your fresh set of clothes and change. Seal everything you wore in the basin in a waterproof bag. Do not open it for a month. Do not try to salvage anything from it. It belongs to the basin now.

You will leave the Warm Shallows with new hinge-holes in your own personal jamb. A part of you will have been "shaved back to true" by the quiet. You will find you notice silence more. You will be more patient. And for a long time, the faint, pattering sound of rain on a tin roof will make you look towards the nearest warm, white stone.
