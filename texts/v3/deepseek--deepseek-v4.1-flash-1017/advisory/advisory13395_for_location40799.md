## Preparing for the Wet Basin

Treat the Wet Basin less like a lake or marsh and more like a **warm, featureless quaking fen with gas vents, no landmarks, and no dry ground**. An ordinary human traveler can visit it, but should visit it briefly, self-supported, and with redundancy in navigation, footcare, water, and shelter. The place does not present dramatic hazards so much as cumulative ones: wetness, heat, disorientation, monotony, and the slow erasure of your tracks.

### 1. Plan the visit as short and self-contained
- **Do not go solo if you can avoid it.** A buddy helps with navigation, gas awareness, camp setup, and morale. The constant hum and flat light make it easy to stop noticing things.
- **Enter and leave from the rim, not the center.** The basin is a shallow bowl; the edges are your safest route and your extraction zone. Mark your portal/entry point immediately with a high-visibility flag, GPS waypoint, and physical tether or line if possible.
- **Assume no local rescue, no infrastructure, and no reliable “dry season.”** Carry everything you need to survive an unplanned extra night.
- **Set a hard turnaround time.** Distances are deceptive. Budget two to three times the walking time you think you need.
- **Plan decontamination and quarantine on exit.** The basin is biologically active in small ways; clean gear, boots, and clothing, and avoid carrying mud or water back through the portal.

### 2. Dress for permanent wetness, not rain
The air is warm and heavy, but you will be wet from the ground up. Your clothing should dry fast and protect against abrasion and insects.
- **Breathable waterproof shell** and quick-dry synthetic layers. Avoid cotton.
- **High gaiters** that seal over boots; they keep silt, water, and insects out and reduce the sucking mud against your legs.
- **Boots with drainage ports and aggressive tread**—not chest waders. Waders can fill and trap you in soft ground. Carry a second pair of lightweight camp shoes or booties kept dry.
- **Two or three pairs of wool/synthetic socks** and a strict sock-rotation system. Foot maceration is one of the most likely ways to end your trip.
- **Lightweight waterproof gloves** for handling wet gear, plus insect protection at ankles, wrists, and neck.
- **Polarized sunglasses and a hat.** The flat luminous grey light and water glare strain the eyes and worsen depth perception.
- **High-visibility colors** for you and your gear. Camouflage is a bad idea here.
- **Trekking poles and a probing stick.** You will use them constantly to test hummocks, pool depth, and soft spots.

### 3. Move slowly and deliberately
- **Probe before every step** in suspicious ground. The hummocks are the only relatively dry footing, but they can collapse or hide holes. Pools may be shallower or deeper than they look.
- **Prefer the largest, most continuous hummocks** and the higher ground near the rim. Avoid crossing wide pools unless you have probed a firm route.
- **Accept the sucking ground.** Every step costs energy. Short days, frequent rests, and a light pack matter more than speed.
- **Do not trust your eyes for distance.** Use a rangefinder, GPS distance, or pace count. A ridge that looks close may take an hour.
- **Mark your route if you intend to return the same way.** Footprints close over within minutes. Use biodegradable survey flags, a lightweight line, or GPS breadcrumbs—but keep the markings minimal and remove what you can.

### 4. Navigation must not depend on landmarks
There are no trees, hills, or structures, and the light casts no shadows. Prepare for a navigation whiteout.
- **GPS with preloaded waypoints**, spare batteries, and a waterproof case. Also carry a **baseplate compass** and know how to use it.
- **Altimeter and rangefinder** help with the shallow bowl geometry and deceptive distances.
- **Pace counting and dead reckoning** as backup. Record headings and times.
- **A whistle and signal mirror**—the mirror may be less useful in flat light, but the whistle carries.
- **A small drone** is optional but useful for scouting a route from above; keep it dry and do not rely on it.
- **Mark camp, entry, and any cache** with distinct waypoints and physical markers.

### 5. Carry your water; do not drink the basin
Water is everywhere and almost none of it should be assumed safe. It may carry silt, minerals, sulfur compounds, and unknown microbes.
- **Carry all drinking water for the visit.** This is heavy, so plan a short trip.
- If you must use local water in an emergency, **prefilter silt**, then use the most robust purification you have—filter plus chemical treatment, or a compact reverse-osmosis system if mineral/salt content is high. Boiling kills organisms but does not remove salts or sulfur.
- **Carry a TDS meter** if you plan any local water use. Taste is not a safety test.
- **High-calorie, no-cook food** in sealed bags. Cooking is unnecessary and risky in wet, windy, gas-venting ground. Electrolytes matter in the warm humidity.
- **Pack out all waste.** Use wag bags; there is no dry ground for catholes.

### 6. Shelter above the water, not on it
- **Freestanding tent with a bathtub floor** and a separate groundsheet. No trees means no hammock.
- **Closed-cell foam pad** or a well-protected inflatable pad; you need insulation from wet ground and a barrier against punctures.
- **Bug netting and a ventilated shelter.** The air is warm; condensation is a real problem.
- **Camp on the largest hummock you can find**, probed for stability, away from bubbling pools and low gas pockets. Keep camp high relative to local water.
- **Dry bags for everything.** Keep sleeping bag, spare clothes, electronics, and food sealed. Use silica packs and dielectric grease on electronics.
- **Stakes may not hold** in matted vegetation. Use deadman anchors with stuff sacks or line, and keep the tent tethered to your pack if wind rises.
- **Earplugs for sleep only if you have a buddy on watch.** The constant trickle and subaudible hum can prevent sleep; fatigue is a real hazard.

### 7. Monitor the air and weather
- The sulfurous bubbles suggest **H2S, CO2, or methane pockets**. Carry a small multi-gas monitor if available. Avoid bending over pools, and avoid low depressions where heavier gases may collect.
- **Camp upwind and slightly higher.** If you smell strong sulfur, feel dizzy, or notice a bubble burst nearby, move.
- The sky is uniform grey, but rain is possible. Rain will raise water levels and make already soft ground worse. Carry a barometer and be ready to move to the rim.
- Heat is ambient and humid; watch for heat exhaustion. Drink and eat even when you do not feel thirsty.

### 8. Protect feet, skin, and equipment
- **Footcare is critical.** Dry your feet at every rest, use antifungal powder, treat hot spots immediately, and change socks. Trench foot is a bigger threat than dramatic injury.
- **Cover cuts and abrasions.** Unknown water and silt should not enter open wounds. Carry waterproof plasters, antiseptic, and gloves.
- **Insect repellent and head net.** The insects are slow but persistent; protect ankles and wrists.
- **Equipment corrosion:** dry bags, spare batteries, repair tape, and a small toolkit. Salt/mineral water and humidity will attack metal and electronics.
- **Tethers for critical items**—portal device, GPS, water bottle, knife—so a dropped item does not vanish into silt.

### 9. Prepare psychologically for monotony and indifference
The basin is not hostile in a dramatic way; it is indifferent. That can be more disorienting. Build a routine: fixed check-ins, timed rests, recorded headings, camp chores, and morale breaks. Do not hurry. The place rewards patience and punishes overconfidence. If you feel lost or turned around, **stop, mark your position, eat, drink, and navigate deliberately** rather than walking in circles.

### 10. Emergency and exit
- Carry a **satellite beacon or equivalent**, extra water and food, a shelter, and a clear extraction plan.
- Tell someone your entry point, planned route, turnaround time, and latest return.
- If the portal or exit is fixed, **mark it heavily and approach it from a recorded bearing**, not by memory.
- On exit, clean and quarantine gear, and do not introduce basin water, mud, or organisms elsewhere.

The intuitive preparation is simple: **assume the ground will not hold you, the water will not be drinkable, the light will not help you navigate, and the place will not remember your footprints.** Go light, go slow, mark everything, keep your feet dry, and leave early.
