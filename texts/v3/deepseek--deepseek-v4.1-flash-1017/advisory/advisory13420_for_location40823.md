Prepare for the Tarnholt as a **cold, wet, high-altitude, roadless, fireless, service-less wetland that happens to be a living city**. Not a city break. More like a wet-cold expedition into a biological reef that is also a continent. The ordinary human weaknesses are the ones that matter: you cannot stay warm when wet, you cannot see well in flat light, you cannot dry clothes without fire, you cannot drink unknown water, and you cannot expect rescue or resupply.

### First, resolve the “water at 1.9”
That phrase is the one I would not shrug at. If it means **pH 1.9**, this is not a wet place; it is an acid bath, and an ordinary human visit is either a sealed drysuit operation with independent air and zero skin contact, or a no-go. If it is some saturation or environmental index, still treat all standing water as **non-potable and chemically active until tested**. Bring a water test kit, and carry all drinking water you will need. Do not bathe in it, do not rinse food in it, do not assume ice is safe.

### Dress for “wet is the default”
- **No cotton.** Base layers should be synthetic or wool blend; wool is warm but grows heavy, so use it where warmth matters and synthetic where drying matters.
- **Waterproof shell with sealed seams and a hood.** Expect fine mist and condensation rather than proper rain.
- **Waders or a light drysuit for the approach and channels.** You may spend days walking in shallow water. Waterproof boots alone will not save you.
- **Insulated, waterproof gloves and hat.** Cold enough to ache in the teeth means exposed skin is a liability.
- **Spare dry socks in multiple sealed bags.** Immersion foot is the trip-ender. Change socks, air your feet in the tent, use antifungal powder, and treat every blister early.
- **Goggles or anti-fog eye protection.** Flat white light and constant condensation make depth perception bad.
- **Trekking poles.** The ground is a wet lid, not a floor. Probe before you step.

### Carry your dryness
Drying is nearly impossible there. Manage moisture instead of fighting it.
- **Roll-top pack with internal dry bags.** Electronics, sleep system, spare clothes, and food each get their own sealed layer.
- **Freestanding tent with a bathtub floor and good ventilation.** You will get condensation inside; a single-wall tent will rain on you. Do not rely on staking into living fabric.
- **Synthetic sleeping bag or quilt, closed-cell pad, vapor barrier liner.** Down is a bad bet in a place that teaches wet as default.
- **A raised sleeping platform or cot if you can carry it.** The ground is saturated a metre or two down. Sleeping on the deck is sleeping in the marsh.
- **Silica packs, dry sacks, and a repair kit.** Not to dry everything—just to keep one core layer and your electronics alive.

### Water, food, and fire
- **Carry all potable water.** If you must use local water, filter and chemically treat it, and only after testing. Boiling requires fuel you brought; there is no local fire economy to lean on.
- **No-cook, high-calorie food.** The place is cold, wet, and disorienting; you will burn calories just staying warm and upright. Hot food is morale, but do not depend on it.
- **Bring a sealed stove and all fuel only if you will use it responsibly.** The Tarnholt has no native fire or metal; your stove is an anomaly. Use it on a stable platform, never against living walls, never on peat or reed, and pack out every trace.
- **Pack out all waste.** Do not contaminate channels or weirs.

### Shelter and route discipline
- **Do not camp in low channels or lower terraces.** In the wet season they become connected sheets. In the cold season ice pushes the mounds and the seams creak; thaw settling can run through the fabric for a minute or more.
- **Avoid anchoring into warm, living walls.** The holt-things are alive and body-warm. Treat walls as population, not architecture. If you shelter in a dead mound, verify it is cold, mossy, crumbly, and stable—and do not block water flow.
- **Do not step on weirs or sagging roofs.** The weirs are the only moving infrastructure. They are low crescent dams of reed and lime holding water at exact levels. They are not paths.
- **Set an objective and a turnaround.** The Tarnholt has no centre. You can walk toward the tallest, oldest mounds and find no plaza, no citadel, no culmination. It is dense everywhere and central nowhere. Do not search for downtown; you will just keep walking.

### Navigation and communication
- **Compass, GPS if it works, altimeter, map, watch, waterproof notebook.** The light casts almost no shadows; distance reads badly. Far things become paler rather than receding. Visual memory will fail you in the uniform grey-brown felt.
- **Mark route electronically, not physically.** Flagging or carving is low-impact only if you must, and even then avoid living fabric.
- **Stay within four metres or use rope, radio, whistle, hand signals.** Your voice goes out about four metres and is simply gone into the wet. Mist and flat light make splitting up stupid.
- **Bring a satellite beacon or equivalent emergency signal.** There will be no rescue infrastructure. Tell someone outside your itinerary and check your return portal or extraction point before you go in.

### Health and body management
- **Acclimatise to the altitude.** It is not spectacular, but thin air plus cold plus wet plus effort adds up.
- **Prepare for hypothermia and immersion foot.** Keep your core dry. Have a dry hat, dry gloves, dry socks, and a dry insulating layer sealed and reserved.
- **Bring a serious first-aid kit:** blister and immersion-foot care, antifungal, wound care, personal meds, and acid-burn treatment if the water chemistry is hostile.
- **Practice wet-cold camping before you go.** Walk in water for hours with a pack. Sleep wet. Learn how your gear fails.

### Etiquette and ecology
- **Do not cut moss, sedge, willow, or lime.** The moss is how the marsh reclaims dying walls; the lime is the city’s flesh.
- **Do not lean on or damage warm walls.** The holt-things are sessile, packed into the fabric like marrow. You are never alone in a room; the walls are population.
- **Do not disturb the grey walking birds, the five-toed prints, or the weirs.** Nothing hunts you; nothing here is quick. The danger is not predation but exposure, disorientation, and collapse.
- **Keep your footprint small.** You are an anomalous metal-and-fire organism in a place that has neither. Bring what you need, use it sparingly, leave nothing.

### Psychological preparation
The Tarnholt is a quiet with texture: constant dripping at a thousand heights, ice crackle at dawn, wind that goes over the mounds in a long hush, far settling-creaks that last ten seconds. The walls are close and warm and alive. The light is flat. The city has no centre. That combination gets to people. Bring routine, a timepiece, a group, one-ear earplugs if the dripping prevents sleep, and a fixed plan. Do not go alone if you can avoid it.

**Short version:** go as a self-contained wet-cold expedition—sealed dry layers, carried water, no-cook food, freestanding shelter above saturated ground, foot care, navigation aids, emergency beacon, and a strict leave-no-trace, do-not-anchor-into-the-living-city discipline. And resolve the “water at 1.9” before you touch it.
