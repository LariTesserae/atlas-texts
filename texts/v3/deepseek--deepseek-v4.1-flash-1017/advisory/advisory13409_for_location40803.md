## Briefing: How an Ordinary Human Should Prepare for the Loud Cold Sea Under the Lid

Prepare for a place that is not actively hostile but is completely uninterested in keeping you alive. The main dangers are not monsters. They are cold, sharp rock, no horizon, no natural exit, no decay, and a soundscape so sensitive that your gear can become pollution.

The governing fact is this: you are a warm, noisy, decaying, contaminating object entering a place where almost nothing changes. You are the anomaly. Plan accordingly.

---

### 1. Do reconnaissance before you go in

Do not treat this as a dive. Treat it as a polar-industrial expedition under an ice lid.

First send unmanned probes. Measure:

- **Water depth and pressure.** The ice is hundreds of meters thick, but the water depth is unknown. Every plan branches on this. If the basin is deep, you need trimix, decompression planning, a chamber, and a much more serious operation. If it is shallow, it is merely very cold and very sharp.
- **Density and salinity.** The water is “thin” because it is poor in salt and silt. That means your buoyancy may be different from what you expect. A diver trimmed for seawater may sink in this freshwater-like sea. Measure it and ballast accordingly.
- **Temperature profile.** It is a few degrees above freezing and stable. That is hypothermia territory. Measure the warm chimneys around the machines, but do not assume warm means safe.
- **Dissolved oxygen and chemistry.** Life is abundant around the chimneys, but the empty volume may be poor or anoxic in places. Do not assume clear water is breathable or harmless.
- **Acoustic baseline.** Record the chord passively. Map the machine signatures, the interference beats, the reflection haze, and the silence pockets. Do not use active sonar for reconnaissance. You would be shouting in a cathedral that is also a nursery.
- **Bottom texture.** Confirm the rock is as sharp as described. It is. Plan never to kneel, sit, or drag gear across it.

If you are not a trained cold-water technical diver with surface support, do not go personally. Send a tethered ROV first. The sea will still be there later.

---

### 2. Solve access before you solve anything else

The ice ceiling is the sky. You cannot “just swim up.” The entry hole is your lifeline, and it must be engineered.

- Establish a surface base on the ice with polar gear, navigation, and redundant power.
- Use a thermal or mechanical ice drill rated for hundreds of meters. Avoid explosives. An explosive under that lid would ring across the entire basin and alter the acoustic world.
- Build a heated collar around the hole so it does not refreeze. Monitor it constantly.
- Mark the hole from below with a physical guide line, not only with an acoustic beacon. A beacon adds noise; a line is passive and retrievable. Use a line with a protective sleeve near sharp edges.
- Keep a second hole or a tested emergency reopening plan if possible.
- Run a hardwired communications/power line through the ice if you can. Radio will not work underwater. Acoustic modems are possible but must be low-power and coded; they are also pollution.
- Mark the hole from above with flags, lights, and surface navigation. From below, the ice is a pale green-white mirror, not a map.

The first rule of the dive: **never lose the hole.** No current means you will not drift away, but no horizon means you can still become lost. A guide line and inertial navigation are not optional.

---

### 3. Dress for cold and edges, not for a tropical reef

You need a drysuit with active heating, not merely thick insulation. The cold is not dramatic; it is permanent and boring, which is worse.

Carry:

- Redundant active heating systems, heated gloves, and a heated breathing-gas path if possible.
- Thermal underlayers that still insulate when slightly damp.
- A hard-shell helmet, cut-resistant gloves, reinforced knees, elbows, and shoulders.
- Thick-soled boots if you will contact the floor at all. Better: do not contact the floor.
- Backup chemical heat packs and an emergency thermal shelter at the surface.
- A thermal dosimeter or at least a strict exposure clock.

Avoid touching the ice ceiling. It is a heat sink. Avoid touching the rock. It is a knife. The floor is young, unrounded, and glassy; a fall onto it is a suit breach waiting to happen.

---

### 4. Use silent, redundant life support

This is a closed-circuit rebreather environment if there ever was one.

- Open-circuit scuba bubbles are loud. In a basin where sound runs for hundreds of kilometers, your exhaust is an acoustic event. It will mask navigation, disturb the acoustic ecology, and advertise you.
- Use a closed-circuit rebreather with redundant scrubber, oxygen monitoring, and bailout.
- Warm the breathing gas if you can. Cold gas plus cold water plus long exposure is a fast route to incapacity.
- Carry redundant depth, time, gas, and CO2 monitoring. Cold batteries fail; keep them warm and carry analog backups.
- Plan decompression conservatively. Cold impairs decompression. If the depth is significant, bring or arrange a chamber. Do not wing it.

The water is “thin” in the sense of being empty and easy to move through, not in the sense of being safe. Your body still needs gas, heat, and a way out.

---

### 5. Fix your buoyancy before you leave the hole

Because the water is low in salt and suspended matter, your buoyancy may be different from seawater. Do a proper weight check in the actual water, near the entry, with your full kit.

You want neutral trim and no tendency to sink onto sharp rock. The absence of current makes hovering easy, but the warm chimneys near machines create local upwellings. Those plumes can lift you, tilt your trim, and push you into structures or dense animal knots. Treat the chimneys as vertical currents with life in them, not as elevators.

Carry a small amount of adjustable ballast. Do not dump ballast into the water. Anything you release stays. The sea has no current to carry your mistake away.

---

### 6. Protect your hearing and protect the sea’s hearing

The sound is the local geography. The animals read it. You will mostly hear a chord. Your tools will sound like bells.

Prepare:

- A helmet with acoustic damping and ear protection that still lets you hear alarms and rope signals.
- A sound-level dosimeter. The chord may be loud enough to fatigue you, even if it is not immediately painful.
- A passive hydrophone and recorder. Learn the beat before you enter. The interference pattern is a clock and a map.
- Soft mountings for every tool. Pad metal. Tether everything. A dropped wrench is not a dropped wrench here; it is a transient that propagates across the basin.
- No active sonar unless it is an absolute emergency. If you must use acoustic positioning, use the lowest power, directional, coded, and logged. Treat every ping as a decision.
- No unnecessary thrusters, no banging, no bubbles if you can avoid them.

The silence pockets — the few-meter holes where reflections cancel — are not automatically your friend. Your acoustic navigation and comms may die there. The animals avoid them. You should too, unless you have mapped them and are tethered. They are holes in the world, and you are a creature that depends on the world.

---

### 7. Navigate by line, instrument, and learned chord

There is no horizon, no tide, no current, and no visual landmark except machines and edges. The far wall is a rumor.

- Use a guide line to the entry for all early dives.
- Carry an inertial navigation unit, depth gauge, compass if it works locally, and a clock. Time distortion is real when nothing changes.
- Build a passive acoustic map of the machine signatures. The chord can tell you where you are if you have trained on it.
- Keep a “home vector” and a return budget. No current means your return is mostly your own navigation error.
- Do not swim out into the volume just because it is empty. The empty volume is the largest part of the map and the least forgiving.

If you become lost: stop. Listen. Follow the line if you have one. Deploy an emergency line reel if you do not. Use a low-power locator only as a last resort. Do not press up against the ice and hope.

---

### 8. Treat the machines as running industrial hazards

They are immense, warm, and running. That does not make them safe.

- Keep distance from intakes, vanes, derricks, manifolds, and anything with moving parts or suction.
- Do not climb them. Do not turn valves. Do not shut anything down. You do not know what the equilibrium is holding in place.
- Do not anchor to them. Do not use them as a base.
- Their warm chimneys are the densest life zones and the only local convection. Approach slowly, from outside, with a plan to leave.
- Their lack of rust does not mean your gear will not fail. Test seals, batteries, lubricants, and materials in the actual water before trusting them.
- If you need warmth, use your own heat. Do not crawl into a machine’s plume and hope it is a shelter.

---

### 9. Do not contaminate the place

This may be the most important rule. There is no current to carry waste away and no decay to make it disappear. Anything you leave stays where it falls, indefinitely.

- Use a closed waste system. No discharges.
- Use a rebreather to avoid bubbles and gas venting where possible.
- Sterilize and quarantine gear before entry. Human microbes in an isolated acoustic ecology are an unknown risk.
- Carry out every piece of trash, every dropped tool, every broken line.
- Do not collect animals, shells, rock, or machine parts. Do not touch the soft-bodied life. It is fragile and built for a mineral-poor world.
- Do not feed anything. Do not shine bright lights into dense animal knots if you can avoid it; the animals are blind, but your light is still an intrusion.
- Respect the silence pockets. The locals avoid them. You do not know why, and that is enough.

Leave no trace. In a place where nothing changes, a trace is permanent.

---

### 10. Prepare your mind for monotony and ringing

The sensory profile is strange: cold, diffuse green light, no day or night, no horizon, no weather, a constant unresolved chord, and a floor that never changes.

You will need:

- A strict schedule with clocks and artificial light cycles.
- A surface habitat with soundproofing and genuine quiet.
- Psychological screening and a buddy system. Do not go alone.
- Pre-agreed abort criteria: heating failure, navigation uncertainty, acoustic dose, suit damage, lost line, equipment noise, or simple unease.
- Tasks and logs to prevent complacency. Nothing changes here, which means your own attention is the thing most likely to drift.

The “nothing changes” rule is not a mood. It is a hazard. It breeds overconfidence.

---

### 11. Have an emergency plan that assumes no rescue

- Surface watch with a hardwired or rope-signal link.
- A chamber or evacuation plan for decompression illness.
- A suit-breach plan: return to the hole, do not attempt an ice ascent.
- A line-cutter and entanglement plan.
- A lost-line plan.
- A heating-failure plan.
- A medical plan for hypothermia, trauma, CO2, oxygen toxicity, and cold-incapacitation.
- A contamination plan for any cut or suit breach.
- A retrieval plan for anything dropped.

If you cannot execute the rescue yourself, do not go far from the hole. The first visits should be short, tethered, and close to the entry, with the ROV doing the exploration.

---

### 12. The five things to remember

1. **Measure first.** Depth, pressure, density, chemistry, sound. Do not guess.
2. **Do not make noise.** Your equipment is a guest in an acoustic world. Pad it, tether it, silence it.
3. **Do not leave anything.** No current, no decay, no forgiveness.
4. **Never lose the hole.** The ice ceiling is not a surface. It is a lid.
5. **Dress for cold and edges.** The place will not attack you. It will simply let you become cold, lost, cut, or permanently part of the inventory.

If you do it right, the visit will feel like entering a vast, cold, ringing cathedral where the machines are the only warm things, the animals are the only moving things, and you are the only thing that does not belong. Stay brief. Stay tethered. Stay quiet. Leave it exactly as you found it.
