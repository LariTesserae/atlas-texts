Treat this as a high-altitude, thin-air, high-UV, cold, wet, fragile archaeological/sacred mineral site — not a viewpoint. The correct preparation is mountaineering preparation plus conservation/etiquette preparation plus a large dose of humility. You are an ordinary human guest in a place that is already busy maintaining itself.

## 1. Before you transit: verify the physics and the permissions

Do not assume “breathable.” Find out, before you go:

- **Pressure and oxygen fraction** at shelf height. If the oxygen partial pressure is low, you need supplemental oxygen, not just grit. If the atmosphere is unknown or unbreathable, this becomes a full pressure-suit trip and most of the rest changes.
- **Gravity, day length, temperature range, wind, radiation, weather pattern.** Nine hours of enormous light is not the same as nine hours of safe light.
- **Whether rescue exists.** Assume it does not. Leave an itinerary with someone outside the trip. Carry redundant communication and test it in the local physics.
- **Local taboos, guardianship, and law.** The crescent of stones, slab, cobbles, stakes, and cairns are not props. Get permission or a guide. If no one will guide you and the local people treat the place as sensitive, do not go.
- **Whether local humans are adapted to this altitude.** They may be. You are not. Their ease is not your ease.

If you can choose your approach body or acclimatization state, choose one already adapted to altitude and cold. Otherwise plan as if the shelf is at 5,000+ metres until proven otherwise.

## 2. Fitness, acclimatization, and medical preparation

This is not a place to “push through.” The shelf is small, exposed, wet, rounded, and crowded with things you must not crush. You need balance, slow-step endurance, and good judgment under hypoxia.

- Build aerobic fitness and leg strength; train with a weighted pack on uneven ground. Practice precise, deliberate steps — heel placement, weight transfer, no swinging pack.
- Acclimatize in stages. Sleep lower than the shelf. Take rest days. Do not go up with a headache and denial.
- Carry and know how to use: pulse oximeter, supplemental oxygen, a wilderness first-aid kit, and altitude medication only as prescribed for you. Know the signs of AMS, HAPE, and HACE and have a hard turn-around rule.
- Hydrate and eat before thirst and hunger vanish. Thin air and cold dehydrate you; altitude suppresses appetite and judgment.
- No alcohol, sedatives, or “I’ll sleep it off” at altitude.
- Protect hands and face from cold and UV even when the vent air feels kind. The warm air around your hands is a trap; your gloves will wet and then freeze.
- Carry a helmet, harness and rope only if you or a guide can use them without damaging the site. Use removable protection or natural rock, never bolts or pitons in the shelf.

## 3. Gear: light, clean, and nothing dangling

You want a small, tight pack with no loose straps to catch on pipes. You want sticky-rubber boots, not crampons, on the shelf itself; crampons scar rounded rock and ice crust. If the approach couloir needs crampons, carry them and switch before the shelf.

Core kit:

- Insulated, windproof and waterproof layers; down or synthetic warm layer; spare gloves; balaclava; goggles with side shields and high-UV category lenses; SPF 50+ and zinc; lip balm.
- Helmet; waterproof gloves; insulated boots with good grip; microspikes only if truly needed and only off the fragile crust.
- Headlamp even in daytime; the blue shadows and cloud arm can hide holes and edges.
- Satellite messenger/radio with spare cold-proof batteries; laminated route notes; compass/GPS; whistle.
- Emergency bivy, first-aid, oxygen, and a small repair kit.
- Gas monitor if there is any chance the vents exhale CO₂ or other gases. Do not put your face into a hollow pipe. Do not sleep beside vents.
- Pack-out bags for all waste, including food crumbs and urine if the local protocol requires it. No washing gear in meltwater. No soap, sunscreen residue, or sanitizer poured onto the site.

Clean and sterilize boots, poles, camera gear, and clothing before arrival. The place has no lichen, no green, no insects. That means it is pristine in a way most travellers have never handled. Your boots are a biosecurity event.

## 4. Route and timing

The shelf is lee-side and sheltered, but the ridge wind is a huge presence just over the lip. The cloud sea below hides the couloir by mid-morning. Plan for that.

- Approach while the couloir is frozen and visible. Arrive after first light if you need to see, but expect the first hour after dawn to be wet, ticking, and slick with meltwater.
- Set a hard turn-around time. Do not be on the shelf when the cloud arm feels its way over the lip unless you are prepared for sudden wet cold and loss of route-finding.
- Do not camp on the shelf. Camp lower, in a sheltered place, and leave the shelf to the pipes and the old camp.
- Move one at a time on the exposed sections. Keep the group small — two or three people, not a crowd. A crowd is a boot among pipes.
- Budget far more time than the shelf’s size suggests. “Forty paces at its widest” is not forty casual steps. It is twenty minutes of choosing where to put your weight.

## 5. Behaviour on the shelf

The field is the site. The archaeology is the edge. Your job is to stay at the edge.

- Stay on durable bedrock and the established line at the field’s edge. Do not thread between pipes for a better photograph. Do not step on crusts to see if they hold.
- Do not touch, lean on, breathe directly onto, chip, sample, or “rescue” a pipe. A fallen pipe is part of the process. A struck pipe is a mark that will outlast your language.
- Do not move the stacked stones, the smooth slab, the river cobbles, the rotted stakes, or the cairns. Do not sit on the slab. Do not add a stone to a cairn. Do not build a new one. Do not leave metal, cord, offerings, or markers unless local custom explicitly requires it and you have permission.
- Photograph from the edge with telephoto. No flash, no drone, no music, no loud radio. The vents hiss low; keep your voice lower.
- Keep hands free and pack tight. A swinging pack or trekking-pole tip can snap a thin pipe.
- If you must enter the field with a guide, go one person at a time, on rock, with slow weight tests. Do not jump. Do not run. Do not cross in a minute.
- Eat and drink at the edge, over a stuff-sack, with every crumb contained. Do not urinate or defecate on the shelf; pack it out.
- If you damage something, report it. Do not try to “fix” it. The place maintains itself; your repair is another injury.

## 6. Health and hazard management on site

- Check each other for frostbite, sunburn, and snow blindness. The enormous light and reflective ice can burn eyes even when the air is cold.
- The warm vent air may feel kinder than the air at your face, but it is wet. Wet gloves and sleeves lose insulation. Keep covered; change gloves before they freeze.
- The rock is rounded, wet, and pocked. Blue shadows are not dark, but they hide holes. Test every step. Use rest steps. Hypoxia makes you stupid about edges; use a buddy and a checklist.
- Watch the cloud arm. When it comes, everything gets a skin of water, visibility collapses, and the cold bites harder. Retreat early.
- Do not look at the sun. Do not look into the lit walls of the thin pipes for long; use goggles.
- If anyone shows altitude illness, descend. Do not wait for morning on the shelf.

## 7. Mental preparation

Prepare to be a temporary contamination. The shelf is not empty; it is a slow fountain field, a breathing mineral archive, and a place where people have been careful for a long time. You are not the discoverer. You are not the first pilgrim. You are a large, warm, clumsy animal with a pack.

Move as if the ground is older than your language and more fragile than your boots. Accept that you may see less than you want, from farther away than you want, and that this is the correct visit. The place will go on growing a skin at a time after your cairns have gone under. Your preparation is mainly about not interrupting that.
