Treat the Provenance as three places at once: a high-altitude construction site, an unguarded chemical distillation plant, and a cleanroom floored with broken glass. The sun and sky are ordinary; that is exactly why they are not reassuring. The hazards are in the ground, the air, and the fact that you are a soft, wet, shedding human in a place with no water, no dust, and no weather to dilute or wash anything away.

My first preparation would be a decision not to enter unless I had a good reason and full kit. The hollow is room-sized and visible from its lip. For an ordinary traveler, the safest and most respectful visit is from the rim, with binoculars, a respirator if the vapor is blowing toward you, and no intention of stepping down onto the slab. If you do enter, make it brief, deliberate, and supported.

**Support and permissions.** Go with a local guide who knows the plateau and the approach, not necessarily the hollow itself. Go with a buddy. Leave an itinerary with someone outside. Carry a satellite beacon or radio, because the quiet is attentive and there are no animals, no water, and no landmarks worth trusting. Establish a lower base camp and acclimatize properly; this is high country with thin air. Tell a medic where you are going. Have an evacuation plan and know the nearest hospital that can handle burns, trauma, and chemical exposure. Do not plan to camp in or beside the hollow.

**Altitude and breathing.** The air is thin, dry, and hot, and the hollow may also be giving off mineral vapor, ozone, and something like sulfur or struck-match chemistry. Do not rely on a negative-pressure respirator alone at altitude; the breathing resistance can turn a bad idea into a worse one. Use a powered air-purifying respirator with combination P100/acid-gas/organic-vapor cartridges, or supplied air if you can arrange it. Carry supplemental oxygen and a pulse oximeter. Get medical advice about altitude medication before you go. If you have heart, lung, or severe allergy problems, view from the rim or not at all. A gas monitor for oxygen, ozone, sulfur dioxide, hydrogen sulfide, and volatile organics would be prudent; if it alarms, leave. Do not crouch or kneel near cracks, where vapors pool and rise. Stay upwind when the thermal wind moves. If you smell ozone or struck matches through your respirator, or if your eyes sting, retreat.

**Clothing and PPE.** Cover every inch of skin. Wear a climbing helmet and a full-face respirator or sealed goggles plus a face shield. Use cut-, heat-, and chemical-resistant gloves; do not touch anything with bare hands, not even for balance. Boots should have thick, cut-resistant, heat-insulating soles and sticky rubber tread; the slab is glassy and razor-keen, and ordinary boot leather or thin soles will not forgive you. Gaiters help. Outer layers should be tight enough not to catch on crystal but loose enough to layer for cold nights outside the hollow. Use a dedicated clean outer layer, because you are the dust in a place that has none. Avoid contact lenses. Use physical sun protection: long sleeves, wide brim, UV goggles, sunscreen applied before you dress and covered where possible. Bring spare warm layers and an emergency bivy; the hollow is warm, the plateau is not.

**Thermal and chemical hazards.** The slab is warm and may be hotter than it looks. Carry a non-contact infrared thermometer and test the floor before trusting it. Do not sit, kneel, or place bare skin on the rock. The condensate is not water; it is a hot mineral liquid closer to molten salt. Assume it is corrosive and a burn hazard. Do not drink it, touch it, or let it drip onto gear. Do not eat, drink, smoke, or open containers in the hollow if you can avoid it; if you must hydrate, use a sealed bottle and do not spill. A spill is contamination. No open flames. No sampling by hand. If you must sample, use remote tools and sealed containers, and only with permission.

**Movement and route.** The stone is new, glassy, and edged. Cracks in the slab are not just decorative; they are where vapor exits and where a foot can catch. Do not jump, run, stamp, or slide. Step slowly on broad flat surfaces, avoid plants and crystal formations, and do not use them as handholds. Ropes and anchors are a problem: the rock may be brittle, the edges cut rope, and there are no reliable placements. For an ordinary visit, do not rope up inside the hollow. Use trekking poles with rubber tips if they help you balance, but do not lean on them hard or scratch the surface. Mark your route with GPS waypoints, not paint, cairns, or scratches. Keep to the rim if the floor looks at all uncertain.

**Contamination discipline.** The hollow has no dust because nothing has ground itself down. You will bring skin flakes, fibers, sweat, breath, and boot scuffs. Keep your outer layer clean, bag contaminated gear after you leave, and pack out every bit of waste, including tissues, dressings, food wrappers, and urine or feces if emergency forces it. Do not spit, bleed, or vomit in the hollow if you can possibly leave first. If you are cut, contain the blood and pack out the dressings. Treat the place like a cleanroom that also happens to be a burn ward.

**Time and psychology.** Nothing in the hollow happens quickly, but your hazards do. Set a timer and a turnaround. Keep a buddy within sight and use verbal check-ins; hypoxia can make you calm, euphoric, or stupid, and the quiet can make you want to stay. The low hum, ticks, creaks, and late-afternoon hiss are not a warning system, but a sudden increase in creaking or ticking is a good reason to leave. Do not split up. Do not sleep there. Do not wait for something dramatic to happen; the hollow’s changes run on stone time, and you do not.

**Emergency kit.** Carry trauma supplies for deep cuts, burn dressings, sterile saline eye wash, sterile water for decontamination, a heat-resistant pad, oxygen, a pulse oximeter, a satellite beacon, and a communication plan. For a chemical splash, brush off dry material first if safe, then rinse with sterile water and remove contaminated clothing. For a cut, control bleeding and leave; do not try to clean the wound with local liquid. For altitude symptoms, descend and use oxygen. For a burn, cool with sterile water and evacuate. Inspect and decontaminate your gear after the visit; bag the outer layer and do not spread unknown mineral salts or biological material.

The best preparation is humility. The hollow is not hostile; it is indifferent. Indifference plus razor edges, hot condensate, thin air, and reactive vapor is enough. View it from the lip. If you enter, go in covered, monitored, clean, brief, and ready to leave at once.
