Treat The Notch as three things at once: a serious cliff-access route, a charged dry cleanroom, and a running archaeological machine inside a tuned acoustic instrument. The correct visit is short, sealed, low-moisture, low-vibration, non-shedding, non-sparking, and hands-off. Your job is not to explore it; it is to be a brief perturbation that the place can close over and forget.

## 1. Before you commit

- **Do not go solo.** You need at least one person anchored outside the lip as spotter/rope-tender, and ideally a third at staging. The entrance is nearly unfindable from thirty meters out; a spotter at the lip is more useful than any map.
- **Have real rope-access or climbing skill.** This is a vertical lee face with two kilometers of nothing around it, a steady drawn updraft, fluted smooth rock, dust, and no ordinary rescue. If you cannot do fixed-rope ascent/descent, self-rescue, and anchor work, do not go. Hire someone who can.
- **Reconnoiter from outside first.** Find the pressure cue at the lip—the “hand flat against your chest”—and mark the entrance only from the outside with a passive anchor/tag/spotter position. Do not put paint, chalk, cairns, or permanent markers inside. Do not rely on visual memory; use an altimeter, a pressure sense, and a human spotter.
- **Do not teleport or portal directly into the pocket.** Arrival displacement, light, noise, heat, and air movement would be a real disturbance. Arrive outside and below, use the same throat in and out, and place any return anchor outside the notch.
- **Check the atmosphere before trusting it.** Carry a sealed gas monitor for O₂, CO₂, CO, and combustibles, with vibration/visual alarms. The drawn air and the machine are not a guarantee of breathable air. If the monitor complains, leave.
- **Set a hard turnaround time before you enter.** The place has no dawn, no evening, and a permanent gray noon. Use a vibration timer and prearranged check-ins. “Just a few more minutes” is how people become part of the sums.

## 2. Approach and route discipline

- The wind is drawn steadily up the face; it does not gust, but it will take loose straps, hair, rope ends, maps, and anything else not secured. Tape or tuck everything. Helmet, sealed goggles, gloves, and a smooth outer shell.
- Do not treat the updraft as lift. It is a pressure field, not a ride. Use normal protection and three-point contact. At the lip, stop, equalize if needed, and let the pressure guide you in rather than fighting it.
- Use a fixed rope and a redundant anchor outside. If preservation is the priority, do not run a thick line through the throat; the throat is part of the instrument. If safety requires a tether, use the thinnest low-stretch line you can justify, keep it low-tension, and remove it. Accept that even that is a disturbance.
- One visitor inside at a time. No drones, no animals, no loud electronics, no rotor downwash near the face. No flashing lights, flares, stoves, smoking, or vaping.

## 3. What to wear

- **Smooth, anti-static, non-shedding coverall** with hood, sealed gloves, and overboots. No fleece, wool, down, loose knit, dangling cords, or exposed hair. Do not wear a fuzzy jacket into a charged dry pelt; you will shed, catch dust, and generate static.
- **Full-face P100/HEPA respirator**, fit-tested. The pale meal shed by the growth hangs in the air and does not settle. Eye protection must be sealed; ordinary glasses are not enough.
- **Double hearing protection**—foam plugs plus helmet-compatible muffs—and a vibration alarm watch. The sound is enormous, fixed, and felt in the chest. Do not remove protection to “hear it properly.” You can feel it through your boots.
- **No exposed skin, no jewelry, no loose pockets, no open water, no food, no gum, no perfume, no insect repellent, no aerosols.** Everything is tethered or sealed. A dropped coin or bolt is both a spark risk and a permanent-looking disturbance.
- **Sealed, intrinsically safe electronics** if you bring any. No flash photography, no exposed metal tools, no non-sparking improvisation near the machine. Use red-filtered dim light, not a bright white lamp.

## 4. Moisture, food, and your body

The place is drier than any desert and has no memory of rain. You are a wet, warm animal; your breath and sweat are the largest disturbance you carry.

- Drink and eat before. Urinate before. Do not plan to eat, drink, or manage waste inside. A short visit needs no hydration inside; a longer one needs a specialist closed system with no spill potential, not a casual water bottle.
- Enter cool and calm. Remove insulation at the lip so you do not sweat inside. Move slowly. Do not climb, wrestle, or exert inside. If you are panting, you are adding moisture and heat.
- If you must stay longer, use a closed-circuit breathing set with a desiccant/moisture trap, not a simple exhalation valve. For an ordinary short visit, keep it short enough that your breath is a negligible pulse.
- No open wounds inside. Cover any cut. If you bleed, leave.
- After exit, do not remove your respirator until you are clear of the dust and upwind/below the lip. Bag the outer layer, clean goggles and skin, rinse eyes with sterile saline outside, and wash before eating.

## 5. Inside protocol

- Enter slowly. Stop in the throat if that is enough. The pocket is full; there is no clean path. You cannot avoid contact if you go deep, so do not go deep without a reason.
- Let the fleece lean away and close behind you. Do not pull, tear, part it with tools, or thrash. Move one limb at a time; if it clings, stop and let it release. Do not use bare hands.
- Do not test the equilibrium. Do not shove an arm through to see what happens. Do not shout, sing, stamp, kick dust, sweep, or shake your gear. The chord may waver and return; that is not permission to keep poking.
- Take no samples. No fleece, no dust, no machine scrapings, no “small” souvenirs. No chalk, paint, cairns, or markers inside.
- Stay away from the machine. It is running, old, charged, and load-bearing in a way you do not understand. Do not touch, lean on, climb, anchor to, open, clear the intakes, cap the pipes, stand on the footings, or “help.” The fleece packed into the intakes is deliberate. Do not clear it.
- If the chord changes and does not return, if the fleece clings or tears, if you feel a shock, if the machine speeds or slows, if the dust thickens, if the pressure changes, or if your gas monitor alarms: leave. Do not investigate.
- Keep your hands away from your face. Do not taste or smell anything. The “lush” growth is not food, not water, and not welcome.

## 6. Sound, light, and time

- The sound is the main sensory hazard. Double protection, short exposure, and a vibration timer. If you feel chest discomfort, nausea, headache, or ringing that persists, abort.
- Communication by voice is nearly useless. Use hand signals, rope tugs, and timed check-ins. Test any radio outside; do not rely on it in the roar.
- Bring a dim red headlamp and a spare, sealed. The pearl light is enough to move by; bright light and flash are unnecessary and disrespectful to the place.
- The permanent gray noon will distort time. Use an independent watch. Do not nap, camp, or “wait for the light to change.” It will not.

## 7. Static, dust, and the machine

- Wear dissipative/anti-static clothing and avoid synthetic fabrics that generate charge. Do not touch the metal machine with a charged body or uninsulated tool. Ground yourself outside before handling metal, and keep metal buckles covered where possible.
- No sparks, no flame, no flash, no open batteries. Use sealed, low-power devices. The dust is fine, dry, crystalline, and charged; treat it as an irritant and a contamination problem, not as something to brush off inside.
- The pelt’s faint blue-white sheen is charge, not invitation. The hair’s-breadth repulsion between curtains is part of the instrument. Do not collapse it, bridge it, or discharge into it.

## 8. Exit and aftermath

- Leave the way you came. The pressure will let you go. At the lip, clip into the fixed rope before you relax.
- From thirty meters out you will lose the notch again. That is why the spotter and outside anchor matter. Do not descend improvising a new route.
- Sit outside, in ordinary wind, and let your ears and body readjust. The chord will follow you down for a while. Do not drive or fly immediately if you feel disoriented or your hearing is dulled.
- Pack out everything. Decontaminate outside. Report any dropped gear, damage, or change in the chord. Do not return “to check” without the same preparation.

The correct visit is not a hike into a cave. It is a brief, sealed, hands-off entry into a held equilibrium: no water, no food, no sparks, no samples, no shouting, no repairs, no testing. If you cannot do all of that, stop at the lip, feel the pressure, listen to the chord through your boots, and leave it alone. That is still a visit.
