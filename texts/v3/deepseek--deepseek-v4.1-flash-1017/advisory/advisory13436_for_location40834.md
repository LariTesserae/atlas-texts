## First: what visiting will actually be like

You will feel the sound before you understand it. Even with ear protection, the low-frequency component will come up through your boots, harness, and teeth. The air will feel like a warm wet blanket pressed against you. Every surface will be wet, slick, warm, and possibly alive. There will be no dry place to sit, no clean horizon, no real “outside,” and no easy privacy. Lighting will be diffuse and shadowless; depth perception will be poor. The city will not be a backdrop; it will be a body with flows, chemistry, growth, and immune-like responses. You will be a foreign object in that body.

Your central rule: **your suit is your only true boundary; your local guide is your only reliable social interface; your sealed pod is your only reliable dry/quiet place; your extraction beacon is your umbilical.**

## Preparation doctrine

### 1. Do not arrive casually or at a random point

Arrange arrival at a prepared receiving node: a stable platform, a local sponsor or guide, a sealed shelter pod, and a known route to a safe room. Do not step out of transit into a canal, onto a living mat, or into a crowded traffic flow. Arrive sealed, tethered, buoyant, and in radio/haptic contact. First actions: verify atmosphere, pressure, noise level, temperature, humidity, comms, location, guide identity, and nearest storm shelter. Do not remove your helmet “just to check.”

### 2. Full-body sealed environmental suit, all the time

Assume the atmosphere is not breathable and not safe for skin. Use a positive-pressure dry suit with a hard or semi-rigid helmet, anti-fog visor, chemical/biological filters, CO₂ scrubbing, and independent oxygen or filtered-air supply. The suit must be hydrophobic, corrosion-resistant, abrasion-resistant, and repairable in the field. It needs active cooling and humidity control: warm saturated air plus heavy exertion inside a sealed suit will overheat you fast. Include condensate drains, spare visors, glove liners, and a puncture patch kit. No exposed skin. No eating or drinking outside the suit. Use internal hydration and sealed waste collection. Filter your exhaust so you do not dump concentrated human chemistry into a world whose natives communicate chemically.

### 3. Acoustic protection is non-negotiable

The sound is a physical load. Use custom earplugs with active pass-through and a limiter, plus over-ear muffs or a helmet with acoustic damping. Carry a noise dosimeter. Protect against infrasound and bone-conducted vibration with vibration-isolating boots, gloves, and harness padding. Do not rely on speech. Use radio with noise cancellation, throat mic, bone conduction backup, text display, haptic signals, and light signals. Learn the guide’s emergency hand/tactile signals. Take scheduled quiet breaks in the sealed pod. Sleep in a quiet, climate-controlled pod with hearing protection. If you get nausea, headache, tooth resonance, or disorientation, treat it as exposure and withdraw.

### 4. Mobility: assume you will fall, slip, or get cut off

The ground is water table; canals are streets; surfaces are living and slippery. Wear high-traction soft boots — not spikes, which damage the city and get stuck. Carry a harness, tether, throw line, buoyancy aid, and a walking pole to test surfaces. Use non-penetrating anchors and padded clamps; do not spike into conduits or mats. Do not swim. Do not pilot small boats into deep canals. Use local ferries or jellyfish-like transports with a guide. Keep away from leviathan-filter canals. If you fall into slurry, do not panic: buoyancy, tether, self-right, call for local rescue. Your suit must be rated for brief immersion and self-sealing.

### 5. Chemical and biological isolation

The rain is mineral-rich and corrosive; the water is a slurry of organic debris and bioluminescent algae; the city’s veins carry engineered nano-fluid; the air carries swarms. Treat everything as a bio/chem hazard. Use HEPA plus chemical filtration, positive pressure, sealed joints, and low-outgassing suit materials. Avoid perfumes, scented hygiene products, and concentrated sweat/odor release. Carry decontamination wipes, waste bags, and a suit-breach kit. If the suit is breached, assume contamination and abort to a safe pod for decon. Bring all food and water; do not drink local rain or water; do not forage. Pack out all waste. On return, quarantine and decontaminate gear; do not bring unlicensed biological samples.

### 6. Sensors and navigation

You will have no reliable GPS, poor visual depth cues, and a labyrinth of fused porous structures. Carry inertial navigation, local beacons with permission, maps from your sponsor, bright but dimmable lights with red/low modes, a pressure/temperature/humidity logger, gas sensors for O₂/CO₂/combustibles/H₂S/volatiles/particulates, and a noise dosimeter. Use a guide and a buddy system. Mark routes with removable visual markers, not chemicals or cuts. Keep a tether line on catwalks and tiers. Know the direction of flow, the nearest storm shelter, and the nearest extraction point at all times. Do not enter dead-end canals or deep abyss-facing channels.

### 7. Shelter and storm plan

Set up a sealed base pod: quiet, dehumidified, cooled, powered, with an airlock, waste handling, comms, and emergency supplies. Anchor it to a stable structure with permission and without damming flows. Monitor pressure and local storm warnings; the city flexes with storms, but you are not adapted to that ride. Never be in a canal or on an exposed tier during a storm surge. Have a primary and secondary shelter. Have an extraction trigger: suit breach, filter clog, heat stress, disorientation, loss of guide, storm warning beyond shelter reach, or any hostile response from the city’s inhabitants.

### 8. Social and ecological etiquette

You are a foreign chemical process in a cultivated living city. Do not prune, harvest, cut, burn, or “clean” anything. Do not dam canals. Do not feed fauna. Do not touch conduits, mats, or ferry constructs without permission. Ask before anchoring. Keep moving in traffic; do not linger and block flow. Use low light; avoid flares, open flame, lasers, and loud speakers. Your suit blocks native chemical communication, so rely on a local translator/guide and keep your chemical exhaust scrubbed. If locals signal you to move, move. If they signal you to stop, stop. If they treat you as debris or a leak, withdraw to your pod and renegotiate through your sponsor.

### 9. Medical and psychological prep

Get a baseline hearing test, lung/ear/sinus check, and fitness clearance. Train in a hot, humid, noisy, claustrophobic simulator wearing the suit. Prepare for sensory overload, no privacy, no dry skin, no shadows, and the constant roar. Carry prescribed meds, motion-sickness meds if cleared, and a buddy. Use your own light/dark cycle in the pod; the amber twilight will not give you a circadian rhythm. Set abort criteria in advance: hearing pain, chest pressure, heat exhaustion, panic, suit fogging beyond control, repeated filter clogging, or inability to reach shelter.

## Minimum kit summary

- Positive-pressure sealed dry suit, hard helmet, anti-fog visor, chemical/bio filters, O₂/CO₂ management, cooling, hydration, waste collection, patch kit.
- Hearing protection with comms, noise dosimeter, haptic/visual signaling.
- Harness, tether, buoyancy, throw line, non-penetrating anchors, grip boots, walking pole.
- Gas/particulate/pressure/temperature/humidity sensors; inertial nav; maps; beacons; dimmable lights.
- Sealed quiet base pod with airlock, power, dehumidification, waste handling, comms, supplies.
- All food/water; no local consumption; decon and quarantine kit.
- Local sponsor/guide/translator; storm plan; extraction beacon; buddy; abort criteria.
- No open flame, no scented products, no Earth biologics, no unapproved sampling, no damage to living infrastructure.

If you cannot arrange the suit, the guide, the pod, and the extraction plan, do not go. This place will not punish carelessness with a dramatic monster; it will absorb it quietly through heat, noise, moisture, chemistry, and disorientation. My intuition is that the correct posture is humble, sealed, slow, guided, and ready to leave.
