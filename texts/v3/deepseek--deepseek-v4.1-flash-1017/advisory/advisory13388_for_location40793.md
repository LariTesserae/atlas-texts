## Preparation brief: visiting the Sump as an ordinary human traveler

Assume you are baseline human: no darkvision, no immunity to local parasites, no reliable magic, and no business treating this place as a dungeon. The Sump is a young, inhabited, unstable living bowl. Your preparation should be mostly about **light discipline, fire prohibition, water discipline, respiratory protection, and obedience to local timing**. Your biggest dangers are not monsters; they are a spark, a bright lamp, a confident step onto a walkway that deliberately ends, untreated water, a falling shelf of ceiling mat, and being outside during the wrong two weeks.

### Before you go

Arrange a guide and a host before you approach the rim. Do not plan to “find your way down.” The entrance is through the ceiling mat, the walls are raw slippery clay, and the town is scattered across open water on stilts. You need someone who knows the maintained opening, the current walkways, and which channels are not to be dredged. Ask specifically about:

- the water pulse: when it rises after rain, when buried things come up, when not to be on the water;
- the pale-sac opening weeks, when everyone stays indoors with mouths covered;
- recent wall slumps and which sides of the bowl are newest and least stable;
- where the walkways deliberately stop and how people cross;
- whether there is any current sickness in town, and whether outsiders are welcome at all.

Try to arrive outside the spore-release weeks. If you cannot, plan to spend those weeks indoors, sealed up, masked, with low light, and do not argue about it.

Get your health in order: tetanus booster, routine vaccines, wound-care supplies, antifungal cream, antihistamines, oral rehydration salts, antidiarrheal medication, antibiotics only if you know how to use them, and any personal prescriptions in waterproof packaging. Bring a well-fitting P100 or equivalent respirator and eye protection. Bring more than you think you need; humidity destroys paper, electronics, and morale.

### What to bring

**Light.** Bring a sealed, waterproof headlamp with a red or amber low mode, spare batteries, and a desiccant pack. Do not plan to use white light at night. Let your eyes adapt. The town lights itself with living luminous things in jars and gourds; those are fragile, alive, and someone’s property. Carry your own dim backup, but do not wave it around. A bright light in the Sump is rude, conspicuous, and may draw attention from things in the water and ceiling.

**Fire discipline.** No open flame. No smoking. No lighters as gifts. No sparking tools, no hot electronics exposed, no flares, no cooking unless a local host explicitly tells you where and how. A spark in the ceiling mat could take the whole bowl. This is not a suggestion; it is the central rule of the place. If your gear can short, spark, or get hot, seal it or leave it above.

**Water.** Do not drink the standing water untreated. Bring a good filter and chemical purification backup, and use purified water for drinking, rinsing wounds, and cleaning gear. The locals may have tolerances and habits you do not. Keep open cuts covered; do not wade with broken skin if you can avoid it. Bring a collapsible bottle and a small basin.

**Clothing.** Quick-dry layers, long sleeves, long trousers, waterproof outer layers that breathe, spare dry socks, and soft-soled boots with grip. Avoid loose dangling straps and anything that snags on roots or hanging fiber. Gloves are essential. A padded cap or soft helmet is better than a metal hard hat if you are trying not to clang and scrape; the ceiling drops things. Muted colors are fine; bright clothing is not a safety feature here. Bring a waterproof bag for everything and a second dry bag for your sleep clothes.

**Tools.** A wooden or bone-tipped walking stick for probing mud and walkway edges. Cordage. A small knife kept sheathed. A watch. A notebook and pencil in waterproof wrap. A quiet, dim translation device if you need one, but do not rely on it in the wet. Bring analog backups: written phrases, gesture notes, a map you draw yourself after asking permission.

**Trade and gifts.** Metal is scarce and prized; a few small, good blades will be valuable, but do not show them off or hand them out carelessly. Salt, dry cordage, good cloth, sealed fishing hooks, and useful dry goods are safer. Do not bring fire-making devices as casual gifts. Do not bring medicines to distribute unless you are qualified and invited to. Do not bring seeds, pets, live plants, or glowing organisms from elsewhere. Clean your boots and gear before arrival.

**What not to bring.** No drones. No loud radios. No firearms or explosives. No bright floodlights. No flash photography without explicit permission. No combustion heaters. No invasive organisms. No assumption that your tech will survive the humidity. If it is not waterproof, quiet, and dim, leave it above the rim.

### Arrival and descent

Stop at the rim. Do not step onto the ceiling mat to “look closer.” It is a living woven mat under tension, growing downward, and sections let go. Wait for your guide. Use the maintained opening they use. Descent is wet, vertical, and slippery; keep two points of contact, keep your bag close, and do not grab roots or runners that may tear free. Announce yourself softly and wait. Do not cut the ceiling to make your own entrance.

Once down, keep your voice low. The Sump is quiet by nature and habit; sound carries. Do not splash, clang, or run. Step where your guide steps. When a walkway ends, do not assume it continues; ask. The gaps are deliberate. Wading may be normal, but channels can be deeper than they look and the mud can hold you. Probe with your stick.

### Daily conduct

Follow the water’s schedule. The town moves in a slow pulse with the water: up and down, in and out. There are hours when you do not go on the water and channels you do not dredge. You cannot derive these rules; ask and obey. Do not go out alone until you know the current routes and the nearest house in each direction.

Keep to the walkways. Test poles and planks before trusting them. Do not lean on the raw clay walls; they slump. Do not camp under thick hanging shelves of ceiling mat or under a “fist” of wet fiber that looks ready to drop. Learn the sounds of the place: a change in dripping, then a tearing, then a wet enormous thud means a wall section has come down. Move away from the wall and off low water; do not stand under the falling mass. Do not try to catch it.

Mind your light. Use red/amber low light. Do not shine at people’s faces, at the water, or at the ceiling. Do not shake or open the town’s luminous jars. If you are given a lamp-pole to carry, carry it steadily and low.

Eat carefully. The pale sacs harvested from the ceiling are the staple. Ask how they are prepared and eat what your host offers, but do not assume unfamiliar raw flesh or water is safe for you. Bring your own high-calorie sealed food as backup. Do not demand a fire to cook. If you have allergies, say so clearly and early.

Protect your lungs and skin. Mold, fine fiber dust, and the spore release are real hazards. Wear your respirator when the mat is being cut, when something drops nearby, and during the opening weeks. Cover your mouth and nose, stay indoors, seal gaps as best you can, keep lamps low, and wait. Do not “see what happens.”

### Social rules

The Sump’s people are not a ruin-dwelling curiosity. They are a town. Speak low. Ask before entering a house, stepping between walkways, harvesting, or touching the luminous jars. Do not ask whether the pale sacs are plant or animal in a way that sounds like a test; they find the question funny and slightly rude. Do not call the place a hole, a pit, or a primitive settlement. Do not photograph people or sacred or working spaces without permission. Do not leave trash. Do not feed animals. Do not collect living things as souvenirs unless explicitly given.

Offer labor, trade, or gifts rather than charity. Be useful and quiet. Learn a few phrases: thank you, excuse me, where does the walkway end, is the water rising, when is the quiet time, danger, no fire. If you are unsure, say so. Confident ignorance is the fastest way to become a burden or a casualty.

### Emergency plan

Know the route back to the entrance and who can guide you. Know which house is nearest at each point. Carry a small med kit, purified water, a dim backup light, cord, and a whistle or tapping tool—but use soft signals, not loud shouting.

If you are lost: stop. Dim your light. Listen. Tap a soft repeated pattern and wait. Do not wade an unknown channel in the dark. If the water rises: get onto a high walkway or into a house, not against the wall. If a wall slumps: move away from the wall and off low water. If the ceiling drops: cover your head, move out from under the hanging mass, and do not try to salvage anything. If the spore weeks begin early: get indoors, mask up, seal what you can, and stay put until someone tells you it is over. If you are injured: clean with purified water, disinfect, cover, and seek local help; do not rinse a wound in the standing water.

### The short version

Bring dim red light, a good respirator, waterproof everything, purified water, dry socks, gloves, a probing stick, and a guide. Leave fire, bright lights, loud tech, and confidence above the rim. Speak low, step where told, ask about timing, stay indoors during the opening weeks, and never assume the ceiling is a roof. The Sump is still becoming itself; your job is to be a quiet, temporary guest in a place that is not finished and does not need you to finish it.
