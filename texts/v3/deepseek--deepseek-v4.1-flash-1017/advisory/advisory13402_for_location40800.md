You should prepare for this place as if you are visiting the inside of a lung that is also a pressure vessel, a workshop, and a lantern. The bright room is not the destination; the water is the destination, and the room is the thin, noisy refusal of the water. Your job as an ordinary human is to add as little load, heat, moisture, CO₂, vibration, dirt, and surprise as possible.

**Before you go, get the boring facts.** Ask—or measure—the internal pressure, depth, atmosphere composition, oxygen and CO₂ levels, temperature, humidity, noise level, light level, gravity, entry and exit procedure, maximum safe occupancy, toilet arrangements, drinking-water supply, power backup, and emergency egress plan. Do not assume the dome is at one atmosphere. If it is pressure-equalized to the surrounding sea, you may need decompression on exit and you must not treat the visit as a quick pop-in. If it is one atmosphere, the hull is under enormous stress and you must treat every seal, cable, and glass panel as load-bearing. Ask what the normal baseline sounds are: the fan thrum, the cable moan, the hiss at the seals. You need to know what “normal” sounds like so you can recognize “wrong.”

**Prepare your body.** Cancel the trip if you have a cold, sinus infection, ear problem, uncontrolled asthma, claustrophobia, or bad motion sickness. The room is small, bright, warm, wet, loud, and in constant low-frequency vibration; that combination finds weaknesses. Bring any regular medication, an inhaler if you might need one, antihistamines for mold/salt/plant allergens, anti-nausea medication, and ear drops or equalization aids if pressure changes are involved. Plan a short first visit—thirty to sixty minutes—not because the room is large but because the air, noise, light, and lack of privacy are cumulative. One extra human in a sealed plant-and-fan habitat is a real CO₂ and humidity load. Do not exercise, sing, or run.

**Bring a small, clean, non-corroding kit.** Wear quick-drying, close-fitting clothes with no dangling straps, no loose jewelry, no long hair down, and no flowing sleeves near the fans. Soft-soled, non-slip shoes are essential: the polished concrete is slick with condensation, and there are gutters. Bring spare socks, a small towel, work gloves, nitrile gloves for wet tasks, eye protection for drips and dust, and musician’s earplugs—enough to take the edge off the fans without blocking alarms or changes in structural sound. Tie back hair. Use a soft, close-fitting bag with zippers, not an open tote. Pack everything in dry bags: the air is humid and salty, and condensation will find electronics. Bring a small personal CO₂ monitor with an audible or vibrating alarm, a red-mode headlamp, a whistle or visual signaling device, sealed drinking water, low-crumb high-calorie food, and pack-out waste bags. Ask about the toilet before you arrive; if there is none, keep the visit very short or bring a sealed portable system and pack everything out. Do not bring perfumes, aerosols, solvents, open flames, smoking materials, unsealed powders, seeds, pets, or anything that sheds lint or dust. Avoid ferrous tools and loose batteries where possible; use titanium, bronze-compatible, plastic, or sealed gear. Bring a sleep mask if you will stay long enough to sleep—the light is constant—and set a personal watch, because the room has no day.

**Arrange arrival like a docking, not a teleport.** Ask for a designated arrival mark away from the fan intakes, the leak pan, the workbench, the plant troughs, the central pillar, and the dome seals. Open your portal or transfer slowly and equalize pressure before you step through; a pressure pulse can knock things over, stress the glass, or spray condensation. Do not materialize outside the dome unless you have a full pressure and thermal plan. Do not open a portal into the water “just to look.” Announce yourself, wait, and let the keeper—if there is one—tell you where to stand. If your ears hurt on arrival, stop, equalize, and abort if it does not resolve.

**While you are there, behave as a guest in a life-support machine.** Do not tap, lean on, or scratch the dome; it is sky and hull at once. Do not touch the great lamp; it may be hot. Keep away from the fans; carved wood and brass can still catch hair and cloth. Do not move the bronze pan under the leak, do not mop unless asked, do not block vents, do not adjust the ventilation, and do not use the local tools as souvenirs or toys. The bone wrenches, shell chisels, and wooden mallets are maintenance equipment; bone and shell can break, and the keeper’s order is part of the safety system. The plants are air processors, not decor: do not overwater them, do not eat the pods, do not disturb the roots or gray-water hydroponic solution. If you touch wet plant water, wash your hands with clean water and soap if permitted, and dry them. Walk flat and slow on the wet concrete; do not chase a dropped object into a gutter or trough—ask for help. Keep your bag off the floor and out of walkways. Eat and drink only from sealed containers, over your bag or a tray, and pack out every crumb and wrapper. No aerosols, no strong smells, no open bottles near the workbench.

**Listen and monitor.** The fan is the heartbeat. If it stops, slows dramatically, or changes pitch, leave. If a seal begins to hiss or spray, if the leak pan overflows, if the dome emits a sharp crack rather than a deep moan, if the lamp flickers, or if your CO₂ alarm sounds, evacuate by the prearranged route; do not try to patch anything. Headache, drowsiness, confusion, or a sudden feeling of stuffiness means get out and get air. If the hull breaches, the sea will not be a warm bath; it will be pressure, darkness, and violence. Do not swim out unless that is the designated exit. Do not open anything.

**Prepare psychologically.** The room is warm, bright, noisy, wet, and small. The sea is a constant, intimate presence: a thrum in your bones, a groan in the cables, a murky blue-green infinity pressing on the glass. Some people find that soothing; others find it oppressive after twenty minutes. Know your exit. If panic rises, sit on the dry part of the floor, breathe slowly, focus on the designated exit, and do not run on wet concrete. Expect no privacy and no quiet. If there is a keeper, they are busy keeping a machine alive; offer to carry, not to fix. Ask before helping. Respect the maintenance.

**On departure, leave no trace and report what you noticed.** Pack out all waste. Wipe down or bag your gear; decontaminate boots and anything that touched the hydroponic area so you do not carry mold, salt, or spores to the next world. Report any new drip, changed sound, cracked seal, fan hesitation, or slippery patch. Do not take tools, plants, pods, water, or “souvenirs.” Clean your gear again on the other side. And keep the first visit short. This is not a place to sightsee; it is a place to be tolerated by a small, bright, noisy machine that is holding a warm and patient sea at bay.
