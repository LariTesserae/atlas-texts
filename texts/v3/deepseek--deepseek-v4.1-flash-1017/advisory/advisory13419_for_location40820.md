## Briefing for an ordinary human multiverse traveler

Treat the Comb-Shelf as a high-altitude cold desert that is also a mild cognitive hazard. The main dangers are not monsters. They are dehydration, thin air, cold, fine abrasive dust, falls in hollow stone, disorientation caused by unstable distance, and the bad decisions you make when the place quietly stops obeying your map. The shelf is not hostile, but it is not accommodating either. Prepare to be small in it.

### 1. Before you go: reality and body checks

- **Do not go without a reliable return anchor.** Set a portal/exit anchor at your arrival point, confirm it works, and give it a dead-man’s switch or timed reopen. Because distances here can recede, multiply, or loop, your exit must not depend on you finding the same physical spot by sight.
- **Record the entry point acoustically and instrumentally.** Note the two-tone groan’s pitch, volume, and apparent bearing at arrival. That sound is the closest thing this place has to infrastructure. If your instruments fail, it may be your only stable reference.
- **Test your gear against the local rules.** Radio, compass, GPS, clocks, cameras, and electronics may behave oddly or become useless. Test them at the entry before trusting them. Keep a mechanical watch and a paper log.
- **Acclimatize for thin cold air.** If you can pre-acclimatize, do it. If not, plan a short visit, carry supplemental oxygen if your physiology needs it, and bring a pulse oximeter. Altitude sickness plus weird distance is a bad combination.
- **Assume no resupply.** There is no water. The wells are dry. There is nothing to eat. Carry all water, calories, and fuel you need, plus reserve. Do not plan to cross the whole 140 km shelf. Plan to visit a section and return.
- **Go with a partner if possible.** Use a tether or strict visual/radio check-ins near the entry. If you go alone, keep the trip short and keep within a fixed line or beacon range of your exit.

### 2. What to wear and carry

You want windproof, abrasion-resistant, sealed clothing and a closed water/food system.

**Clothing and protection**
- Layered insulation for cold altitude air, with a windproof outer shell.
- Abrasion-resistant trousers and jacket; seal cuffs, ankles, and neck against fine mineral dust.
- Goggles or sealed eye protection. The wind carries a fine grit you cannot feel but will find.
- Respiratory mask or filter. The air smells clean, but it is thin and dusty. Do not assume it is harmless.
- Gloves, helmet, sturdy boots with hard soles, and gaiters. The grit is crunchy on top and packed below; falls and ankle turns are real.
- No loose straps, dangling cords, or unsecured gear. The wind will worry them, and the place does not need your litter.

**Water and food**
- Carry more water than you think. Cold, altitude, and exertion increase loss. Use a closed bladder or bottles; keep the bite valve covered.
- Electrolyte tabs and high-calorie, cold-tolerant food. Do not rely on cooking, but bring a stove and fuel if you can use it safely in shelter.
- No drinking from wells, channels, or condensation. There is no trustworthy local water.
- Pack out all waste. This is not a place to leave anything behind.

**Shelter and emergency**
- Low-profile windproof shelter, sleeping bag, and pad if you might need to stop. Use rocks or weighted bags rather than driving stakes into stone. Do not camp deep inside a comb.
- First-aid kit for cold, dehydration, altitude, dust in eyes, blisters, and sprains.
- Emergency beacon, spare batteries, headlamp with red-light mode, and a mechanical backup light.
- Rope or fixed line for the first stretch from your entry, if you can do it without damaging the stone.
- Notebook and pencil. Log times, water consumed, bearings to the sound, and anomalies. Do not rely on memory here.

**Do not bring**
- Weapons. They will not help and may make you feel brave in a place where bravery is usually bad navigation.
- Drones, loud speakers, music players, or anything that fills the acoustic space. The sound is the local map. Do not interrupt it.
- Collection containers for droppers, stone, or “samples.” Leave the shelf as a state, not a souvenir shop.

### 3. Arrival protocol

1. **Stop at the entry and listen for several minutes.** Let the two-tone groan establish itself. Note whether it is steady, fading, or deepening.
2. **Confirm your anchor and exit.** Test the return signal. Mark the entry with a beacon, not a carving or paint.
3. **Set a turnaround threshold before you move.** Use water, time, and fatigue—not visual distance. “When I have used one-third of my water, I turn back” is better than “when I reach that comb.”
4. **Choose a movement rule.** Keep the sound at a consistent bearing and volume if you can. Walk beside the worn path, not on it; the path belongs to the walkers. Do not chase a comb that looks close. If an object recedes as you approach, stop and return to the last stable reference.
5. **Keep quiet.** Speak softly, avoid echoes, and do not test the place by shouting. If the wind fades into the “drawn breath” quiet, stand still and listen rather than filling the silence.

### 4. Conduct on the shelf

- **The shelf is not a trap; it is a state.** Do not try to solve it, break it, or prove it wrong. Do not count the striations in the combs. Do not answer the syllable if you hear one. Do not repeat it. Note where you heard it and move away.
- **Avoid overhangs when the wind tone deepens.** That is when droppers let go. Do not stand under a comb’s underside during a deepening groan. Do not pick up fallen droppers; they are not yours to handle.
- **Ignore the finches-that-are-not-finches.** They are not a threat. Do not swat at heat-shimmer or follow the glass-struck sound.
- **Step off the path for walkers.** If you see a tall, spindle-limbed figure, move aside, stay quiet, and do not try to speak, follow, or touch it. Do not block the worn line. If you meet something that might be a person going the other way, give it distance and do not initiate contact.
- **If you are watched, do not confront it.** No sudden scanning with lights, no shouting, no aggressive posturing. Leave slowly and calmly.
- **Use shallow lee alcoves for rest, not deep chambers.** The chambers have sills worn by something entering and leaving. They are not empty rooms. If you must shelter, choose a shallow windbreak and keep your exit clear.
- **Do not rely on prints.** Your own prints may not match you when you turn around, and unfamiliar prints may be older than your visit. Treat mismatched prints as an anomaly, not a trail.
- **Do not stare at the moon-sized thing.** It will not set, and it will not help you keep time. Use your watch.

### 5. Anomaly protocol

If any of these happen, stop, hydrate, and return to your last stable acoustic or beacon reference:

- A comb you were walking toward recedes or multiplies.
- You arrive where you began, or at an identical comb that was not there.
- Your footprints are not yours, or are older than they should be.
- The sound hollow’s bearing or volume changes without wind explanation.
- Your time log and your sense of elapsed time disagree badly.
- You hear the syllable, or the quiet becomes an interior quiet that feels like held breath.
- You feel the air pressure change when a shadow touches the ground.

Do not push deeper to “see what happens.” The shelf does not mind you, but it does not owe you coherence. If you cannot return to your anchor, shelter in a shallow lee, stay quiet, conserve water, and activate your beacon. Do not wander while lost; wandering is how the shelf turns a short visit into a long one.

### 6. Mental preparation

Expect long silences, a dim sky that never resolves, shadows that move in minutes-per-metre, and a persistent sense that the rules here were agreed to and could be un-agreed. That feeling is not a command to test the agreement. Ground yourself with routine: water checks, time logs, quiet meals, sleep schedule. The lack of day/night means you must impose your own rest cycle. Keep one ear open while you sleep, but use earplugs only if you must and only in a secured shelter.

The shelf’s emptiness is the point. Do not try to fill it with noise, names, theories, or conquest. The phrase is still being spoken; the mouth has worn to its shape. Your job is to visit quietly, leave no trace, and come back with your own shape intact.

### 7. Return and decontamination

Exit through your anchor, not by sight. Check your elapsed time and water use against your log. On return, brush and wash off the mineral dust, quarantine your outer gear, and inspect for anything you did not intentionally bring back. Do not carry droppers, grit, or chamber air in sealed containers unless you have a proper containment protocol and a very good reason.

If you are an ordinary human traveler with no special resistance to disorientation or local physics, the correct preparation is: short visit, fixed anchor, all water, all food, dust protection, cold protection, acoustic navigation, quiet conduct, and a hard turnaround rule. The Comb-Shelf will not kill you for being small. It will simply remain larger, older, and more patient than your map.
