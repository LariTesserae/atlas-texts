Treat Glisten-Reach as a wet cave, a sensory-deprivation chamber, a foreign monastery, and a biosafety-sensitive village all at once. Your main survival tools will be a local guide, patience, dim red light, dry socks, and the ability to keep your mouth shut and your hands off the stone.

**First rule: do not arrive unannounced, and do not arrive inside the city.**  
Get permission and a local guide before you go. Do not portal or drop directly into the city; descend by an established route from the periphery. If you cannot get a guide, do not go. The city is a single slow-growing acoustic structure, and a stranger arriving by a new route is not just a tourist—it is a change in the hum.

**Before you go**
- Learn how to refer to the city and its people. Do not call it “Glisten-Reach” unless a guide says it is acceptable. Its true name is a resonance you probably cannot pronounce; ask for a polite human-safe way to say “your city” and “your people.”
- Prepare physically for cold, wet, slippery rock: caving fitness, crouching, crawling, balance, low ceilings, narrow passages, and no handrails. The inhabitants are small and nimble; you may not fit everywhere. Check scale before committing.
- Practice in wet caves and in darkness. If you are strongly claustrophobic, light-sensitive, noise-sensitive, or bad at monotony, do not go.
- Get a local guide who understands the resonance, the water channels, the fauna, and the etiquette. A universal translator may not help with acoustic communication; learn basic phrases and guide signals.
- Clean your gear obsessively before entry: no mud, seeds, pets, invasive microbes, or biological samples from other worlds. Do not visit if you are sick. Cover cuts. The locals may be immunologically isolated; you are a walking contamination risk as much as they are.
- Ask about the river cycle and high-water times. Avoid arrival during unusual resonance, flooding, or sensitive periods. Plan a short first visit; a local “year” is about forty Earth years, and a single chamber may take a lifetime.
- Establish an extraction point, check-in schedule, and emergency plan outside the city. Your beacon may not penetrate rock. Do not rely on loud alarms or radios.

**What to bring**
- Small, waterproof, low-profile pack; no dangling straps, no jingling keys, no Velcro, no crinkly wrappers.
- Soft-soled, high-traction, waterproof boots. Test them for squeaking on wet rock. Bring wool socks and spare dry socks in a sealed bag. No cotton.
- Merino or fleece layers, waterproof shell, warm hat, thin grippy gloves, kneepads, bump helmet. Everything fitted and quiet.
- Dim red headlamp and spare sealed batteries. No white light, no flash, no bright screens, no drones, no open flame, no laser, no active sonar or ultrasound.
- Glasses with anti-fog and a strap; contacts are a bad idea in wet mineral dust.
- Waterproof analog notebook and pencil; sealed electronics; desiccant; no beeping devices; vibration alarms only.
- First aid for slips, cuts, blisters, foot rot, hypothermia, and nausea. Repackage it quietly. Bring personal medications; do not distribute them.
- Water filter and your own water. High-energy, soft, quiet food. Wag bags and pack-out waste bags. Unscented toiletries; no soap, perfume, repellent, smoking, or vaping.
- Earplugs for sleep only. Do not block the resonance while navigating.
- Caving gear if descending: helmet, harness, static rope, ascenders/descenders, webbing, padding. Wet rock anchors are poor; use the guide’s anchors, no bolts, no leaving rope behind.
- A small gas monitor if you have one, set to vibration, not alarm. The ozone/metal smell can irritate; if it becomes strong or you get a headache, leave.

**Arrival and sensory adaptation**
Descend slowly with your guide. Announce yourself from the periphery and wait. Let your eyes adapt; use red light only, pointed downward, and ask before you illuminate anything. The people are pale and dark-adapted; white light will be painful and rude.

Expect the resonance in your teeth and wrist. It may fatigue you or make you nauseous. Acclimatize gradually. Do not try to “fix” the hum with headphones or noise-canceling gear while moving. If the vibration makes you ill and does not pass, leave.

**Sound etiquette**
The city is an instrument and a communication network. Do not tap walls, strike quartz-veined pillars, drum on stone, whistle, sing, play music, or mimic the resonance. Do not drag metal, scrape boots, or let wrappers crackle. Speak softly; allow silence. If you must attract attention, use the guide’s approved signal. Emergency whistles only if agreed; a loud noise may be read as damage or alarm.

**Touch and contamination**
Do not lean on walls, sit on ledges, touch fossils, handle isopods, disturb luminescent dust, or collect water, stone, or biological samples. The city is built by mineral accretion; skin oils and foreign materials matter. The blind isopod-like creatures produce acid; do not touch them. If you get acid on you, use your own clean water and seek the guide’s help. Do not wash anything in the channels.

**Water and waste**
The channels are streets, plumbing, and music. Do not dam, redirect, splash, spit, urinate, pour soap, or drop anything into them. Walk where the guide walks. Wear waterproof boots. Pack out all waste, including tissues, wrappers, batteries, and menstrual products. Use wag bags; do not bury anything.

**Food and water**
Bring your own water and filter it if needed. Ask before drinking city water; do not assume your gut can handle it. Eat quietly in private or in a designated place; no crumbs. Do not offer human food without permission. If offered food or drink, ask what it is, accept politely, and use judgment; do not distribute your medicines.

**Cultural etiquette**
Be slow. Do not hurry them. Do not offer to “speed up” their work, “improve” the city, or demonstrate your technology. Do not call them primitive, cave-dwellers, or exhibits. Do not stare at their pale skin or small stature. Do not touch people unless invited. Ask before photography or recording; no flash, no bright screens, no drones, no continuous filming. Do not publish exact routes or locations without consent. If a guide says leave, leave immediately and politely.

Gifts are not automatic. Ask first. Avoid foreign resonant stones, beeping electronics, bright lights, scented things, organics, or anything that could alter the hum or contaminate the water. A quiet story, careful attention, or thanks may be better than an object.

**Navigation and emergency**
Stay with your guide. Use a guide line if needed, with permission, padded and removed afterward. Do not mark stone with chalk, paint, flags, or carvings. Learn resonance landmarks by asking the guide, not by tapping. If separated: stop, stay still, listen, keep warm, and use the agreed signal. Do not wander. If injured: splint, warm, wait, and let the guide manage evacuation. Do not expect fast rescue.

**Psychological preparation**
There is no sky, no sun, no day, no night, no seasons, and very little change. The hum is constant. This can wear you down faster than the physical environment. Keep a silent watch, maintain a sleep schedule, sleep with earplugs in private if permitted, and give yourself permission to leave early. Do not overstay. A short, respectful visit is better than a long, fraying one.

**Afterward**
Leave no trace. Clean and quarantine your gear. Do not carry samples, seeds, or microbes out casually. Report any contamination or illness. Respect their privacy.

In short: go small, dim, quiet, dry, slow, and humble. If you cannot manage that, do not go.
