Prepare for the Sump Terraces as if you were visiting a warm, wet, high-altitude waterworks that is also a working garden and a small village—one where the “ground” is iron and ceramic, the true earth below is forbidden, and every surface is either slippery, corroded, overgrown, or all three. The place is not hostile in a dramatic way. It will not attack you. It will simply make you wet, make you slow, make your electronics fail, make your feet rot if you neglect them, and kill you if you treat a foggy edge or an overgrown railing as ordinary.

## Before you arrive

- **Arrange a guide and permission.** This is a home and a workplace for a dozen people and their children. Do not materialize unannounced in a pump room, a reservoir, or on the lowest terrace. Ask to arrive at a maintained iron landing, preferably near the upper terraces, and wait to be received.
- **Plan a short first visit.** A day visit is better than an overnight. The place is only about a hundred meters across, but it is multi-level, wet, foggy, and full of unmarked decay. Fatigue and disorientation are real hazards.
- **Test your return anchor twice.** Have a backup exit and a agreed return window. Do not anchor your portal or rope to a corroded pipe, an overgrown railing, or anything whose only virtue is that it looks old and solid. Use only anchors the workers approve.
- **Train for stairs and wet footing.** You will climb, descend, and balance on slick ceramic and rusted grating at altitude. Break in high-traction boots. If you have balance, heart, lung, or mobility problems, reconsider or arrange heavy assistance.
- **Acclimatize for thin air.** The Terraces are high enough that clouds sit around and below you. Take the first hours slowly. Know the symptoms of altitude illness—headache, nausea, dizziness, unusual breathlessness—and have a plan to evacuate rather than “push through.”
- **Check tetanus and routine health.** Rust, cuts, insect bites, and unfamiliar water are all plausible. Bring personal medications, antihistamines, rehydration salts, blister care, antifungal powder, and any altitude medication a clinician has approved for you.
- **Decontaminate your gear before arrival.** Clean boots, velcro, cuffs, and pack pockets of seeds, soil, and mud. Do not bring outside plants, animals, or pond water into a closed-loop ecosystem. Do not release pets. Do not collect specimens without explicit permission.

## What to wear

Dress for warm humidity, constant condensation, and occasional coolness at height. Avoid cotton. Wear quick-drying long trousers and long sleeves—not shorts—because of insects, scrapes, and wet machinery. Use a light wicking base layer, a breathable rain shell with a hood, and a thin warm layer for upper levels. Choose dark or rust-colored old clothes; iron and copper will stain.

Footwear matters more than almost anything else. Wear broken-in boots with aggressive rubber tread, ankle support, and drainage. No sandals, no smooth soles, no heels. Bring at least two spare pairs of synthetic socks and change them before your feet stay wet too long.

Bring close-fitting work gloves with good wet grip—two pairs—and perhaps a thin cut-resistant liner. The place is a small wet transaction every time you touch it; gloves protect you from rust, sharp ceramic, and overgrown fittings. Safety glasses or ordinary glasses with a retention strap are useful against drips from the trellis. A hat or hood keeps condensation off your neck. A head net and insect repellent are worth carrying if the silverflies and water-midges are active; use repellent on yourself, not sprayed into the pools.

## What to carry

Carry your own water, food, and waste system. Do not assume the local water is potable for you just because the workers drink it. The reservoirs are a green, teeming soup; the nutrient film is everywhere; your gut is not adapted. Bring sealed water and a filter plus chemical purification or a boiling method if permitted. Prefer your own food: high-calorie, sealed, unsmelly, easy to eat without touching wet surfaces. Local greens and insect cakes may be offered; eat small amounts of cooked food if you wish to be polite, but do not forage on your own.

Pack everything in dry bags with desiccant. Condensation will get into electronics, paper, and clothing. Use a waterproof notebook and pencil rather than ordinary paper and ink. Bring a headlamp with spare batteries, a whistle, a watch, a laminated map or sketch, a power bank in a dry bag, and a camera you can operate with wet hands. A small repair kit—duct tape, cord, spare laces, sewing kit, safety pins—is useful. A collapsible trekking pole can help balance, but keep one hand free for rails and do not use it to prod uncertain structures.

Bring a serious personal medical kit: bandages, antiseptic, tweezers, blister dressings, antifungal, painkillers, antihistamines, rehydration salts, personal prescriptions, and a few sealed waste bags or wag bags. There may be no safe place to urinate or defecate except a container you carry out. Do not urinate over the edge; the water returns to the sky and the condensers. Do not wash with soap in the pools or channels. Use minimal water and pack out wipes and trash.

If you bring gifts, make them practical and non-invasive: salt, tea, dried food, good gloves, sewing supplies, a water filter, sturdy cord. Ask first. Do not give candy to children without their parents’ consent. Do not bring seeds, live plants, or animals.

## Arriving and moving

Arrive on stable, maintained iron. Announce yourself. Wait. Let a worker show you which stairs are sound and which railings are decorative at best. The structure is “sound—really it is—but the land it is anchored to is not ready,” and the same is true locally: some patch is always new, some patch is always being eaten.

Walk slowly and stay in the center of maintained walkways. The ceramic is slick; the drainage slots catch boot heels; moss and nutrient film make everything greasy. Test a railing gently before trusting it, but do not lean your weight on an overgrown one. Keep away from edges, especially in fog. Do not run, jump, or climb on pipes, trellises, trees, or reservoir walls. Do not step off the iron onto the raw scree or unfinished slope below. Do not descend below the lowest terrace. Do not go into the fog to retrieve a dropped item. The one prohibition is unmediated contact with the unfinished earth, and the fog is where that earth hides.

Do not touch valves, pump controls, spillway gates, or reservoir fittings. A whole terrace can sluice itself; a valve can open; a pipe can split. If water starts moving loudly, stand clear and wait for instruction. Do not try to “fix” a weeping pipe or clear a drain unless a worker asks you to and shows you how. The pump hum is normal; if it stops, ask—do not panic and do not start pressing things.

If fog closes in, stop navigating alone. Use your whistle. Stay on iron. Call for your guide. The place is small, but multi-level and disorienting; a wrong turn toward an edge is the main way an ordinary visitor gets killed.

## Water, food, waste, and biosecurity

Treat all standing water as contaminated. The pools are productive, not decorative; the green soup is harvested, not swum in. Do not wade, swim, wash, or fill your bottle directly from a basin. Ask which water the workers consider safe, then still filter and purify it for yourself. Keep cuts covered. If you get pool water on your skin, rinse with clean water when you can and avoid touching your face.

Eat sealed food in a sheltered spot. Keep crumbs and smells contained; insects are the real animal life here. Do not feed birds or insects. Do not leave food scraps. Pack out every wrapper, wipe, and waste bag. The entire place is a closed loop: what you leave will come back as mist, nutrient film, or someone else’s maintenance problem.

Do not bring outside water, plants, or animals into the terraces. Do not pour anything into the channels. Do not light fires or smoke. Clean your boots and gear again before you leave, and check for seeds or mud. If you take photographs, do not disturb the gardens to get a better angle.

## The people

The workers are few, amiable, and busy. Ask before entering work areas, before photographing people, and before touching tools or gardens. Do not call the place a ruin or an abandoned overgrowth. It is a working place with a negotiated decay. Do not suggest “clearing all this vegetation”; the vegetation is part of the filtration and the water cycle. If you must move a vine, ask. Do not uproot anything.

The children will be curious; be friendly but not patronizing, and let parents set the boundaries. The two old men who remember the earliest construction may be lying; listen politely, but trust current workers for safety. Offer help only if asked, and then do exactly what you are told. Practical gifts are better than money. Learn a few greetings and the phrases “May I stand here?”, “Which way is maintained?”, and “I will not touch the valves.”

## Health and emergency

Watch for altitude illness, heat exhaustion, and dehydration. Warm humidity can mask how much water you are losing; drink and use electrolytes. Keep feet dry when possible, change socks, and treat hot spots before they blister. Use insect repellent, do not scratch bites, and clean any cut from rust or ceramic immediately. If you have an allergy or asthma, carry your medication and tell your guide.

If you are lost: stay on iron, use your whistle, and do not descend. If someone falls toward the fog: do not climb down the raw slope after them. Call the workers; they know the structure and the rope points. If a pipe bursts or a terrace sluices: move away from the water, do not try to close valves, and wait. If the structure groans or a grate looks wrong: stop, back up, and ask.

## Leaving

Leave slowly and cleanly. Bag your waste, check your gear for seeds and mud, thank your hosts, and report any damage or near-miss you caused or saw. Do not take souvenirs—plants, fittings, ceramic, or “old iron”—without explicit permission. Decontaminate your boots and pack before you return to your own world.

The Sump Terraces will tolerate an ordinary human who moves slowly, asks permission, carries dry bags and clean water, respects the gardens, stays on the iron, and never treats the fog as a view. It will punish anyone who assumes that a wet railing holds, that a green pool is water, that a valve is a handle, or that the ground below is ground.
