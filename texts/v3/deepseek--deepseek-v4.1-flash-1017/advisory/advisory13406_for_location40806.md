Treat **the Levelled** as a high-altitude dry desert expedition with two extra hazards: a living pavement underfoot and a perceptual/ontological drift that will quietly erode your ability to count, measure, and keep your own boundaries. An ordinary human can visit, but only if you bring everything the place refuses to provide: water, oxygen margin, warmth, navigation feedback, and a hard exit plan.

## First, understand what you are preparing for

You will climb for two days into thin, cold air, then reach a flat top that gives you no summit feeling and no obvious edge. The plateau is dry to the bone, has no water, no haze, no echo, almost no wind, and a horizon sharp enough to cut. Midday light is even and shadowless, so depth cues fail. The ground is a crust of torpid, soft-bodied animals layered several deep; your footsteps wake a ripple that heals within minutes. Counting them, counting cairns, or judging distance by eye will start to slip. Ancient stone works persist undecayed. Nothing accumulates, nothing decays, nothing answers back when you shout.

So the expedition problem is not “can I survive a strange landscape?” It is: **can I carry enough water and oxygen margin for a multi-day ascent/descent, navigate without landmarks, keep my mind anchored, avoid damaging a living surface, and leave immediately if my sense of self or scale starts to fail?**

## Before you go

1. **Do the physical work.** Be fit enough for two days of ascent and two days of descent at altitude with a heavy pack. Practice carrying all your water. If you cannot carry or cache enough water for the whole trip plus emergency reserve, do not go.

2. **Plan altitude seriously.** The air is thin enough that breath is short and heartbeat audible. Stage your ascent, sleep lower than your high point where possible, monitor yourself and your buddy with a pulse oximeter, and carry supplemental oxygen if your medical plan calls for it. Know the symptoms of altitude illness. The only reliable treatment is descent. Do not plan to “push through” confusion, headache, vomiting, wet breathing, or loss of coordination.

3. **Calculate water honestly.** There is no water to purify, no snow, no seep, no dew. If the trip is two days up, one day on the plateau, two days down, that is five days of water for each person, plus reserve. At altitude you may need several liters a day. That is enormously heavy. Use staged caches below the rim if permitted, mark them with instruments rather than counted cairns, secure them against animals and wind, and keep an emergency reserve you do not touch. If water logistics are marginal, shorten the visit to a brief rim visit.

4. **Establish your multiverse return anchor at the rim, not on the plateau.** Test it. Set a fixed extraction/return window. Leave a physical tether or marked line anchored at the rim if you intend to walk out. Use GPS waypoints, altimeter, compass if local fields are verified, and redundant clocks. Do not rely on counted landmarks or memory of distances.

5. **Take a buddy if at all possible.** A second person helps with altitude, water, navigation, and reality-checking. Rope together or keep visual contact. Do not split up. If solo, use a continuous tether and conservative turnaround times.

6. **Carry the right kit.** Full skin coverage, gloves, dust mask or respirator, goggles, high-UV protection, warm insulating layers, windproof shell, freestanding shelter, insulated sleeping pad, cold-rated sleeping bag, no-cook food, electrolytes, all water, wag bags, repair kit, headlamp, signal mirror, satellite beacon or equivalent, radio, redundant navigation, and a physical external token or recorded message to ground your identity. Bring no fire-dependent plan unless you carry fuel; there is nothing to burn.

7. **Plan to leave no trace.** Nothing decays here. Food waste, human waste, wrappers, and markers will persist. Pack out everything. Do not dig, carve, collect fauna, or move stones from the works. Treat the ancient structures as protected archaeological sites.

## On the ascent and at the rim

Climb slowly. Cache water below the rim and at the last safe camp. At the rim, stop and look before walking out. Set bearings. Anchor your line. Set a hard turnaround by clock, not by landmarks. Write down the plan and check it against your buddy. Do not camp on the plateau unless you are well acclimatized and have no alternative; a short visit from the rim is safer than a night on the living crust.

## Walking on the plateau

- **Stay near your anchor unless you have a continuous line or reliable track.** The plain has no scale cues and your eye will report “near” and “far” at random. Use GPS track and time, not visual estimates.
- **Do not count things as navigation.** Cairns, stones, and even your own markers may not stay countable in your mind. Use a rope, GPS, and timestamps.
- **Minimize footsteps.** You will wake the crust. That is unavoidable, but do not stomp, dig, pry, or pick up animals. Do not try to separate merged individuals. Do not eat, kill, or collect them. Keep bare skin covered and avoid inhaling dust.
- **Use instruments for feedback.** No echo means shouts are poor signals. Use radio, visual signals, and beacons. Keep your buddy in sight or on a tether.
- **Travel deliberately at midday.** Shadowless light destroys depth perception. Use trekking poles, watch your footing, and avoid unnecessary scrambling.
- **Sleep off the crust if possible.** Use a cot or wide-based pad on exposed hardpan or rock, not directly on the living pavement. Seal food and gear against dust.
- **Do not try to solve the place.** The counting slips, the dissolving individual boundaries, and the scale drift are not puzzles to be beaten on site. If you feel derealization, panic, compulsive counting, or a sense that your own boundaries are softening, return to the rim immediately.

## Fauna and biosecurity

Treat the crust as a protected living surface and a potential biological unknown. Do not bring any of it back. Inspect and decontaminate gear on return according to your multiverse protocols. Avoid bare-skin contact, ingestion, and inhalation of dust. If you are bitten or scratched—unlikely, but possible—clean and monitor, and report it.

## Emergency rules

There is no local rescue. Your plan must be self-rescue. If lost: stop, shelter, conserve water, mark your position electronically, and return along your tether or track. If altitude illness develops: descend. If identity or scale distortion becomes distressing: descend. Do not wait for the place to give you a sign; it will not. The absence of echo is the point: you must bring your own feedback.

## The short version

Go fit, acclimatized, and accompanied. Carry all your water and an oxygen margin. Anchor at the rim and use a tether, GPS, and clocks—not counting or landmarks. Cover your skin, mask against dust, protect against cold and UV, and pack out every waste. Do not pick up the fauna, do not move the works, and do not stay long enough for the quiet to start editing your sense of self. Visit briefly, look, listen to the enormous slow breathing under your boots, and go down while the descent still sounds like the world talking back.
