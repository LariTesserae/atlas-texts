The people who live in the Basin of the Warm Shallows are not settlers so much as maintainers. They do not live *on* the place; they live *inside* it, the way a hand lives inside a glove. They are low-built and wide-spread, like the town, and they move with the particular care of people who have learned that the ground is a crust and the crust is a rumor. A child here learns two sounds before it learns words: the hollow ring of a plate of travertine under a heel, and the fine sift of shrimp against water, which is the loudest thing in the basin and quieter than breathing.

The wall-dressers are the oldest seeming of them, though not by much. They walk the streets with long planes over their shoulders and shave the growing stone back to true. They are patient in the way of barbers, and they read a jamb the way other people read a face — counting filled hinge-holes like tree rings, deciding where a door has sagged and where the stone has fattened. The shavings fall white at their feet and are swept into the channels, and the channels carry the town’s own body downstream to become wall again. The wall-dressers do not think of this as strange. They think of it as sweeping.

The rakers work the drying floors in the harvest weeks, pulling flat shovels through pink slurry and white windrow, and they wear their cuffs bound tight and their boots thin enough to feel the difference between a plate that will hold and a plate that will ring and give. They do not talk much over the pans. The shrimp are under everything, making their dry rain sound, and the rakers have learned to work inside that sound the way you work inside weather. When the springs breathe high, the sluices open and the channels run and the pans fill; when the springs ease, the sluices close and the pans stand and the crust begins to take. The rakers do not need a bell. The water is the bell.

The pump tenders and boiler men are the town’s iron nerve. They paint the ferrous things every year and watch them bleed orange anyway. They know the springs breathe twice a day, roughly, and they run the pumps against the breathing so the pans are never quite empty. They are practical men with soft voices and rust-stained knuckles, and they drink from the town supply, not the ground, and they will tell you why without making a sermon of it. Their machinery is iron and ceramic and steam and belt, and some of the buildings they tend are working and some are not, and some have been deliberately flooded and are now just deep square pools with a wall around them. The pump tenders still walk past those and check the walls out of habit. Nobody shaves those walls back to true anymore, but the habit is older than the neglect.

The town’s people are shaped by the damp. Their clothes do not dry; nothing dries here except in the pans, and the pans are engineered for it. So they wear salt-stiffened cuffs and soft hats and boots that never quite lose their wet. They smell of wet chalk and hot iron and something faintly like clean laundry, because the basin itself smells that way and because they have stopped trying to smell like anything else. Their houses are low and their doors do not fit; every door has been re-hung and re-drilled and re-lengthened, and a house’s age is a matter of how many filled hinge-holes you can count in its jamb. They are not a nostalgic people. They do not mourn the wall that was true last decade. They shave it, re-hang the door, repaint the pump, and go on.

And under them, around them, above them, the rest of the population. The microbial mats are the base of everything — copper and orange and a green like old bronze, slick underfoot, thick as felt in the warmest runs. They are not plants and they are not quite a carpet; they are a film that has learned to hold the whole basin up. In the sediment beneath them live worms and snails with shells of the local mineral, small and pale and patient. Over the warm vents the flies rise in columns. And in the water the shrimp are the overwhelming fact. In the cool of the morning they are a faint suspension, a dust in the water; by afternoon the pools are pink with them; by evening they are opaque, a soup that thickens against the light. They make no sound you could call a sound. They make a texture of noise, a fine sift, a pattering like rain on a distant tin roof, that never rises and never stops. They are the crop the town harvests and the crop the birds come for, and both parties are being paid by the same warm water.

The birds are the basin’s silent gentry. Stilts and avocets and a few heavier waders stand in the warm shallows in their thousands, motionless for hours, their reflections broken by the shrimp beneath them. They nest on the dry ridges, on the crust, and their eggs are speckled exactly like the ground. They are almost entirely silent. Once, near dark, one will call — a single rising note, thin as a wire — and then nothing for hours. In the harvest weeks they stand at the fence and watch the pink rows being raked, and the men ignore them, and the birds ignore the men, and the whole arrangement is old and unsentimental and exact.

If you ask who lives here, the honest answer is a maintenance crew inside a warm breathing machine. The springs rise and ease, the pans fill and stand, the shrimp thicken and settle, the birds shift and feed and nest, the stone grows and dissolves, and the people shave and re-hang and repaint. Nothing is ruined and nothing is new. The basin does not much mind. It has been sitting at that balance long enough that its residents have stopped fighting it and learned instead to keep it true — a hand’s width at a time.
