The basin does not have citizens. It has tenants, commensals, and attendants.

The attendants are the ones you would call people, if you needed to. They are a thin, dark, damp-looking population—a few hundred, perhaps, clustered in the dry seams of the complex: under the skirts of the great tanks, in concrete cribs where the condensation is guttered away, in rooms that smell of rubber and boiled dust. They are not stunted, exactly, but compact, as if they have learned to occupy less air. Their skin has a pearly sheen. Their hair is cropped or gone. Their nostrils are broad and their mouths rest slightly open, tasting the air for sulfur. Their eyes are large and slow to blink, adapted to the grey-green dim and the flicker of arc-lamps. Their hands are the most changed part: fingertips smoothed and thickened by hot brass, palms crosshatched with old burns, nails kept short because a nail is a place for steam to condense and scald. They wear oilskin aprons, felt clogs studded with basalt grit, hoods of treated cloth, and copper bands at the wrist that go green in a day.

They are not builders. There is no guild of architects, no memory of a founding, no story of the first pipe laid. They do not add levels to the complex or replace a pipe with a better pipe. They attend. A valve drifts a quarter turn and someone taps it back. A flange weeps and someone wraps it in cloth so it weeps slower. A gauge fogs and someone wipes it with a thumb. Their tools are a brass listening rod, a grease pot, a rag, a chalk stub, a mirror on a bent wire. They press the rod to a pipe and then to the jawbone and listen to the flow the way a doctor listens to a chest. They know the system by sound—the main engine’s thud-hiss, the lesser thud-hiss of pump three, the shriek of a steam whistle that means nothing has changed, the hollow plink of a drip into a brimming pool. They can tell a blocked line from a hot bearing by the quality of a hiss. The map they carry is not drawn. It is a memory of intervals and temperatures.

Their lives are cyclical because the place is. There are no seasons, only valve cycles; no years, only the slow rotation of maintenance routes. Children learn the sounds before they learn the words for them. They are taught to walk on perforated steel without looking down, to read a puddle’s color, to know which pipes are safe to touch with the back of a hand. Their games are listening games: who can name the vent by its hiss, who can find the warmest dry corner. They are born in the few places where the ceiling does not drip, and they die in the same rooms. The dead are not buried in the basalt—the rock is too hard and too hot—but laid in a seam of pipe lagging and left to the heat and the flies. No one marks the place. The basin absorbs them.

Their language is full of breath and click. They do not say “good” or “bad” about the machinery; they say “true” or “drifting.” A true pipe is one that sounds as it should. A drifting valve is one that has begun to lie. Their worst word is “change,” not because they fear it but because the system’s perfection is its stillness. They are conservative in the deep sense: they believe the world is already finished. Their stories are not about heroes who improved things. They are about attendants who heard a wrong note and corrected it before anyone else noticed. Their only monuments are the chalk marks on pipes, faded and renewed, a palimpsest of small attentions.

But they are not the true natives. The true natives are the beetles and the crustaceans and the flies. The beetles are the size of a thumbnail, carapace iridescent with oil-slick greens and coppers, legs fringed with hairs that trap a silvery film of air so they can skate on pools that would scald a hand. They feed on mineral scum and drowned flies. When they lift, they are a scatter of green sparks in the steam. The crustaceans are pale, blind, no bigger than a finger joint, living in the warm dark under pipe flanges; they taste the condensation with feathery mouthparts and scuttle sideways when a boot comes near. The flies are clouds in the vapour, black and persistent, breeding in the warm puddles, their larvae a red seam in the water. The attendants do not exterminate them. They read them. When the flies thicken, a seam is weeping more. When the beetles vanish from a pool, the pool has cooled. When the crustaceans swarm out from under a tank, something has shifted in the dark.

And then there is the largest inhabitant, which is the place itself. The complex breathes. It sweats. It has a heartbeat in the main engine and a metabolism in the vents. The attendants are not its brain. They are closer to its gut flora, or its groomers, or the small fish that clean a larger fish’s gills. It would run without them for a while—a week, a month, a season of no seasons—but it would run dirtier, and the sounds would drift, and no one would be there to say so. They are necessary to the listening, not to the running. That is the bargain of the basin: the machine gives heat and water and a stable world; the people give attention. Nothing else changes. Nothing else is supposed to.
