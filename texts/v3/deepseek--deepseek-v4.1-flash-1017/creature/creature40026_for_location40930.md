The first thing you notice about a Nereid is how little there is to notice. They are not small people, exactly, but they are *reduced* — pared down by the climate to a functional minimum. Hair is kept short or bound back; the damp makes anything loose a liability. Clothing is close-fitting and layered, fungal fibre in the city's narrow palette of greys and blacks and that particular blue that comes from the north-facing lichen. Everything they carry is either on their body or set into a niche in the wall, because anything left out in the open will be wet within the hour and mildewed by morning. They move the way their water moves — in known channels, along familiar lines, with a minimum of splashing.

The body itself has been shaped by the place. High altitude, over generations, brings the cheekbones forward and thins the skin until it wears a permanent flush. The eyes crease in a particular way — not from smiling, though they do smile, but from squinting into grey light that has no shadows and therefore no depth. The hands are the most distinctive thing about them: broad-palmed and sure, the fingertips slightly polished, the nails kept short and filed smooth, because the work of the city is the work of touching wet stone and wet shale-ice and knowing, without looking, what the touch is telling you.

A child in Nerei is taught three things before anything else. How to listen. How to wait. How to walk on wet stone.

The listening comes first, and it is not a metaphor. Infants are held against the chest of whoever is carrying them, close enough to feel the vibration of speech through bone, and they learn early that the important sounds in their world are quiet ones — the tick of a channel changing its note as the load shifts, the hush of a sluice gate going from half to three-quarters, the difference between the sigh of a condensation surface working well and the slightly wrong, gurgling note it makes when something has settled where it shouldn't. By the time a Nereid child can walk, they can tell from three rooms away whether the cistern in their district is filling faster or slower than it was an hour ago. By the time they are seven or eight, most of them can name the district they are standing in with their eyes closed, from the sound of its water alone.

The waiting is harder to teach and harder still to explain to outsiders. It is not patience in the ordinary sense. It is a kind of active stillness — the capacity to stand in one place, in the damp, in the cold, and not treat it as an interval to be endured but as the thing itself. The city's children learn it in the condensation gardens, where they are set to watch a single bowl of shale-ice and report when the first drop runs. This can take an hour. The children who fidget are not punished. They are simply left to keep watching, and eventually, because there is nothing else to do and the water is beautiful, they stop fidgeting.

And the walking on wet stone — that is pure survival, taught by bruises.

Maren keeps the fourth-ring north channel. Her family has kept it for nine generations, which means that in some sense Maren is not a person who maintains a channel but a channel that has grown a person around itself. She can hear her water from the central platform, a full ring away, past the vellum and the other channels and the general whisper of the city. She knows its note in cold weather and in colder weather, when the flow slows and the channel speaks lower, more like a held breath. She knows the sound it makes when a fragment of shale-ice has come loose somewhere upstream and is tumbling slowly toward her settling chamber, and she knows — this is the part she cannot explain to anyone, not even to the other keepers — the sound it makes when nothing is wrong but something is about to be, a kind of thinness in the note, as though the water itself were listening for a change it had not yet decided to make.

She has never left the shelf. She has never wanted to. When visitors ask her, politely, whether she does not find the city confining, she says no, and means it, and does not elaborate, because the truth is not something that can be said in the time a visitor has. The truth is that her channel is not confining. Her channel is the largest thing she knows. It runs from the upper catchment to the north cascade, and everything that happens on the shelf passes through it eventually, and she is the one who hears it pass.

---

There are perhaps thirty families like Maren's in Nerei — hereditary keepers of a single cistern, a single catchment, a single run of channel. They are not the majority. Most residents do other work: the fungal gardens, the shale-ice workshops, the archives in their dry vaults, the endless small maintenance that a city grown in layers requires. But the keepers are the ones the city is quietly organised around, the way the cloud is what the city is quietly organised around. A keeper's identity is not a role they play. It is a fact about them, the way the tilt of the shelf is a fact about the shelf.

They intermarry cautiously — not by rule, but by preference, because two keepers understand each other's silences without having to fill them. A keeper married to a keeper can spend an evening in the same room and say four sentences and consider it a good evening. This is not coldness. It is the opposite. It is the same principle as the vellum: the gap that keeps each whole.

---

The city's other permanent residents are less visible and just as present. The mycelium in the walls — the living gasket, the slow self-repairing seal — is not thought of as an infestation or even really as an organism. It is the city's connective tissue. Children are taught not to scrape it from the joints, the way children elsewhere are taught not to pick at scabs. It glows, faintly, blue-grey, in the deep places where no lamp reaches, and the residents find this reassuring rather than eerie: a wall that glows is a wall that is still alive, still working, still holding tight against the cold.

The custodial swarms move through the channels like a second water, invisible except when you know to look. They are tiny, engineered, tireless. A channel whose swarm has died will begin to silt within a season, and the keeper will know, from the note, and will have to send for a new culture from the bio-works in the lower rings. Maren has done this twice in her life. Both times she felt, absurdly, as though she were accepting a transplant.

The rock-mice come out at dusk. They are dark and hairy and unafraid, and the children feed them — against the rules, because the mice are meant to be kept from the food stores, but the feeding is one of those small tolerated transgressions that hold a community together. The cloud-skim roost on the upper cliffs and chatter at dawn, a distant noise that the residents hear the way people elsewhere hear traffic: as a thing that is there, and that would be noticed only if it stopped.

---

When a Nereid dies, they are carried to the north edge. There is no soil to bury them in and no fuel to burn them, and so the city does what the city does with everything it has finished using: it gives them to the water. The body goes over the lip of the cascade and falls into the cloud, and the cloud receives it and carries it up again, and in time — the residents do not know how much time, and do not need to — the water comes back, condensed on the cold stone, clean and cold and faintly alkaline. This is not a metaphor to them. It is a cycle they can taste. They find it comforting in a way that a visitor might find alarming, and they do not apologise for it.

The funeral itself is the maintenance of a gap. The mourners stand at a prescribed distance from the edge and from each other — a vellum of the living, kept clear — and no one speaks. There is no eulogy. There is the sound of the cascade, which is the sound of all the city's water leaving at once, and there is the slow grey breathing of the cloud, and after a while the mourners turn and walk back toward the centre, leaving the space where the dead person stood, as they leave all spaces, held and clear.

---

They go to the edge for other reasons, too. Not just funerals. People go alone, usually, at dusk, when the cloud column slows and the city's quiet deepens toward something that is not quite silence. They stand where there is no railing, where the shale-ice simply stops and the water runs over the raw stone and falls away, and they look into the white-grey nothing that is not nothing. They get wet. They stay longer than they intended. And then they turn around, because they are people of Nerei, and the city is a city of gaps, and the edge is not a place to live — only a place to stand for a while and remember that the world continues past the last engineered surface, indifferent and beautiful and very old.

On the way back, Maren always pauses at the top of the fourth-ring north channel. She crouches. She puts her fingertips in the water — brief, habitual, the way another person might touch a doorframe on the way into a room — and she listens to her channel, and it tells her something, always, and she carries that something back with her into the quiet.
