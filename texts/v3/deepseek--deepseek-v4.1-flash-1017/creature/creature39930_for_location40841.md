No one lives in the Rain-Cup. That is the first true thing about its population. The cup itself is a held breath, and held breaths do not house anybody. What lives there lives at the margins, in the thin zones where the place is neither fully water nor fully stone, and it lives by refusing to be interesting.

The nearest thing to a resident is the pale animal in the shallows under the mineral skins. You will not see it at first. It is no longer than a fingernail, colourless except for a faint milkiness where the gut shows through, and it has no eyes. Eyes would be a waste under a ceiling of sinter. It does not swim so much as ooze along the stone, a quarter inch at a time, flattened to the rock, grazing the faint microbial scurf that gives the new crusts their first colour. Its world is a wet gallery between the basin floor and the crust forming over it—a room that thickens by a film each day. When the mineral skin closes over it, it does not panic. It slows. It waits. It lets the cold and the chemistry do what they will and then goes on being a fingernail's worth of patient life. There are perhaps dozens in the whole basin. They leave no grand sign: a few polished tracks, a few tiny eggs fixed like amber beads in cracks, a faint disturbance in the film. They are the only mouths in the water, and they are not interested in you.

At the overflow lip, where the spray keeps things wet but not drowned, the moss holds on. It is not soft moss. It is hard, dark, wind-combed, a doormat woven out of survival. Each cushion is a small city of tiny plants holding water like a sponge, frozen and thawed and soaked again, and it is the greenest thing for a long way in any direction. With it, on the stones that stick out of the water, are the lichens: orange, grey, the pale green of new copper. They are slow partnerships, fungus and alga eating light and stone dust, and they are the place's first skin. They are not decoration. They are the only soil-making going on.

The shrub is the largest plant and the loneliest. It has rooted in a crack on the leeward side and grown sideways, because upward is not survivable. Its wood is dense and its leaves are small and leathery, and it is not a tree so much as a decision—a decision made once, in a crack, and kept. It flowers rarely, if it flowers at all. It is the biggest thing that lives there, and it is still smaller than a person.

The bird is not a resident. Once or twice a day it comes over the saddle, drops into the lee of the cairn, shakes itself, and is gone. It does not nest. It uses the place as a windbreak, a place to pause between one better place and another. It is a commuter.

The people who left the posts and the cairn and the iron staples did not live there either. They measured something and left the measuring behind. Their marks are not habitation; they are handwriting.

And beneath all of it there is the grinding—the low ongoing work under the floor. If something lives in that sound, it lives at the scale of the basin and the pace of mineral deposition, and it has no body you could point to. It may be the mountain adding itself, film by film, to the bottom of its own cup. It may be a fever in the stone. Either way, it is not a neighbour to the moss or the pale grazers or the sideways shrub. It is the condition they live in.

So the Rain-Cup has no keeper. It has a crew of edge-specialists: a handful of eyeless, slow, mineral-skimmed animals; hard cushions of moss; crusts of lichen; one sideways shrub; one bird that only visits. They live in the loudest, coldest, newest place on the ridge, and they live there the way people live on a frontier—not because it is kind, but because the margins are where there is room.
