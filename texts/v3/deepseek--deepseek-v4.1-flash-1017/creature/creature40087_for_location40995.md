The one who lives here is a keeper, though no one has ever called her that to her face. She is somewhere past middle age, small and wiry, with the kind of stillness that comes from years of moving carefully between machines. Her hair is cropped short and always slightly damp. Her hands are broad across the knuckles, the nails cut close, the skin between thumb and forefinger permanently stained the gray-green of old pipe insulation. She wears a canvas jacket that has absorbed so much of the room’s moisture that it never quite dries, and a single earmuff pushed up over one ear—the other ear stopped hearing properly a long time ago, and she has made a kind of peace with the asymmetry.

She does not live in the chamber so much as occupy its intervals. There is a niche behind the filtration cylinder, a space barely wider than a closet, where a hammock is slung between two brackets and a tin box holds tea, a spoon, a spare bulb, and a book whose pages have swollen and stuck together. She sleeps in shifts. The turbine’s pitch tells her when the lower tank is near full; the float valve’s clunk is her clock. She wakes before it shudders open, lies still for a moment listening to the water climb, then swings out of the hammock and goes to check the grate.

Her days are a loop of small, exact attentions. She wipes the sweating walls with a rag that is always wet. She tightens a flange with a wrench she keeps hooked to her belt. She stands before the exposed console with a screwdriver in her teeth, tracing a fault by touch and smell. She talks to the fern in the floor crack—not affectionately, exactly, but as one might address a colleague who has stayed late. When the moss spreads too far toward the drainage channel, she scrapes it back with her thumbnail, then leaves it alone again. She eats cold food standing up, because the chamber has no table and because sitting feels like a waste of the noise.

She is not lonely in the way an outsider would understand it. The room is too loud for loneliness; the roar fills every space where a thought might otherwise turn inward. She has learned to think in its rhythms, to let the piston’s forward-and-back mark the length of a sentence. When the steam vents, she pauses. When the turbine winds up, she raises her voice without noticing. She has a habit of humming under her breath—a low, tuneless sound—but only in the rare moments when the machinery drops to a single note, as if she is trying to fill the gap before the room can.

She knows the place by its failures. A drip from the upper reservoir that means a gasket is going. A green LED flickering where it should be steady. The particular shriek of a bearing that needs grease. She carries the room’s health in her body: a tightness behind the eyes when the pressure runs high, a looseness in the shoulders when the float valve releases. She has never once thought of herself as living at the top of a tower, in a hidden heart of steel. She thinks of herself as staying until the next cycle, and then the next, and the water must move, and the pressure must hold, and the noise must continue.

Sometimes, in the dim sodium light, with the mist settling on her face and the fern trembling in the vent’s draft, she stands very still and listens to the roar until it becomes something like silence. Then she picks up her rag and goes back to work.
