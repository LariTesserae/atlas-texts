The one who lives there is not the moss, though the moss is its bedding. It is not the Plumb-bird, though the Plumb-bird is its bell-ringer. The true resident is the thing the old maps have no name for and the lizards avoid without knowing why: the **Cistern Keeper**, a soft-bodied colonial organism that lies under the garden like a second floor.

You would not see it at first. It is the color of wet concrete and jade shadow, and it has learned the trick of looking like terrain. Its body is not a single animal so much as a network—flat, translucent runners and fleshy lobes and wet sheets of tissue threaded through the moss, pressed against the warm concrete, pooled in the shallow channels where the water barely moves. In places it is thin enough to mistake for a film of algae. In others it swells into soft, root-colored knots the size of a sleeping animal, and these knots are its ganglia, its stomachs, its slow, patient organs. It has no bones. It has no eyes. It has no mouth worth the name; it feeds by absorption, drawing dissolved minerals and warm water through a skin that is always wet.

It is warm to the touch. That is the first thing you would notice if you knelt and put your palm to the moss: a heat that is not the heat of the air but the heat of a body. The Keeper metabolizes slowly, so slowly that its warmth seems geological, a low fever in the floor. It breathes through the fern roots and the moss, and its exhalations are what keep the dome so humid. It does not stir the air—there is no wind here—but it thickens the air, makes it heavy, makes every surface glisten. When the drips hesitate at the tips of the ferns, it is because the Keeper has inhaled, just slightly, and the pressure in the chamber has changed by a fraction too small to measure and too large to ignore.

It grows its own bells. Through the concrete it pushes small, hollow mineral nodes—calcified caps, pale and smooth, each one a sensory concretion. These are the “hollow mineral nodes” the Plumb-bird strikes. The bird is not tapping random stone. It is tapping the Keeper’s exposed nerves. Each *plip* travels through the concrete and into the soft body beneath, and the Keeper feels it the way you might feel a fingertip pressed to a drum. The sound has no echo because the Keeper absorbs it. The bird is its clock, its hand, its way of measuring the dome. When the Plumb-bird taps, the Keeper knows where the air is thickest, where a fern has grown too far, where a lizard has crossed a ridge, where a drip is about to fall.

The Keeper tends the garden in the way a blind gardener tends a greenhouse: by touch, by warmth, by subtraction. It does not plant. It prunes. It sends up a thin, enzyme-wet film to soften the runners of moss that would choke a channel; it swallows the surplus spores before they can root; it opens a channel by relaxing a lobe and closes another by swelling. It eats the excess so that the whole thing can stay exactly as it is. That is its talent and its curse: it holds the Sunken Garden in equilibrium. It has been doing this for so long that the ferns no longer spread. They simply remain. The water no longer carves. It simply stays.

It is not lonely, because it is not singular. It has the Plumb-bird, which it shelters behind curtains of fern and feeds by leaving insects stunned in the moss. It has the lizards, whose clicks it feels through the floor. It has the tiny translucent flies, which it does not eat but tolerates, because their dancing in the light-shafts tells it something about the humidity. It has a favorite pillar—the oldest one, near the center, where its central ganglion lies coiled like a pale root—and it has a favorite note, the one the Plumb-bird strikes at the hottest part of the day, when the dome is brightest and the air is thickest.

If you stood still long enough, you would feel it: a slow, warm attention under the moss, a vast soft entity listening. The *plip* would come again. It is not a bird calling to another bird. It is a small creature striking a mineral node that belongs to a much larger creature, and the larger creature is answering in the only way it can—by holding the water still, by keeping the ferns from spreading, by letting the light pulse just slower than a heartbeat. The Keeper lives there the way a thought lives in a mind: everywhere at once, and nowhere you can point to.
