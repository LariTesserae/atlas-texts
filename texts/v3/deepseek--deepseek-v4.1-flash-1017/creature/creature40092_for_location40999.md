They are small people, or something close enough to people that the difference rarely matters until you look closely. Their bodies are compact, low to the ground, built for ducking under low stone lintels and moving sure-footed over rubble. Their skin is pale in the way things are pale when they have never known sun—not white, exactly, but a soft grey-pink, like the underside of a mushroom, or clay that has been worked too long. Their eyes are large and dark and patient, and in the firelight they catch the flame and hold it a moment too long, as if they are listening with their gaze.

Their hands are their most articulate feature. The fingers are long and the palms broad, the nails worn flat, the knuckles thickened from stonework. They touch everything. They touch the walls when they walk past, not for balance but for information—feeling for damp, for new cracks, for the almost imperceptible shift of a slab that has begun to settle. They touch each other in passing, a hand to a shoulder, a knuckle tapped against a forearm. Their language is full of soft clicks and sibilants, and some of those clicks are words and some are simply the sound of being near someone in the dark.

They wear layers: woven fiber, cured hide, strips of cloth wrapped and rewrapped against the damp. Nothing is ever fully dry. They smell of smoke and wet earth and the faint mineral sourness of groundwater. Their hair is sparse and dark, cropped close or bound back with sinew. Children are carried in slings against the chest, where the warmth is, and they learn early to be quiet—not because they are punished for noise, but because quiet is how you hear the stone.

They are always busy, but it is not the busyness of panic. It is the busyness of a household that knows exactly how much work it takes to keep a roof from leaking. Water must be read and redirected. Cisterns must be cleared of grit. The fungi terraces must be tended, the wet clay coaxed and patted and watched. Fires must be fed small and sheltered, because fire is precious here and careless fire is a rival for the air. The people move through their crowded city with a precision that comes from knowing where every loose stone is, every slick patch, every low beam that will crack a skull if you forget.

They are builders, but not in the way that outsiders mean. They do not raise monuments. They make joints. A wall is not finished; it is simply at a stage where it can hold a roof for now. A stair is a negotiation with a slope. A house is an accretion—one room grown against another, sharing walls, sharing heat, sharing the same trickle of seepage that has been diverted three times and will need diverting again. They build with stone against stone, with clay and patience, with bone tools and sinew lashings and hands that have done this work for a very long time. When a crack opens, they do not curse it. They study it. They ask what it wants to become, and then they persuade it, gently, to become a doorway instead.

They have no single word for themselves that is spoken aloud. The low tongue-click that means *home* also means *the digging*, and when they make it they often touch the nearest wall. Names among them are small private things—a breath, a click, a rhythm tapped on stone. You learn a person’s name by the way they knock on a lintel before entering, by the particular shuffle of their feet on scree. In the dark, they know one another by sound.

They keep fires in shallow stone bowls, sheltered under ledges, fed with dry fungus fiber and the fat of blind fish and whatever else will burn slowly. Around these fires they eat and mend and sleep in nests of hide raised on ledges away from the wet. The elderly sit closest to the warmth. The oldest of them become listeners—men and women who spend their days pressed against the walls, palms flat, ears close to the stone, feeling for the slow tensions that run through the cavern. They are the ones who decide when a gallery must be shored up, when a fissure must be packed with clay, when a chamber must be sealed.

And they do seal chambers. There are places in the hive where the stone has been closed with fitted rubble and mud and lashings, where the cracks have been stopped with resin and clay and the openings left as blank as a held breath. No one speaks of what is behind them. The seal-keepers do their work quietly and do not answer questions. Children are taught not to press their ears to those walls. Sometimes, in the deep quiet, someone will hear a sound through the stone—a slow scrape, a settling, a tap that is almost regular—and will move away without mentioning it. The people do not say that something is waiting. They do not have to. The silence in those galleries is not the silence of emptiness, and they have lived beside it long enough to know the difference.

They are not a sad people. They are careful. They laugh in low voices, they sing in clicks, they tell stories about the time the lower cistern flooded and the time a ladder broke and someone fell into the fungus terraces and came up grey to the eyebrows. They are proud of their city in the way that people are proud of a thing that has never been easy. They know that the earth is not finished. They know that nothing here will hold still forever. So they build for repair, not for permanence. They keep their fires small. They keep their voices low. They keep their hands on the stone, always listening for what it is becoming.
