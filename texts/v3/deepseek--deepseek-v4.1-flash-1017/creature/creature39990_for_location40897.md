The people here are low, broad, and unhurried. They do not so much walk across the basin as flow over it: a gait of soft feet and bent knees, splayed toes gripping the moss, each step accompanied by a wet, patient sound. Their skin is the color of wet bark and mushroom caps, mottled grey-green and always beaded with moisture. It is not scaly and not quite furred; it has the cool, elastic give of the underside of a leaf. They have wide faces with heavy lids, nostril slits that can close against the damp, and mouths that are quick to croak and slow to shout. Their hands are the center of them—long-fingered, soft-padded, webbed at the base, without nails. They can press a vine into a new curve and hold it there for an hour without tiring.

They wear almost nothing. Sometimes a cape of cured leaf, sometimes a belt of fungal leather, sometimes a smear of waxy sap across the shoulders to help the condensation run off. They smell of crushed stems, wet stone, and the sweet rot of the fungi they harvest. Their voices are low and resonant, closer to the croak of the hidden amphibian than to human speech, and they use them sparingly. Much of what passes between them is touch: a hand on a shoulder, a palm pressed to a vine, a long silence shared while water drips from the roof. They are quiet people in a quiet place, and they do not mistake quiet for emptiness.

They are born in the seeps. The young begin as soft, gill-frilled things in nursery pools lined with moss, breathing the warm water and learning the taste of it. When they climb out, their gill slits close and their skin thickens, but they never lose the damp. A child is not considered grown until they have guided a vine through its first full arch—not cut it, not forced it, but asked it, over months, to grow where the household needs a wall. By then they know the difference between a seep that is breathing and one that is about to open, between a fungus that feeds and one that dreams.

Their homes are living things, and so are their families. Each dome of woven vine is a lineage: a grandmother’s pruning, a mother’s patience, a child’s first careful splice. The walls are layers of waxy leaves that shed the wet air and hold in the faint, cool light of the fleshy flowers kept in niches. The floors are bowls of moss. Small pools are set into the corners for drinking, for larval kin, for the slug-things that graze the fungi in the shadow of the walls. When a room dies—when the vine weakens or the leaves rot through—they do not repair it with stone. They let it go soft, cut it back, and coax a new room from the same root. A house is never finished. It is only well tended.

They are gardeners and herders in the same breath. They harvest the pale fungi, ferment them in seep-water, trade cuttings and spores along the winding mud paths. They keep no flocks in the open; instead they encourage colonies of grazers near the homes, culling a few, leaving the rest to keep the fungus healthy. Their tools are bone and hardened wood, but mostly they use their hands. They do not burn, because nothing here burns well; they mold, ferment, rot, and make use of all three. Their dead are laid in a seep with vine cuttings, and the household tends that vine for generations. Memory is held in the shape of a roof, the curve of a path, the particular croak a family uses at dusk.

They are not the only minds in the basin, and they know it. The large amphibian in the seep is listened to; its low call is a calendar. The six-legged gliders are left to their leaf-pools. The gnats are food for something else. The vines themselves may be slow and patient intelligences, remembering every pruning, every hand that has pressed them into an arch. The people do not conquer the land. They negotiate with it. When a new spring bubbles up through a path, they reroute the path. When the ground trembles and the mud shifts, they wait. When the great fleshy flowers bloom, they mark the season by light rather than by sun.

And they are not finished either. Their children still come out of the water with gills. Their houses still change shape as the vines learn them. Their language still grows new words for new seeps. They are a people caught in the act of becoming, a knot of warm, damp life in a nursery of a landscape, learning slowly—by touch, by patience, by the sound of water—what they are meant to be.
