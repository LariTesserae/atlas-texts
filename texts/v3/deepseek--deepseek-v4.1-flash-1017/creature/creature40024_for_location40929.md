What lives there is not one thing. It is a vast, single organism and a scattered people who have learned to be its guests — and the distinction between those two is not as clean as you might want.

The moss lives there first. It is the floor and the ceiling, the pillars and the curtains, the light and the weather. It is one body spread through miles of stone, or many bodies so interwoven that the word *many* has stopped meaning anything. It lives by eating its own dead, by holding water in its tissues, by generating light that is not for seeing but for signaling, for remembering, for keeping the far parts of itself in conversation. It is always working. If you put your palm against it, you feel a warm-cold pulse that is not a heartbeat but is not nothing. It is a storm in slow motion, a churn of chemistry and electricity that has been going on for longer than the rock above has known rain. It does not want anything from you. It is not hostile. It is simply total.

The people are harder to describe because they are few and because they do not live the way people live above.

They are small and broad and heavy-boned, with skin the color of wet ash and a faint green cast at the joints where the damp has worked itself in. Their eyes are large and dark, and when they listen, the eyes go flat and distant, as if they are watching something behind the light. Their hands are their most alive feature: wide, knuckled, the nails thick and blunt, the palms hard as horn. They wear layered wraps of dried moss-fiber, cured in mineral water until it is soft and sour-smelling and waterproof, and they carry no lamps. They do not need them. They carry stones instead.

They are nomads, but not in the way of people who follow herds. They follow the bright patches. When the bioluminescence migrates through the moss — when the pale gold shifts to ochre, when a blue-white node brightens and begins its slow crawl through the thallus — the people move with it, or away from it, depending on what the pattern says. They read the light the way you read a sky. A certain flicker means a chamber is about to shed a curtain of growth. A certain slow pulse means water is pooling somewhere below. They have a word for the moment a pattern changes and a place must be abandoned, but it is not a spoken word. It is a pressure of two fingers against a wrist.

They live in the spaces the moss leaves. Their shelters are woven from dried stalks and bound with cordage made from the plant’s own fibers — small, low, temporary things, built in the lee of pillars or under the arches where the growth is thin. They do not clear the moss away. They find the hollows it has already made and they settle into them, and when the moss begins to close in again, they move. The abandoned villages you find are not ruins in the ordinary sense. They are places the moss has been allowed to reclaim. The paths between the shelters stay clear because the moss remembers footsteps and does not grow where it has been walked. The people know this. They keep the paths inside the memory of the plant.

They eat what the moss can spare: the pale new growth, which is tender and faintly sweet; a sour paste made from softened fiber; the blind, translucent things that live in the cold pools, no longer than a finger, tasting of iron and stone. They do not eat the bright nodes. That would be like eating a word. They drink from the drip-pools and from the water that collects in the cups of old growth, and they leave the dead to the moss, and when one of their own dies, they carry the body into a quiet hollow and let the growth take it. Later, they build a cairn from stones carried a long way. The moss lights the cairn. The light is the grave.

Their cairns are their writing. A cairn at the mouth of a passage says *this way is open*. A cairn beside a pool says *the water is sweet here*. A cairn in the middle of a vast empty floor says *we stopped because the light was changing and we did not know which way to go*. Some cairns are older than any story they can tell. Some are furred with moss and nearly absorbed. Some are clean and new. The people build them slowly, with great care, balancing the stones the way you balance a sentence. They do not carve marks into the stone. They do not need to. The shape is the mark.

And deeper, in the root-chambers where the light is white and slow and the cold is absolute, there are the Rememberers. They are not a separate species. They are the old ones, or the ones who chose to stay. They sit in the hollows between the columnar growths, thin as cord, their skin furred with pale moss, their pulses slowed to match the long white light. They do not speak. They do not move much. They are tended by the younger ones, who bring them water and sit with them and listen. The Rememberers answer in light. A flicker here, a sustained glow there, a pattern that traces the same route through the moss over and over. They are the moss’s older memory made local. They remember the deepest cairn, the hill of stones in the final chamber, and they remember why it was built, and they will not say.

So the Hollow Garden is inhabited by a storm that thinks in light and by a people who have made themselves quiet enough to live inside it. They are not masters of the place. They are not its pets. They are the soft, deliberate, tool-using edge of a world that mostly does not need tools. They build cairns and weave shelters and follow the bright patches, and when they die the moss takes them, and the light goes on tracing its slow, patient, half-legible sentences through the dark.
