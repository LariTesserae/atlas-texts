What lives there is patient enough to be mistaken for landscape.

It is a root-mind, a colonial organism whose body is the cavern’s vegetation. Not one tree, not one vine, but a single connected appetite spread across miles of stone: cable-thick roots that double as arteries, moss that acts as a damp and hungry skin, shelf-fungi that breathe out amber light and take in rust. It has no face. It does not need one. Its senses are spread through every wet fiber and questing tip—pressure, moisture, mineral gradients, the vibration of the old engines grinding somewhere below. When you feel that deep thrum through the floor, you are feeling its pulse borrowed from a machine it has already half-swallowed.

It thinks in slow tides. A thought might take a season to move from one end of it to the other. It remembers the factory not as a history but as a warmth, a taste of iron and oil, a rhythmic intrusion it grew toward. It does not hate the machinery. Hate would be too quick. It simply opens the girders with root-tips the way a stomach opens meat, and it drinks the warm mineral water from the ruptured pipe, and it lets the last turning engine beat inside its root-nest like a second heart. That engine is the metronome of the whole place. The pods swell on its beat. The breathing sacs contract. The pale tendrils push another inch through a gear’s teeth.

At the center—if such a thing can be said to have a center—there is a chamber knotted around the oldest machine. The root-mind has grown through it, not to kill it but to keep it. Iron vertebrae. Sap veins. A marriage of rust and lignin. The engine turns, and the organism answers: a shiver of spores, a gland opening, a slow redoubling of growth. The two have become one inhabitant with two histories. The factory built the engine; the garden made it a pacemaker.

The smaller lives are there too, but they are brief flickers in the root-mind’s long dream. Pale many-legged things scuttle along the vines, their shells the color of wet bone. Blind moths rest on the glowing pods, drinking nectar and dusting themselves with spores. Iridescent flies hover over the warm pools in little clouds, their wings throwing back what little light there is. Glassy worms coil in the shallows. They live in the folds and sluices of the great organism, feeding on its leaks and its sweetness, and their whole lives fit between two beats of the engine-heart. They are not masters here. They are fleas on a whale.

And perhaps, somewhere in the rusted dark, a last maintenance thing still walks its old rounds—a squat automaton furred with moss, following a route through a factory floor that no longer exists, oiling hinges that have become roots. If it lives, it is not in charge. It is another organ now, another slow servant of the pulse.

So the inhabitant is not a monster in the thicket. It is the thicket. It is the green patience that has decided the machinery is fertilizer, and the cold damp is a womb, and the grinding roar of growth is simply the sound of its body continuing.
