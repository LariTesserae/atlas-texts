What lives in the Kiln is not a people. It is a maintenance.

The first fact is the small ones. They are the length of a finger joint, pale as the inside of a shell, soft-bodied under a thin casing that is not quite shell and not quite skin. They have too many legs for their size, and the legs are not for walking far. They are for holding on. They live in the mortar lines, in the seams between blocks, in the hairline cracks that would otherwise close. A single block might carry a hundred of them, pressed into the joints like seeds in a cracked loaf. They have no eyes. There is nothing here to see, and nothing in them has ever asked for eyes.

Their world is not the mound. Their world is the seam: its width, its temperature, its taste. They move by touch and by chemistry, following the mortar where it is soft, where it is old, where it has been repaired before. They rasp at it with mouthparts like tiny files. They swallow the grit. They digest what can be digested out of crushed shell and ash and old cement, and they excrete the rest as a paste very close to what they ate, only slightly different in color. Then they pack it back into the crack. They do this not because they are keeping the building. They do it because the seam is where the food is, and the food is where the seam is open, and the seam stays open because they keep repairing it. It is a loop with no mind in it at all. It has simply been going on long enough to look like devotion.

They are also the light. Their bodies give off a faint milkiness, so weak it does not illuminate color or detail, only edges. The Kiln is lit by the things that are eating it. If you stand still, you can see the walls by the glow of the mouths at work. It is not a hospitable light. It is the light of digestion.

They do not swarm, exactly. They are simply dense. They do not communicate; they do not need to. When one is eaten, another takes the seam. When a seam floods with silt, they work around it. When a block shifts by a fraction, they follow the new crack. They are not builders. They are grazers on a mineral crop, and the crop is the building, and the building is the crop. The mound has the shape it has because these small mouths have held every joint in place for longer than anyone could say.

The larger things live in the rooms, and they are fewer.

There is the one with the shell. You hear it before you see it: a slow dragging, a scrape of something hard over stone, a sound like a chair being moved in another room. It is perhaps as long as your forearm, maybe longer, with a low coiled shell that has been worn smooth and blotched with mortar. It moves through the lower chambers and the wider corridors, grazing the seams. It eats the small ones. It does not hunt them so much as mow them, scraping whole stretches of wall clean with a rasping tongue and leaving bare stone behind. The small ones return to the bare places within days or weeks, and the seam opens again, and the dragging shell comes back. It cannot turn quickly. It follows walls. It has no eyes either, only a soft sensitive front end that reads the water for pressure and warmth and the nearness of stone.

There is the folder. It lives in the pinch-corridors, the ones you must turn sideways to enter. It is flat and jointed and soft, longer than it is wide, and it can close itself up like a pocketknife or a folded leaf. When it is closed, it looks like a shadow in the seam, like a wedge of darker stone. When it is open, it is a mouth with legs. It does not chase. It waits in the narrow places where anything larger must come close, and when something touches it, it folds. It eats the leaf-fish, mostly, and sometimes a small one that has wandered out of the mortar. It does not move when you pass. It has learned, in whatever way a thing like that learns, that stillness is cheaper than flight.

The leaf-fish are the only things here that swim. They are thin as a leaf and the color of the water, and they turn away from your warmth without haste, as if warmth were a minor inconvenience. They drift through the rooms in ones and twos, grazing on the eggs of the small ones, on shed casing, on whatever soft matter settles out of the still water. They have no eyes and no hurry. They navigate by pressure and by the faint temperature of stone. When they die, they settle into a low corner and are eaten, and the small ones repair the seam around the stain.

And in the largest chamber there is the soft mass. It covers part of the wall and part of the floor. It pulses slowly, a broad, wet, wrinkled sheet of something that is either one animal or a colony of animals that have stopped disagreeing. It does not react to you. It does not react to much at all. It moves water through itself in long, slow waves, taking what dissolves, returning what does not. It may be the oldest living thing in the Kiln. It may have been there since the rooms were still rooms. It has no face and no front and no need to know which way is up. It is a current that has learned to stay in one place.

These are the inhabitants: the mouths in the seams, the dragging shell, the folded trap, the leaf-fish, the pulsing sheet. Nothing here is curious. Nothing here is hostile. They are not waiting for anything. They have already arrived.

The whole of it is an economy with no money and no memory. The small ones eat the mortar and make more mortar. The shelled one eats the small ones. The folder eats what comes too close. The fish eat the scraps. The soft mass eats what dissolves. Everything that dies settles into a low room and is taken apart and packed into a crack. Nothing grows in the way a garden grows. Things only persist, replace themselves at the rate they are eaten, and hold the shape they are in.

If you stay too long, you will begin to feel that you are part of the maintenance too—another warm body in a room, settling slowly, being repaired around. The inhabitants will not notice. They have no eyes for you. They have no need to speak. The only sound is the rasp of small mouths on stone, like sand poured very slowly, which is the sound of the whole hill keeping itself exactly as it is.
