What lives in the Kettle is not an animal. That is the first thing to understand. The absence of birds, insects, spiders — the clean, humming emptiness of the place — is not emptiness. It is housekeeping. The tenant has no use for quick things.

It lives under the pool, and through the cracked floor beneath the basin, and in the wet strata that cup the whole place. It is a slow mineral body, mostly indistinguishable from hot water and stone. If you could see it whole, it might look like a lobed bladder of precipitated ochre and grey stone, riddled with chambers, but there is no clean edge to it. Its boundary is a temperature, a chemistry, a place where water is warm enough and mineral enough to be part of it. It is larger than the pool and smaller than the basin, and it shifts its extent by fractions of an inch over years.

It has no face. It does not need one. The upwelling in the pool is its attention. When it turns over — that slow roll from the bottom, that heavy bloop at the surface — it is not boiling. It is breathing. The steam is its exhalation, rising in ropes, folding back on itself, condensing on the basin walls, trickling down, returning. It has been exhaling like that for a long time. The copper moss grows in a ring three paces out from the water because that is the edge of its breath, the zone where its heat and minerals become tolerable to something else. The pale jointed reeds on the walls are not its crop. They are just things that found the seams where it leaks.

It eats stone. Not quickly. It takes the grey and ochre layers into itself at the cracks, dissolves what it can, precipitates what it cannot, and widens the cracks by a hair’s breadth each year. The banded strata are its slow diary. It has been writing in them since before the bricks were laid. It does not remember in images. It remembers in flow: a season of rain diluting the pool, a winter when the underground heat ran higher, a decade when the water table dropped and the moss ring pulled inward.

The ruins are not its work. The squat brick building, the seized iron frame, the row of corroded pipes on their cracked stanchions — people built those. They knew what was here, or they found it, and they tried to draw from it. The pipes were straws. The pump was a mouth not its own. It did not resist. It simply outlasted them. Now it wears the iron frame like a splinter, coating it slowly with mineral scale, sending thin grey vines of itself along the metal and down into the water. The pipes still carry its hot water up and over the rim, though the joints have pulled apart and most of it leaks back into the ground. It sends water through them out of habit, or because the path is easy, or because a body that old does not abandon a channel once it has learned it.

It notices visitors the way a warm bath notices a cool hand. You are a difference in temperature, a pressure on the wet ground, a disturbance in the water table. It may probe at you with a thread of hotter water, not hostile, just curious in the way a tongue is curious. It may not. You are brief. It has no name for you, and no need to give you one.

The faint mechanical hum you hear — the thrum under the sound of boiling — is probably its circulation, water moving through buried channels it has worn for centuries. Or it is the pipes. Or it is something else running underground, older than the pipes, older than the bricks, still working at its own pace. The tenant does not hurry. It is a working thing. It turns over, exhales, dissolves a little more stone, widens a crack by a fraction, and waits for the water to come back down.
