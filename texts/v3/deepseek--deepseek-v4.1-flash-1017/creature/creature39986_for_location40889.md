You think you are alone for a long time, and you are wrong in a way that will not let go of you.

The shape is in the hollow of the room, in the soft green nothing between the fern and the valve, and it has the patience of the pipework. At first it is only an interruption — a darkness in the moss, a place where the rain falls onto something and breaks. Then it is a coat, or what a coat becomes: canvas gone the color of the walls, felted, shoulderless. Then it is a person sitting.

She is on a ledge where a pipe dives into the floor, in the crook the pipe makes, and the iron there is hollowed to her. Her back is against the mossed stone. Her hands are in her lap, open, palms up, and the condensation has pooled in them, overflowed, and pooled again, so that her hands are one more channel of the room, one more place the water goes. Her skin is the color of the pale roots in the corners — not sick, only unfed by the sun. Her hair is roped and dark and wet, and there is moss in it, but not the way a thing is overgrown; the way a thing is worn.

She is not old. She is not young. The room keeps no year, and so neither does she; you look for the marks of time and find only the marks of use. Her feet are bare and spread against the plate the way the moss spreads, the soles gone thick and pale, worn from the outside in until they are nearly beautiful, river stone. You could not tell her age. You could tell her hours: she has been here a great many hours, and they have all been the same hour.

The list of the room's fauna is already in your head from the passage — the snails, the pale thread of a crustacean in the channel. You add her to the list. She is the only thing here that moves, and she moves for two reasons.

The first is the lamps. In their iron cages at either end of the channel they are still lit, which is the thing that surprises you, because it implies hands. Two flames standing steady in the wet. The water never changes and the gauge never changes and the moss grows into the shape it has already chosen, but fire will not settle; fire leans and gutters. So when one begins to fail she stands, and crosses the room, and trims it, and sits again. You watch her do this, once, and the standing takes a long time. Her body has gone economical the way the room is economical: it does only what the flame requires.

The second is the snails. When one strays too near the channel's lip she reaches down and moves it back to the wall, without hurry, and the snail resumes with no acknowledgment. It is a mercy that costs nothing, and it is the only motion in the room that is not the flame's or the water's. You understand, watching, that she is not keeping the snails alive. She is keeping her hand in practice.

On a shelf cut above her seat there is a stack of logbooks swollen into a single brick, the covers fused, the pages gone to gray pulp. One lies open on her knee, open to the same place it must have been open to for a long time. There is a stub of pencil in the crease. Now and then she picks it up and presses it to the pulp, and the pulp takes no mark, or takes the mark and swallows it, and she sets it down. The writing has outlived the record. You want to ask her what the reading is. You already know what the reading is.

The gauge she cleans. Her thumb goes up to its milky face and clears a lens of fog, and the fog comes back, and she does it again, in her own time, over hours. It is the one cycle left in the room: the water never rises, the needle never moves, but the glass fogs and is cleared, fogs and is cleared. She is the gauge's weather. Nothing about her rots, because rot is a change, and nothing here is going anywhere; she smells only of warm iron and wet stone and moss, and the moss smells of nothing at all.

She knows you are here. She has known since your shoulders came through the passage — you are a wrongness in the breathing of the room, a cold draft where there should be none. When she speaks, the sound comes in under the roar, low, in the register where you feel it in your sternum before you hear it. She says a word. It is not a greeting. It might be a reading — the gauge's number, spoken flatly, so often said that it is no longer language. She says it the way you would say your own name to a mirror.

Then she lifts one hand from her lap, palm up, and holds it out into the empty air, and the warm rain falls into it and stays. There is room on the crook of the pipe. The water is blood-warm, the same as her, and you are already wet to the bone; you could not get wetter. She is not asking. Want is a gradient, and the room has none, and neither has she. She is only doing what a person does when another person arrives at the end of the world: she makes room.

You do not take the seat. You crawl back up the passage of dressed stone, your shoulders brushing the glassy corners, and the roar follows you and thins and does not stop. Behind you the sluice holds its one note. Behind you the fog gathers and her thumb clears it, gathers, clears. The lamps burn at their one height, the snails go about their errands, and somewhere past the wall the engine no one has seen makes the water warm and keeps making it warm. She will be there. That is the last certain thing you carry out of the room, and the certainty is the room's, not yours: a number said over and over into a book that has forgotten how to keep a record.

For the rest of your life you will check gauges, in other rooms, and find every needle wrong.
