The people of Hart’s Fold are not settlers so much as a slow growth in the seam between the land and its past. They are small and solid, low to the ground, with wide feet that spread on wet moss and broad hands whose thick nails have been worn into tools for testing stone. Their skin holds the damp like river-stone; their hair is coarse and dark, usually cut short or braided back with split reed and plant-fiber. They dress in layers of pressed leaf, woven stem, and soft seed-down, waterproofed with plant wax. At throat or wrist each person wears a flat, worked stone—an anchor, a personal piece of “settled” matter. Children are given one early; adults carry several and exchange them when the world begins to feel too new.

They are quiet people, but not solemn. Their quiet is practical. Words sometimes hang in the air as colored shapes, so they speak in low, unhurried phrases and let sentences finish before beginning another. They have a grammar of caution: *the path is young* means wait; *the water is remembering* means do not stare; *hold your stone* means reality is thinning. They do not say goodbye easily. They say *settle well*.

Their common sense is a set of small rituals for a world with no fixed rules. A Folder walking a stone channel may stop, press a palm to the rock, and wait until it feels old again. They mark routes with cairns of worked stone—not to remember the way, but to remind the way to remain. They carry polished pebbles that show old valleys when held; these are not curios but compasses of a kind. They work quickly and finish by dusk, because a tool may be a hand-axe in the morning and a simple iron blade by evening. The few iron pieces they own are scavenged, reworked, and never quite trusted to be the same blade twice.

They tend the Lichen Rivers without owning them. The blue-green mats are combed with wooden rakes, the seepage diverted through shallow stone channels, the outer growth harvested so the pulse continues. At dusk the mats brighten, and the Foldfolk work by that light—weaving fiber into mats and baskets, carving the soft ancient stone into bowls and weights, mending the water-channels against the slow creep of moss. Their houses are excavations as often as buildings: low chambers cut into hillsides, capped with turf, entered through stone doorways worn smooth by shoulders. Their streets are water. There are no towers, no monuments, only the persistent low-grade organization of people who have learned to live in the seams of a giant.

They are few. A gathering is a circle in a round house, everyone seated on woven mats over stone benches, speaking until the words settle. Elders are those who have learned to be unremarkable to time—people who can stand in a thin place without being pulled. Children are taught not to chase the colored words, not to pick up image-stones unless they are ready to carry them, and not to clear too much of the fleshy green canopy at once. The plants hold the soil; the soil holds the water; the water holds the town. The dead are returned to the moss with a worked stone in each hand and a channel diverted over them, and their names are spoken less each year until the land has settled around them.

With them live the lichen-rivers, the immense dark-green plants rooted into bedrock, and the past itself, bleeding through the worn present. There are no animals—no birdsong, no rustle of small creatures—so the people are the only quick things, and their silence is a form of attention. They are not masters of this place. They are attendants: carrying stones to keep the seams from opening too wide, speaking softly so the past can pass through without breaking, and living as a low, continuous negotiation between an ancient land and its unsettled memory.
