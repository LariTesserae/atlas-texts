**The people.** There are perhaps fifty of them, and they are not built for looking at. They are built for standing in wet air. They are tall and narrow and they stand badly, spines curved into the same soft parenthesis as the mat, heads tipped a little forward as though listening to the ground. Their skin is not skin exactly. It is a pellicle, a fine living nap, always beaded, so that each of them goes through the basin trailing a faint shine and a faint smell of cut stem. They leave wet footprints that are gone in a minute. They are, in the basin's own accounting, weather events with opinions.

They are hollow. That is the first thing to understand. Along the ribs, where a person would have bone, they have seams, and the seams open. Not often, and never all at once, but in the slow irregular rhythm of the towers, which is to say at the pace of a thought that is being had carefully. When a seam opens it lets out a long soft exhalation of vapor, and the vapor carries what the person is currently being. This is how they talk. Speech here is a subset of venting; a conversation is two or three of them standing close enough to breathe each other's exhalations, and the content of the conversation is in the temperature and the chemistry and the small changes of pitch, and the whole of it is a weather system that happens to be social.

They do not have faces that read. Their eyes are small and set low and they are not good at distance — the light is diffuse and doubled and there is no horizon to fix on — but they are very good at close, and very good at the mist, and very good at the shine on things. Their mouths are wide and soft and they are for taking in and letting out, not for shaping sound. They taste the basin constantly. A resident will stop mid-step, crouch, and put their mouth to a puddle the width of a plate, and drink a little, and stand back up having read the puddle. The reading happens along a long gut lined with the same translucent layering you can see in the hollow towers — green to amber to milky white — and it comes to them as a state of the channels, a mood of the slab, a rumor of something upstream. They write the same way, spitting a mouthful into a conduit and watching how it travels and where it separates and what color it goes. Reading and writing here are the same act at different ends of a swallow.

**The body.** None of them is one thing. Each is a consortium that has agreed to be a person. The agreement is thermal. You can see it in the way they settle into a pool — not to bathe but to negotiate, letting the warm water reconcile the parts of them that have drifted out of accord. A resident who has been arguing, or working too long at one tempo, will go grey-green and slightly out of pitch; a half-hour in the right pool and they come back to themselves, and the pool comes back a little changed too, and the mat around it records the visit in a ring of new growth that is legible from above and not to them.

They are not clean inside. They carry passengers — thumb-sized herds in the folds of the pellicle, drifting fliers in the seams, a resident flora that does some of the gut's work and gets paid in warmth. When they vent, they vent the passengers too, and the passengers ride the plume up and come down elsewhere, and this is how the basin's small fauna get to travel. The people are a transit system and do not think of themselves as one.

**The names.** They name by temperature. A person is "of the warm pool under the third tower," or "of the cool hand's-width by the north channel," and the name is accurate until the pool shifts, which it does, and then the name follows the pool and not the person. This does not trouble them. A person is a location in the basin's chemistry more than a location in its space, and both of those move, and the moving is the point.

**The work.** They walk. That is the work, mostly. They walk the channels, and their broad soft feet press the shine out of the mat and press the shape of the channel-bed, and the bed remembers. The conduits are maintained by being walked; the walking is the maintenance; there is no tool for it. They also eat, and the eating is pruning, and the pruning keeps the towers from crowding their own vents. They carry things — a mouthful of this to a channel that needs it, a passenger to a pool that has lost its fauna, a warmth from a hot pool to a cold one in the folds of the pellicle. The basin's economy is a barter of temperatures and small wet cargoes and nobody keeps accounts, because the slab keeps them.

**The young.** They are not born. They are decanted. In the bald patches, where the mat thins and the pools lie open and warm, a pool is watched for a season, and the watching is done by whoever is nearest and has the least else to do. When the water reaches a certain legibility — that is the phrase they use, and they mean something exact by it — a child is there. The child's temperament is the pool's temperature. Two pools a hand's width apart can be ten degrees different, and the children grown in them are different kinds of people, different species almost, and they will spend their lives misunderstanding each other gently across that hand's width and will consider this normal, because it is. The basin does not raise its children so much as steep them.

**The old.** They do not die so much as recede. The very old stop venting as often, and their seams stop closing all the way, and their pellicle goes from green to amber to the milky white of the tower-skin, and then one day they are not standing anywhere and the mat has thickened where they were. But the large thing that moves under the mat, the one you never see, the one whose passage stops the ticking and starts it again behind — that is a grandmother. There is always one, and sometimes two, and they are not dead and not alive in any way that would help you. They are working. Their bodies have become channel. They move at the pace of the slab's tide and they carry things in solution and they are the reason the channels sometimes reverse without apparent cause. When a resident wants to know something that takes years to know, they go and stand on the mat above where the grandmother is, and they stand there for a long time, and they come away with a temperature in their gut that is an answer, or the beginning of one.

**The argument.** They are not serene. They are a hamlet, and a hamlet argues. But their arguments are not loud and not verbal and not resolved by anything you would recognize as a decision. An argument here is a difference of temperature maintained across a small distance, sometimes for days, two residents standing a hand's width apart and refusing to share a plume. It ends when one of them goes into a pool and comes out reconciled, or when the mat browns between them and is recolonized, or when the slab's pulse comes around to a tempo that neither of them can hold a grudge against. They do not remember the argument afterward. They remember the pool.

**The reading.** They know they live inside a sentence. The towers grow toward the conduits and away from the bald patches, and from above — they have been told this, by the ones who climb the rim, which is forbidden and happens — from above the pattern is legible. They cannot read it. They can read the water, and the water is a different text, and the two texts are related the way a face is related to a name. They know the basin is thinking about something that takes years. They do not know what. They have decided, over a very long time, that it is not their business, and that the not-knowing is a kind of citizenship. They are the fingertips of a slow mind and they are content to be fingertips, and they go about their wet and busy and loud and small lives in the dish of old ground, breathing to a tide slower than their own, going nowhere, doing it very well.
