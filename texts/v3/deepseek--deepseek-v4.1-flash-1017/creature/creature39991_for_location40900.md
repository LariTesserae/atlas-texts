The first mistake is to look for people. There are none. The thing that lives in the Sieve is the Sieve itself—or rather, it is one continuous colonial growth that has made a habitation out of its own body and then settled into it.

It has no face and no center. It is a single wet, pale, many-roomed organism spread through the hollow, and what you take for streets and chambers are the spaces it has left inside itself. Its growing tips are everywhere: at the rims of cisterns, in the plaited gutters, under the woven roofs, in the root-felt floor. Where a wall is packed solid, that is old body pressed tight. Where a roof is woven, that is body taught to hold. Where a pipe of hollow stem runs through the wall, that is body arranged around a column of water. It does not build with parts. It grows organs and then lives in them.

It is not intelligent in the way a person is intelligent. It is intelligent the way a habit is intelligent—by repeating what works. It has learned that cold air pools, that condensation beads on a surface colder than the air, that water runs downhill, that dead growth can be made into lining and floor and eventually into more growth. It has learned these things over a few hundred years, on fresh stone, and it keeps the rules exactly. Nothing is thrown away. Nothing is built that was not grown. Nothing is grown that does not catch water. The whole town is a sieve that lives by being a sieve.

Its life is slow and hydraulic. It feels the cold as a slowing, the wet as a fullness, the weight of its own roofs as a pressure it can answer by thickening a stem. It has no eyes, but the growing tips turn toward moisture and away from dry rot. It has no stomach, but the cisterns are its reservoirs, and the old dead layers under the floor are its midden and its soil and its slow meal. It drinks the weather. It breathes through surfaces that are always wet. It is colder than the air, so the water comes out on it, and it has arranged every frond and blade to shed that water where it wants it. No one tends it. It tends itself.

It is also, very slowly, leaving. At the low end it grows into the marsh, and at the dry rim it dies back, so the whole settlement creeps downhill a handspan a decade. The oldest chambers are behind it now: collapsed, hollow, hung with dry rot and the papery skins of old cisterns. The newest growth is at the wet edge, still sharp, still finding its edges on the young stone. It builds ahead of itself and abandons behind. It is not a town that was founded. It is a town that is migrating through its own lifespan.

The other residents are few and mostly invisible. They are not citizens; they are lodgers, scavengers, gut fauna. Something gnaws spiral burrows inside dead stems. Something sheds a skin in a cistern and leaves. Something makes a warm empty burrow in the root-mat. A slow pale amphibian sits in the standing water with its throat going, unbothered. Pale shelf-fungi grow from the root-walls in the lower murk and give off a weak green-white gleam—not enough to see by, just enough not to walk into a wall. The single low note you hear, three times, and then an hour of dripping, belongs to one of these. You will almost certainly find its leavings instead of its body.

So the inhabitant is a composite thing: one great wet colonial self, no more than a few hundred years old, still sharp on young mountain, still arguing with the cold; and inside it a thin scatter of small pale animals and fungi that live on its waste and its water and its warmth. If you must give the town a pronoun, it is “it.” If you must give it a face, the nearest thing is the amphibian in the cistern, throat going, unbothered. If you must give it a name, the name is the place.
