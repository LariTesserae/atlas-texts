What lives here does not live on the plain so much as in its rhythm. The visible inhabitants are the scuttlers: fist-sized, jointed, plated in the same pale gray as the crust. Their legs are thin as needles and jointed like wet hinges; they move in short, ticking bursts, always with the vent cycles. They have no proper eyes, only rows of pressure-pits that read warmth and steam. They cross your boot without alarm. They scrape mineral film from the crust, eat their own shed shells, molt constantly. The ground is full of their half-dissolved bodies, and the living ones walk over the dead and become them. They are not a population so much as the plain’s ticking, grooming, recycling surface.

The vent-pillars are the plain’s slow organs. Rust-red, soft-bodied, pillar-shaped, they anchor themselves around the vents and never move again. Vapor passes through chambers in their bodies and they whistle—high, tonal, eerily musical. They filter the steam for whatever mineral dust it carries. They pulse. They grow by accretion, old ones ringed like trees that never had a season. When a vent closes, the pillar collapses into a rust stain and the stain becomes crust. Their buds drift as rust-colored spores in the mist, looking for another warm mouth in the ground. They are the choir, and they are also the filters, and they are also the floor.

The colonies are harder to call a creature. They are spills of animate gravel, aggregations of small crawling parts that move with one sluggish purpose. Each part is lentil-sized, jointed, pale, and only loosely committed to the whole. A colony pours over the crust in slow tides keyed to the draining of the pools. It ingests molts, pool scum, the soft debris of collapsed pillars. Step near one and it scatters into a thousand independent ticks; stand still and it reassembles around your feet, not curious, just continuing. It is scavenger and soil-maker in a place that has no soil. It is many things agreeing temporarily to be one thing.

The black pools are not empty either. Things live in them that are more membrane than body: thin, cold, faintly luminous sheets that unfold at the surface, drink the condensation, and fold back into the dark. They do not swim. They spread. They are the source of the pale phosphorescence that seeps up through the fog, and when they breathe out, the mist thickens and the geography loosens. They are patient in a way that makes patience look like a kind of hunger.

And then there is the one with no body. It lives in the vents and the pool cycles and the swarming of the scuttlers. It is the stamping rhythm, the syntax of opening and closing, the way a creature can stand on a frozen pool that a pebble would break. It is not a god and not a ghost; it is the resident law. It does not hunt. It does not notice you. It notices only whether the process continues. It uses the scuttlers as moving parts, the pillars as whistles, the colonies as cleanup, the pools as lungs. It rearranges the landscape when the fog thickens, not because it is cruel, but because the landscape has always been every version at once and your path only settles one of them.

So the place is not abandoned. It is packed. It is occupied by functions: scuttle, anchor, filter, aggregate, dissolve. Each creature is a way the plain has found to keep water moving through stone. To live here is to be a temporary shape the Process is holding. To die is to stop being a shape and become ground. The place does not mourn. It hums.
