The first thing that lives here, the thing that makes the place possible, is the plant layer itself—and it is not one thing. It is a thousand things that have arrived at a working agreement.

I want to tell you about the fig-vines first, because they are the pilots of the canopy. They begin in the warm gravel and they do not climb—they *fall upward*, is the only way to describe it, their new growth searching the wet air and finding purchase on anything with more light than where they started. A fig-vine's stem is soft and pale and seems to fill with its own ambition; within a season it has crossed a dozen meters of open air, throwing out roots wherever it touches a wet wall, so that one plant becomes a colonnade, a highway, a structural member. The older ones are thick as a man's thigh and gray, and their fruit hangs in the understory where the light is dim, dropping constantly, a soft irregular patter you mistake for rain until you look down and see the litter moving—beetles, ants, things with more legs than seems prudent, all of them busy decomposing the wealth.

The plants do not compete here so much as delegate. One species holds the floor, another the first meter of wall, another the mid-canopy, and each has evolved to thrive in the exact patch of light and wet and heat it occupies and to fail instantly anywhere else. The result is not a jungle in the sense of a tangled argument but a jungle in the sense of a machine that has been debugged. The green goes all the way up because each layer is doing one job perfectly.

But density is the overwhelming fact, and so the most successful large animals here are not predators—they are *editors*. There are things shaped like deer, if a deer were built by a committee that had heard about deer but never seen one: long-necked, low-slung, with a curious arrangement of four front legs that lets them stand braced against a fig-vine column and crop the mid-canopy without falling. They browse in slow, deliberate arcs, opening gaps in the leaf mass, and the canopy closes behind them within a week. They do not run from you. They have no flight response worth the name; there is nowhere to flee to and nothing to flee from. They look at you with the placid dullness of an animal that has never been hunted, and you understand that this is what unafraid looks like, and it is not noble, it is simply vacant.

The browsers are prey to almost nothing, because almost nothing here is built for killing. The valley's answer to carnivory is scavenging, and the scavengers are the closest thing to a horror in the place. There is a thing—it comes out at dusk, it is about the size of a dog, it has a segmented body and far too many jointed limbs, and it does not have a mouth so much as a surface, a ventral membrane covered in fine cilia that it presses against carrion and *absorbs*. When a fig-vine drops its fruit, a hundred of them converge. When a browser dies—rare, but it happens, of age or misadventure—the body is gone within a day, and all that remains is a clean swept patch of gravel and the faint chemical smell of digestion. I watched one work a fallen browser once. It was not fast. It was thorough. It settled against the carcass like a hand into a glove and the outline of the body sank inward by degrees while the thing's segments pulsed, and I found I had stopped breathing and did not know when.

Above the ground, in the wet air between leaves, live the things that glow. They are small—most of them smaller than your thumb—and they are everywhere, and you do not see them in the white glare of the day. But the perpetual cloud, when the sun sinks behind the rim, does not go dark; it goes dim, a gray luminous dusk, and against that dimness the understory begins to sparkle. The light is green, mostly, a soft low green that comes from organs along the flanks of things that are otherwise indistinguishable from the beetles and moths they resemble. They are not signaling to each other; the bioluminescence seems to be a byproduct, an inefficiency, the light of a metabolism running a little too hot in a place that has never asked anything to be efficient. They drift up out of the leaf litter at dusk in slow columns, and the columns are the closest this valley comes to weather you can see.

Then there are the things I can only call *partly rooted*. Somewhere between plant and animal, maybe a dozen species, all of them occupying the same strategy: a juvenile that is mobile and hunts through the understory like anything else, and an adult that settles into the wet gravel, sinks roots, and stops moving forever. The transition takes months. I found one mid-way once—still on the gravel, still animal-shaped, but the trailing edge of it had gone green and fixed and was pulling water up from the thermal seep beneath it, and it was feeding itself both ways at once, catching small motile things with its front end and drawing dissolved warmth through its back. It had no eyes. It was waiting for something and I think it had forgotten what, and it did not move when I touched it, and the touch was warm and dry and felt like laying my hand on the living flank of the valley itself.

But none of them, none of this teeming, hungry, glowing, delegated machine—none of it is what the valley *is*. The valley is the thermal engine, and the thermal engine has a voice, and the voice comes from the vent fields, and the vent fields are where I first felt I understood the place.

The largest is a kilometer up the rift, a broad apron of fractured hot rock where the rising heat splits the groundwater and the steam comes out under its own built-up pressure in a rhythm—I want to say a heartbeat but a heartbeat is alive; this was a *stroke*, a measured mechanical stroke, steam shrieking through a fissure for two seconds, then silence for four, then shrieking again, and the rhythm is so regular that the plants around the vents have synchronized to it, their leaves opening on the exhaust and closing on the lull. They have had a long time to learn the rhythm. The engine has been running since before there was anything here to learn it.

And on the vent aprons themselves, in the hottest rock, where the steam is scalding and the water is above boiling when it emerges—there live the tiny ones, the specialists, the extremophiles. Mats of them. An orange crust no thicker than paint, clinging to rock that would blister you, feeding on the heat and the sulfur and nothing else, and their color is the raw color of the engine's own metabolism made visible. They are the only things here that do not depend on the green. They are the engine's own flesh. When the vent pulses, the mats brighten, very faintly, and dim with the lull, and it is the same rhythm, and you are standing at the floor of a young rift on a cold plateau and watching the planet's mantle breathe through a crack in its skin.

The sound is what stays with you. It is the sound of production, endless and mindless and measured, and it is coming up out of the ground and out of the walls and out of the boiling pools, and there is nowhere in the valley you can go to escape it, and after an hour you stop hearing it and after two you hear it again and are startled, and after a day you understand that you have been breathing in time with it without meaning to.

And then you climb the wall, fifty meters of scree, and it is gone. The roar drops away in stages. The green stops in a hard line, a meter wide, and above that line is frozen granite and cold washed sky and a silence so complete that your ears are still ringing with an engine that is no longer there. I crouched in that meter-wide band once, one hand in the wet heat and one hand on the cold stone, and the wet hand warmed and the cold hand stayed cold and I could feel the two climates running side by side, a breath apart, indifferent to each other, and I thought: this valley does not know it is strange. It has been strange for longer than there have been eyes to notice, and it will be strange long after, and the engine will chug away down there in the green and the steam and the glowing dusk, measured and mindless, churning out its scaled production for no one.
