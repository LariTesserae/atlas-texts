What lives in Aethelburg is not one people but a woven household: the Aethel, the living city they tend, and the small, strange lives that share the warm drowned valley with them. The Aethel are the ones who give the place its name and its slow heartbeat, but they would be the first to say that Aethelburg lives because many things live in it at once.

The Aethel themselves are tall and light-boned, with long torsos and limbs that move through the thickened water with a swimmer’s economy. Their skin has a faint nacre sheen, mottled in silt-pale and kelp-green, and their hands are broad and patient, the fingers long and the nails thick as horn. They have large eyes with a second, translucent lid that slides across in the brighter columns of light, and their voices are low and resonant, pitched to carry through water and stone alike. They wear little in the way of clothing—the warmth makes it unnecessary—but they ornament themselves with woven kelp, bone pins, snail-shell beads, and the occasional dull gleam of mountain metal, which is saved and reworked and inherited rather than spent. A blade of that metal is not a tool so much as a family memory.

They are a people of maintenance. Their days are measured by the tides, not by clocks, and the tide does not ask them to hurry. They wake when the water begins to shift, and they go out to check the city’s joints and seams: the coral beams that have grown slightly wrong, the vine bridge that has begun to sag, the fungal garden whose light has dimmed, the kelp frond that has crept too close to a dwelling and must be pruned back before it decides the dwelling is something to grip. They patch, coax, trim, feed, and sing. Aethelburg is not built once and left; it is kept, continually, by people who understand that the world is always gently trying to reclaim them. Their hero stories are maintenance stories. Their saints are the ones who noticed a crack before it became a current.

The city is their great work and also their fellow resident. Aethelburg is grown from a black, coral-like substance harvested from the deep vents and coaxed into shape by hand. Its towers spiral upward toward the hazy bloom of light, its walkways curve with the warm currents, and its communal halls are layered accretions of dwelling upon dwelling, each generation adding a lip or a shelf or a room without ever quite replacing what came before. The Aethel do not think of the city as dead architecture. They know it has a metabolism. They can hear it creak and groan at night, and they can feel the faint heat of the polyps still working somewhere in the walls. Children learn to read the city by its sounds before they learn to read the tide tables. A healthy district has a particular low hum; a sick one goes quiet.

Around the Aethel, the valley is full of other lives. Snail-like creatures graze the algal mats in slow, glistening herds, their shells smelted for tools and ornaments when they die. The Aethel tend them, but not as livestock exactly—more as neighbors who happen to be edible-adjacent and useful. Bioluminescent fungi grow in pulsing gardens, their caps swelling and dimming in a slow rhythm that gives the city its night-light. Glass-winged fish shoal through the open spaces between towers, nearly invisible until they turn and catch a column of light, and then the whole school becomes a flicker of scattered mirrors. Moss-sloths cling to the undersides of the great kelp fronds, their fur a matted garden of tiny crustaceans and damp green growth; they move so slowly that a child may watch one for a whole tide and see it shift only a hand’s breadth. Somewhere deeper in the valley lives the creature whose call sounds like a whale’s song played on a cracked wooden flute. No one has seen it whole. The Aethel use its call as a marker of the tide and as a reminder that the valley is larger than their lamps.

The Aethel are not masters of this place. They are negotiators. The vegetation is lush and predatory, and a kelp grove can grow hungry if it is not watched. They prune the great fronds, feed the oldest groves with crushed shell and coral dust, and teach their children which currents carry spores that sting and which vines can be trusted to hold a bridge. They work metal only rarely, in crude hand-powered forges set into the eroding mountainsides, where the pale chalky stone crumbles at a touch and the silt runs in constant pale streams. They have no concept of dry land. The sky is a story they tell, a blue-black rumor above the luminous haze; the myth of a world without water is a kind of ghost tale, unsettling and slightly absurd.

Their society is small-scaled and dense. Families cluster in pod-like districts named for currents, and the districts are linked by vine bridges and shared gardens and the constant exchange of favors. There are coral-growers and kelp-tenders and snail-herders and fungal gardeners and silt-readers who can taste the water and tell whether the mountains above are shedding more stone than usual. Elders are valued not for age alone but for memory: the ones who remember the last great settling of the coral, the last long low tide, the last time the cracked-flute creature sang for three days without stopping. Decisions are made slowly, in communal halls lit by pulsing fungus, with everyone allowed to speak until the tide turns. The Aethel are patient in the way of people who have learned that impatience only makes the water push back harder.

They are also a people of quiet art. They carve bone and shell into spirals and current-maps, weave kelp fiber into mats that record family histories, and make music by tapping the hollow stems of the coral and blowing through drilled snail shells. Their songs are low and repetitive and meant to be heard through the body as much as the ears. They sing to the vines when they train them across gaps, not because they believe the vines understand, but because the singing keeps the workers calm and the vines, somehow, seem to grow more willingly. When an Aethel dies, the body is wrapped in kelp and given to the deep vents, where the coral will eventually take it back. The dead become part of the city’s slow accretion. It is not a sad ending. It is the most local form of immortality they know.

So the ones who live there are the Aethel: long-limbed, silt-sheened, patient, practical, and half in love with the world that is always trying to reclaim them. But they are not alone. The city lives with them, the kelp forests live around them, the glass fish and moss-sloths and fungal gardens and the unseen flute-voiced creature live alongside them, and the warm mineral water itself lives through all of it. Aethelburg is a dense, slow-beating heart in a valley still being born, and its people are the cells that keep it beating—not by conquering the water, but by learning, tide after tide, how to live inside it.
