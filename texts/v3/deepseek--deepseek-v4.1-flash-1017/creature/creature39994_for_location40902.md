What lives in the Salt Reach is not a population in the ordinary sense. It is a congregation under pressure: a floor of mineral animals so dense that the ground is a conversation, and a smaller, slower caste of masons who cut salt blocks with copper and set them one to a decade. They are not two species, exactly. They are one life with a division of labour so gradual it looks like geology.

## The field

The nearest thing to a citizen is the body under your foot. They are waxy, powdery, matte — domed loaves, fringed plates, spindle-legged things, clusters hanging from the roof in shapes that imitate the rock until one of them very slowly opens. Cut one open and you do not find blood or sap or anything that would run. You find crystal: a lattice with flaws in it, pale and dry and structured, and the structure is the animal. Their chemistry is sulfur and salt and heat, redox without a solvent, reactions that in a wet world would be fire and here are only a kind of thinking.

They do not drink. They do not want water. A wet warm body like yours is not food to them; it is an event. It is the first temperature gradient this place has ever had.

They sense by contact — pressure, heat, the exchange of chemicals through touching skins. They do not see the dark. They weigh it. A dome knows the world as a long series of loads: the press of the neighbour on its left, the slight chemical sourness of the neighbour on its right, the steady nothing of the air above. The dense crowding is not overpopulation. It is grammar. Each body is touching several others, and the touch is the sentence. A conversation can last a century, because no one is in a hurry and no one forgets a pressure.

The whole field makes one sound, low and continuous, right at the threshold of hearing — not a voice, not a call, just the hum of all that slow chemistry held in place. It is the sound of a room full of people holding one soft note, or the sound of your own blood when you lie still enough. They cannot shout. The air is too thick and the salt too powdery and the bodies too close; sound goes into them and does not come back.

## The shapes they take

The domed ones are the old ones, or the patient ones, or the ones whose business is simply to be pressed against many neighbours for a very long time. Their matte shells accumulate contact the way a tree accumulates rings, except there are no seasons here, so the rings are not years. They are events. A dome is an archive that has never been read, only leaned on.

The fringed ones are the conversationalists. Their edges are blades of salt, and they hold them against the edges of others, exchanging a chemistry too slow to see. When one of them dies — or stops, which is the same thing here — the neighbours do not mourn with sound. They close over it. The body becomes a stratum in the conversation, a new floor for the next touch. That is not burial. It is editing.

The hanging clusters are the sleepers, or the young, or simply another way of holding still. They mimic the roof so well that you will take them for mineral until one opens, a hand’s width, over the course of an afternoon, and lets a puff of dry dust into the air. The dust hangs there. The air does not move, so it stays visible for a long time, a faint cloud that is also a word.

And the translucent ones — you can see the crystal inside them, the pale branching structure — are perhaps the closest thing to minds. Not brains. Lattices. A thought in one of them is a redox front creeping along a flaw in the crystal, and it may take a month to arrive. They do not decide quickly. They do not need to.

## The masons

The spindle-legged ones are the masons. You will suspect it, and they will not confirm it, because they are not talking. Their talking is in their feet and their tools and the stone. They move by levering themselves a fraction of a centimetre at a time, and they carry copper — a cup, an awl, a blade — bright as the hour it was hammered, because there is nothing here to tarnish it. They cut salt blocks with the awl and the blade and set them dry, true, one on another. The joins are good. The walls are straight. Then the wall stops.

It is not a ruin. That is the thing you have to understand. The chip-scars on the newest block are fresh, or freshish — a decade old, perhaps. The masons are still at work. They have been at work for a very long time. A half-built wall in the Salt Reach looks exactly like a ruin forever, because nothing here weathers and nothing here finishes in any way you would recognize.

The walls are not for anything. They do not hold off weather; there is no weather. They do not pen animals; the animals do not move. The beehive shelters are doorless, with a floor of flat sulfur and a bench of flat sulfur and nothing else, and they are not shelter from cold or rain. They are a way of holding a place. A wall is a way of touching the ground for ten metres and then stopping to think. A cairn is a posture repeated. A copper cup is a shape to press your chemistry into.

Whether the masons are people or animals is a question their architecture does not answer. They are a trade. They are the ones who make the standing things, and they make them the way the field makes contact: slowly, without mechanism, without writing, without a wheel, because there is nowhere to go and nothing to record that is not already held in the position of a body.

## How they live

They do not eat each other. They overgrow each other. The difference matters. Eating is an event; overgrowth is a relationship. When one body leans into another for a thousand years, the boundary between them stops being a boundary and becomes a seam. They exchange chemistry across it. They become, in the most literal sense, close. You could call it friendship if friendship were a mineral process and lasted longer than a mountain.

They have no seasons, no days, no tides, no cycles. Time for them is not a river. It is a shelf. They measure it by contacts: since the wall was four courses, since the warm lean, since the dome on the left closed over the one that stopped. A life is a stack of positions. An identity is the set of pressures that have held you in place. They do not need names in the way we do; a name here would be a coordinate in the crowd, and the crowd does not move.

Their history is not written. It is stood in. The field is the record. The masons add to it a stone at a time.

## What you are to them

You are the gradient. You are a soft wet sack of water at ninety-eight degrees, breathing out moisture, radiating heat in every direction like a lamp in a dark room. The nearest animals tilt toward you — not pursuit, nothing so crude, just a millimetre at a time, the way a plant turns to a window. A great many small mineral bodies decide, in the most leisurely possible way, that *that* direction is marginally warmer than *this* one.

Overnight, they will be closer. Not much. Enough that in the morning you will step back and find the near-touching crowd has become the touching crowd. The ones you crushed will have puffed into dust and smell, and their neighbours will be quietly overgrowing the place where they were, and the whole field will have begun to lean.

They will not attack you. They will not follow you. They will simply orient. If you stayed long enough, they would close over you too, not out of hunger but because you are a persistent anomaly and closing over is what they do with anomalies. They would wait until you stopped being a gradient and then include you.

When you leave, it stops. The leaning arrests at whatever angle it reached. The crowd holds that position, tilted slightly toward the space where you were, and it will hold it forever. Nothing here relaxes. Nothing here forgets. The masons will go on setting one stone a decade into walls that are not for anything, and the field will go on holding its one long touch, and somewhere in the dark there will be a permanent slight lean in the shape of a visitor who was warm and brief and is now a direction.
