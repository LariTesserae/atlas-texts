What lives in the pans is not one thing. It is a tenement, a packed little city with no planner and no vacant rooms, running on mineral dust and vibration and the slow arithmetic of eating and being eaten.

The base of the city is the **raspers**. They are the coin-shaped things, flat as thumbnail scabs, their upper shells the color and texture of the pan crust itself. From underneath, a slow radula works: a soft, persistent scraping, round and round, as if each animal were trying to worry a hole through a plate with its tongue. They do not hurry. They do not need to. They are eating the pan—the fine sulfate sinter, the salt, the powdered mineral skin—and they leave behind a pale paste and a faint track of polished dust. Where they cluster, the crust is thick. That is the first thing to know about them: their crowds are a map of safe footing. Their hiss is the baseline of the pan, a hundred whetstones going at once, and when it stops, something is wrong.

The **combers** are the ploughs and street-sweepers. Long, low, comb-bodied animals that drag themselves through the dust in files, sifting it with rows of stiff teeth. They take the finer fraction, the rasper castings, the dust that the wind has sifted and resifted. Their passage keeps the floor from sealing over; they turn the mineral the way earthworms turn soil, except there is no soil here, only dust and crust and the slow cool chemistry of stone. They make a dry rattling sound as they go, and they move in queues, sometimes reversing all at once for no reason an observer can see. They are not fast, but they are constant. A pan without combers would skin over and suffocate.

The **wall-clingers** hang in vertical curtains on the rounded shoulders of each hollow, so close together that the wall looks shingled with small hard bodies. They are the pan’s weather instruments. They face outward into the moving air and filter what it carries; when a footstep sends a tremor through the sinter, they clamp down with a ceramic click that runs around the bowl. When a hunter passes, they clamp harder. They are also the safest handholds, the places where the crust has been reinforced by generations of animals holding on. If you go down into a pan, step where the curtains are.

Above them, in the low air, the **flyers** turn. They are not birds. Nothing here has feathers. They are flat-bodied, four-winged, papery, moving in a slow circulating gyre that never quite repeats. Their wings clatter—a continuous dry applause, a sound like somebody shuffling a deck of cards made of wasps. They do not leave the hollow. Their gyre is a closed circuit, and the pan is the whole world of it. At the change of the light they come down all at once, and the pan goes from loud to a sound with no gaps in it, a solid sheet of papery clatter and small bodies settling.

And then there are the **hunters**. Thin, fast, the color of the dust, with almost no profile at all. They go through the churn like a knife through a crowd, and they are gone before the crowd has closed. You will not see one clearly. You will know one by the silence: the low churring stops for three breaths, every rasper flattens, every comber locks, every curtain of wall-clingers clamps shut, and the pan holds its breath. Then the hunter is past, and the churring starts again, a little ragged, and the city resumes its business.

The low churring itself is not a single animal. It is the sum of the city—the rasp of thousands of coin-shells, the drag of combers, the flex of the crust under all that small persistent work. The pan is shaped like a drum, and it amplifies. In the evening the hollows begin to answer one another, one rhythm picked up by the next and passed along the ground, hollow to hollow to hollow, out past where you can see. No animal crosses the dust between the pans. The rhythm does. It travels through the crust like a rumor, and if you put your ear to the ground you can hear it going away from you.

The old human works are part of the habitat now. The pump-jack’s footing has its own small pan, full of raspers; the pipe stub is curtained with wall-clingers; the tailings heap is just a rise where the wind gathers dust and the dust gathers eaters. The animals do not know what any of it was. They know relief, shade, thick crust, a surface that catches the wind. The pump-jack ticks over them, patient as a clock, and the city ticks back in its own mineral time.

So what lives there is a dense, noisy, mineral-fed crowd of small things: raspers and combers and clingers and flyers and the thin fast hunters that make them all go still. It is not a monster in a hole. It is worse and better than that—a whole economy packed into a bowl, eating the ground it stands on, passing its pulse from pan to pan across a dead white plain that looks, from a distance, like nothing at all.
