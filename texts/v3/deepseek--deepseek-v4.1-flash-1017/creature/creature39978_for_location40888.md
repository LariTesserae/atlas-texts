The Kettle is kept by someone you will almost certainly meet only as evidence.

You will know them first by a lashing that was not there yesterday, tightened in the local way—three turns, a locking hitch, the tail tucked under so neatly it looks grown. You will know them by a trough replanted overnight, by a tendril that has been turned back toward the hum of the weave, by the dull blade put into its gourd the wrong way round. They are not hiding, exactly. They are simply always at the far end of the work.

They are small. Not child-small; compact-small, built like one of their own knots—a hard little centre with limbs lashed out from it. Their hands are the most finished thing about them: broad across the palm, short-nailed, the knuckles knotted and the fingertips soft from water and leaf-sap. They have a habit of holding a gourd against their ear before they hang it, not to test the water but to hear the note, and their head tilts a little when they do it, like a bird listening to the floor. Their eyes are not for distance. They look at things close—the underside of a leaf, the fit of a wedge, the place where a fibre has begun to soften. In the green-gold haze they squint; in the red underglow they do not.

They wear very little, because very little survives the wet: a wrap of split reed, a pouch for the awl, a cord at the wrist. Bare feet, always. The lattice tells them where the roots have come through, and they would rather feel it than be told. Their skin has the same damp warmth as the walls. In the creases of their knuckles there is a faint green, not dirt—something that has decided to grow there and been allowed to stay.

They move unhurriedly, and yet they are across the room before you have noticed they crossed it. They go hand over hand on the ropes between spires as if the white sea below were a floor of ordinary kind. They speak seldom. When they do, it is in the local language, which has nine words for *trained* and none for *wild*, and they use the words precisely, and the plants lean toward the sound. Sometimes they hum under their breath—not a tune, a tuning. They will stop in the middle of a phrase to listen to a drip that has found a new path.

They are the one who pollinates the dark flowers with a feather, a fingertip, the corner of a leaf. They prune things into shapes that have no name in your language. They eat what fruits and tolerate the blind pale things in the troughs because those things eat what would otherwise need eating. They leave the feather beside the gourd that is still wrong. That is not forgetfulness. It is an invitation, and it is also a test, and it is also—though they would not say so—the nearest thing they have to company.

They do not want to be thanked. Gratitude makes them leave the room. If you wait for them, they will not come. If you stop waiting, you will find a fresh lashing on a beam you were leaning against, and the wrong gourd will have been tuned, or not, and you will not be able to say when.

They are not old. Nothing in the Kettle is old; the place replaces its rot too quickly for age to settle. The keeper is renewed the same way—a cut healed by morning, a worn habit quietly swapped for a new one. There may have been others. There may be others still, on the lit spires off in the white, each with a room the size of a bed and a gourd that is still wrong. The current keeper is only the current one. They are not finished. They are still being tuned, and they know it, and they keep working anyway.

If you ask them what they are, they will probably give you a word that means *the one who notices the third ring*. If you ask whether they live here, they will look at the far trough—farther now than it was this morning—and say nothing. The Kettle is not their home. It is their keeping. They are very good at it. They do not care to be thanked.
