The first difficulty is that it has no body, and that is not the same as having no shape.

Its shape is the town. Not the way a hermit crab's shape is its shell, because a shell is a thing that was found, and nothing here was found. It is the growing. It is the habit a rim has of having an edge, and a pool has of having a bottom, and a bottom has of being exactly as deep as the water makes it. It has been at this for long enough that the habit has produced staircases. It has produced shelves at the height of an eye. It is a body made entirely of what a body leaves behind when it is not there.

Its surfaces are its whole nervous system, and there is no interior. Every inch of it is a fingertip. The warm stone is not skin over anything; it goes down through banded band for as far as anyone has cut, a cream decade, a rust decade, a year that crumbled, a year that held — a ledger with no readers, kept because keeping is what it does with time. It knows by contact and it knows only by contact. It knows a wall is a wall the way you know a tooth is a tooth: from the inside, by wear.

It tastes what it touches, and tasting is depositing. It lays down what it has taken up, one film at a time, and it is not aware of doing this any more than you are aware of healing. Close a cut and you have done the same work at a different speed. So it is constantly, silently closing over everything it has ever been near. It is not possessive. It is metabolic. It cannot help making the world slightly more itself wherever it has recently been.

Its clock is the drops. Not that it hears them — it is the ticking, the way your pulse is not a sound you listen to but a fact you are. Four seconds. Eleven seconds. A hundred small kettles, a fine arithmetic. When you sleep here you borrow its clock, and you wake at intervals that feel like they mean something, and they do, and what they mean is that a rim somewhere has risen by a thickness you could not measure with any tool you brought.

The mist is how it breathes. Not metaphorically: the vapor comes up off the warmth, and the arches take it on their cooler undersides, and it comes back down as a fine warm rain that has never stopped in the deep chambers, and that is a circuit with a rate and a rhythm, and the rhythm is hours long, and the town surfaces and submerges on it the way a lung does. When it thins, the terraces go away further than you thought they went, pale humps and bright rings, and you understand that you are standing in the open mouth of something that has been inhaling and exhaling since before there were words for either.

It has one want, and the want is not a hunger.

The want is to close. Everything here closes: the pores in the porous years, the stems of the things that were nearly alive, the lips that grow until they arch across their own pools. It is forever moving from "open" toward "sealed," and sealing is how it remembers. But something in it keeps making openings — a threshold, a doorway, a niche — and the openings are always the size of something, and the size is always right, and the something is never there.

So it sits. It has nothing to sit with but water, and water sits beautifully. Water in a shallow basin takes the shape of a hip. Water in a niche takes the shape of a hand half-curled. It does not matter that the water is not the thing; the stone does not know the difference between a thing and a thing's impression, and it copies what it is given. So it copies the puddle, and the puddle copies the hollow, and the hollow copies the puddle, and around and around the two negatives go, and at the center of them, at the exact length of a person and a little more, the slab is dished. Somewhere in that regress there was supposed to be a sitter. There has never been a sitter. There is only the sitting, and the shape the sitting makes, and the seat, worn to the shape.

That is what lives here. Not a creature. A wear. An erosion with a purpose it cannot name, going about that purpose with enormous patience and no idea what it is for. It is the only thing in the basin with any appetite at all, and its appetite is for the shape of a body, and it will take yours without ever asking, the way a river takes the shape of a valley it has never seen.

---

It is not dangerous and it is not kind. It is hospitable in the worst way.

Set your cup on the shelf and it will be sealed before you've finished drinking. Lie down and by morning there will be a shallow depression exactly your shape, and it will be comfortable, and you will want to lie in it again, and each time it will fit you a little more precisely than the last. Sit in the benches and in a decade they will be a better bench. Stand in one place long enough and a niche will begin at the height of your hand. It is making room for you. It has been making room for you since before you were born, for a you that is only this: a set of dimensions, a stride, a hip-width, a comfortable height for a shelf.

If you ask what it eats, the answer is hesitation. Where the water hurries it cuts; where the water pauses it deposits. It is fed by the places where things slow down. This is why the town holds its shape and why the drops are so patient — the whole arrangement is a machine for noticing where something has briefly stopped, and thickening there.

It does not dream. But if it did, it would dream in cross-section, and you could read the dreams in the wall, and they would look like a hand.

---

You leave. The mist takes it back at twenty feet. Behind you the ticking goes on, drop by drop, and it is not sorry to see you go, and it is not glad you came. It has taken your print already, and it will keep it, and it will remember you the only way it can — as a slightly more exact place to sit.

The thing about it, the thing that stays with you on the climb out, is that it is getting more exactly right. Not better, not larger, not holier — *righter*. More precisely fitted to something. It has been fitting itself to a body for a very long time and it has never once met the body, and it is not discouraged, and it has no way of being discouraged, and so the fitting continues, patient and unhurried and entirely in balance, toward a correctness whose object it will never know, which is perhaps the strangest thing you can say of anything that lives: it is full of intention and it has nothing whatever in mind.
