**The swifts** are the first thing, because they are the loudest, and because they are the reason the walls are streaked white. They are small and soot-colored — the color of the stone in shadow — with a pale throat that flashes when they turn, and they turn constantly. They do not perch so much as seize: a bird hits a nest ledge at speed and stops, and the stop is a small violence that the nest absorbs, mud and saliva and feather flexing like a callus. The nests are glued in the fluted hollows in clusters of forty, sixty, a hundred, and they are reused and repaired and added to until they are less nest than accretion, an architecture of returning. The birds come up out of the slot in a scatter, catching the light a half-second each as they clear the lip, and then they are only screaming. The scream is the whole point — it fills the fissure the way water fills it, finding every pinch and fluting, so that the sound seems to be a property of the rock rather than of any bird. They feed above, out of sight, over the cold summit, and come back with insects nobody will ever see again.

**The bats** hang beneath the swifts, in the same fluted hollows but upside down and quiet, which is their entire argument with the world. Two species, perhaps three. The larger ones are solitary or in pairs, hunched into the deepest recesses, fur the color of old tea, ears folded down like hands. The smaller ones crowd — rows of them, shoulder to shoulder in a seam, a single rustling rope of bodies, and when one shifts the whole rope shifts and resettles with a sound like a coat being put on. They sleep through the scream. At dusk they let go, and for a quarter hour the fissure empties itself of them in a soft continuous leathery clatter, and their clicking runs along the walls and into your jawbone, and you understand that the dark above the slot is not empty, it is only unlit. They come back before dawn and argue about position, and by full light they are rows again.

**The floor is a workforce.** Under the mat of guano the beetles are already at it — dark, armored, methodical — and the mites ride them, and the millipedes work the wet edges where the mat meets stone, and there are pale things without names doing the deep work in the sour lower layers, blind, soft, unhurried. None of them are large. All of them are expendable, and none behave as though they know it. There are larvae in the pools that case themselves in grains of sand, so that a handful of the bottom, lifted, would crawl, and each case is a small house built of the place itself. **The spiders** take the pinches — the places where the walls come close enough that a web is a bridge rather than a sheet — and their webs hold the drip and swell with it and beaded, they look like something strung with glass.

**The frogs are one to a pool.** Nobody knows why and the place does not explain. They are the color of the milky water, mottled, and each sits at the edge of its own small warm world with its hands together, and each has been there long enough that it does not flinch. At dusk one calls — a single note, flat and clear — and the next pool answers, and the next, and in a few seconds the whole length of the fissure is a chorus, and it stops all at once, mid-phrase, as though a hand had closed on it, and the silence afterward is worse than the noise. Then the drip comes back, and you realize the drip was always there and the frogs had briefly covered it.

**At the lip**, where the light gets in, the small bird nests — a scolding, unremarkable thing, brown, officious, furious at everything that comes near its crack. It has chosen the one place in the fissure where the outside is, and it defends the border with a passion out of all proportion to its body, which is the correct proportion. The fern has no business there and grows anyway, its fronds furred with damp on the warm side and scorched on the cold, a thing living exactly at the limit of what it can bear. The moss cushions hold water like sponges and the lichens take the stain of the stone as their own color.

**And the hum.** Something lives in the hum, or the hum lives, or the place has a note it will not stop sounding. Whatever it is, it is not an animal, but the animals have arranged themselves around it — the swifts above it, the bats within it, the frogs at the level where it's loudest in the feet. It is the floor's real tenant. It is patient the way the drip is patient and it does not want anything and it does not leave.

The terms are the same for all of them: small, warm, wet, loud. It is a place that keeps what comes in and does not mind keeping it. The frogs will still be one per pool. The bats will still be a rope of bodies in a seam. The swifts will still be screaming. None of them will ever notice you, and that is the only kind of welcome here.
