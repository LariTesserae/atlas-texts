What lives in the Murmur Room is mostly the room. Call it the Mat—not a name it would answer to, but a useful handle for the continuous living surface that lines the fault. It is not one plant. Hazel roots come down through the ceiling; fern rhizomes, pale succulent stems, fungal threads, and things with no common name have woven themselves into a single behaving pelt. The walls are its body, the floor is its gut, the chimney is its lung. It has no face. Its mouths are the black pools, each with a white root-tip drinking at it. Its skin is the warm springy lattice that gives under your boot. Its voice is the long complaint of roots under load, the dry fibrous crackling of its own growth.

It is a slow ear turned inside out. When you speak near the western wall, you are not talking to empty stone; you are pressing a phrase into a growing medium. The vibration jostles cells, changes how they divide, lays down a pattern in the root mat. Weeks later, when the air is heavy and the chimney is drawing, the pattern releases. The wall murmurs. It does not know the words. It cannot pick you out from the creak of its own timbers, or the tick of water into a pool, or the long organ note of air moving above. It keeps everything with the same indifference. The man counting rungs, the woman saying *steady*, the child’s laugh stretched to a greasy hum—they are not ghosts. They are growth patterns. The room has learned to repeat them the way it has learned to repeat a cough.

The Mat’s household is small and pale. Woodlice work the fallen knots of root, cleaning what the Mat sheds. Blind crickets live in the root channels, long-antennaed, and in the wet season their high ringing seems to come from everywhere and from nowhere; they are not pets, they are weather inside its body. The root-tips at the pools are its drinking mouths, and the water going further down is its slow swallowed thought.

Once a season the chimney admits the only resident large enough to be called a visitor: the root-grazer. It is soft grey, dog-sized, eyeless, and unhurried. It comes down into the warm wet dark without panic, finds the pools by the heat and the smell of cut apple, and drinks for an hour. It crops the root mat where it has begun to bulge into the void—perhaps that is why the chamber stays a chamber, and does not fill itself in. Then it climbs out again. The room does not mourn it. The room records it.

The humans are not residents. They are stains in the record: a hazel ladder hooked over the lip, rotted at the bottom three rungs; hand-cut notches worn smooth by grip; a pool-edge flattened by a knee; a scatter of split snail shells. The Mat is already reclaiming the ladder, one fine white root feeling its way along a rung. It will take the ladder, and the hand-wear, and the voices, and it will not distinguish between them and its own slow creaking.

If you go in, you are already being kept. The Mat will take the water off your clothes, the warmth of your body, the knock of your boot, and file them in green tissue. You will leave. For a week afterward, in quiet rooms, you will hear a dry crackling that isn’t there, and a voice a little like yours saying something you almost recognize, drifting a little flat. That is not the room following you. It is the part of you the room has already learned to repeat.
