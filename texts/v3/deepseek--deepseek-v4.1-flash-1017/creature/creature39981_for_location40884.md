The ones who live here are easy to mistake for a fold in the plain. At a distance a household is a low pale rise, a scatter of fan-crests, a place where the grit has banked against something and stayed. Up close they resolve slowly, the way a word does when you have stared at it too long. They are broad and low and the color of weak tea, and they do not stand so much as unfold.

A person here is a stack. Not a metaphor: their flesh is laminated, thin sheets of a flexible horny stuff laid down season by season, each sheet slightly offset from the one beneath, so that the body has a grain and a history you can read if you know how. The surface is dry and warm and faintly rough, like the underside of a leaf pressed too long in a book. At the joints the sheets separate into pleated fans; when they move, the pleats slide over one another with a sound like papers being sorted in another room. They have two legs and two arms, and the arms end in hands of four long flat fingers that may split along their length in old age. Their heads are wide and low. The eyes are dark seams. There is no nose to speak of; they taste the air through a soft vertical mouth, and they breathe in a slow shallow way that seems optional.

From the back of the head and the shoulders grows a crest of pale fiber, the same stuff as the mats in the scours. It is not hair. It is a garden and a ledger. It takes dust and warmth and records the years in faint rings, and when a person is old the crest is wide and complicated and other people read it the way you might read a shelf of books.

They do not drink. There is nothing to drink. They take in the pale grit and the warmth that comes up through the panels, and their bodies sort and use and return it as a finer dust. They are warm to the touch and stay warm. When they sleep under a shallow drift of grit they are indistinguishable from a stone that has held the day.

They are not many. A household may be two or three or six, and the next household may be a hundred kilometers away, and a visit between them is a season’s event. They live around warm seams, which they clear of grit and sleep beside. They do not build walls. They lay shards of laminate on edge in rows where the wind will find them, and the wind makes a low irregular chime, different every dusk. They draw patterns in the grit with their flat fingers, knowing the wind will erase them; the drawing is the point. They tend the pale fibrous fans, not as crops exactly, more as relatives and instruments. They clear dust from a seam. They press a lifted edge down. They carry a thread from one fan to another and wait a century to see what it does.

They speak by tapping. A voice is almost nothing—a click, a rasp, a low tick in the throat—but they listen through their feet. A conversation may be one hand laid on the ground and another hand answering from ten paces away. A name is a rhythm. A greeting is two short taps and one long. Some of them lie for days with an ear to the laminate and listen to the deep circulation, and those listeners can feel a seam opening before it opens, can predict where the warm air will pool. They are consulted. They are not priests. The machinery below is not a god to them; it is weather, colleague, floor. They have a rule: do not dig below your own depth. The world is still running. It does not need to be fixed.

Children begin as knots in a fan. When a fan buds, the knot is carried in a warm fold of cloth—if cloth is the word—to a new scour, and there it is fed dust and heat and left to separate into sheets. It may take a century to become a person. Old people thicken and slow and are called deep. They sit a great deal. Their laminations pack close, and their crests grow wide, and people come to sit beside them and read. When they die they lie down, and the plain accepts them, and the crest keeps growing for a while, and the descendants read the last years in it.

They know they are late. Their oldest stories say the world was finished before they were grown from a seam that opened once and never closed. They have no word for water; the closest is a phrase that means “the old story that isn’t true.” A stranger from a wet world smells sharp and quick to them, and too loud, and they will notice you by your wetness before they notice your face. They will tap a greeting and wait. They can wait a long time. They are not alarmed by you. You are an event, not a threat. If you stay, they will bring you a pinch of grit and place it before you, and then they will go back to their slow work, which is the plain’s own attention turned on itself.
