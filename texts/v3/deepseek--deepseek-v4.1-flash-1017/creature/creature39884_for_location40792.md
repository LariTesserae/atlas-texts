## The people

They are not small, but they are low. Something in the way they stand — knees a little bent, head a little forward, shoulders drawn down and rolled in — makes them seem perpetually about to go through a door, which they are. Their shoulders are the most-used part of them. The jambs are rounded because the shoulders have rounded them, and the shoulders have been shaped by the jambs, and after a few centuries of that you can no longer tell which is wearing which.

The skin is pale and cool and faintly damp to touch, like the stone, though nothing here is actually wet. The sun never gets high enough to color anyone, so there is one complexion in the Hesk and it is the color of the inside of a mushroom. Hair is worn short or bound back. The hands are too large — wide across the knuckle, the pads of the fingers thickened, the nails kept down to nothing because they are used as tools: to test moss, to feel the temperature of the fourth tank, to find the notch in the stone in the dark.

The eyes are what a visitor notices last and thinks about longest. They look at you for longer than is polite anywhere else, and while you are talking they are not watching your face. They are watching your hands.

## The voice

They can speak. They do speak — full sentences, ordinary talk, dry jokes about weather that does not change. But the voice is the tool of last resort, and everyone here knows precisely how far it carries: about four paces, fewer if you are facing away, fewer still near the moss wall.

So they built a language out of contact, and it is not a code for speech. It is its own thing. A flat palm on the sternum means *stop, look at me.* Two fingers tapped on the back of the wrist means *come.* A grip on the forearm that tightens, then eases, means *wait* — and then *now.* Anything too complicated for that gets said at a distance of six inches, mouth near the ear, and the listener closes their eyes. Everyone here does that. No one can tell you why.

And the knock. Two knocks, pause, one. It goes on door frames, tank rims, the undersides of floors. It is how a mother calls a child down from three terraces up. It is how someone tells you they have come into your room while you slept, and means no harm. It is short and hard and the stone carries it the way stone does, which is to say barely, which is to say exactly far enough.

They are not a silent people. They laugh, and the laughter is all shoulders and breath, and if you are across the bowl you will see it happen and hear nothing at all.

## The hands

A child learns the seep before it learns to walk — a hand on the back of the neck, guiding. By six it can tell, from the sound of the topmost channel, whether the spring is running true, which is a thing no visitor can do and which is also, here, a matter of life. By nine it has been taught the wedge and the antler and the soft copper hammer and has made something useless and been told it is good.

There is a particular kind of person in the Hesk and you will meet three in a day: the one who knows the moss. Not moss in general. The moss on this wall, this roof, this decade. Whether it is thickening over the north drain. Whether the pruned tree is pushing a shoot the wrong way. Whether the pale frilled thing in the fourth tank should be taken now with the fingers or left another eight days. The seep does not vary, so the moss does not vary, and yet somebody is always watching it, and that is the whole of the discipline.

## The room

There is one empty room at any given time and everyone knows which one, and no one says so. When someone dies it is left as it stands — not cleared, not swept, not formally closed. The Hesk simply waits. It can wait a year. It can wait a week.

When a child is born, or a roof takes on water, or someone's hands stop being good for fine work, the room is opened and occupied, and it is the same room, and it has always been that room. They have no word for *new.* They have a word for *the one that has been replaced*, which is a different word and not a small one.

## The climb

They know the way out: the rope, the notch, the hauling frame at the lip. Every child learns it. Most people use it ten times in a life. Mostly they go up in pairs — not for safety, though it is safer. Up on the plateau the sound comes back. The wind has a voice. A footstep rings. A shout carries. It undoes something in them and they cannot be alone while it happens.

Some go up often. Some go up once, at fourteen, and are never seen on the rope again.

## The visitor

They will feed you from the tanks, and they will touch you. If you flinch — and you will flinch, everyone flinches — they will notice, and they will not comment, and in a day or two they will do it again, gently. A person who flinches at a hand on the shoulder will be very alone here, and the Hesk does not permit that. It is not kindness exactly. It is closer to maintenance.

They will take you to the rim at dusk and stand with you and not explain.

And eventually, in one way or another, with a hand flat on your chest, they will ask whether you are staying. Not as a threat, not as an invitation. As a piece of information they need, to know where to put you and what to teach you and whether to learn your name the long way. And you will say no. They know you will say no; they have heard no before. They will nod, and touch your chest once more, and go back down to the tank.

Years later you will come back up the rope and the notch and the hauling frame, and the moss will be on the same stones at the same thickness, and the same person will put the same flat hand on your sternum, and you will find that somewhere along the way you began knocking two, pause, one, on doorframes in the loud world, on tabletops, on the arms of chairs, waiting — for no reason you could explain to anyone — for a wall to knock back.
