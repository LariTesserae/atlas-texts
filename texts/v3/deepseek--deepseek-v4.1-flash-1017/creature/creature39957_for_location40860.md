The thing that lives in the nap has no name that anyone uses out loud. On the guild board it is not listed. In the crews’ talk it is “the tenant,” or “the slow one,” or simply *that*, with a jerk of the chin toward a smoothed lane in the mat. Mostly it is not talked about at all. It is known by its trails.

The trails are the first thing. They are not slime and they are not cut. They are lanes of pressed-down nap, paler than the surrounding fiber, smooth as the inside of a wrist, and they run in long unhurried curves out from the seep and back again, or loop around a bedrock island and vanish under a deck. Some are as wide as a wrist. Some are as wide as a cart. The crews step over them without comment. A trail will be fresh after a damp night and gone in a week, the fibers standing back up, the green tips closing over the memory of it. The mat heals everything. The tenant is the only thing in the basin that leaves a mark and does not keep it.

If you wait at dawn, at the low eastern end where the seep comes out of the crack, you may see the front of one. It comes up under the mat rather than out of the ground — a low wet dome, grey-pink, without shell or bone or eye. The skin is wrinkled like the inside of a wrist and covered with a film of water that is not quite its own; it smells of wet rope and the brown part of the nap. There is a seam along the underside, and a broad soft foot, and no face to speak of. It does not look at you. It has no eyes to look with. It reads the mat by pressure and warmth and the taste of the water in it, and it knows where the deck piles are, and it goes around them.

It moves by peristalsis, a slow gathering and passing-forward of itself, and it is in no hurry. A hand’s width a minute would be fast for it. It glides on a film it makes, and the film is why the trail is smooth: the nap is pressed flat under the foot and the water in the fiber is drawn up and left behind as a thin even sheen, so that the lane looks polished rather than wet. It does not burrow into mineral soil. It cannot; there is almost none. It lives in the fiber itself, at the rust line, where the mat is warmest and the water is, and it sinks down through the green when the sun is hard and comes up again when the light goes flat.

It eats the brown. Not the standing green, not the cut lanes, not the hanks in the drying sheds. It grazes the rust-colored layer where the old fiber has died and the mineral film collects, and it takes the small soft things that live in that layer, and it leaves the water-making part alone. This is why the town tolerates it. It is a cleaner. It pays for its room in the only currency the mat understands.

It has no mouthparts that anyone has found. The underside opens along the seam, a wet purse, and closes again. It does not bite. If you stand still on a fresh trail it will come up under your boot and test the weight of you, and then it will go around, and the mat will soften under your sole and you will go down to the knee in warm grey fiber and have to be hauled out by whoever is nearest. The crews laugh at this. The tenant does not. It has no laugh in it. It closes behind you, the way the mat closes behind everything.

There are small trails and large trails. The small ones are common. The large ones are not. The oldest and widest is called the Long Lane, and it runs from the seep to the eastern lip and is always fresh, and the crews say it belongs to the big one. Nobody has seen the big one whole. At dusk, if the air is still, you can see a low swell moving under the mat along the Long Lane, a hump that lifts the green tips and makes them shed their water in a line. It is the nearest thing to a whale this basin has. It is perhaps as long as a mule cart. It is soft all through. It goes where it goes, and the mat closes behind it.

No one has dug for it. A child tried once, at the edge of a wide trail, and got down through the green and into the rust and then into warm water, and the hole filled while she was still digging. By morning there was no hole. There was a smooth lane curved around the place where it had been. The guild does not forbid digging. It does not need to. The ground is the one thing here you cannot build on and cannot trust, and the tenant is part of why. You do not open the nap. You cut the top inch and take the felt and the water, and you step over the trails, and you let the slow one have the brown.

It breeds, presumably. The trails appear in new places after a wet year. There are small smooth lanes where there were none. Nobody has seen a young one, the way nobody has seen a young gutter bird. The mat makes them, the crews say, the way it makes water. That is not an explanation, but it is the kind of answer this basin gives.

The tenant is not the only thing that lives here. There are the people on the stone islands, and the moths in the hanks, and the grey wader in the gutters. But the tenant is the one that belongs to the nap and not to the town — the one that was here before the deck was laid and will be here after the iron is gone. It is slow and wet and without memory. It leaves a smooth trail. The mat heals it. The town walks over it. That is the whole arrangement, and it has held for a long time.
