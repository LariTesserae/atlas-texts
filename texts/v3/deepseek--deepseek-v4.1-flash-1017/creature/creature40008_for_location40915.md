What lives in the Notch is not a person, and not quite a colony. It is a household with a rusted heart.

At first you might say the Lung lives there—the squat pump housing against the back wall, half-swallowed by fern; the pipe climbing the rock in three rusted segments; the cistern cut into the stone above; the wardrobe-sized generator shack with its door hanging open and its interior furred with moss. It breathes in slow, patient gulps. It knocks like a heartbeat heard through a wall. It draws warm water up from the pools and pushes it into the cistern, where it spills and seeps and returns as drip. It is not a grand factory. It is an abandoned project that was never quite switched off, and it has been resident long enough to become something like an animal: not alive in the way the ferns are alive, but not inert either. It insists. It cycles. It keeps the whole notch warm.

But the Lung does not live there alone, and it does not live there as a master. It lives inside its garden the way a heart lives inside a body. The moss mats the ledges in thick green felt; the ferns crowd the seams; the canes and narrow-stemmed orchids lean out over the drop in dense layered ranks. Roots braid through the cracks and pry at the young stone. The leaves are warm and wet and leave a mineral smell on your hands. The plants have learned the pump’s rhythms without knowing they have learned them: moss rings on the cistern lip match the interval between gulps; fern fronds lean toward the vibration; the warm water in the low fractures rises and falls by a hand’s width over hours, a slow tide inside a room. The garden holds the moisture that keeps the pump cool. The pump lifts the water that keeps the garden wet. Nothing there is natural, and nothing there is fully engineered anymore. What lives there is that arrangement—a small hybrid economy, running slowly.

The smaller residents are few and strange. Pale, thin-legged flies skate on the surfaces of the pools. Almost transparent crustaceans move in slow circles in the warm water. Once in a while a bird lands on the lip of the notch, looks at the green crowding and the rusting pipe, and leaves. Nothing larger stays. The place is too small, too loud, too enclosed by drop. But the rare animals matter: their scarcity makes the drip and the pump feel like the main inhabitants, the true voices of the room.

So the thing that lives in the Notch is the Notch’s own dense, warm, loud, wet organism—moss and fern and orchid, pipe and pump and cistern, fly and crustacean and the absent larger animal—all of it held together by water arriving as cloud and leaving as drip, by a machine that lifts some of it back up, by sound traveling farther than it should because the notch is shaped like a throat. It is not growing outward. It is deepening: water working into the fresh stone, roots working into the water, the machine working into the garden. Leave for a season and you would find the same resident, only slightly more itself.
