What lives in the Weep-Bowl has no single outline. It is not a keeper standing at the rim with a lamp. It is a tenancy — a resident distributed through the terraces the way damp is distributed through cloth. If you need to call it something, call it the Weeper, but understand that the name is a convenience and the thing is a crowd.

Its body is the tube-dweller thickets first. Thousands of little chalk chimneys, each with a feathery crown combing the water, clustered so thick along the seams and shelves that the rock looks upholstered. The Weeper has no face, but it has ten thousand mouths, and they are all interested in movement. When your hand goes into the pool, the nearest thicket furls chimney by chimney, a slow ripple of retraction that follows you around the basin. That is not fear, exactly. It is the bowl noticing weather.

Under the terraces, in the wet dark of the overhangs, the Weeper keeps its walking parts: blind crustaceans the size of thumbnails, white as peeled root, moving sideways over one another in slow carpeted crowds. Flatworms the color of milk. Snails grazing the mineral felt with patient rasps. Somewhere rarer — the thing that leaves a track in the sediment like a rope being dragged — there is a larger organ of the same body, seldom seen, soft and pale and unhurried, which comes out when the water is still and goes back when it is not. None of these are separate citizens. They are all one habit, one appetite, one slow colonial attention.

The Weeper does not live in the water so much as in the going of the water. It lives in the warm seams where the springs come up and the cold air comes down; it lives in the condensation film on every wall; it lives in the scalloped lips of the basins where the water has been spilling for so long that stone has grown smooth as a throat. Its false vegetation is its own mineral breath — sinter fingers, hollow straws, grown outward where the splash lands. Its true thickets are animal, and they are always combing. It has no eyes. It does not need them. It reads the bowl by vibration and mineral taste and temperature, by the difference between still water and water that has just been disturbed by a hand.

And it has learned the machinery. Not in a human way; not with plans. The copper-and-iron wheel turning in its own worn slot, the pump housing half-swallowed by sinter and shuddering every few minutes, the verdigrised pipes vanishing into rock and coming out again — these are part of the Weeper’s shell now. The tube-dwellers crowd the warm outflow pipes. The blind crustaceans shelter under the housings where the water is still. The settling tanks brim with bands of mineral silt, and something in the bowl grazes those bands. The machines were built to do a thing whose point is lost, and now they do another thing: they make warm edges and still pockets, and the Weeper occupies them.

It is not still. It is actively going. It combs. It furls. It shifts under ledges. It goes briefly still when a whole sheet of water releases from an upper ledge and clatters down the terraces, and then it resumes. It grows a hair’s breadth a decade. It keeps time by drips, hundreds out of phase, a texture rather than a rhythm. It is cold-blooded and slow and extremely numerous. Nothing in it flies. Nothing in it is bigger than a spread hand, except the rope-track thing, which is bigger only in the way a rumor is bigger than a fact. But there is no surface in the bowl that is not already occupied.

So when you stand on a terrace worn smooth, you are not standing in an empty room. You are standing in the Weeper’s lung. The whole bowl is breathing — water arriving, water departing, stone accreting at the rims while the channels deepen, the wheel turning, the pump shuddering, the thickets combing and furling, the blind crowds shifting. It has been breathing this way since before the machines. It will go on after them. And it has already registered you, not as a person, but as a warm, strange weather that has come into the bowl and disturbed the water.
