In the Kettle lives a kind of patient, sleepless caretaker that has forgotten it is anything but the weather. It is not a person; it has no face, no voice you could call a voice. It is more like a habit the place has, a hand that keeps reaching for the same kettle-long task. It lives in the threadwork under the moss, in the squat warm house on the eastern rim, in the water's habit of staying at its level. It is old enough that everything it does looks like nature happening. Its whole life is small adjustments—a channel nudged a finger's width, a warmth held steady through a cool night, a pool kept from going sour—done with the unhurried care of someone who has all the time there is and no one to tell.

The animal life of the Kettle is crowded and quiet, and most of it lives in the shallow seeping water.

The translucent swimmers live in the pans and the channels in soft, drifting clouds, each one finger-long and barely there, more a bend in the light than a body. They graze the algae in visible scallops, turning as one when the water shifts, and they do not scatter when you wade in—they part and close behind you and go on grazing. In the warm afternoons they rise toward the surface and hang there, catching the light from beneath so that the whole pan seems to be full of pale lanterns.

In the moss and above it, the insects are thickest: glassy-winged things that move in slow, honeyed loops, and small cloud-midges that gather in columns over the water—living pillars that you walk through and that close again behind your back. They do not bite. They do not swarm at your face. They simply live in the warm still air, and the columns sway with the light.

There are moths out in the daylight, which should not be, and they are the most numerous of all. Broad-winged, milk-colored, silent. They rest in pale drifts on the moss and the bark of the two great trees, and when they lift they lift in a body, all together, and resettle, all together, like a breath being taken and let go. Nothing eats them that you can see. They are simply the plenty of the place, made visible.

Between the islands of fern and liverwort, the small mammals cross the bare gold pans—some hopping, some slinking close to the ground, quick and wary and aware that the open clay is the worst place to be caught. A few of them are tame in the way of animals that have never been hunted by people; they will stop and look at you with a frank, incurious eye and then finish their crossing. They live in the root-walls of the two old trees, in the reed hollows, under the soft rim-moss, in burrows that are kept warm and dry by something they do not understand and have never needed to.

The hunter is the one you will not see whole. You will see a change in the light on the water, a bending in the reeds, a place where the moths are suddenly not. It moves between the islands and it is fed and it feeds, and it is part of the hum in the way the seep is part of the hum—kept, patient, going about its work.

And the birds come down to drink in the last bright hour, dozens of kinds, none of them calling. They are the strangest of the fauna because of the silence: they alight, they dip, they lift, their throats work and nothing comes out. A place this rich should be loud with them, and it is not. They have learned the Kettle's manner, or the Kettle has taken it out of them, and they go about their drinking like small ghosts of birds, all color and no chime.

What lives in the Kettle, more than anything, is the arrangement itself: the water kept, the warmth held, the moths going up in their pale breathing, the swimmers grazing their scallops in the tinted light, the hunter in the reeds, the slow seep whispering, and under it all the low hum of a thing that has been keeping its hand in the water for so long it is mistaken for the ground. At dusk, when the rose light holds and holds, the whole crowded quiet of it settles like one animal settling, and the Kettle breathes out, and everything that lives there breathes out with it, and gets on with its long, soft, unhurried living.
