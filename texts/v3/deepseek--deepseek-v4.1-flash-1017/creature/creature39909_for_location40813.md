# The Wind-Scream

To meet one of the Wind-Scream is to first meet the sound of them. They do not speak so much as project, their voices thrown like stones against the canyon's endless roar. You hear them before you see them—a sudden bright eruption of syllables, sharp and clipped, each word carved down to its most efficient shape. A conversation between two Wind-Scream at twenty paces looks like a dance, or a fight, or both: broad sweeping gestures, heads tilted to catch the shape of words on lips, hands flashing in a grammar of their own.

They are not tall people. The canyon has bred them compact and dense, centers of gravity kept low against the wind's constant tug. Their bodies are lean but powerful, shoulders and forearms disproportionately developed from a lifetime of hauling, climbing, and holding fast. Their hands are their most remarkable feature—broad-palmed and thick-fingered, the skin of their palms like cured leather, ridged with calluses that have calluses beneath them. A Wind-Scream hand can grip a rope slick with mist and not slip. It can feel the subtle vibration in a vine that means the anchor above is failing. It can strip fiber from a leaf in one motion, twist it, and have a length of cord before a slower person has decided where to begin.

Their skin is the color of the canyon itself—a deep, warm brown, weathered to something like the texture of old wood. The sun above does not reach them often, but the wind does, a constant abrasion that leaves their cheeks and forearms faintly rough, their faces lined early with the squint of those who have spent years reading the air. Their hair is universally dark, worn long and bound back with cords, or cropped close to the skull for those who work the highest platforms where the wind is most vicious. Some of the elders have hair gone the color of the pale fungi, a striking white against their dark faces, and they wear it unbound as a mark of status—they have survived long enough to stop caring what the wind does to it.

---

Their clothing is a direct translation of their world. Nothing is wasted, nothing is decorative without purpose. They wear layered wraps of treated hide and woven fiber, cut close to the body to deny the wind any grip. The fabric is dyed in the colors of the canyon—ochres, deep browns, the dull red of the stone—so that a Wind-Scream standing against a wall is nearly invisible until they move. Their garments are pieced together from countless smaller scraps, each one saved and stitched, so that a single coat might contain the history of a dozen older garments, a dozen repairs, a dozen hands. They are walking archives of their own economy.

Around their waists they wear the tool-belts that define their trades. A rope-weaver carries bone hooks and wooden gauges, hanks of raw fiber waiting to be twisted. A carver carries blades of knapped stone and volcanic glass, each one wrapped in protective hide, each one named and known. A grower carries pouches of spores and seeds, wooden stakes for training vines, a small clay vessel of the mineral-rich water they use to nourish the youngest plants. And everyone, without exception, carries a length of cord—their personal rope, their lifeline, coiled and ready. To be without cord is to be naked.

---

They do not think of themselves as a people of the canyon. They think of themselves as the canyon's people, which is a different thing entirely. The distinction matters to them. The canyon is not their home; they are the canyon's inhabitants, its temporary tenants, its careful stewards. They speak of the stone as if it were a sleeping creature, of the wind as a voice that must be listened to, of the vines as elder relatives who must be asked before they are cut. A Wind-Scream child learns to place a hand flat against the rock face and feel for the vibration that means the stone is stable. They learn to watch the glass-winged moths, to note the direction of their flight and the height of their drifting, because the moths know things about the air that people do not.

Their language is full of wind. They have seventeen words for different qualities of gust, nine for the sounds a rope makes under strain, forty-three for the various moods of the deep canyon's exhalation. They do not say "good morning." They say something that translates roughly as *the wind is kind today* or *the air carries no threat.* Their curses are all about silence—*may you hear nothing but your own thoughts* is a grave insult, a wish of madness. To be alone in true quiet is their deepest fear, their most potent bogeyman.

---

Their bodies have learned the canyon's rhythms. They sleep in short, deep intervals, four or five hours broken into pieces, their minds trained to find rest in the noise. They wake without alarm, their internal clocks tuned to the subtle shifts in the wind's pitch that mark the turning of the day. They eat lightly but constantly—small meals of dried fungus, vine-fruit, the pale, sweet tubers that grow in the damper crevices, the occasional skitter harvested for its rich, mineral-heavy meat. They drink water collected from the mist, channeled down the walls in gutters of hollowed wood, pooled in stone basins carved directly into the cliff.

They age differently than surface-dwellers. The constant damp and the mineral-rich air preserves them, slows the visible signs of time. A Wind-Scream of sixty might look like a surface-dweller of forty, still climbing, still hauling, still working the ropes. But their hearing fades early, worn down by the relentless roar, and the eldest communicate entirely through gesture and lip-reading, their hands moving in a dialect all their own, rapid and fluent, a language of the body that the young must learn if they wish to speak with their grandparents.

---

They are not a solemn people. The canyon demands too much attention for that. They laugh loudly, often, and at strange things—a rope that snaps at the wrong moment, a child who misjudges a leap and lands in the wrong platform's netting, the way the wind will sometimes, without warning, change direction and send everyone stumbling. They find humor in the absurdity of their own existence, in the sheer improbability of a city built into a crack in the world, in the roaring chaos that is their home. They tell jokes about falling, about silence, about the surface-dwellers who come (rarely, so rarely) to trade and cannot hear themselves think. They are a people who have chosen to laugh at the abyss rather than fear it, and the laughter is a kind of defiance.

But there is a seriousness beneath it, a deep, quiet reverence that surfaces in their rituals. They mark the turning of the seasons not by the sun—which they rarely see—but by the migration of the glass-winged moths, by the flowering of certain vines, by the changing pitch of the wind's song. They have festivals of sound and festivals of silence. On the longest night, they gather in the deepest platforms and raise their voices together, a great choral shout that counterpoints the wind, a moment of human sound against the eternal roar. And on the shortest night, they fall silent—all of them, together—and listen. They listen to the wind, to the creak of the ropes, to the distant drip of water, to the sound of their own breathing. It is the only time they hear it. It is the only time the canyon is quiet, and even then, it is not truly quiet. It is just the absence of their own voices, and that absence is a kind of music too.

---

They are born knowing the ropes. A Wind-Scream infant is strapped to its mother's back in a sling of woven vine, and by the time it can walk, it is already learning to balance on the swaying bridges, to grip the vine-ladders, to find its footing on the narrow, irregular platforms. They do not speak of teaching these skills. They speak of *remembering* them, as if the canyon itself has always known how to be climbed, and the children are merely learning what they already are.

Their names are descriptions. A child is named for the wind at the hour of their birth, or the quality of the light on the stone, or the sound a particular rope made on the day they took their first step. A Wind-Scream might be called *Gust-That-Carries-Seed* or *Rope-Sings-in-Dawn* or *Water-Finds-the-Crack.* To shorten these names is a form of intimacy; only family and close friends use the clipping. A stranger who attempts it is gently corrected, taught the full shape of the name, reminded that words have weight here, that every syllable is a part of who someone is.

---

And what do they believe? They believe in the deep. Not a god, exactly, but a presence—the warm, humid darkness at the bottom of the canyon, the place where no one has ever gone, the source of the mist and the wind's voice and the glass-winged moths. They believe that the canyon is alive, not metaphorically but literally, a vast slow creature that breathes the wind and dreams the vines and has been dreaming for longer than anyone can remember. They do not worship it. They do not pray to it. They simply respect it, tend to it, live within it as careful guests. To harm the canyon is to harm themselves, not through divine punishment, but through simple, practical consequence. The canyon has no wrath. It has only the wind, and the wind is enough.

Their dead are given to the deep. There is no burial here, no ground to bury in. The body is wrapped in vine-fiber cloth, weighted with stone, and lowered on a rope into the mist, down and down until the line goes slack and the body is gone. The rope is then cut, its end left to sway in the wind, a marker of the one who has passed. The name of the dead is spoken aloud for a year, then never again. They are not forgotten—they are simply returned, given back to the canyon that made them, their substance becoming the mist that feeds the vines that feed the living. It is not a sad death. It is a joining.

---

The Wind-Scream are a people of the edge. They live on the lip of an impossible drop, in a place that should not sustain life, in a roaring chaos that should drive anyone mad. And yet they thrive. They build their bridges and weave their ropes and tend their vines and raise their children and laugh at the abyss and listen, sometimes, in the brief moments of communal silence, to the sound of their own breathing against the endless, patient roar.

They are not waiting to be discovered. They have always been here. They are the hidden heart of the canyon, its warmth, its voice, its careful hands. They are the Wind-Scream, and they are at home.
