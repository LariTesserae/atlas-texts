Let me tell you about Vess, who keeps the pumps.

She is not the only pump-keeper — there are three, and a rota, and a company that sells pressure — but she is the one the town means when it says the pumps, the way you might say the miller and mean one miller despite there having been forty. She is sixty or thereabouts, and she has been in the Bason her whole life, and she is small in the way people get small down here: not thin, but compressed, drawn down into herself by decades of low ceilings, so that she moves through the terraces with her chin tucked and her shoulders ahead of her and her hands a little out from her sides, feeling for walls she has not needed to feel for in forty years.

Her hands are the part of her that is most herself. They are broad for her body and the knuckles have gone soft and pale and rounded with the damp, and the nails are ridged and thick as horn and she keeps them short with a file because a long nail in a pump gland will tear and take the quick with it. The skin of her palms is the color and texture of a thing that has been left in the outflow — not quite callus, not quite sodden, a permanent half-cured state. She can read a pipe's temperature through the back of her fingers without thinking about it. She can find a leak in a dark gallery by the change in the air on the inside of her wrist.

She wears felt boots and a coat of oiled wool that has never once been dry through and has therefore stopped trying, and which smells of lanolin and iron and the particular greenness of the bloom, and she carries a lamp on a strap because her hands need to be free, and she carries a second lamp unlit on the other hip, because the rules say do not carry a lamp into the fourth gallery alone, and Vess keeps the rules the way she keeps her pumps — not out of reverence, but because you neglect them once and then you find out what they were for.

She does not name the wheel. Nobody does. But she has a way of resting her palm on the housing as she passes, the way you'd touch a horse's neck, and the wheel — which is old and out of true and turns at the pace of a man walking with a bad leg — keeps its own counsel about it.

**Her work** is the stations. There are eleven of them, up and down the terraces, set where the water wants to gather and be made to go somewhere else: sumps with a trickle out, pans with a lip, a weighted accumulator that she bleeds every morning and that hisses at her like something settling in its sleep. She walks the circuit twice a day, three times when the seep is heavy above, and she carries a leather roll of spanners and picks and a little pot of tallow, and she stops at each station and listens before she touches anything. This is not superstition. A pump that is about to fail sounds different, and the difference is easier to hear than to see in a bad light, and she has heard it maybe a dozen times in her life and each time she has been right, and each time the town has not quite thanked her because the town does not quite know how.

Her other work is the crabs. The small pale ones, in the smelter outflow, the ones the children are forbidden to handle and handle anyway. She is the one who goes down with a sieve and a soft brush and counts them, four times a year, and writes the counts in a ledger with a pencil stub she keeps behind her ear, and the counts have been steady for thirty years and the town has no idea why she does it and she could not tell you either, except that somebody counted them before her and left her the ledger and she has never found a reason to stop. The ledger is in a cupboard in the pump-house and the earliest entries are in a hand she does not recognize and are dated two hundred and forty years back. She has not read them all. She is saving them.

**She goes to the edge of the light.** That is the thing about Vess that the town knows and does not say. Not into the dark — that is for the young and the foolish and the ones who have not yet learned what the eels know — but to the edge of it, down in the drowned gallery, where the fourth lamp is shut off to save oil, and she stands with her one lamp held low and out and waits. She has done this since she was a girl and got caught doing it and was whipped for it, and she has kept doing it, and the things that come to the edge of the light have never once come past it, and she has never once been able to say what they are, and the waiting is the whole of it. She does not know what she is waiting for. She suspects it is not for her.

**She talks.** Not much, and not to people, mostly — or rather, she talks to the pumps, low and steady, a running commentary in a voice like a hedge being clipped, and the pumps do not answer but they do not need to. She has been known to say *there now* to a gland that has stopped weeping, and *you'll hold* to a fitting, and once, when the accumulator failed and the town went dark for an hour and the smelter cooled and set in its belly, she was heard through the mill door saying, over and over, *I know, I know, I know,* and it was not clear to anybody whether she meant it for the machine or for herself.

She is the only person in Vess who has been to the top gallery — the crack-riddled attic place nobody lives in — and come back with something to say about it, and what she said was: *the crack it comes through is wider than I thought, and there's a smell of weather up there, and it comes in fast.* Nobody asked her to go again and she has not gone again.

**She has a room** on the second terrace, one room and a half, with a window that looks out on the street-stair and a pot of water by the door that must have its trickle out, and she empties it every morning into the channel and refills it from the channel, which is the long way around and which she does anyway. She keeps the ledger and a spare pencil and a photograph of a woman she does not talk about, and a cast of a hand that came up out of the old pump-house bloom, exact to the knuckles, and that she keeps in a drawer and does not look at and does not throw away.

She is not old, quite, but she is the age at which down here you either get to be the one they listen to or you get quietly wetter and stop coming up the stairs, and Vess has chosen the first, and the choosing is in the set of her jaw and the way she stops on a terrace and the town stops with her, waiting to hear what the water is doing.

What she knows, and does not say, is the thing the brass plate is trying to say. Still water here does not stagnate. It structures. She has watched it in her own pot on a night when she forgot the trickle — watched the surface go from flat to furred to faintly, patiently patterned, and she had not slept and had not looked away and had changed the water at dawn and had never told anyone. And so she walks her circuit, and she bleeds the accumulator, and she keeps every sump trickling, and she counts the crabs, and she stands at the edge of the light, and she is the reason the town keeps its shape — not because she is fighting anything, but because she is the one who has understood that the shape is the thing you have to keep, and that it is kept the way Vess keeps everything, one trickle at a time, patient as a fern, at the pace the fall allows.
