There are no people in the Settle, and no animals. The population is a bureaucracy of tissues — a single vast plant-polity, or a negotiation among parts of one, in which every resident is also a job. You will not meet a person here. You will meet a task that has grown a body.

The layer-keepers are the most numerous. They are broad, blind, creeping sheets of pale fiber that move a few centimeters a day over torn weave, pressing dead leaf into board and board into older board. They are road crews and archivists at once: when a limb tears loose, they are the reason it does not fall far; when a machine sheds a plate, they find it, swallow it, and keep the pattern of it in their tissue like a copied page. They have no faces and no front or back, only a warm leading edge that tests the surface ahead and a cooling trail behind. If you cut into the Settle, they will find the wound before you have finished making it.

The conductors are taller and slower to replace. They are the colonnades — pith-cored trunks and conduits that carry hot carbonate slurry in slow peristaltic pulses, and with it something that functions as instruction. They do not speak. They lean, they press, they change temperature, and the change travels. A conductor that has stood for a thousand years may lie down over an afternoon, and the pressure wave of its falling is not a disaster but a sentence in the local dialect. Their “attention” is a gradient. If you stand near one long enough, you will feel it adjust: the heat on that side of you rising a fraction, the conduit shifting its pulse, the whole line considering whether you are weather, wound, or ore.

The radiators are the workers of the factory floor. They look like door-sized leaves and are not leaves; they are exothermic organs, flushed and dull, running a chemistry that eats heat and mineral and movement. They hang in rows wherever the growth has been trained into halls, and they exhale. Around them the air is thicker and hotter, and the salt glaze on their veins is the same white scab that forms on any torn stem. They have no interior life worth the name — or rather, their interior life is the work, and the work is enough.

The movers are the strangest to meet, because they move. A bladder inflates, rolls a little, anchors again. A hooked organ snaps shut on nothing, then opens, then snaps. Tendrils feel their way along a seam and withdraw. They are not beasts; they are hands, immune cells, scouts, the plant’s equivalent of a person sent to check a noise. They will come to a heat leak, a new wound, a boot-print, and test it with the patience of a root. They are curious in the way a root is curious: not with attention, but with contact. If one anchors to you, it is not biting. It is asking whether you are part of the floor.

And the quieters are everywhere, though you will not see them as residents. They are the baffles and spiral chambers and foam-fleshed traps that eat sound. They are ears and mufflers at once. The Settle is deafening to itself — constant collapse, constant birth, pressure waves turning over like a sea — and the quieters metabolize that turbulence into another centimeter of warm tissue. They will register your footfall as a small irregularity in the local noise and route it away. They are why the place sounds like a library while it is being rebuilt.

The old machines live here too, but as livestock or bones. The pumps still pump; the plant sleeves them, feeds them, half-digests them, keeps their rhythm. The engineers who built the first of it are gone past asking, but their shapes survive in the growth the way a trellis survives in a vine: not as ghosts, as scaffolding. The plant will finish digesting them eventually and grow replacements from the pattern.

The whole of it is coordinated without a center. There is no brain in the Settle, no throne, no foreman. There are gradients — heat, pressure, mineral concentration, the slow chemistry of wound and repair — and the gradients are the government. It does not watch you. It registers you as a new warm irregularity, a wet-adjacent thing in a world with no water, and it begins, very slowly, to decide what office you might be made to serve. It will not hunt you. It will route around you, seal around you, sleeve you, and — if you stay long enough — incorporate you into the next layer.

So the inhabitants are not a species. They are a finished settlement still metabolizing its own changes: layer-keepers pressing the past into floor, conductors carrying heat like orders, radiators exhaling the factory’s work, bladders and hooks testing the dark, quieters swallowing the noise of it all. Ask who lives there and the answer is the work itself — very old, very patient, and still, in its muffled and turbulent and utterly dark way, growing another body for whoever comes after.
