No one lives in the Throat. That is the first fact, and it is not the whole answer.

The thing that lives there is the splint. Not a person, not a beast, not even, strictly, alive — but resident all the same, in the way a held breath is resident in a chest. It is the lattice of ceramic pins and grown-glass struts rooted into both faces of the crack, the long warm skeleton that refuses the mountain’s slow wish to close. It has a body, if you allow the word: pins for bones, struts for tendons, a fine mineral tracery blooming at the joints like scar tissue that has learned to be ornamental. It runs the whole height of the fissure. It is under enormous load. It ticks.

It does not eat. It does not reproduce. It has no face, no hunger, no memory you could hurt. But it has a metabolism. It takes in the mountain’s patience — the patient grind of young rock settling into itself — and pays it out as amber light, as pumping, as the small constant labor of keeping the galleries dry during the draw and wet during the climb. It is brightest at the pins where the load is greatest. It groans on schedule. It has a temper only in the sense that a machine has a temper: during the draw the pins run hottest, the air thins, the vapor scalds, and the lattice complains like something with a grievance. During the climb it drips. That is its conversation.

The closest thing it has to a population is the chemistry in the seep basins. The salt and sulfate and not-quite-frost bloom there in whorls, fans, thin dendritic feathers. They are not life. Nothing in the Throat eats anything; no moss, no lichen, no weed, no insect, no bird. But the blooms organize themselves anyway. They break and re-form with the nine-hour tide. If you sit in one of the empty rooms for the length of a climb, you can watch them decide. They are the Throat’s transient skin — not residents exactly, more like a rash that knows the schedule.

The old people are not there. They emptied the place tidily a long time ago and left no ghosts worth the name. What remains of them is habit written into machinery: the rule of the draw, the promise of a width, the basins carved to catch water, the galleries kept dry and full by something that no longer needs anyone to maintain it. Their absence is resident too. It has a shape, and the shape is forty tidy rooms strung up and down a hot dark crack, each one a pocket of amber light with nobody in it.

If you stay, you become a temporary resident, and the Throat will teach you its etiquette. You climb during the climb. You do not climb during the draw. You learn to hear where you are in the cycle from the syncopation of the dripping. You learn that the long low groan every nine hours is felt in the sternum before the ears. You learn that the vent hush underneath everything is the sound of the whole world breathing through a narrow place. You are a guest with sweat and intention, and the Throat does not need you. It will keep its schedule whether or not you are there to keep it.

So the occupant is not a who. It is a what, and the what is a patient nonliving caretaker: a splint with a metabolism, a city like a spine, a column of hot damp dark that condenses and evaporates, expands and contracts, ticks and glows and holds itself open against a mountain that would very much like to close. It lives the way a held breath lives — by continuing to hold.
