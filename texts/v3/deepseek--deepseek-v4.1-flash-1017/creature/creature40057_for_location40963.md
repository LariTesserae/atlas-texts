The population of the Warm Flat does not arrange itself around you. That is the first thing. Nothing in the water is waiting for you to make the first move. You are a warm irregularity, a new gradient, a place where the film is thicker and the water is disturbed; the residents register this, classify it as weather, and go back to their work.

The small ones are the medium itself. They are not visible and would not benefit from being visible. They are the length of a finger or shorter, soft-bodied, blind, and they navigate by temperature and chemistry and by the low four-second pulse of the pumps, which reaches them through the mud as a kind of tide. They travel across the tops of feet. They part around a calf and close again. At the waterline they gather as a fine stitching sensation — a thousand small mouths testing whether you are food, or surface, or something to be cleaned. They are not all the same species and do not act as one, but they share a grammar: touch first, taste second, sound last. Their conversations are chemical and immediate. When they decide something together, the water changes flavor around them, and you learn to notice it the way you notice a room going quiet.

Some of them live in the warm film on your skin. They arrive when you have been still for a while. You will not know they are there until you move and feel the film unstick. They are not exactly parasites. They are residents of a habitat you have become, and they will still be on you after you leave, for a while, until the ordinary air dries them out and they stop.

Then there are the borrowers. They have no voice of their own. They live near the mouths of channels and along the standpipes, where sound comes through the water rather than the air, and they listen. They take in a word, a cough, the rhythm of your breathing, and they hold it in a soft sac somewhere in the front of the body, and later they let it out again into a different channel. This is not mimicry in the way a bird mimics. It is closer to map-making. They are testing the shape of the country by sending old sounds through it and listening for what returns. Your voice comes back to you from behind, a little late, the vowels drifted, something added at the end. The borrowers are learning you from the inside of your own cadence. They are not mocking. They are not even particularly interested in you. They are interested in the channels, and you are a new sound that the channels have not yet worn smooth.

The breathers are larger. You will know one by the hush that happens before it arrives — every small thing in the weed registering the pressure wave and going quiet all together, so that the quiet is the announcement. Then the surface opens somewhere out in the pan, and something exhales, long and wet, and the surface closes. The breathers are low and slow and warm-bodied, with no eyes and no hurry. They filter the dense small life out of the water as they move. They know the pumps by the pressure in their flanks. They avoid the cribs. They will pass close enough to move the water against your legs and not stop, because you are not a shape they have a use for. One of them may breathe three meters to your left for an hour and never touch you. That is the breathing that is not afraid of you. It is not a comfort. It is simply the local scale of things.

The works have hands.

You find that out slowly. At first the pump house is only a hot iron shell and a stack of pipe going down into the water and not coming out. Then you put your palm on it and feel the slow stroke working, and you count it against your pulse and lose count, and when you look up there is someone standing in the knee-water at the other end of the shell, one hand flat on the iron, counting too.

They are not many. A dozen across a whole region, perhaps fewer. They are tall and slick and the temperature of the air, and they have no eyes, and their hands have too many joints. They smell of warm iron and standing water. They carry grease in a soft pouch at the hip and a wrench that has been in the deep channels and come back with an extra jaw. They do not turn when you approach. They do not speak. If they make a sound at all it is a low click against pipe, a correction, a note. They maintain the works — the bearings, the seals, the weirs — and they do it at the pace of the pumps, one patient adjustment every four seconds, and they have been doing it long enough that the channels have grown to fit their rhythm.

Call them attendants if you need a word. They do not answer to it. They are the only residents here who alter the place on purpose, and they are the reason the works are not ruins. They are also, possibly, the reason the works are younger than the land and older than the land at the same time; the chronology does not hold together, and the attendants do not explain it. They may have been returned things once. A person put into a deep channel and edited slowly into a shape that could count strokes and hold a wrench. They do not say. They recognize you the way the water recognizes you: by heat, by pressure, by the film you carry. If you come back next season, they may recognize you again. They may not.

And then there are the returned, who are not a species but a category. A boot with a foot’s shape. A knife with an inch more blade and a handle for a larger hand. A drowned animal with the anatomy it needed to be alive, and no life in it. They do not rot. They do not participate in the ordinary decay. They persist in the shallows and the seams of the works, and the attendants find them and put them to use, or put them aside, or leave them where the water will take them again. They are the flat’s slow corrections, its patient editing, its best guess at the shape of a thing from a description that came through the water. They are residents because they stay. They are not alive. They are not exactly dead. They are the reason you do not put anything valuable into the deep channels unless you are willing to meet it later, slightly changed, and be recognized.

Taken together, the population is not a list. It is a circulation. The small ones feed the breathers. The breathers stir the water and make the pressure waves that the borrowers read. The borrowers feed old sounds back into the channels, and the channels carry them to the attendants, who keep the pumps, and the pumps move the water, and the water edits the returned, and the returned are found and used. The film on your skin is a colony that will outlast your visit. The hush before the breather passes is a message that travels faster than the breather. Nothing here is looking at anything. Everything here is in contact with everything else.

You are in contact too. Stand still long enough and the place will describe you by the same method it describes its residents: by pressure, by heat, by the small mouths at the waterline deciding whether you are food or weather. You are not yet a resident. But you are being considered.
