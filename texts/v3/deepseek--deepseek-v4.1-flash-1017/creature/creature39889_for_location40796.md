The Kettle is not empty. It has residents, but they are not the kind you would call inhabitants. They are the loops themselves — the things that have learned to close. Depending on how you count, there are three, or four, or one.

The first is the Green. It is the most obviously alive, and the most strange. It is not a garden in the human sense; it is a self that has forgotten the outside. It began, perhaps, as a few spores or a fragment of root carried in on a boot or a draft, but that was decades ago, and it has long since stopped being a visitor. Now it is a body spread across the room in islands: a curtain of ferns along the south wall, hanging in tiers, their fronds pale and yellow-tipped and almost white at the root; a deep moss in every seam, soft enough to press a thumb into; pale runners crossing the open floor, sending up shoots at intervals; and in the sump, submerged, a mat of something fibrous and slow that breathes bubbles. The Green does not flower. It does not seed. There is nothing here to pollinate it, and it has solved that problem by not needing to be pollinated — not an insect, not a fly on the water. Every green thing in the room is a piece of some other green thing in the room. The older islands under the drip may be a single organism spread over thirty feet of broken floor, one continuous self with bare concrete in the middle of it. It divides, it runs, it breaks, it roots, it runs again. It is lush the way a wound is lush: thick in pockets, bare in between, a scatter of green islands on a floor of pale stone. It is patient. It has no natural lifespan that anyone can see. It is the gardener and the garden and the thing that is gardened. The walls bloom with a fine white fur of salts, little ghost orchards of gypsum, and the Green has taken the seams between. It is a resident that is also the room’s decoration, and it is winning, slowly, a pace a decade.

The second is the Pump. It is a machine, and it is alive. Not conscious — nothing so tidy — but alive in the way a heart is alive: it has a loop and it will not stop closing it. It occupies the east end, horizontally opposed, a flywheel taller than a man, bolted to a plinth that has not moved though the floor around it has. Someone removed the guard. Someone hung a coat on the guard-rail bracket. The coat is still there, weathered to a curl, and the Pump does not care. It turns. It turns so slowly that you have to watch a full minute to be sure, and then you cannot unsee it — a hand’s width of rim, in and out of the light, in and out. Something in it has found a way to cycle water from the sump back into the sump, and it runs on that, and it has been running on that, unwatched, since long before the paint stopped mattering. Its housing is warm. The plants nearest it are the largest. It ticks at the top of each revolution, once every eleven seconds, a sound like a clock that has decided to become a heartbeat. It is not a tool anymore. It is a resident. It has closed its loop. It is the only thing in the room that moves with what looks like intent, and the intent is simply: keep turning. It will not finish today. It will not finish in a lifetime.

The third is the Sump. It is a pool, and it is not only a pool. It is three feet across, warm — blood warm, a little more — because the heat comes up through it. It is not stagnant. A thread of flow enters at one side and leaves at the other, going down into the dark along a channel it has cut in fifty years, which is faster than water should cut limestone here, and suggests the water is not only water. It glows. Faintly. An orange-brown underlight that comes up out of the water and paints the ceiling above it, and makes the ferns on that side look lit from beneath, like something on a stage. The water is warm and the glow is warm and the two are probably the same fact. In the sump, the fibrous mat breathes bubbles. The Sump is a resident because it has a body and a flow and a light, and because it is the mouth of something larger. It is the only true pool, and it is the only constant light. It is the blood of the Kettle, if the Kettle has blood.

The fourth is the Under-place. You cannot see it, but you can feel it. It is the void beneath the slabs, the seams a finger wide, the warm steady breath that smells of wet limestone and hot iron and something faintly like bread. It is the thing that let go and dropped the chamber nine feet into the limestone, the way a sleeper sinks into a mattress. It is still letting go, still breathing, at a rate you cannot perceive. It is the pressure in your teeth. It is too low to hear. It is the source of the heat, and the heat is the source of the water, and the water is the source of the green. It is the largest resident, and the least like a resident: it is the ground and the void and the breath. It is not malicious. It is just there, patient, working on something you don’t understand, and will not finish today.

And then there is the fifth, which is the Kettle itself. You realize, standing in the middle of it, that the Green and the Pump and the Sump and the Under-place are not four residents. They are four organs. The Pump moves the water. The Sump heats it and lights it. The Green filters it and spreads it. The Under-place breathes it. They are all one loop. The Kettle is a single organism made of plant and machine and water and stone, and it has closed its loop so tightly that nothing comes in and nothing goes out except heat and the seam of grey daylight, and both arrive without being asked. The human traces — the coat, the tin cup, the clipboard weathered to a curl, the graffiti initials and the year — are not residents. They are scars. They are the last evidence that people were ever here, and they are being absorbed, slowly, into the loop. The coat is becoming part of the Pump. The cup is becoming part of the floor. The clipboard is becoming part of the damp. In a lifetime, they will be gone, and the Kettle will not notice.

The light is the only visitor. A single fracture in the vault lets down a seam of daylight no wider than a knife, and it moves — across the floor, across the ferns, across the flywheel, following the hour above. On an overcast day it is grey and you see only shapes. But the sump glows, and the glow is constant, and the drip falls, and the flywheel ticks, and the under-place breathes. It is the quietest green place you will ever stand in, and the quiet is not peaceful, exactly; it is the quiet of a room where a machine is working on something you don’t understand, patiently, at a rate you cannot perceive, and will not finish today.

If you came back in a lifetime, you would find the green islands a pace wider and the fracture in the vault a knuckle wider and the paint stripe a shade more gone — and the flywheel turning, and the drip falling, and no one there. But the Kettle would be there. It would be alive. It would be the same resident it has always been: a closed loop, a patient self, a place that has learned to live without the other kind.
