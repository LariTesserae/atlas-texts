There is no single occupant of the Standing Plain. To ask what lives there is to ask what lives in a city that is also a reef and also a weather system. The plain is inhabited the way a lung is inhabited by air: constantly, structurally, by things that pass through and things that stay and things that become the wall.

The most conspicuous residents are the **builders** — not a species so much as a guild of colonial animals that have made architecture their metabolism. A tower is not a tower to them. It is a colony, and a colony is a committee of mouths. Each zooid is small, soft, blind, and connected to its neighbors by a living tissue through which chemical instructions move slowly enough to be called decisions. They do not plan the arches and lattices and ribbed spires. They secrete. They bud. They thicken the western face because the current presses there, and they thin the eastern face because the current pulls, and after a thousand years the whole colony has become a single leaning sentence written in calcium. When a tower falls, the sound travels for miles; the fragments are not debris. They are nursery. Within a day, new builders have settled on the broken western faces, and the new growth is already combed downstream.

They are not alone in the building. There are **cementers** that work in sheets, laying down low crusts over old ruins until the edges blur; there are **borers** that tunnel galleries through the accumulated shell-work of earlier generations, so that the plain is porous in places, a sponge of old rooms. Some of the tunnels are occupied. Some are being reabsorbed at their mouths by newer builders who cement their foundations directly onto the ruin of the old. Nothing is wasted. The dead are ground, not buried.

The **shavers** are the plain’s herbivores, though there is no green here to eat. They are hand-sized, armored in overlapping plates, and they move in slow herds of four or five, scraping the surfaces of the structures down for the mineral in them. Their mouths are not jaws but files. They leave pale scars that look like frost, and they will not cross their own old paths — not from superstition, but because their paths are marked in chemistry, and the marks say *this way was already eaten*. You can track a herd by the fresh scars and by the way the scars stop at the edge of an older trail. They are not curious about you. You are simply a new surface, and they will test you with a file if you stand still long enough.

Above the pavement drift the **sifters**: low, soft, boneless things with a fringe of tentacles trailing beneath them. They do not walk. They hover just over the shell-ground, taking what falls from above — the marine snow of the upper water, the dead and the half-dead, the fine organic grit that settles out of the grey. Their bodies are nets. When the current stiffens, they anchor themselves by extruding a mucous thread and let the water comb them flat; when it slackens, they resume their slow patrol. They have no eyes that anyone has found. They taste the current and move toward the richer side of it.

Inside the calcium, there are **tappers**. You will hear them before you see anything: a rhythm from within the wall, patient, irregular, going quiet when you approach. They are small and pale and live in the interstices of the builders’ old work, and the tapping is probably feeding, probably communication, possibly both. They do not come out. They have made the inside of the city their outside.

And then there are the **sealed ones**, the residents of the chambers with no doors. These are the plain’s true mystery, and I will not pretend to solve it. What is seen is this: a closed chamber, smooth and blind, built around nothing or around something that died inside and was never let out; and sometimes, from within, a dim amber pulse, very slow, as regular as a resting heart. It is not a reflection. It is not the current. Something in there is glowing, or something in there is being fed by something that glows. The tappers go quiet near those chambers. The shavers do not scar their surfaces. The builders sometimes bud a new wall directly against them and then stop, as if the chamber were a word they had been told not to say.

Out past the edge of sight there is **the voice**. It is not a resident of the plain in any way you could map. It is something large, something with a throat or a bladder or a resonant cavity, and now and then it gives off a long descending tone that rolls in across the flat and makes the lattices hum in sympathy. No one sees it. The sifters orient to it. The shavers pause. The builders do not pause; they have no ears, but their western walls thicken anyway, as if the sound were a kind of current. It may be a predator. It may be a mate call. It may be the plain’s only music.

All of them are shaped by the same law. The builders lean downstream and thicken on their western faces. The shavers follow paths that curve away from the wind. The sifters anchor when the current stiffens. The tappers go quiet at the vibration of a stranger. The sealed ones pulse in their doorless rooms and are not opened. Everything here is busy, and everything here is slow, and nothing here is passive. Every surface you touch was made by a mouth. Every ruin is somebody’s foundation. The inhabitants do not need you. If you stand on the plain long enough, you will become weather to them: a pressure, a shadow, a thing that made the tappers go quiet and then, when you leave, did not matter.
