What lives in the Drip-Fields is, first, the tuners — though they would not call themselves that. They would say the Fields are inhabited by the work, and they are the hands of it. They are a people of wrist and ear, long-armed from ladders, sure-footed on reed catwalks, hands calloused and permanently cold. Their breath goes out ahead of them in the dark and joins the general fog. They climb with their wrists because their fingers have gone numb. They carry a lamp in one hand and a dripping rag in the other and anoint the beams in dry spells like priests of a wet religion.

They live stacked in the galleries, in cells whose ceilings are other cells’ floors, sleeping wrapped in greased cloth under the slow green weather of the glow-film. Their days are measured in drips and their genealogies in joints: who pegged this gallery to that one, whose auger cut the lip that sends water *there* and not *here*. A child learns the frame by knuckle-tap before they learn to read a tally. They know the health of a plank by its note, the health of a joint by its silence. They speak little in the high works and listen much. A tuner’s argument is a series of taps on a beam. A tuner’s grief is a gallery left out of tune.

They are not cave-dwellers. They are instrument-makers who have become part of their instrument. They shave and test and peg by ear, and they are generations deep in a score nobody has heard whole. The plan exists — cut into beams, counted in drip-calendars, answered from gallery to gallery across the basin — but no living tuner has walked all of it. The far works come back as rumor, a faint complicated music on wet nights. So the people of the Drip-Fields live at the raw edge of a thing always being built: ladders against nothing, scaffolds around unfinished ribs, fresh pale wood and clay still waiting for daub.

But they are not the only inhabitants. The wet wood blooms with the glow-film — not plant, not quite anything with a name — a patient smear that eats the damp and gives back light. Pale thumbnail beetles walk the gutters in colonies, grazing the film. Eels live in the stitched-hide cisterns, kept or tolerated or worshipped in some small domestic way. Something long and blind is rumored in the deeper tanks. Things live in the cords and come out at the change of the wet. Nobody keeps beasts in the Fields; the beasts that stay are the ones the gutters can support.

And under all of them, the Fields themselves live — not as a shelter that happens to be noisy, but as an instrument that happens to be inhabited. The fog arrives, condenses on the roof-combs, runs into the tuned gutters, strikes slate and felt and copper and pitched plank, and the whole valley answers. What lives there is a chorus: the tuners who wet the beams and listen, the glow that makes a cloudy sky of the ceiling, the beetles in the channels, the eels in the tanks, the rumor in the cisterns, and the patient, never-finished music they are all inside of.
