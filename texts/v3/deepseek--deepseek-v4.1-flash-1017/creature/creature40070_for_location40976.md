There is no single resident. The place is inhabited the way a lung is inhabited by air—everything is passing through, everything is held, everything is being used. If you demand a face, though, take the ants.

The leaf-cutters are the clearest “who” in the Warm Floor. They are not a swarm; they are a city with too many hands and no walls. Their roads run under the litter, worn smooth and slightly sunken, swept clean by decades of traffic, and they cross and recross until the forest floor has a nervous system. At the junctions there are rules—yield, pass, queue, turn—enforced not by officers but by contact, by smell, by the sheer pressure of bodies. The columns carry green confetti: pieces of leaf, flower, fungus, anything cut to size and hauled home. They do not eat the leaves. They chew them into pulp and feed a crop that feeds them back, a fungus that exists nowhere else and does nothing else. The colony is a furnace and a farm at once. Its nest mounds breathe: dozens of openings exhaling warm, damp air because inside, millions of workers are composting, planting, weeding, pruning, regulating temperature, removing waste to heaps that steam faintly and stink sweetly and are themselves farmed by other things. The queen is there, but she is less a ruler than a source. The colony is the individual. Its body is the road system, the fungus garden, the brood, the waste heap, the warm exhalation from the ground.

Around the ants, the smaller residents live in layers. Millipedes process over the roots in slow armored caravans, their legs moving in a wave. Beetles are everywhere, in the litter, under bark, in the old treefall hollows, in the ant waste heaps, in the bodies of the dead. Tree frogs sit in the water-holding leaves of bromeliads and aroids, small and brown and silent until rain, and then the air fills with them. Each bromeliad is a private reservoir with a private community: larvae, mites, tiny predators, detritus, a whole village in a cup of warm water. Mosquitoes find the thin skin at wrists and ankles. Midges hang in the columns of dim gold light. Something larger moves in the canopy every few minutes—never seen, only heard: a branch, a rustle, a cascade of displaced drops, a call like a hinge. That hinge-call is a resident too, even if it never shows itself.

The plants are residents as much as the animals, only slower. The trees stand close enough that you must turn sideways, and their roots run over the surface because the surface is where the air is. They cross, graft, form lattices. Lianas are residents that use other residents as scaffolding. Ferns live in crotches. Aroids climb. Moss furrs the north side of every stem. Every leaf comes to a drip-tip so the understory is a slow irregular rain that never quite stops. The trees compete for light, and they choke each other, and then one falls and lets a column of light down and the scramble begins again somewhere else. The dead do not lie here. A fallen trunk is gone in a season, chewed into the economy, distributed, converted. Fungi and bacteria are the invisible majority, the ones who make sure nothing accumulates except the living. The waste heaps are their cities too.

And the water itself is inhabited. The pools in buttress-root basins and old hollows and your own footprints are tea-colored and still, filmed with pollen and the bodies of small drowned things, and they are colonized before evening. The air is inhabited—spores, pollen, fine dust of chewed leaf, midges, a haze that is water choosing not to be rain. The surface of every leaf is inhabited by things too small to see, and every surface is used. If you stay long enough, you are used too. The mosquitoes find you. Something runs a thread from your shoulder to a branch because you are a surface now, and here every surface is a surface.

So the Warm Floor is lived in by a confederation: the ant colony as its industrial heart, the fungus gardens as its crop, the waste heaps as its compost, the frogs and millipedes and beetles as its scavengers and small citizens, the unseen canopy thing as its voice, the trees and lianas and bromeliads as its slow architecture, the fungi and bacteria as its clearance system, the water and air as its medium. It is not a place with inhabitants. It is an arrangement that keeps running, at temperature, processing. The residents are the running.
