# The Residents

The bench is not empty, and it is not crowded. It holds, at any one time, a few hundred small lives and four or five large ones, and it holds them in the way a hand holds sand — loosely, individually, without much pressing.

What lives here can be sorted by when it wakes up.

## The Drinkers

They are the bench's morning crowd, and they arrive all at once, so that for about twenty minutes after first light the wet stone is moving.

The flat ones come up out of the seams. They are grey, many-legged, the width of a thumbnail, and they are not fast — they walk with a deliberation that reads as fussiness, each foot placed, each foot tested for grip. They go to the threads where the water crosses a low lip and they drink at the very edge of it, standing in single file along the runnel as though the line were drawn for them. They touch antennae when they meet. They do not crowd. It is the only courtesy you will see on the bench and it is observed almost perfectly.

The rounder ones do not walk to water at all. They sit in the basins and let it come to them, and their skin takes it in by contact, and every so often one shifts a little in its scallop — a slow rotation, sunward, a few degrees a day — so that you can come back in the afternoon and find them turned, like the hands of a clock that has decided not to alarm anyone.

The pale moth-things are the only ones that move with any excitement. They come up out of the cracks dry and stone-colored and they land on the wet lips and their wings go translucent, and then they are glitter, briefly, everywhere, in a way that has no order to it at all. By midmorning they have folded themselves shut again and gone the color of the bench, and you could put your hand down among them and not know.

## The Lip-Birds

There are four kinds and none of them are bigger than your two fists. They come in on the morning wind, low, from the shelves below, and they land in the shallow scallops and work them in a rolling hop — down the lip, around, down the next lip.

They are the bench's only real argument.

Each bird has a basin. Not a territory, not a range: a basin, one, a particular scallop among hundreds, and the ownership of it is old enough that it does not seem to be about food or water at all. A bird will drive another off a scallop it has already drunk from and will not drink from again that day. It will return to it. It will defend it in a fight that is mostly posture and a single hard note — and the note goes out dull and small, as everything does here, as though the fight were happening in another valley.

The lines shift by inches over a season. You can watch it. A young one takes a corner of an old one's lip in the fourth week of dry and holds it, and the next morning the boundary has moved and will not move back.

## The Below

The oldest residents do not come out.

They live in the wet dark of the sponge, in the riddled interior of the stone, and they are the reason the stone is riddled. Pale, soft, slow, patient, eyeless or nearly so. They keep the water in the rock and give it back on the slow cycle, and whether they do this as a byproduct of breathing or as a system somebody built them into is a question the bench is not going to answer.

They surface when it rains. All at once, up through the pores, a whitening of the stone in the first minutes, and then the whole bench is crawling with things that have no business being on a surface, and by the time the rain stops they have gone back down.

They are the reason the small drinkers can live here at all, and the small drinkers are the reason the birds can, and the birds are the reason the thin hunters come up from the gullies in the afternoon heat to sit at the windward lip of a tinaja and wait — because that is where the rule says to drink from, and the hunters have read the rule too.

## The One Who Walks the Rounds

And then there is the other large one.

It is person-shaped, and this is the least interesting thing about it. It is squat, and its proportions are wrong in a way you can feel before you can name — the arms a little long, the trunk a little deep, the head set low, the whole of it built more like a thing that crouches and reaches than a thing that stands and speaks. Its skin is the grey-buff of the wet stone and goes pale as it dries, over the same hour, at the same rate. It is heaviest through the shoulders. It does not appear to have a neck that turns well.

It comes out at the drinking hour. It always comes out at the drinking hour.

It walks a circuit, and the circuit does not vary: from the near fold, up the tilted steps, along the four great flutes, out to the scatter of posts, and down again to the housing at the far end, where it goes in and is gone until the next morning. It does not walk fast. It stops at every third or fourth basin and crouches and puts two fingers into the low lip of it — hands, let's say hands, something that does the work of hands — and clears the silt out of the throat of the runnel so the water can find the tinaja below. It does not clear them all. It clears the ones the bench needs cleared, or the ones it has always cleared, and there is no way to know which.

It drinks from the windward lip. It leaves the rest.

It does not acknowledge you. Not in the way of a thing that is afraid, and not in the way of a thing that is being rude; it does not acknowledge you the way you do not acknowledge the wind when you are working. You are weather to it. You are a thing that has arrived in the middle of its hour and will be gone by noon, and it has outlasted a great many noons.

You will try, once, to speak to it. Your voice will go dull at a dozen paces and it will not turn, and afterward you will not be sure it heard, and you will suspect that the bench has eaten the middle out of your question the way it eats everything.

Whether it is the same one, season after season, nobody could say. There are no marks on it. It makes no marks. It does not name itself and does not seem to keep a name. It may be one long life or it may be a line of them, each learning the circuit from the one ahead of it, each indistinguishable from the last because the work is the same and the work is all there is to see. It is the only thing on the bench that does unnecessary work — the flutes would run without it, the tinajas would fill, the bench sweats whether or not anyone is watching — and this is how you know it is alive, and not a part of the machine: that it does its rounds anyway, out of habit, out of care, out of a fidelity so old it has no content left in it, only the shape.

It goes into the housing and the pulse inside slows slightly and the drinking hour ends and the bench goes dry to look at and quiet and empty.

And it is there in the morning. It is always there in the morning. The dew comes up through the stone and the flat ones file along the runnels and the birds begin to argue over their scallops, and the thing comes out of the housing at the far fold, crouching a little, pale as the wet rock, and begins — patiently, without hurry, without any visible reason — at the near fold, going up.
