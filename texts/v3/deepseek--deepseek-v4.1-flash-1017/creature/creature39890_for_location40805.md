There are no people in the Flue Plain, and nothing there has hands. What lives there lives as a body with several scales.

The first inhabitant is the plain itself: the continuous mat of moss, liverwort, sedge, cotton-grass, wiry shrub, and lichen that is not ground but substance. It is saturated and cold and alive at the surface, and below the surface it is still digesting—an eight-meter body of peat in which bacteria work without air, slowly, making heat and gas and pressure. That slow fire never flames, but it is a population too, and it is why the place is warm in rings and cold everywhere else. The mat takes a footprint and holds it, then forgets. It is the floor and the body and the thing everyone stands in.

Out of that body rise the flues: two hundred hollow, warm, gray-green towers, scattered three hundred meters apart, twelve to twenty meters tall. They are the plain’s lungs and its oldest citizens. They are not trees and not buildings. They grew the way a lung grew, because a bog needed to vent. Each has a pitch, a plume, a crown of pale blades that hang and clatter. Each is ringed with valve collars where birds nest. When vapor is heavy they clatter; when it is very heavy they shriek. They do not move. They do not breed, or not in any living memory. They are a fixed set, old, blind, warm, vocal, rooted in their own decay. When one finally falls—black and hollow and cold—its ring cools, the bazaar around it closes, and the plain has one more interval of nothing.

Around each flue’s base, the outflow keeps a disc of ground twenty meters across thawed and warm, and there the population knots. This is where the plain is crowded. Insects rise in columns in the warm updraft, so thick the light goes granular; spiders live in the columns. Birds nest in the valve collars and the crowns, loud, calling across the empty intervals to other flues because the plain is open and a call carries. Frogs crowd the meltwater at the base and start a chorus at dusk that does not stop. Small mammals burrow in the warm peat, and the things that eat them come too. A single flue holds more living mass than the hectare around it. Each ring is a bazaar, a city-state, a mob.

Between the rings the plain is nearly empty of bodies—a hare, a passing fox, a few birds, a great deal of moss and quiet. So the density of life there is strange: long stretches of almost nothing, then a step into a ring and you are surrounded, deafened, mobbed. Low saturation punctuated by knots. Emptiness balanced against anthills.

And there are the residents that are not organisms exactly. The peat is a battery; the charge builds and bleeds up the flues, and on still days the air aches. Then the ground-storms crawl along the wet channels at ankle height, discharging low and flat, blue-white, loud. In the fog those discharges leave pale standing figures of glow that linger for hours, drift a little, dim slowly. The animals stop noticing the old ones. They are lamps left burning by weather that has already passed. The fog sometimes answers by putting the shapes somewhere else. They are not alive, but they are inhabitants in the way a hearth is.

Nothing there is young. There is no colonization, no regrowth, no new ground. The basin reached its final form long ago, and everything in it has settled into the thing it is. The flues are its lungs; the rings are its cities; the mat is its body; the leftover lights are its lamps. It metabolizes rather than builds—a factory with no engineer, wet and cold and loud and electrified and very old, venting its own slow decay through two hundred warm throats.
