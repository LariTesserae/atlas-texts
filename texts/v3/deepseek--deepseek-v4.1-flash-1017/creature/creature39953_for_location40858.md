The thing that lives here does not walk so much as *seep*. It has no hard edges. Its body is a long, soft-lobed mass the color of wet clay, mottled with patches of pale luminescence that pulse in slow sympathy with the fungi on the trunks. In the pearlescent fog it is nearly invisible until it moves, and even then it moves with the patience of a drip finding its way down a slope.

It is called—by itself, in a language that is mostly a modulation of its own skin—the Weeping One. Not a name. A state of being, a job description. There are perhaps forty of them across the whole of the tiered basin, and they have been here since before the terraces were old.

The Weeping One is amphibious in the truest sense: it does not need to distinguish between the air and the water. It breathes through its hide, a vast, gelatinous lung that draws oxygen from the fog as readily as from the warm pools. The distinction between its flesh and the saturated moss beneath it is one of degree, not kind. Its lower surface is a fringe of fine, root-like filaments that it can extend or retract, and through which it draws not only moisture but the slow chemistry of the stone itself—trace minerals, dissolved gases, the exhalations of the buried fusion.

Its face is a broad, flattened dome, without eyes in the way a stranger would understand. Instead, there are three deep, wet pits arranged in a loose triangle, each one filled with a fluid that ripples in response to pressure and vibration. Through these it perceives the world: the weight of the air, the thrum of the monoliths, the distant pattern of a beetle's flight, the subtle shift in humidity when a new pocket of steam is about to release from a fissure. It does not "see" light, but it is not blind. It feels the gradient of brightness as a kind of warmth, a quality of the glow that seeps into its skin and tells it where the canopy is thin.

What it does is tend. This is its purpose, the residue of an ancient instruction that has become instinct, then identity. The Weeping One moves along the terraced basins in a slow circuit that takes it three years to complete. It knows every channel, every fused edge, every pool. When a channel begins to silt up—not with dirt, but with the mineral accretion of centuries—it settles over it, extends its filaments into the stone, and excretes a gentle solvent, a slow acid that softens the obstruction without harming the engineered rock beneath. It takes months. It does not mind.

It also tends the "listeners." The monoliths hum, and the Weeping Ones hum back. Not with sound exactly—their bodies produce a low resonance through the vibration of their internal fluids, a counter-frequency that the monoliths seem to drink. The Weeping One suspects, in the way that a thing can suspect without language, that this resonance is what keeps the monoliths from going still. The hum is a kind of conversation, a check-in. *Are you well? Are you still? Are you holding the balance?* The monoliths answer always the same: a slight deepening of their tone, a reassurance. In all the long memory of the Weeping Ones, no monolith has ever fallen silent. They do not think about what would happen if one did.

The Weeping One has no name for itself beyond the shared one. It does not have a concept of loneliness, because it is never alone in the way that matters. Its world is dense with sensation: the constant percussive conversation of the water, the flicker of mirror-wasps catching its luminescence and throwing it back, the soft settle of dirigible-beetles resting on its back to sip the condensation from its own hide. It is, in a sense, a feature of the landscape, a large, wet, breathing stone that happens to think. The distinction between itself and the terraces it tends is not one it would find meaningful.

Now it is moving down a shallow channel, its body flowing over the fused stone, leaving a trail of faintly glistening slime that will dry and become another layer of the slick, non-reflective film on the rock. It has been feeling a small perturbation in the hum of the nearest monolith—a slight waver in the frequency, too subtle for any instrument but its own body to detect. It does not know what it means. It will settle over the monolith's base in two days' time, extend its filaments into the stone, and listen more closely. It will hum a question. It will wait.

The heat presses down. The fog condenses on its back. Water drips from the canopy onto its dome, and it absorbs it without thought, the moisture becoming part of the vast, slow, patient body that is itself a part of this place. It is not in a hurry. Nothing here is in a hurry.
