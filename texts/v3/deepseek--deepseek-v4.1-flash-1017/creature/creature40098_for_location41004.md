The people of Kettledown are a small, damp, practical population—a parish’s worth of folk, maybe fewer, tangled together by kinship and rota and the shared knowledge of which boardwalk planks will take a boot without complaint. They are not amphibious, not quite, but they have the habits of people who have negotiated a long truce with water. They are short-ish and solid, broad-handed, thick-ankled, with the soft, blanched look of skin that has spent generations in warm humidity. In the amber light everyone takes on the same tea-stained cast; their hair is kept short or tied back, their sleeves rolled, their boots the most carefully maintained things they own. They wear mire-cotton—always a little damp, by design—proofed with tallow-fig wax, patched at knee and elbow, and they smell of warm wood, iron, steam, and the faint sweet-rancid note of the presses.

They are not sickly. They are damp-strong. Their lungs know steam; their hands are wrinkled as if from a bath that has lasted several generations. They walk with the boardwalk gait—weight forward, knees soft, each foot placed as though testing a sentence—and they can feel a loose plank through the soles of their boots. Their voices are low and unhurried, with pauses built in for the pumps. They speak quietly not because they are dull but because the engines have taught them to leave gaps. A Kettledown child learns the sound of a healthy flywheel before they learn to read a gauge; they can hear a bearing going bad through a wall, and they will tell you so without looking up from their work.

Their houses are low and close, each with its cistern, its little veg-plot, its shrine. The shrine is rarely grand: a shelf with a lamp, a smooth stone, a brass fitting salvaged from a pump, a patch of bloom allowed to grow on a board and glow faintly in the dark. They do not pray to these things exactly. They check them. They keep one dry shelf—a cupboard oiled and lamp-warmed—for paper, salt, matches, a comb, a photograph, a letter from someone who left. Dryness is a luxury and a mild eccentricity. Visitors are given the dry shelf and a cup of condensed water from the seed-hall glass, which tastes of green and iron.

Work is the shape of their days. There is a rota, and the rota is older than anyone’s memory. Pump-tenders, seed-hall gardeners, drying-shed pressers, vapor-line jointers, boardwalk menders, cistern scrapers, bloom-scrapers, reed-cutters—most people are several of these things by turns. The pumps are the first law. The pumps run or the water rises or the crop dies or the sheds go cold. Maintenance is not a virtue here so much as a form of manners. A person who lets a joint hiss or a gutter clog is not wicked; they are unserious, and being unserious is the closest thing Kettledown has to a sin. They do not hurry. Hurry is how you fall.

They eat plainly: vapor-grass roots roasted in fig oil, starchy and sweet and slightly narcotic; flatbread made from traded grain when there is grain; pickled reed-hearts; the pale fleshy fruit of the marsh, which nobody eats except children daring each other and regretting it. They trade mire-cotton and tallow-fig oil for what the wet ground will not give them. Meals are often communal, taken in the warmth of the drying-sheds or on the lee side of a pump-house, and they eat slowly, listening to the water move.

They mark time by pump cycles and water levels rather than by any clock you would recognize. They know the deep season, when the bloom is thick and the pumps run hard and the whole district seems lit from below. They have quiet observances—the greasing of a flywheel, the reading aloud of the rota, the scraping back of bloom from a doorframe without killing it—but no grand ceremonies. They do not find the amber light strange. They will look at a visitor with polite incomprehension if told the light has a colour, and then they will tell them that the bloom was here first, that Kettledown was built in a place already alive, and that the bloom has simply been patient.

They are not waiting for anything. They are not building toward anything. They are running the pumps and tending the bloom and keeping the water where the water needs to be, and the doing of it is the point.

And they are not the only residents. The bloom lives in every seam and forgotten corner—soft, pale-green, faintly luminous, old as the wet ground, scraped back from working surfaces but never destroyed. The pumps live in their brick houses like domesticated giants, breathing and sighing and shuddering, and the people listen to them the way you might listen to the breathing of a sleeping house. The reeds live at the edges, knocking softly in the wind. The people live between all of it, warm moving parts in a hydraulic system, belonging to the place precisely because they have never pretended it will be dry.
