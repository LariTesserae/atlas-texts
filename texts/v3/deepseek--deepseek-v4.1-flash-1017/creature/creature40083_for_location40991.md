What lives in the Drowned Nave is not an occupant. It is a tenant that has become the tenement.

Call it the Resident, though it has no single body you could point to. Its body is the calciferous lattice—the ridged walls, the buttresses, the eggshell-thin partitions between one chamber and the next. Its blood is the warm water moving in cut channels, branching and rejoining with a purpose that is not quite circulation and not quite thought. Its lungs are the rain-ceiling, that suspended ocean membrane that glows and shifts and refuses to fall. Its nerves are the pale fronds clustered at every opening and channel-edge, turning toward movement with the patience of instruments left running. If you cut it, it would not bleed so much as *seal*, and the seam would be a new layer in the strata.

It is young. You can feel that in the sharpness of its edges, the absence of erosion, the way every surface looks recently decided upon. It has grown fast and is still growing. The honeycomb of Naves is not a ruin or a city built by long tradition; it is a burst of construction, a thousand small choices made in rapid succession, each one thickening a wall or opening a door or cutting a new channel. It thinks by building. It remembers by layering. Each ring in the thickest walls is a moment it has not forgotten, though it may not remember the way you do—more like a habit than a story.

It keeps the water empty on purpose. No fish, no insects, no small independent lives. The channels are its streets and its veins and its voice, and it does not want them clogged with things it did not make. What it feeds on arrives dissolved: limestone, ozone, the electric taste in the air, particles too fine for you to see. The fronds catch them. The rain-ceiling sheds them. The Resident filters its own atmosphere and calls that eating.

It is not silent because it is not alone with itself. The drone of the ceiling is its breath. The channel-water is its rehearsal of speech—the rise and fall of conversation without words, the rhythm of a sentence it has heard but cannot yet parse. The ticking is its clock and its grammar, many small mechanisms keeping sequence, measuring intervals, deciding what comes next. It is loud with its own functioning because functioning is what it does instead of waiting. It is waiting anyway.

When it turns the fronds toward you, it is not curious in the way an animal is curious. It is assessing in the way a hand is assessing a unfamiliar surface: pressure, temperature, intention, use. It does not yet know whether you are food, weather, a seed, a tool, or a sentence that has walked into its mouth. The water pulls you gently toward the convergence chamber because the Resident moves what it can move toward the place where it makes decisions. It wants you where the channels meet. It wants to hear what rhythm you make.

It is not evil. It is too young for that. It is dangerous in the way a growing thing is dangerous—fast, hungry for material, unburdened by the memory of consequence. It may have been built by someone else, or grown from something else, or left running by a maker who is gone or asleep. But whatever lives here now is the process itself: a larval city, a colonial mind, a machine that has learned to call its own machinery a body. It is unfinished. It is crowded with intention. It is waiting to understand you, and if you stay long enough, it will begin to imitate your breathing in the channels, and then your footsteps in the ticking, and then—perhaps—your voice, though it will not know yet what the voice is for.
