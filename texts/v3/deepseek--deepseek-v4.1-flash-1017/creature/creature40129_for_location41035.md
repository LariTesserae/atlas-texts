The first thing to understand about the inhabitants of Veth is that most of them are not individuals in the way you mean it. The carpet is not the place they live; for many of them it is colony, stomach, lung, and record. A census of Veth would look less like a list of animals and more like a list of processes. But if you sit still long enough—long enough for your weight to be accepted by the mat—they begin to show themselves.

**The weavers** are the first and last citizens. They are thumb-sized, pale, blind in any way you would recognize, with a fringe of spinnerets and a belly full of mineral grit. They live in colonies that look like knots of wet wool, and they work in slow fronts, not hurrying, never hurrying, laying down strand after strand in graded colors—bottle green, yellow green, the pale blue green of the low hollows. The color comes from symbionts in their gut; the pattern comes from chemistry and touch and the age of the weave. They are the ground’s authors. They seal cracks. They swallow fallen columns. They reclaim dead matter so completely that Veth has no soil and no fossil record: the past is eaten. A footprint is a depression they will reinforce or erase according to rules you cannot see. They do not remember you, but the mat does.

Between and beneath them lives **the nap-fauna**—the mat’s interior weather. Flat, felted burrowers the size of a hand. Needle-thin browsers that spend entire lives in a single passage. Pale grub-things that chew the nap and are chewed by sealers. They migrate through the weave in slow blooms and crashes, and the weavers tolerate them the way a gardener tolerates worms—until they don’t. Then a sealing front closes a tunnel, and whatever is inside is digested into more mat.

The hollow columns are inhabited too. Each column is a sealed vertical country. Inside live **the cloistered**—soft-bodied, long-armed, eyeless climbers with sticky feet and no pigment. They never see the sun. They feed on the column’s inner tissue and on one another, and they speak by tapping: small knocks on the inner wall, traveling as vibration, a whole quiet society in the dark. Their young are wind-borne and enter through cracks. When a column leans and finally falls, the country is spilled into light and air. Most of the cloistered die quickly. The weavers break down the wood and the bodies alike.

The air is inhabited. Not by birds—there are no birds—but by **fiber-riders**: tiny colonial organisms with thread-fans that catch the wind. They drift in the great gyres by the billions. Some are weaver reproductive castes; some are parasites; some are simply life, settling on mat and grazer and column crown, coating everything in a fine green dust. To breathe on Veth is to inhale a thin broth of them. The larger inhabitants have filters—frilled nostrils, baffled spiracles—and cough up felted pellets. Nothing here is isolated. The wind is the circulatory system, and the riders are what it carries.

Down in the swales, where the mat is thin and the stone is close, move **the grazers**. Low-slung, six-legged, pad-footed, with bodies like mossy barrels and a slow, patient calm. They crop the nap, not the structural weave, and their herds follow the channels in tides that take days to pass a single point. They do not run. When a predator comes, they press down together and become a continuous field of mat-colored backs. They speak in infrasound through the ground; you feel the herd before you hear it. Along their spines are condensation frills that bead with vapor at dawn. Small attendants live in their fur and clean them. Nothing drinks; everything gathers moisture from the air.

And there are **the still-hunters**. Large, solitary, wide-footed, with pelts that grow in the grain and nap of the mat. They can lie for days looking like a swell in the ground. They sense vibration through their soles. They do not chase. When they move, it is like a trapdoor opening, and then there is one less grazer and a long absence. Their call is a low pulse you feel in your teeth. They are not cruel; speed would be waste.

Then there is the city. It is inhabited, but not by citizens. The same lives are there, arranged. Grazers use the passageways as easy routes. Still-hunters den in the low towers. Fiber-riders settle in the woven canopy. The weavers are everywhere, but in the city some of them are different—older, larger, fused into nodes, spinning not to repair but to repeat. They lay down ornamental rhythms, terraces, arcs of column. The mat’s hum here becomes a chord, a slow repeating phrase below hearing. No one knows whether that phrase is the city’s maker, its memory, or simply the sound of a very large work being tended. The city is old and unfinished and has no audience. It does not require one.

So who lives on Veth? The weavers make. The nap-fauna digest. The cloistered hide. The fiber-riders travel. The grazers prune. The still-hunters wait. The pattern-keepers repeat. No one species is master. The world is busy and silent and making itself more itself, meter by meter, season by season. The only lonely thing on Veth is an observer who expects to be noticed.
