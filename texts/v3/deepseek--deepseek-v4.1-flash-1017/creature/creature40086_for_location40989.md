What lives there is not one thing. It is a layered ecology, a boiling mineral garden with a food web made of shell and glow and steam—and, near the top of it, a small four-legged creature that has just begun to do something new.

At the bottom are the crustaceans: thousands of them, armored and iridescent, scuttling over the ochre and rust-red crusts in dense, glittering herds. They are not quite prey and not quite sediment. They feed on the warm mineral weep, on each other, on the faint organic film that condenses wherever steam lingers. Their shells crack and re-form with the chemistry of the pools. When they die, their bodies are reabsorbed into the crust, so the ground itself is partly made of them. They are the place’s blood.

Above them are the filterers: soft, pulsating blobs clinging to rock lips and pool edges, glowing with a faint internal light. They do not hunt. They breathe the steam and strain it. They pulse in slow synchrony with the hiss of the fissures, and when the light is strange and shadowless, they are one of the few things that seem to have a definite edge. They are the place’s lungs.

In the larger pools live the older residents: things with too many jointed legs and smooth chitinous bodies, moving with a slow, purposeful grace. They are patient. They wait at the edges of the warm mirrors and take what comes. They do not use tools. They do not need to. They are the established order of this pocket world, and they have been established for a long time.

Then there is the dipper.

It is about the length of a forearm, sleek and wet like a salamander, with four legs and a wedge-shaped head. Its skin is slick and warm, translucent at the edges, and it does not drink—it absorbs. It breathes through its skin and through small gill slits that flutter when the steam thickens. Its eyes are lidless and dark, better at finding the glow of filterers than at judging distance in this shadowless light. It feels the world mostly through heat and chemistry and vibration: the *plink* of water, the *gloop* of mud, the faint pressure of a crustacean moving under its foot.

Its forelimbs are long and spindly, and each ends in a single curved claw, hard as salt crystal. The claw is not a hand. It is a spur, a hook, a lever. And that is the new thing.

The dipper lives in loose kin groups around the warm seeps. It builds nothing. It clears shallow sleeping hollows in the mineral crust by pressing its body into the ground; the hollows fill with warm water and re-form behind it. It leaves faint claw scores on ledges where it has fed. It communicates by tapping the crust and by flushing color along its throat—a slow bloom of rust and pale blue that means *here*, or *mine*, or *wait*. It remembers individual pools. It remembers which ledges yield.

And it has learned to pry.

A young one watches an older one at the edge of a pool. The older one does not drink. It dips one long forelimb into the water, twists the claw under a cluster of armored crustaceans, and lifts. The cluster comes free. The young one repeats the gesture. At first it fails—scrapes rock, gets a clawful of mineral lace. Then it succeeds. The crustaceans come away in a knot, and the young one eats.

That is the moment. It is not instinct. It is imitation and memory and a body used as a tool. The first technology here is a claw used as a lever, and the first culture is a gesture passed from one wet, four-legged creature to another.

They are not people yet. They have no fire—the air is too wet for it. They have no shelter beyond the hollows they press into the crust. They have no names. But they have a habit, and the habit is spreading. Some of them now approach the pools with a certain tilt of the head, a certain slow reach. Some of them fail. Some of them learn. The young watch the old, and the old do not teach on purpose, but the young learn anyway.

They also live with the weirdness. They know that some pools are *slow* and some are *fast*—that time can pool and eddy there, that a creature can age a year in a minute or stay an infant for hours. They avoid the bad water by feel, by a wordless map passed down through imitation. They are careful at the edges. They have to be.

So what lives there? A dense, boiling ecology of shell and glow and steam, and at its top a small, wet, four-legged creature that has just learned to pry. It is not a person. It is a prelude. It has a claw and a memory and a pool, and it is beginning to use the world instead of merely enduring it. The whole hidden bowl is a cradle, and the dipper is the first thing in it to wake.
