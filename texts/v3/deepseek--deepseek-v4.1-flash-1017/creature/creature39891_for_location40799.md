The largest thing that lives in the Wet Basin is easy to mistake for part of it. You will see it first as a hummock—low, grey-brown, hung with the same pale creeping plants that mat the higher ground. Then the hummock takes a breath, and the plants on its back rise a finger’s width, and you understand that the basin has a tenant.

Call them the low ones, if you need a name. They do not.

A low one is roughly the length of a person lying down, but it never stands. Its body is broad and flat, wet as a just-dredged stone, the color of silt and old ash. The skin is not smooth: it is pebbled and porous, always beaded with water, and in the shallow hollows along its spine the pale-leaved creepers root and spread. From a distance it is a hummock with a slow pulse. Up close it is something between a salamander and a seal and a thing that has decided, over a very long time, to become a piece of the ground.

The head is wedge-shaped and almost neckless. The eyes are two clouded beads set deep under a film; they are not much used. The real face is in the jaw and along the flanks—rows of sensory pits that read pressure, vibration, the shape of a pool by the way it answers a sound. The mouth is underneath, wide and soft-lipped, fringed with horny bristles for straining silt. The front limbs end in broad, spatulate digits; the hind limbs fold flat against the body. The tail is thick and muscular and drags behind, rudder and broom both.

They do not walk so much as flow. A low one lengthens, settles, pulls with its front limbs, pushes with its tail, and arches just enough to keep its plant mat above the waterline. It leaves a shallow trough that fills in minutes. It chooses its route by listening: the drainage hum under the basin tells it where the ground is firm, where a pool deepens, where an old channel runs just under the silt. When it hums back—a low note from a throat sac, felt in your chest before you hear it—the sound goes out and returns changed, and the low one knows the shape of the world around it. Others answer, rarely, from far off. That is how they hold their distances. No scent marks, no boundaries you could point to; only the places where one hum stops and another begins.

They eat patiently. A low one sweeps its broad mouth through the silt and strains out the translucent crustaceans, the worms, the small darting fish. It licks mineral crust from stones. It does not hunt; it waits. When the water is highest—if “highest” means anything in a basin that never truly dries—they pair by hum, and the eggs are laid in the deepest pools. The larvae are transparent, almost fish-like, and most are eaten. The survivors grow slowly, flatten, acquire their plant mats, and become hummocks that breathe. A low one may live for centuries. None of them seem to be in a hurry.

They build nothing. Their bodies are their only structures. A low one deepens a pool by lying in it; it opens a channel by dragging its tail through the silt; it smooths a bank by hauling itself over it. The water erases the work almost as soon as it is done. When one dies, it does not leave a skeleton on the surface. It settles into a pool, its hum stops, and the plants on its back root into it. In a few years it is a low rise among other low rises. The others adjust their territories by a note or two. The basin does not notice.

If a low one notices you, it will surface at a distance—just the top of its head and the long ridge of its back—and hum once, a sound like a finger drawn around the rim of a wet glass, but far lower. Then it sinks. It may follow you for a while, testing your footsteps through the ground, but it will not guide you and it will not threaten you. It has no tools, no fire, no names, no language you would recognize as language. It is not the keeper of the basin. It is one of the basin’s conditions: wet, old, patient, and content to remain.
