What lives on the Breathing Stair is not a population so much as a habit of occupation—life arranged in layers, each layer eating, sheltering, or slowly revising the one beneath it.

The first resident is the floor itself. The moss is not a surface; it is a colonial organism, a wet green mat of bryophyte and sedge and dwarf willow stitched together by roots that have been making the same hand-width ropes for a hundred thousand years. It breathes in the wind, rocking slightly underfoot, and it holds water the way a sponge holds a rumor of rain. The lichens are older still—orange and grey sheets eating the soft stone at a pace that makes glaciers look hasty. The moss is the lung of the place; the sedges are its stitching; the dwarf willow is its stubborn low architecture. Together they are the first and most important resident, because everything else lives in the gaps they make.

Those gaps are crowded. In the water films between moss leaves live the true multitudes: springtails, oribatid mites, nematodes, rotifers, tardigrades—small enough to inhabit a droplet, numerous enough that a handful of moss contains a metropolis. They graze, breed, dry out, and come back. They are the stair’s invisible livestock, and the whole visible ecology rests on their quiet accounting.

Above them, the insects. The air on the first terrace is thick with them—beetles massing at the terrace edges, flies holding station over the pools, wasps working the grass, things with too many legs and things with just enough. The sound is layered: close up, a dry crackle of chitin; farther out, a whine; farther still, a bass hum you feel in your teeth where the beetles gather. They are not an infestation. They are the metabolism of the stair. Their larvae work the root mat; their adults feed the frogs and the birds; their bodies fall back into the moss and become moss again. There is no silence here. Silence has been eaten.

The middle terraces belong to the amphibians. The shallow pools—some a meter across, some a hundred—are each their own small state, and each has its own frog dialogue, a crowd of wet-slate frogs calling at dusk in a chorus that is not peaceful but political: a hundred bad musicians agreeing loudly with one another. They are small and numerous and unhurried, because the moss meters out the water so slowly that the frogs have never had to learn to rush. Herons work the pools on long, careful legs. Buzzards hunt the terrace edges. Their calls cut through the insect hum like bells through fog.

And then there is the large one—the simurgh, if that is what it is. It is seen mostly as a shadow crossing the third terrace at midday: enormous, slow, feathered, silent. On a mountain where every other resident announces itself, it has learned the one skill that matters: it hunts without adding to the noise. It may nest in the root-matted risers between terraces; it may only visit. Either way, it is the largest body here, and its silence makes it feel like a resident of a different order—less an animal than a weather system with wings.

The third terrace is where the absent residents left their marks. The low wall that follows the edge and then stops; the turf-roofed pit; the hearth-stone, still bare after generations. The people who built them are gone—empty of people, the stair is—but their residue is habitat now. The wall’s gaps hold shrews and voles and insects; the pit’s turf roof is a shelter for something with small feet; the hearth-stone is a bare mark in the moss, used by birds as a place to crack snails, by beetles as a warming stone. The builders are not residents anymore. They are a stratum. Their hands are gone; their shapes remain as rooms for other lives.

So the Breathing Stair is inhabited by a collective: the slow lichens eating stone, the moss holding water, the root mat shifting underfoot, the springtails in the water films, the beetle hum that never stops, the frogs debating in their separate pools, the herons and buzzards cutting through the noise, the simurgh crossing in silence, and the small hidden tenants of the old wall. It is loud and dense and old, and almost everything here is small. The largest resident is a shadow. The most important is the floor. And none of them are in a hurry, because the stair has nowhere to go and the biology is already home.
