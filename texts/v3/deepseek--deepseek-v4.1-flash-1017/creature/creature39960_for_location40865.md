## The tenders

You know them by their lamps before you know them by anything else — a small yellow moving point in the wet grey, and then the shape of someone walking a lattice with the unhurried care of a person on stairs they have climbed ten thousand times.

They are not tall. Nobody here is tall; the halls are built low and the pipework hangs low and a tall person would spend their life ducking. They are wrapped, mostly — waxed canvas over the shoulders, a hood that sheds the drip, wraps on the forearms where the scraped crust would otherwise get under the skin. The cloth is the colour of the rock and has been patched so many times that it is more patch than cloth, and it is never dry. Nothing here is ever dry. They smell of wet wool and hot metal and the flint-sharp tang of the vents, and they have a settled way of standing that says they have been wet for so long they have stopped noticing it, the way you stop noticing your own breathing.

Their hands are the thing you notice second. The knuckles furred with a fine pale crust that never quite comes off, the fingernails gone glassy, the skin along the sides of the fingers thickened and translucent where the salt has got in and set. They work bare-handed where they can, because you cannot feel a plate's temperature through a glove, and feeling the temperature is the whole job. So they have grown a little of the plateau into themselves. It is not a disease, they'll tell you. It is the deposit. It comes off in a season if you go below. Nobody goes below much.

### How they live

They keep to a few halls that are more sheltered than the rest, where the floors have been laid flat with a deliberate flatness that reads almost as defiance on this tilting rock, and where somebody long ago cut windows that look out on pipe instead of sky. Inside: bunks of oiled canvas, a stove that burns something that comes up in the pipe bundles, a kettle that has been on it since before you arrived and will be on it after you leave. A shelf of jars. A board by the door with the day's valve changes chalked on it in a hand that has stopped bothering with vowels.

They do not keep clocks. They keep the vents. A tender knows it is time to sleep because the third stack has gone quiet, and time to rise because the low run is warm again and the plates will be ready. The day is the argument with the ground, and the ground does not keep hours, so neither do they, except approximately, and they all keep the same approximate.

There are perhaps forty of them. You will not be sure. The works is spread and the shifts are staggered by the ground's convenience and the count is not written anywhere, and asking produces different numbers from different tenders, delivered with the same total confidence. Some have been here all their lives; they were born in the low halls and have never been down, and the thought of a place where the air is thin and the stone is cold fills them with something between pity and distaste. Others came up for a season forty years ago. There is no season. There is the crop, and the crop is always.

### What it does to them

Watch a tender work a lattice for an hour and you will see the trade the place makes with its people. They are calm in a way that is not quite restful. They move slowly because there is no reason to move fast, and they speak slowly and in small amounts, and when they are quiet — which is most of the time — it is not the quiet of a person holding something back. It is the quiet of a person who has been listening to drip for so long that conversation has become a kind of weather: something that happens, that you stand in, that passes.

They are hard to surprise. A vent will open under a lattice they are standing on and they will step off it and go round and turn a valve, and the whole of it will have registered on their face as a small interest, no more. They have the patience of growers, which is a patience for a thing that will not be hurried and cannot be argued with and will, in the end, do what its temperature tells it to do. And they have the particular gentleness of people who spend their days handling something fragile that is not alive — turning a plate, scraping a runnel clean with a hooked blade, breaking off a fan of green crystal into a sack with the flat of the hand, carefully, so the next crop has a clean face to start on.

At night, in the bunk hall, they are quiet and they breathe slowly and they sleep in the wet dark, and the crust on their hands catches the lamp and shines.

### The old ones

Some of them are going the way of the plate.

It is slow and it is not painful and it is not quite a dying. The crust creeps past the knuckles and up the forearms. The voice roughens and the air goes out of it, so that speech becomes a dry small sound that carries only a little way and the other tenders learn to stand close to hear it. The skin on the face takes on the wet sheen of the pipes. They stop minding the drip. And then one day a tender is not on the shift and there is a lattice that nobody tends, and the others will tell you, if you ask, that so-and-so has gone to sit in the warm, and will point out past the town to a low hollow where the vapour stands thick and the crust is deep and there is, if you look, the shape of someone sitting with their back to a warm vent and their hands in their lap, furring over, a millimetre a year, being grown.

They will not be sad about it. They will not be glad about it either. It is what the place does to those who stay, and staying was the choice, and the choice was made so long ago that it has become the shape of a life rather than a decision inside one. The old ones are still there. They are part of the crop. In a way that the tenders do not find strange at all, they are the finest thing the Sinterworks produces — a surface that has been given the right warmth for the right number of years, and has bloomed.

## The sump-crawlers

The only other residents, and the only ones that are alive in the way you would call alive.

Flat, pale, the length of a finger and the thickness of a leaf, they move through the warm runoff on a fringe of hair-fine feet at a pace you have to stand still to see. They have no eyes; they have a pair of long feelers that they sweep along pipe undersides and across the crust, and they can tell one degree of warmth from another across the width of a puddle. They feed on the sinter itself — the soft pale crust, the new growth, the bloom before it hardens — and they are, by the standards of anything, very slow, and very few, and very patient. They are the only thing here that eats.

The tenders do not mind them, exactly. They are a nuisance on a fresh plate and a sign of a healthy warm run and, on a cold night when the vents are low and the halls are quiet, a kind of company. A tender will sometime crouch for a while at a warm pool with a lamp held low and watch one cross the bottom, feeling its way, and the two of them — the tender and the crawler — will be the only two moving things for a hundred metres in any direction, and neither of them in a hurry.

## Whatever crosses at dusk

It comes up from the lower ground, sometimes, when the mist is standing in the hollows and the light is at its dimmest, and it goes along the edge of the works without coming in. The tenders disagree about it. They will tell you it is a kind of animal, and then argue about which kind, and the argument will be old and comfortable and will not be settled. It is big — bigger than a person, the track suggests, if the track is a track and not a seep — and it does not stop to drink, because there is nothing to drink, and it does not stop to eat, because there is almost nothing here to eat, and so it must be crossing to somewhere, and the somewhere is out past the town, and nobody has followed it to see.

What they agree on: it is not interested in them. It has come up onto the plateau for the warmth, maybe, the way anything warm-blooded will come to a warm stone, and it stands in a low seep for a while with the vapour going up around it, and then it goes back down. The sump-crawlers go still when it is near. The tenders put their lamps out and wait, and the waiting is not fear, exactly. It is the waiting of people who have learned that the place has other residents and that the other residents are not theirs to know. Then it is gone, and the lamps come back on, and the drip continues, and someone says something about it and someone else disagrees, and the night goes on being the night.

## The place's own

And under all of it — under the tenders and the crawlers and the thing at dusk — the plateau itself, which is not alive and does not need to be, and which keeps its people the way it keeps its plates: giving them the right warmth, on the right surface, for as long as they will stand there, and growing them slowly into something that catches the little light there is and shines.
