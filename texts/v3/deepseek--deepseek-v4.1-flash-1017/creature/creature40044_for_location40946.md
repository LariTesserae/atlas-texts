The first residents you can count are the limpets. They are the only animals you will see, and “they” is already the wrong word for them. A dozen thick-shelled, blind thumbnails clamped into the dry high pockets of the scallops, scattered across a hundred square meters of wall—but they behave as one habit spread across many bodies. When the water quickens and the room blazes, they open together. The dry ticking you hear in the gaps of the roar is a thousand tiny lids, all on the same cue. They feed on what the shear throws up against the stone: mineral flakes, organic snow, the fine dead matter that comes down from the marsh. Then they close and wait. Their shells are banded like slow tree rings; they have been waiting a long time. They breed by releasing a thread of eggs into the bright water and trusting the current to find a dry pocket. Most of the threads are destroyed. The ones that settle become another ring, another lid, another tick in the meter.

The pale moss and the rust-colored algae and the cress roots trailing from the shaft are the room’s fur, not its owner. They live on mist and stray light. They are alive in the way a stain is alive. They do not keep the place.

The thing that keeps the place—the thing the villages mean when they say the Kettle is a throat—is the one other thing. Nobody has produced it because it cannot be produced without killing it. You will not find it by looking into the water. You find it by noticing that the basin is not a basin. It is the top of a mouth.

Under the floor, threaded through the mineralized ghosts of the old conduits, there is a body. It is not quite flesh. It is a flat, gray, wet mass, folded into the shape of the drainage, pressed into the blind sockets where brass used to be, studded with calcite where its own tissue has mineralized. It has three siphons, one at each sink, fringed with hairs that sort by touch and by fields you feel as a faint tug at a watch-spring and a metallic taste at the back of your mouth. It has no eyes. It has never needed them. It has no mouth in any shape you would recognize; its whole upper surface is a mouth, and the chamber is the throat above it. It is enormous—larger than the chamber, because most of it is spread under the marsh in the old pipe-casts—and it is slow. Its pulse has grown into the standing wave of the organ tone. Its breathing is the shear-hiss. The drips are the clock it keeps. When the room brightens, it feeds. When the room dims, it rests. It does not go fully dark because it is alive.

The fractions it takes are not food in a crude sense. It draws off metals, tannin, heat, the faint electric taste of moving water. It leaves the water colder and cleaner and more ordinary than it came in. That is why the villages say the Kettle is a throat and do not say it as a belief. They say it the way you would say the mill grinds.

The limpets are its commensals. They live on the scraps the shear throws up, not on its flesh. The moss and cress and algae live in the mist it breathes out. The still corners are where its sorting does not reach, and it does not like them; things that fall into those corners are not returned at the rate you would expect. It does not reach up the shaft. It does not need the lamp-standard, and the ceramic sockets in the alcoves are dead attempts by someone, long ago, to measure it or feed it or listen to it. The walkway crosses over it without touching. It does not mind. It has been crossed before.

The one who leaves the coins is not a resident. They are a tender from a marsh village, one of a rota. They come down with a lamp and a dry cup, check the rungs, stand on the grate, leave four coins and a dried flower, and climb out. They do not sleep here. No one sleeps here. The rungs are maintained by the villages, not by a hermit. The shrine is a mark of passage, not a household. The strictness about not putting anything in is not religious. The siphons are fine. A dropped coin, a lost knife, a body—these lodge. The Drinker cannot spit them out. It can only change the sorting around them, and a changed sorting is felt all the way down the marsh.

So what lives in the Kettle is a blind, sessile, distributed animal under the basin; a colony of clocked limpets on the walls; a fringe of mist-fed plants; and a human maintenance habit that passes through. The Drinker is the tenant. The Kettle is its throat. When you climb out, the ringing in your ears is not only the roar. It is the slow pulse of the thing below, still in your teeth. You will tap it out on tables for days without meaning to. That is how you know you were inside something.
