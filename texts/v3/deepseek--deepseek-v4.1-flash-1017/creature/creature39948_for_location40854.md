The people of Sallow Bottom are, first of all, listeners. They live with their ears slightly tilted toward the ground — toward the pump house’s slow clank, the mill’s flywheel pulse, the hiss of a steam vent, the particular suck and bubble of a braided channel deciding to take a new thread. There are only a few hundred of them, small enough that most know which house is on which jack height and which street has been renumbered since spring. They are not rustic and not quite urban; they are industrial people in oiled canvas and wool, rubber boots by the door and a crowbar beside the boots. Their hands are stained with coal smoke, tar, wet brick, and the green scum of cistern screens.

They do not think of themselves as living in a drained lakebed. They think of the creek as a neighbor with seniority. The Drainage Board’s surveyors are the closest thing to gentry: men and women in patched coats carrying painted lath and folding rules, whose marks decide whether a shed stays, gets winched onto new piles, or is jacked up four inches by Tuesday. People bring them tea and argue with them politely. The Board’s clerk can tell you the jack height of half the town from memory. Everyone else can tell you their own: “She’s up four since spring,” they say of a house, or “That’s old number nine, new number twelve — creek took the lower foot.”

Most residents work the machines or work the water, and many do both. Paper-mill hands come home with the stampers’ rhythm still in their shoulders; felt-works people carry the smell of wet wool and dye; foundry men step out red-faced into the cool air with ferns crowding the shaded side of the building behind them. The pump-house operator sleeps light, because if the clank misses a beat half the street wakes. The mill’s steam vents mark shifts and tea breaks. People time their conversations around the noise without noticing. They are loud themselves — a loud town for its size — but it is a layered loudness: machinery, water, frogs, rain on corrugated iron. They speak in the gaps.

Their houses are practical and adjustable. No one has a cellar; they have crawlspaces with screw jacks and lamps, thresholds at different heights, doors that stick after a rain and then stop sticking when the ground settles. Furniture sits on blocks. Cisterns hang under eaves. Gutters are cleaned like teeth. Sheds on rails are winched aside when the creek chooses a new thread, and a house-raising is communal, not exactly festive: neighbors bring jacks, planks, tea, and opinions. People keep their windows fogged and their boots dry-ish. They own more crowbars than umbrellas and more folding rules than mirrors.

The children are river-literate before they are book-literate. They know which puddles are old foundation pits and which are new; they know the survey pegs are not to be moved and move them anyway to see what happens; they are forbidden the young ravine’s undercuts and go there to watch kingfishers. They catch tadpoles in jars, race sticks down the gutters, and learn to jump the braided channels without soaking their socks. They grow up knowing that a street can change its number and a shed can change its address without anyone moving house.

The town’s nonhuman residents are not background. The frogs are the true majority, colonizing the cooling ponds and reed beds until dusk becomes a wall of croaking. Herons stalk the shallow channels like inspectors. Kingfishers work the raw clay of the ravine. Bats pour out from under the mill roof at dusk, stitching the insect clouds around the electric lamps. Foxes trot through the wet meadows between shifts, unimpressed by industry. Mice and shrews live in the woodpiles; cats sleep on the warm pipes; dogs know the creek has shifted before their owners do. Moss furrows the pipes, ferns crowd the foundry’s shaded side, and nettles take the vacant lots with or without permission.

What lives in Sallow Bottom, then, is a population of adjusters and opportunists — human and otherwise — who have agreed without saying so that the water has right-of-way. They are patient, damp, practical, and not gloomy about it. They complain about the rain, the moss, the Board, and the creek, and then they jack the house up another inch and go back to work. They are a small machine for living with a young river, and they are always, slowly, rearranging themselves.
