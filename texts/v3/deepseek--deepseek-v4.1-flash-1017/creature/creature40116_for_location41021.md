The Kettle is lived in the way a wound is lived in: continuously, by things that have learned to keep the edges open.

The town’s people are the most obvious residents, though they would not call themselves that. They are trimmers first. A Kettle-dweller is born into the roar and learns early that the basin will seal itself shut with its own generosity if no one cuts back. So they walk the seam-lips with hooked copper blades and cold-hammered scrapers, paring new sinter from doorways and channel mouths and the rims of the streets, carrying the pale parings in baskets to the middle pan and letting them sink. They are patient in a way that has nothing to do with virtue; patience is just the shape a life takes when the only task is the same task forever. Their hands are hard and precise, their nails worn flat, their wrists strong from the angle of the cut. They keep moving. A person who stands still too long in the Kettle becomes a thing with a crystalline skin, and everyone knows it.

They are ear-people. The light is flat and shadowless under the steam lid, and depth is a liar, so they read the world by sound: the pitch of a channel, the hollowness of a seam, the difference between water running over stone and water running under it. In the centre of the pan, where the bowl focuses the roar into a standing tone you feel in the sternum and the teeth, they work in short shifts with wax and hide stuffed in their ears. There they speak with their hands—quick, economical signs, taps against stone, a whole language of fingers and knuckles and held palms. At the rim, where the noise thins, they unplug their ears and sleep.

Their houses are grown, not built. A frame of bone and shell is set over a diverted trickle of mineral water; the water skins it with stone; the trickle is moved; a new room is started against the old. So the town is a stack of accretions, rooms grown onto rooms, arches of lashed bone, awnings of oiled hide, roofs you can walk across from one side of the Kettle to the other. The streets are the seam-lips—narrow wet walks with water rushing on both sides—and people go by foot or by flat raft, poling along the channels. Their only metal is a little copper, green-stained, worked cold into pins and blades and the small hooked tools of the trimmer’s trade. They do not count years. They have no before, no season, no harvest, no arrival. Their calendar is the flow, the ringing, the cutting-back, and the roar. The only story they tell is the balance: if it ever tipped, the basin would fill solid or open into a dry crack in the ground. Everyone knows it. It is warning and lullaby at once.

But the people are only one scraping edge of the Kettle’s life. The wet stone is furred with the slick—orange and bone-white and dull iron grey, a mineral bloom too simple to call a plant, spreading faster than it can be eaten. It is the bottom of everything. The cup-shells live in the seams by the million, thumbnail-sized ridged cups packed shoulder to shoulder, and when the water moves through them they knock and ring against each other and the stone. That is the choral clatter riding over the roar. The grazers wade the shallow pan on stilt legs, pale and blind and shell-backed, calling to one another in long falling tones that carry strangely through the din. The warm mist is alive too: dense with airborne microbes and with the small hard-winged things that hunt them, moving in clouds thick enough to dim the already-dim light when they pass. And down in the deeper seams, where the water is hottest, something larger moves slowly and does not come up. The people know it by displacement, by a wrongness in the flow, by the way the channels hush for a moment and then resume. They do not name it. It is not a neighbour you visit. It is a fact of the lower dark.

So what lives in the Kettle is a dense, continuous, mineral-fed crowd held at a knife’s edge: trimmers and cup-shells, grazers and wing-clouds, grown houses and the slick that would swallow them, and the slow large thing below that never surfaces. They are all wet, all listening, all being skinned and cut back and given back grain for grain. The people trim the town so the basin will not drown in its gift; the basin trims the people in its turn, laying a pale skin on tools and doorframes and the still. Nothing here erodes. Nothing here arrives. The water comes up, crosses, sinks, and the sound goes on.
