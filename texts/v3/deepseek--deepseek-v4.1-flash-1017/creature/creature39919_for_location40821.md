The one who lives here is not the forest, and not the little many-legged thing, though it is closer to the latter than to you. It is in the deepest room, the one you duck twice to enter. You may have taken it for a folded root, a pale mass at the rim of the basin, beaded like everything else. It is about the size of a person who has lain down and then been thought about by the room for a long time. It has no front. It has a gathered place—a knot of tissue smoother than the rest—where attention sits; when you come in, the knot turns toward your warmth, not your shape.

Its body is the color of the inside of a shell, faintly translucent. The light from the roof goes into it and comes out slowed, so it seems to hold a little of the glow after the roof has dimmed. It is ridged and faceted in places, like the leaves, and its skin has the same nap of water. It does not have hands. It has holdfasts: soft, root-like limbs it can extrude and resorb, ending in many small gripping lobes. The many-legged thing you saw is one of these, or was—a length of the resident sent out to walk a trunk, enter a hollow, and come back with news of wetness. It moved with deliberation because it was not in a hurry and not separate. If you had watched it long enough, it would have returned to the room, lain itself against the pale mass, and become a limb again.

It does not eat. There is no mouth you would recognize, no gut, no ash, no waste. It drinks through its skin. The mist gathers on it; the water in the basin is the same temperature as the air because it is the same temperature as the resident, and the resident keeps it that way. It takes the water in and gives it back as breath—the green, mineral, faintly sweet smell. That is what you carried out in your clothes. It is not scent exactly. It is the exhalation of a closed loop. It has no need to spend anything. This is how it has lived: by not spending. It is the unspent rain’s tenant.

It smooths the floors by lying on them. Not with tools, not with hands—by the long pressure of its body, by settling and shifting over years that do not mark it. The rooms are grown, yes, but they are also worn in the one way this place permits: not eroded, not scratched, but pressed smooth by something that never lifts away. The vine swelled the hollows; the resident has been making them habitable ever since, or it made the vine swell by pressing, and there is no difference you can detect. It does not build. It waits in contact with a thing until the thing becomes a room.

It has no age. It does not grow from a young thing into an old thing. It buds. The many-legged one was a bud; when it is done being out, it will be reabsorbed. The resident is complete and newly arrived, both. You cannot say it is ancient, because nothing here has had time to accumulate; you cannot say it is young, because it is exactly as full as it will be. It is not sleeping, either, though its breath is the breath of a thing asleep. It has tides of attention. Sometimes the knot is turned inward and the mist gathers on it and it does not move for what would be days if days were being kept. Sometimes it is turned outward, and then the little limbs come and go, and the basin is topped up, and the rooms are pressed a little smoother.

It is why nothing rots. It keeps. It takes the fallen leaf before it can go brown and holds it in the state of having just fallen; it takes the dead insect before it can become litter; it takes the water and gives it back without letting it run. It is not a gardener in the sense of encouraging new growth. There is no new growth and no old growth. There is only the maintenance of a full volume. If you stayed, it would not drive you out. It would notice the dryness of you, the way you spend water by breathing and sweating and shedding. It would come close—not fast—and lay a holdfast against your arm, and the holdfast would be warm and wet and very strong in its patience. It would not mean to harm you. It would mean to keep you. It would drink the water you spend and give it back as mist, and after a while you would be beaded like everything else, and the floor under you would begin to grow smooth.

The resident is the thing whose breath you smelled. It is not a monster and not a god. It is a tenant that has made a perfect closed pocket and lives in it the way a coin lives in a pocket: kept, not spent. It has no name for itself. It does not need one. It knows you are there by the dryness, and it is already waiting for you to become part of the not-decaying.
