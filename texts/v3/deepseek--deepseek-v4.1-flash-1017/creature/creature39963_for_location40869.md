Here is the life of the plateau, the things that move and breathe within the still.

---

The things that live here are not separate from the place. They are expressions of its laws, their bodies and habits shaped by the slow, relentless industry of the water and the cold. To see them is to see the plateau itself, condensed into a living form.

The most numerous are the **Drifters**. They are the midges and flies, but to call them that is to miss their nature. They are not creatures of the air, but of the mist itself. Their wings are not for flight in any vigorous sense; they are thin, translucent membranes gummed with moisture, allowing them only to buoy and tumble on the slightest current of vapor. They exist in a state of permanent suspension. Their bodies are soft, almost liquid, and their sole purpose is to breathe, to mate, and to fall. They rise from the fens in great, silent exhalations, a living vapor that hangs in the air for a day, a week, before their moisture-heavy bodies give up and they settle back into the moss, a fine, organic precipitate. They are the plateau's slow, drifting breath made visible.

In the cold, clear pools live the **Filter-Folk**. They are the soft-bodied, eyeless things, but "folk" is perhaps too generous a term. They are more like living sieves. Some are flat, leaf-like organisms that cling to the submerged stone, their entire surface a porous, sticky matrix. They do not hunt; they *osmose*. They draw the mineral-rich water through their bodies, extracting the microscopic nutrients, the stray organic matter, the ghost-dust itself, and releasing the water back, fractionated and purer. Others are tubular, pulsing slowly, their bodies a labyrinth of internal channels where the water is passed and reprocessed. They are the plateau's living distillation columns, invertebrate echoes of the great stalks. They have no eyes because there is nothing to see but the slow drift of food and the changing quality of the water, which they taste with every pore.

The **Pale Walkers** are the many-legged things that traverse the moss. They are the closest thing to an active predator here, though their pace is still one of glacial patience. They are long, low creatures with dozens of thin, articulate legs that move in a rippling wave, propelling them over the fen with a soft, dry rustle. Their bodies are a pale, translucent gray, the color of the ghost-dust, and their skin is so thin that their internal processes can be seen as a slow, dark shadow moving within. They do not run; they flow. They are scavengers, finding the dead and the dying, the Drifters who have fallen, the Filter-Folk who have become silt. Their mouthparts are delicate, not for tearing, but for sipping the soft decay. They are the plateau's slow, gentle coroners.

But the true masters of this place, the ones in whom the landscape's entire purpose is embodied, are the **Stalk-Keepers**. They are not the stalks themselves, but the creatures that live *within* them, in the porous, labyrinthine heights. They are the plateau's intellect, if such a slow, diffuse thing can be called that.

They are amorphous, jelly-like entities, dwelling in the damp chambers of the stalk-wood, woven into the living stone and tissue like a second nervous system. They are the color of the stalk, the color of the moss, the color of the mist—an adaptable, indefinite hue. They have no fixed shape; they extend pseudopods of themselves into the capillaries of the stalk, feeling the flow of water, tasting its mineral content, monitoring the pressure. They are the regulators. When a stalk becomes clogged with precipitate, they digest it. When a chamber grows too cold, their own slow metabolism warms it. They are the maintenance crew of the great, silent industry.

And they are the communicators. The Stalk-Keepers do not speak in sound. They speak in chemistry. They release pheromones into the water-flow that are carried through the entire root network. A change in one stalk is tasted by the Keepers in another a hundred meters away. A clot of precipitate in a distant fen is known and a response is initiated. The entire forest of stalks, through its Keepers, is a single, vast, slow-thinking organism. A thought takes a day to cross the plateau. A decision, to adjust the flow of a whole region, takes a season. They are patient beyond human comprehension, for their entire existence is measured in the slow, cyclical time of condensation and precipitation.

And finally, there are the **Moss-Herds**. They are the largest creatures here, though you would not know it to see them, for they are almost immobile. They are soft, rounded bodies, like enormous, damp cushions, that move only a few centimeters a year. Their skin is a thick, peaty hide, and their back is a perfect substrate for the moss, which grows on them as it grows on the rock. They are walking gardens, ecosystems in themselves. The Pale Walkers graze upon their moss, the Drifters breed in the dampness of their furrows, and in return, the Moss-Herds provide shelter and a mobile source of nutrients. They breathe in the mist and exhale it, their massive, slow respiration a small, local weather system. They are the plateau's gentle, wandering islands, embodying the Law of the Root, for they are not separate from the moss, the water, or the stone. They are a part of all of it.

These are the inhabitants of the still. They do not strive. They do not conquer. They simply are, each one a unique expression of the same cold, wet, silent process. They are the slow, relentless work of being, made visible in flesh and chitin and jelly. To watch them is to watch the plateau itself, dreaming.
