**Hessa of the Cold Fins, third gallery under the Sill, west side, by the blue transformer**

She is small and she is crooked, and both came from the work. The scraping is done bent — the fins hang at chest height for an adult and the good fins hang below and above that, and after thirty years of reaching down and reaching up she has gone a little sigmoid, shoulders rolled forward, spine curving out away from the rock like a bracket holding up nothing. When she stands straight she can't, quite; there's a permanent fold in her middle. Her hands are the part of her that's beautiful, though nobody would say so at a glance: broad, dry, the knuckles thickened to knobs, but the palms are pale and smooth as a drumhead because they have never once in fifty-one years been wet. She can judge a fin's temperature by the sole of her foot through a sock. She is never wrong about when the bloom will set.

In the morning she is up before the light, because the bloom comes off best in the hour when the fin is coldest and the air stillest and the crystal is thick and unslumped. She wears layers she doesn't take off for days — a quilted vest stuffed with shredded flue-wing membrane, a scarf wound across her mouth against the grit, gloves with the fingertips cut out because she must feel the fin under the blade. She carries three blades of different flex on a belt loop, a flat sack folded into a pouch, and a lamp she rarely lights, because she does not need to see the bloom; the bloom announces itself. It makes the fin ring faintly under the scraper, a tiny singing, and where the crop is good the ringing is constant, like walking through a field of very small bells. She works by that sound. She can tell a friend's scraping from across the gallery by its tempo.

She has a husband, or had; he is a winch-man on the second gallery and their marriage consists largely of two households in the same slot that share a mushroom-shelf and a drying rack and the same shift at the turn, when they tie down each other's shutters. They speak softly and with long silences and understand each other well enough. Her daughter went up, five years ago, to see the plateau, and did not come back down, and Hessa does not talk about it and does not go near the hairline; she scrapes. There is a shrine on the strut above her sleeping-nook, three tin plates and a lamp, and she does not think of it as a shrine to anyone in particular.

She keeps hoppers. Not for meat — the meat is a waste of the bloom — but because their dust is good for the shins and because she likes the sound they make with their hard little feet in the grit, a sound like someone thinking at a desk. She keeps them in a cage wedged where the pipes cross, and she has trained them, more or less, to come when she taps twice on the cage wire. She has a theory about the blue signal the scrapers make on the fins at night; she thinks she has cracked part of it and she is wrong about most of it and she doesn't care, because the parts she's right about have earned her four extra fins' width of yield, and she is respected for it in an unemphatic way.

She has never seen the sea and has never been thirsty, and she thinks of the word *wet* as a mild obscenity, the way other people think of a curse understood but not used in company. She has never been in a room with a window that opened onto weather. Once, up on a strut during a rare repair job, she put her face into the slack air of a windless afternoon and felt for the first time something she could not name, and went down and did not mention it to anyone.

At the turn she goes still. She has been through the turn maybe eighteen thousand times and she still stops for it, one hand on a cable, watching the vanes swing and the cloth stand out and the whole hive lean its weight the other way. She is not religious; it's just that the turn is the one thing in the Throat that no one built and no one patches, and she likes to be present when it happens, the way you'd want to be present for a birth or a death in the family, even if it isn't yours. Then the wind settles into its new direction and she goes back to the fins, and scrapes, and the bloom comes off the cold metal in long grey curls and falls into her sack with a sound like someone sweeping a very small room.
