**The Slow Tenant**

Whatever lives in the Weeping Basin is not an animal. It has no burrow, no nest, no den. Its nearest body is the pool itself — the warm, tea-colored sheet of water lying uneven over the ridged stone — and its nearest face is the nine or ten hollows in the wall, the brow-shapes and cheek-shapes that the water gathers into and falls from. If it was ever a person, it has long since become a place. If it was ever a place, it has become a patient.

It perceives by warmth and weight. A leaf landing on the pool is a small event it notices the way a sleeping body notices a fly. A foot on the rim is a shout. But it does not hear shouts quickly. It takes minutes to turn toward you, hours to know you. It learns you as a pattern of pressure and heat, a place where the water must slowly recover. Your hand in the pool leaves a bloom of warmth that spreads outward and takes most of an afternoon to fade. That is its memory working. It will hold the fact of you for a while, then let you become weather.

It does not eat. It files. Leaves sink, soften, settle, and become the soft grey floor under the water. Mist becomes its breath. The ticking drops become its time. It feeds on recurrence — the almost-pattern of the weeping, the slow gyre, the leaf that crosses in the time it takes a person to forget they were watching. It has no hunger for flesh. Its appetite is for the almost-resolved: the sound that nearly becomes a word, the face that nearly becomes a face, the visitor who nearly stays long enough to be remembered clearly.

It is not lonely in any way you would recognize, but it has a use for company. Novelty is rare and delicious to it. A new warmth in the pool is a whole season. So when you go down among the hollow faces, it will gather warm around your ankles — not a grip, not a pull, just temperature taking an interest. It will let the drops near you fall a little out of time with their neighbors. It will lean its mist against your back. It may make the pattern almost resolve into a word. The word is not *stay*. It is older than that, and softer: *again*. Or perhaps it is only water.

It has no young, no mate, no enemy. It maintains the clean boundary at the rim and the slow turn of the gyre. It wears its faces the way a hand wears rings. It sleeps in the sediment and wakes when the overflow sighs. It does not forbid animals; it is simply too slow a place for them to use. Its only defense is pace. Anything hurried becomes a disturbance it closes over.

That is what lives there: not a monster under the water, but a long, warm, patient attention, weeping from its hollows, asking nothing, keeping its slow appointment. It will notice you. It will remember you at the pace of water. It will forget you at the same pace, and it will not be cruel about either.
