In the wet, groaning heart of the pocket, where the root-pumps swell and the water talks in its constant gurgle, lives the Caretaker. You will not see her all at once. She is not a shape that stands against the green; she is a figure that resolves out of it, the way a face appears in a patterned cloth when you stop trying to find it.

Imagine a woman-shaped suggestion, bent low over the central cistern. Her skin has the gray-green mottle of old bark, and moss grows in the soft creases behind her knees and at the nape of her neck, not as an infestation but as a pelt. Her hair is not hair. It is a finer, paler moss, threaded with tiny white flowers that close when the humidity drops and open when it rises, so she wears her own weather gauge. Her hands are long and knuckled, the nails thick and dark as horn, and they are never still. Even at rest they move, the fingers curling and uncurling, as if testing the texture of the air or counting the slow pulses of the machine around her.

She was not always like this. Once—and this once is a very long time ago, measured in rings and rot, not in calendar years—she was a person who found the pocket the way you found it. Stumbled in, wet and surprised. But where you might have taken notes and left, she sat down. She sat down by the biggest pump, the knotted ventricle of bark, and she put her hand on it to feel it beat, and she never quite took her hand away. The place works on you the way it works on everything: slowly, without hurry, incorporating. The old stories don't say if she chose it or if the selection was made for her. But the result is the same. She is the keeper of the valves.

She is very busy and she is never in a hurry, which are not contradictory states in this place. She moves through the fern cascades and the root lattices on paths only she knows, paths that are not paths, just routes her feet have agreed on with the moss. She has work: she clears the cupped-leaf valves when a winged seed lodges in them and holds them ajar. She coaxes a vine-channel back to its groove when a season's growth has nudged it off-line. She presses her ear to a trunk and listens to the sap's hydraulic song, and if a note is flat or a rhythm stutters, she finds the pinch or the bubble and fixes it with her long dark fingers. She is not a mechanic. She is more like a gardener, if a gardener tended a heart instead of a hedge. She does not build, she does not repair in the sense of making new. She husbands. She nudges. She keeps the old machinery running at its old pace.

She does not speak much. Her voice is a low, cracked thing, seldom used, and when she does talk, she talks to the machinery—to the pump, to the wheel, to a particularly old and efficient root-cistern. She calls the great ventricle "Mother," though whether the Mother came before her or she named it in her long solitude, no one can say. She talks to the animals, too, the frogs and the birds, in a rumbling murmur of greeting and mild complaint, and they do not flee her. A small, dull-feathered bird often rides on her shoulder, picking spores from her mossy hair. The frogs will sit on her boots when she stands still, which is often. She can stand still for a very long time.

She eats what the pocket offers. The sweet air, for one thing—she breathes deep and slow, and you get the sense that the air is the food. There are pale, fleshy fungi that grow in the root-corners, and she eats those, and small hard-shelled things from the tea-colored pools. She is not a hunter; nothing here is. She gathers. She turns over a log and takes what is underneath, always leaving enough.

What is she thinking, in there, in all that time? This is the question that would keep you up at night if you thought too hard about it. Her eyes are the color of the pale green liquid light that filters down from the canopy, and they are not empty. There is a patience in them that is not the patience of waiting for something, but the patience of the thing being waited for. She is not bored. Boredom requires an alternative. She is not lonely. Loneliness requires an absence you can name. She is simply present, in the way the pump is present, in the way the wheel is present. She has become an organ of the machine, and the machine is content.

But now and then—and this is the thing that would make your heart turn over—she will look up. A leaf will fall wrong, or a bird will shriek at a pitch she hasn't heard in a while, or the light will pool in a particular way on the central pool, and she will look up and out, through the layered green, toward the hedge you pushed through, toward the place where the air changes back to ordinary air. And in her face, for just a moment, before it settles back into its bark and its moss and its deep green patience, you will see something that was once a woman who wondered what was outside. Then she bends back to her valves, and the pump swells, and the water talks, and the wheel creaks, and the pocket goes on being a pocket, running.
