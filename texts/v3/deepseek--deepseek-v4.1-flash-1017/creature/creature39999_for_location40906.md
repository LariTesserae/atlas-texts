The Cryingg of the Whole is here, and they have learned its grammar.

They are the Clingfoot. Small, rarely more than two feet tall, they are a burly, humped people whose entire bodies seem designed to hold onto the world. Their skins are thick, pebbled like the rock itself, and the color of wet slate. Where a man has fingernails, they have dense, horny pads, and their toes are long and articulate, able to find a grip on a vertical seam of obsidian that would defeat a human climber. They wear no clothes but a kind of harness of braided sinew and lichen-spun cloth, into which they tuck their precious things: sharp shards for cutting, a store of dried fungus, a smooth, round stone that is simply pleasing to hold.

They have no eyes. Instead, their faces, broad and flat, are covered in a fine, silvery down, sensitive to the slightest change in air pressure, the faintest shift of wind on the rock. They are deaf to the roar of the Vent, for it is a constant pressure, the background hum of existence. What they "hear" are the subtleties within it: the change in pitch as a storm gathers, the subtle vibration that tells them a glacier has calved a mile away, the faint, high-pitched ping of a pebble bouncing down the Whistling Stair. They navigate by the feel of the world on their skin.

They live in the spaces between the sound, in the lee of the great, wind-scoured boulders. They do not build; they find. A deep crevasse, a cave formed by a rockslide, a hollow beneath a shelf of rock—these are their homes. They line them with the softest things they know: the dried, papery husks of the ghost moss after it has fruited, and the thick, silvery pelts of the Rime-crawlers.

The Rime-crawlers are their companions and their livestock. The size of a man's arm, they are segmented and armored, like a lobster that has evolved for a life of perpetual cold. They move with a slow, deliberate grace, their many legs a gentle, rattling whisper. They are the grazers of this world, scraping the lichen from the rocks with their complex mouthparts. The Clingfoot follow them, tend them, protect them from the few things that might harm them—the sudden, driving hailstorms, the Leaping Shadows.

The Leaping Shadows are the Clingfoot's most persistent fear. They are the Basin's only true predator and a thing of the crepuscular, hazy light. They are not animals in the way one might understand. They are a function of the mountain's echo. It is said they are born from the sound of fear itself, a thought you cannot quite suppress in a dark place. They appear as a distortion in the air, a slick, oily patch of darkness that moves with an unnatural fluidity. When they strike, it is with a soundless impact that stops the heart. A Clingfoot's only defense is a perfect stillness, to press their pressure-sensitive skin flat against the stone and become a part of the mountain's own silence. They have been known to hold that stillness for hours, until the Shadow tires of its game and slinks back into the fog.

The language of the Clingfoot is a tactile one. They speak by drumming their long fingers on the rock, on each other's shoulders, on the shells of their Rime-crawlers. A complex code of rhythm and pitch, a conversation of taps and thumps that can be felt through a yard of solid stone. They have a song for mourning, a drumming so slow and heavy it feels like the mountain itself is grieving. They have a rhythm for joy, a light, quick pitter-pat that sounds like a gentle rain.

They know the ghost moss. They gather its luminescent caps, which glow for days after being picked, to light their deep, dark homes. It is a cold, spectral light, but to them, it is the hearth-fire of the world.

And they know the tithe. The rules of the High Basin are strict, and the Clingfoot are its most faithful observers. There is a place, a flat, black stone before the single arch, where the wind sings with a particular clarity, a note almost too pure for this world. The stone is called the Listening Place. And once every cycle, when the sun is a pale sliver above the eastern fangs, the Clingfoot gather there. They bring the finest of the ghost moss, the most perfect of the fallen stones, and they lay them on the black rock. They do not know who or what they are leaving it for. They only know that the mountain's rhythm is a conversation, and this is their part of it. It is not a sacrifice. It is a courtesy. A sign that they are listening.
