No one lives in the Quill Field in the way a village lives in a valley. There is no keeper in the seams, no hermit on a dry shelf, no colony of glass. What lives there is sparse enough to be counted, and all of it is small. But it is not empty. The field has residents the way a struck bell has a note: not many, not going anywhere, and impossible to ignore once you are inside.

The most nearly personable of them is the **facet-grazer**.

It is a pale flat thing, the width of a coin and thinner than the coin’s shadow. It has no face, no front, no back—just a soft irregular disc with a rim that frills faintly when it contracts. Its upper surface is ribbed like the inside of a shell; its underside is a wet membrane. When it clings to a column, the mineral’s fluting shows through it, and for a moment you mistake it for a patch of bloom. Then it moves, and the mistake is over.

It moves by peristalsis, a slow wave travelling from rim to centre. A handspan takes most of an hour. It follows the condensation thread—not out of intention, exactly, but because the mineral film is richest there. It feeds by absorbing that film through its underside. Behind it the facet is clean for a while, a faint track like a fingernail drawn through frost; then the water lays the film down again, and the track closes. It does not scrape. It drinks the surface. If your shadow falls over it, it tightens its rim and waits, and if you wait longer it resumes, because there is nothing here for it to be afraid of. Its caution is vestigial. Its patience is total.

They are rare. Along the edge of the field you will see perhaps one every half hour, and you will learn to be glad of them. They do not congregate. Two meeting will slide around each other with a soft refusal and go on. When one has thickened itself on mineral film, it divides—not grows, exactly, but replaces: the disc pinches into two thinner discs, each settles on a facet, and the film closes over the seam. The total number stays the same. The field does not bloom. The grazers are the only soft things in the valley, and the only evidence that anything in it is not already finished.

In the channels are the **glass drifters**.

They are shrimp-shaped only by courtesy. They are transparent, blind, segmented, with feathery appendages and a dark thread of gut visible through the body. They drift with the flow, and the flow is a closed loop. They do not swim so much as correct themselves—a flick of the tail, a slow turn, then carried on again. They gather in the lee of column bases, hang there a while, and are lifted away. They feed on whatever the grazers leave suspended: mineral silt, shed film, the fine dust of the columns. They do not appear to grow. You may guess, after watching them long enough, that they are the grazers’ other phase—that a grazer which loses its hold becomes a drifter, blind and glassy, until it blunders against a wet facet and flattens and begins again. Or you may guess not. The field does not explain.

They are the only things in the valley that look like they are going somewhere. They are not. If you watch one for an hour, it will pass the same column twice and be no further along. They are commuters on a road that is a circle, and they do not seem to mind.

The third resident is not an animal at all. It is the **Held Chord**.

The water is thousands of threads falling at fixed pitches into a shallow pan, and the aggregate of them is a chord that never resolves. It has no melody. It does not vary by so much as a hair between one moment and the next. It is not an echo—shout, and your voice goes out a few metres and is simply eaten. From the ridge behind the field you hear nothing; step over the lip and it is on you like a hand over your ears. The columns are its body. Some of them are resonators, tuned to the condensation that finds them. Put your hand flat against one and you feel the hum in the mineral like a tuning fork that has forgotten how to stop.

The chord is not a mind. It does not greet you, and it will not answer. But it is the thing that makes the field feel occupied, because it is attention without object: a held note that has nowhere to go. It is the closest thing here to a god, and it is not interested.

If you want a single resident rather than three, it is the **equilibrium**.

The field is one organism in the only sense that matters here: it maintains itself. Water leaves the columns and arrives at the water sheet; the sheet gives it back to the air; the air gives it back to the columns. The mineral the water carries away is laid down again somewhere you cannot see. The carving is finished. The chord is held. The level is a palm deep and does not rise or fall. The population of flat things and glass things stays constant, because they are not its thoughts—they are its tolerances. Small lives the equilibrium allows because they do not perturb it enough to matter.

They do not need you. They will not remember you. The grazers will go on being one every half hour; the drifters will pass the same column twice; the chord will hold. The only thing that ages in the valley is the visitor.

That is the whole of its population: the held, the worn, and the rare soft thing that feeds on the difference.
