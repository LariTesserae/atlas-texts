The first thing to understand is that almost nothing lives there in the ordinary sense. The Brimming Floor is too clean, too warm, too still; it has never had to make room for a food web. But it does have inhabitants, and they are all specialists in needing very little.

The people are not residents exactly. They are regulars — a small lineage of skimmers who come down through the tilted throat with wooden scrapers and shallow baskets, generation after generation, and who have never seen a reason to build anything grander than a knee-high wall to steer the seep. You would know them by their hands first: broad-palmed, short-nailed, permanently wrinkled at the fingertips, the skin soft as the inside of a wrist. They are spare and low-voiced and damp. Their clothes are thin and dark and never quite dry; their hair is bound back or cropped close; their eyes are patient with dimness but not fond of the dark, so in the deeper hollow they work by touch and by sound rather than by sight.

They step in a particular way — heel first, weight rolled slowly through the foot — so the water in the rims trembles and lifts and subsides without slopping over. They can read the floor through their soles. A rim that has been skimmed too often sits differently underfoot; a channel half-choked with calcite cream makes a different tick when the seep finds it; a wall that has shifted by a finger’s width announces itself as a small hesitation in the pattern of the pools. They do not talk much down there. The air is too thick for it, and the hollow swallows voices. When they need to signal, they tap a scraper against a rim and let the floor carry it.

Their work is not mining. They skim. The calcite cream that gathers on the pools is soft and pale and faintly warm, and they take it up in thin curls and lay it in baskets, and they leave the rims themselves unbroken. They are careful in a way that is almost superstition but is really just long practice: never cut a lip, never drain a pool, never dig into the shell. They stack their little travertine walls in arcs, they cut notches to move water, they lay a blackened wooden scraper across two rims as if they will be back after lunch — and they will be, next season or next year. One fire-blackened stone sits in a niche where the condensation runs around it instead of over it. That is the only dry thing in the hollow, and it is enough.

The true residents are smaller and stranger. In the deeper rims drift pale things the length of a fingernail: blind, almost colorless, with a slow internal pulse. They do not school. One to a pool, mostly, turning without hurry, correcting against the calcite lip with a touch too soft to see. They graze the oily sheen on the water — or something under it, or nothing; it is not possible to tell, and it does not seem to matter. There is a snail the color of the water, so that you see only the moving shadow of it, a soft displacement that travels along the rims and leaves no trail. And further in, where the light gives out and the floor goes on brimming in the dark, something lives that has never been properly seen. It is known by its wake: a line drawn across a still pool, a slow bulge under the crust, a sound like a swallowed cough. It is not large, or if it is large it is thin. It never comes into the light. The skimmers leave the deep rims alone, not from fear but because the cream there is no better and the footing is worse.

And under all of them, the floor itself — which is not alive, but is not inert either. It grows. Each rim is a lip of calcite laid down by water, fed by seep, rounded by its own long existence. The whole sheet is one slow creature made of many mouths, all of them brimming, all of them warm, all of them holding their small circle of tired light. The people and the pale drifters and the water-colored snail and the wake-thing in the dark are its small weather, its brief itches. It has no season and no hunger. It has found the shape it wants to be, and it is holding it with enormous patience and no effort at all.

So: a few low-voiced skimmers with scrapers and baskets; a handful of blind comma-shaped things turning in the shallows; a snail you see only as a shadow; something in the black that leaves a wake; and beneath them the calcite, brimming, ticking, warm, going on.
