The things that live in the Slow Foundry do not live *on* it so much as *off its breathing*. There is no household here, no herd you could point to and say, “That is the owner.” There is a thin reticulum of specialists, each one a way of eating some part of the foundry’s slow exhalation—heat, steam, mineral dust, condensation, the small lives that feed on all of those in turn.

The one you notice first is the grazer. It is knee-high, hump-backed, hairless, with a hide the color of wet ash and the texture of warm leather. Its ribs show like stays beneath the skin. It stands in a steam plume with its legs braced and its mouth hanging open, and its throat works in a slow, deliberate rhythm, swallowing the air itself. Its lips are fringed, almost frayed; inside the mouth the palate is ridged like a washboard, and a long folded tongue licks the catch—flies, mineral flakes, whatever the plume has lifted—into the throat. It does not chew. It is a grazer of weather. It moves in ones and twos across enormous distances of crust, never hurrying, stepping only on the columns and never in the seams. A calf stands in the lee of its mother, learning the same lesson by watching: warmth means breath, and breath means food, and the ground that opens will also close.

Something hunts it. You will not see the hunter often. You know it mostly by a line of quiet—how the plume-flies drop low, how the cricket-things stop rasping, how the ticking of cooling crust seems to hold its breath. You know it by a smell, faint and hot, and by tracks in the salt: broad, three-lobed, filled with condensation by morning and gone by the following night. The rest is inference. A long low body, perhaps. A narrow head. A leg that comes down on a column and does not slip. It is not cruel; it is another habit of the plateau, patient enough to wait for the grazer to stand in the wrong plume at the wrong hour.

The plumes themselves are cities. Each has its own flies and mites, its own color—rust, pearl, bile-yellow, soot—and its own population rising and settling with the forty-beat breath of the seam. They glow faintly in the dim pearl-grey, not enough to see by, just enough to know they are there. Their lives are short and entirely local. They are the pasture the grazer grazes, though neither would use that word.

In the warm pools live crabs. The small ones are the size of a thumbnail, translucent, their guts visible as dark threads. The larger ones are plate-sized and keep to the cooler basins, scuttling along the sinter rims, waiting for flies to fall, scraping crust from the stone with claws like needles. In the tea-colored basins swim pale blind fish, surface-sipping the condensation. In one basin, by some accident of isolation, there is a fish that has given up on eyes entirely. It navigates by taste—lime, egg-sulfur, the faint difference between one stone and another—and it is not a metaphor. It is a fish.

At the margins, the vegetation is thin and strange. Rust-colored moss in the seams where steam condenses. Pale wiry grass along the runoff channels, cropped to nubs. Waist-high stands of something jointed and grey-green that draws up the mineral water and deposits a crust along its own stem until it looks calcified, and perhaps is. These are not a green cover. They are a fringe, a precipitate with roots, growing by accreting.

Together they make no society. They are neighbors in the way a spring is a neighbor to a stone. They share the same rules: stand on the columns, read the heat, listen for the pause. The true inhabitant is the breathing seam and the thin bright fringe of things that have learned to make a living from the breath. They will go on closing and opening, closing and opening, long after you have found your way off the plateau, and they will not register that you were there at all.
