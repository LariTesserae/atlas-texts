No one lives on the Kettle. There is no village, no hut, no path worn by feet, no fire ring, no mark that survives a season. But the warm seams are inhabited, and the inhabitants are not spread across the mountain. They are packed into small hot pockets, separated by kilometres of wet grey stone. The population is an archipelago. Each island is a seam. The census is taken in centimetres.

At the bottom of every seam-town is a mat. It is orange and white and wet, a bacterial skin growing where warm mineral water seeps over the rock. It is the pasture and the floor and the first resident all at once. It grazes on what the water carries up—sulfur, iron, dissolved stone—and it grows in sheets and crusts and soft wet rims. When the vent shifts, the mat dries and dies and a new mat starts somewhere else. Nothing tends it. It is the crop that feeds everything above it, and it is also the architecture: the colour on the rock, the slick underfoot, the reason the seam looks alive before you have seen a single animal.

The mites live in that mat like livestock in long grass. They are tiny enough to be a texture, a moving grain in the orange and white. They graze the bacterial skin. They do not travel. Their whole geography is the wet patch they were born on.

The cricket is larger, squat, wingless, dark. It lives its whole life within a metre of one vent. It has no wings because it has nowhere to fly. It sits on the warm stone and moves when the temperature shifts, and if it walked away from the seam it would die of cold in an hour. It is not brave or settled; it is bounded. Its world is a warm circle, and the edge of that circle is death.

The flies are the visible crowd. They are the size of rice grains, and they stand on the warm stone with their wings folded, so dense that the surface looks furred. Disturb them and the fur lifts in a dry papery rustle and resettles. They are the surplus of the seam-towns, the thing that turns bacterial pasture into meat. Everything that eats meat here eats them, or eats something that ate them.

The spiders have learned the vents. They are small and dark and patient, and they web across the warm mouths and wet angles where the flies funnel. Their webs are strung in the steam. Each one is a trap in a warm corridor, and the spiders sit in the draft and wait.

In the wet cracks where the steam condenses there are pale things—small, soft, damp, keeping to the film. They are almost certainly new to anyone who would care. They are certainly local. They live in the condensation and the mineral film, and they do not exist ten metres away.

The martins are the commuters. They nest on the warm faces, in cracks and under ledges, and they hunt the fly-sheets. They are the only inhabitants who can leave a seam and come back, who can cross the cold stone between towns and use the whole mountain. They make the seams part of a larger map.

The grey raptor is the only large animal regularly here. It does not live in a seam; it lives in the air above them. It hangs on the updrafts off the warm slopes without a wingbeat, and it watches the fly-sheets, and it drops. It does not call. It has the patience of a fisherman who knows the tide.

And at night, when the cold is worst and the warm ground is worth the climb, something with hooves comes up. You will not see it. You will find where it lay: a pressed oval in the mat, a little borrowed warmth, a bed the frost will take by morning. It is not a resident. It is a visitor. It comes for the heat as a body, not as food, and it leaves nothing but the place where it rested.

That is the population: a bacterial pasture, a few grazers, a sheet of flies, spiders, martins, a raptor, and a night visitor with hooves. Between the seams, nothing. The life is punctate—long emptiness, then a city. Each city is a small economy of heat, and the warmth is the only wealth. A metre is a home, ten metres is a journey, a hundred is a death.

They are not a community in any human sense. They do not know the mountain. They know a warm circle, a wet mat, a vent, a draft. The Kettle is not their home; it is the furnace and the floor. It does not remember them. It will go on running exactly like this whether or not anyone ever stands in the steam again.
