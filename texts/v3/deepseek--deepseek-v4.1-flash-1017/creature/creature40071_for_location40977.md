What lives in the geode does not live *on* it. It lives *in* it the way a pulse lives in a nerve. The place is not a room with creatures in it; it is a body with a small, closed bestiary moving through its lattice, all of them held in the same long, ringing instant.

The first inhabitants are the **chime-beetles**. They are the size of a thumb-joint, six-legged, with carapaces grown from the same glassy stuff as the world around them. Their shells are translucent enough that you can see the faint amber glow of their organs, a little lamp of digestion and nerve moving through a body that looks like a blown bubble. Their feet end in hooked pads that test each filament before trusting it. They walk by tapping—*tick, tick, tick*—and the tapping is not random. It is how they speak, how they feel the stress in the lattice, how they find the hairline fractures that no eye could see. They are the geode’s physicians. They lap the perpetual condensation, dissolve a splinter of cracked glass with a sour spit, and reprecipitate it as a milky seam. They do not repair the place. They *maintain* it, which is a slower and more patient thing. A beetle may take a century to cross a shelf you could span in three steps, and it will not be frozen. It will simply be walking at the speed of the world’s own time.

The **veil-moths** are the second kind of life. They are so thin they are almost a rumor: wings like wet gossamer, bodies like smoke held together by intention. They have no eyes. Their antennae are tuned to the hum, and they drift on the minute currents the vibrations make in the thick air. They feed on the condensation, on the mineral film that beads along every filament, and in doing so they carry droplets from one strand to another. They are the geode’s pollinators, though there are no flowers—only crystal growth, which they fertilize without knowing it. They cling rather than land; they are too light to settle. When they die, they do not fall. They become inclusions, tiny gray ghosts sealed in the glass, their wing-shapes preserved like fossils of a breath.

Deeper in the lattice, where the filaments fuse into thick trunks, there is something larger. You hear it before you could ever see it: a wet, sucking pop, a rustle like a sack of silk dragged over glass. Call it the **bell-eater**. It is a blind, soft-bodied thing, long as a human arm, with no shell because a shell would ring and give it away. It moves by softening the glass with its breath and squeezing through the softened seam, sealing it behind itself. It is the only inhabitant that changes the structure rather than tending it. It follows the tap-code of the beetles. It opens chambers that have been closed for ages, breathes their stale air, and moves on. It is the reason the place has a pulse at all—the reason the equilibrium is not quite death.

And then there is the one who is not there. The **Carvers**—or the one Carver, or the handful of them—left the marks near the bottom: the spiral, the row of dots, the crude stylized hand. They were not glass. They were warm and heavy and out of tune, things that breathed and fogged the filaments and brought an outside with them. They crawled down the fissure with tools and intention and carved their signs into the thickest trunk they could find, because in a place where nothing changes, a mark is the only way to say *I was here*. They are gone now. No bones, no tools, no body sealed in the glass. Only the graffiti, perfect and deliberate, the one alien gesture in a world that otherwise only grows.

So what lives there is a maintenance crew of glass beetles, a drift of mineral-drinking moths, a slow blind thing that eats sealed rooms, and the absence of a visitor who could not stay. None of them know there is an outside. None of them are trying to leave. They are the geode’s organs, its small moving thoughts, held in a single chord that will not resolve—and you, standing on the shelf with your warm breath and your changing body, are the only thing in the whole bright, ringing, weeping place that is already leaving.
