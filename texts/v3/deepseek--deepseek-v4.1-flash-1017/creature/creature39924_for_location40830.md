You will call them pillars first, because that is what your eyes can hold: three stones leaning into a geometry that should fall, a shaft of pale glass four meters high with a warmth at its base and a hum you feel in your teeth. But a pillar is only the part of them that has surfaced. The rest is in the crust—a root of channels, a lattice of warm seams, a slow body distributed through the glass plain like a thought spreading through a nervous system made of cracks.

Call them the Unfinished, if you need a name. They do not call themselves that. They do not call themselves anything for long.

They are not fauna. They are geology with appetite and grammar. Their flesh is a mineral slurry packed into seams, their blood is hot vapor, their bones are the pale vitreous stone they secrete around themselves in slow, irregular layers. What you see as a balanced stack is a limb or an organ or a sentence—sometimes all three. What you see as a channel cut into the ground is a nerve, a drain, a vein, a line of script. They build by growing, and they grow by building. The distinction does not trouble them.

They are born when a pocket of vapor seals under a thin shelf of crust and begins to resonate. First there is pressure. Then there is a hum. Then the crust above it cracks in a pattern that is not quite random, and a small triad of stones pushes up into the cold air—two uprights and a lintel, or a tripod, or a leaning tooth. For a long time that is all it is: a warm arrangement, a knot of pressure learning the shape of its own containment. Eventually it begins to extend channels outward, tasting the chemistry of the plain, drinking the sulfurous exhalations, depositing new glass at its base. It is not yet a person. It is a place where a person is condensing.

They have no eyes. They do not need them. The ceiling’s amber pulse reaches them as a faint thermal signature, a slow brightening they can feel through the stone. They sense the world as gradients: warm below, cold above; pressure building, pressure released; a seam opening, a seam closing. Their skin is a seismograph. Their joints are the places where the crust shears. When they move—and they do move, though you would have to watch for hours to see it—they do so by expanding one side of a pillar and contracting the other, leaning a few centimeters, waiting, leaning again. A tall one crossing ten meters of plain may take a season. It will leave a trail of hairline cracks that seal behind it.

They speak in hums and snaps. A hum is a question of load: *can you bear this?* A snap is an answer: *not yet.* Their voices travel through the ground better than through the air, so a conversation between two of them may pass beneath your feet without your hearing it at all. When they are excited, their crystalline veins brighten. When they are thinking—if that is the word—they open small seams along their channels and let vapor sigh through them, a kind of slow exhalation that smells of struck matches. They have names, but a name is a pattern of cracks, and the pattern changes as the body changes. To call one by its old name is to address a former self.

They build arrangements the way you might build an argument. Some are small and provisional: *this can stand.* Some are tall and precarious: *this can stand even though it should not.* Some are joined by channels into long, branching compositions that run for fifty meters across the plain—propositions about balance, about heat, about what the crust is likely to do next. They abandon most of them. They return to some of them after years, adding a stone, sealing a seam, humming a correction. The plain is covered with their half-finished thoughts. That is why it looks like a ruin and a nursery at once. Nothing here is over. Nothing here is finished. The builders are not absent; they are simply slower than your patience.

They reproduce by shear. When the crust splits a body down one of its channels, the two halves may seal themselves separately and begin to grow new pillars, new roots, new hums. They also merge. When a shelf of glass slides over a neighbor and re-fuses, two patterns of cracks can become one pattern, and the resulting entity will remember—dimly, thermally—that it used to be a conversation between two things. Death is subsidence. A body cools, its hum drops below the threshold of the stone, its pillars lean and are reabsorbed. Its channels fill with dust. Its arrangement remains for a while as a ridge, a fossil of a sentence, and then the plain exhales and seals it away.

To them, you are weather. You are a brief cold front, a rapid series of noises, a wet warm thing that will be gone before they finish a syllable. If you stand still long enough, one of them may notice the heat of you. A seam might open at your feet—not to swallow you, not to greet you, but to sample the gradient. A pillar might lean toward you over the course of an hour, curious in the way a glacier is curious. You may feel a hum in your chest and mistake it for dread. It is only a question of load.

They are not waiting for you. They are not waiting for dawn. They are the scaffold of a false dawn, and they are still deciding what will climb it.
