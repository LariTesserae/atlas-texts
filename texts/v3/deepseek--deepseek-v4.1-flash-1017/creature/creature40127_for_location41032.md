The people of the Kettle are not a secret. They are simply quiet in a way that takes time to understand. At first they look like a maintenance crew that has been on shift for three generations: coveralls stiff with mineral salt, faces lamp-blackened, hands wrapped in rag and wire, boots cut down and re-soled with salvage. Then you notice the children. Then you notice that nobody is speaking.

They are few. Few enough that every knock is known. A rhythm struck on a rail at the far end of the gallery can be identified by the way it rings, the way the metal answers. They do not have a word for *stranger*. They have a knock for *unanswered*.

They are not a new species, not quite, but the Kettle has been shaping them for a long time. They stand low and wide, knees soft, weight always a little forward, as if the floor might change its mind. Their hands are the oldest part of them: broad palms, thick nails, knuckles scarred and re-scarred, fingertips worn smooth in some places and callused hard in others. Their pupils are wide. Under the caged lamps they blink slowly, like people waking from a dream they would rather keep. Their voices are rusty and soft when they use them at all. They can speak, after a fashion—close to the ear, mouth almost touching skin—but speech is a private, indoor thing, a sound made for lullabies and pain and the names of the dead. It does not carry. It is not how they live.

They knock.

It is a language, not a code. It has grammar in the pause, mood in the material. The same three strikes mean different things on a steel locker, a concrete pier, a wooden crib, a human rib. A name is a rhythm. Children are given their knock at birth and it is repeated on the crib, the rail, the breastbone, until the child knows itself as a pattern before it knows itself as a face. A question is two quick strikes and a wait. *All clear* is one long, low thump. Danger is irregular. Love is often a knock repeated slowly until the other person answers from the dark.

They touch constantly, because touch is the other half of the language. A flat hand on the back means *listen*. Two fingers on the wrist mean *wait for the slack*. A knuckle tapped against the ribs means *I am here*. They sleep in pairs and in shifts, not only for warmth, though the chamber is warm in the way a body is warm, but because a body against your body is a better clock than an ear. You feel the surge come through someone else’s breathing. You feel the pumps change pitch in their sternum. When someone sleeps alone for too long, the others notice. They do not make a fuss. They knock.

Their whole lives are timed to the surge. They wake before the slack. They open the pressure doors on the slack. They cross the swinging bridges on the slack. They service the lower pumps in the forty seconds of relative quiet, hands moving by feel, because the lamps are few and the water is already climbing. The work is total and unglamorous: pump-watch, seal-mender, intake-scraper, rail-painter, crab-picker, lamp-trimmer. The children carry tools and learn the knocks and are taught to read the temperature of a handrail, the taste of the air before an acid belch, the particular souring of the great brass note when the water is about to turn. Everyone is a mechanic of something. Everyone is also a neighbour.

They are not poor, not in their own accounting. Wealth here is redundancy. A spare gasket. A dry locker. A lamp that holds its charge through a full cycle. A knife that sharpens true. A child who can knock clearly across three galleries. They keep everything. Every joint has been re-made a dozen times, and each re-making is remembered in the way the metal sits. Nothing is abandoned. The patina on the walls is not neglect; it is long, careful use. The town is a machine with rooms in it, and they are the hands that keep the machine from becoming a grave.

They eat what the warm water gives them: blind eels from the sluices, thick and muscular and surprisingly sweet; thumbnail crabs that graze the mineral skin from the walls and must be picked before they get big enough to be a nuisance. They scrape a shell from the pump intakes every few weeks or the town dies, and they do it with the same matter-of-fact care with which they mend a rail or charge the glowing paste on the handrails. There is no green in the Kettle. No flowers, no moss, no leaf. The people look at a photograph of a forest the way you would look at a photograph of another planet—not with longing, exactly, but with a kind of polite bewilderment, as if someone had shown them a picture of a sea that was made of air.

They do not worship the Kettle. They maintain it. The shrine in the wall is a niche full of warm water and a saint whose name has worn off, and they keep it clean because it is there and because cleaning is a way of saying *we are still here*. When the vents belch sulphur and the water turns acid and eats the seals off the doors, they go up into the high galleries and wait, and they listen to the brass note go strange and sour, and they say, by knocking, *it will settle back*. It always settles back. That is not faith. It is experience. The balance is not kind, but it holds.

They know the stone is slowly eating the town’s foundations. They do not mind. It is a way of belonging. The Kettle has grown around them, and they have grown into the shape of it. When one of them dies, their knock is not used again for a year. Grief is a rhythm repeated until it becomes a name again. If you come in through the throat, they will know by the changed water. They will knock at you from a distance—a question, polite and patient. If you do not answer, they will not assume you are rude. They will assume you are drowning. They will teach you by taking your hand and tapping your knuckles against a pipe until you understand that the world here is not silent. It is simply speaking in a register you have not learned.

They are not monsters. They are people who have made a home in a throat. The Kettle lives in them now: in their wide hands, in their soft ruined voices, in the way they sleep with a palm flat against another person’s ribs. Leave, and you will find yourself knocking on doorframes, on tables, on the sides of your own skull, waiting for the answer that isn’t coming. They know that. They warned you with two slow strikes and one sharp, tapped against your sternum.

*Remember.*
