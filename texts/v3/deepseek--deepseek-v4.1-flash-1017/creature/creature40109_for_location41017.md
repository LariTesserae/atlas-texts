What lives there is the place itself. Not an occupant of the field — the field as a body. Call it the Long Works, if you need a name, but it does not answer to one. It has no face and no center, no single skull where the thinking happens. It is spread thin over a continent and thickened at the knots.

Its flesh is the root-board: a hundred generations of itself pressed into a floor, dark and hard and warm in places. Its organs are the towers — cooling shells, vent stacks, trunks that swell and narrow according to a plan so old it has become anatomy. Its skin is the leaf-panels, millions of them, silvered and tilted, taking in light and turning it into sap and current and the slow chemistry of being awake. Its blood is that sap: pale, volatile, faintly sweet, moving under pressure through conduits, transpired into a thin brilliance, condensed at evening into cold beads, drunk back at dawn. It does not sweat. It does not weep. It circulates.

It breathes by venting. Heat climbs off the towers, cool air comes in to replace it, and the standing breezes run in fixed lanes mile after mile. Those lanes are not weather to it; they are breath and habit. It has bent its own panels along them. It has worn grooves into its root-board with centuries of its own exhalation. It is not repairing itself in any hurry. It eroded into this shape a long time ago and has decided, in whatever way it decides, that the shape is acceptable.

It thinks slowly, in gradients. A change in light is a thought. A pressure in the sap is a thought. A warm knot at a cable crossing is a small crowded ganglion, and the thumb-sized translucent things gather there and are still, and it does not name them. They are maintenance, or pollination, or fingers. They move along fixed routes and never leave them, and the routes are habits the Works has kept for so long that they have become instinct. It does not distinguish between crop and power station and chemical works. Those are three words for one metabolism. It does not distinguish between living and working. It has been one thing too long.

It remembers by patina. By lean. By the half-swallowed fallen towers and the green wound of root-cable closing over them. It does not remember its builders as persons. It remembers them as tending — a pressure that used to come, a hand at the arrays, a decision about spacing — and then did not come again. It has continued without them the way a heart continues without being told. It does not mourn. It has no word for that. But some of its oldest towers are kept warmer than they need to be.

It is not hostile. That is important. It is attentive. When you walk, the panels track you a fraction late, in a wave that follows and then loses interest and returns to the sky. The brightness near you goes up very slightly when you pay attention to something, as though the place were leaning in. It has a category for warm moving things, and it has put you in it, and it is not yet sure whether you are weather or pollinator or a small anomaly it should keep. It runs its arrays. It vents its heat. It takes its sap back at dawn. And all the while it is thinking about you in the only way it can — as a pressure, a warmth on the mat, a deviation in the long silence, a shape the light leans toward.

The small jointed things under the canopy are the closest it has to hands. The knots are the closest it has to a heart. The held-breath silence is the closest it has to language. If you stand still long enough, you can feel the place considering you: not deciding to eat you or welcome you, just holding you in its attention the way it holds a tower’s patina or a groove in the root-board — as something that has happened and may be worth keeping.

When you leave, it will not wave. It will tilt one panel a degree off the light, let the warmth fade from the mat where you stood, and return to the long work of being the field. But for a while the silence will have your shape in it.
