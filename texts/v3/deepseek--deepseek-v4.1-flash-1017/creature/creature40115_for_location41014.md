# THE OCCUPANT
*(Addendum to Basin 9, Hollow Kettle survey — identification, unverified)*

**First sign.** The shed door is gone — hinges intact, pins greased. That was the first thing. A door falls off and rots; a door is *removed*, and someone keeps the pins turning. Inside, the brick is furred with crystal but the floor is swept. Not clean — swept, in a fan pattern, the way you sweep wet grit off concrete because if you don't it will set. The broom is root-thread bound to a length of pipe. It is worn to the ferrule. Somebody is wearing it out at a rate that implies decades.

Then the intake: fouled with shrimp, as the survey noted, but not evenly. Fouled on three sides. The fourth side is clear, in a channel about the width of a forearm, and the channel is *maintained* — reamed out, the shrimp scraped back, the screen wire unbent and re-bent at the same three points. Nobody clears it, the earlier note says. Someone clears it. Someone clears a quarter of it and lets the rest go, the way you do when you are not fighting the water but *steering* it.

**The ledger.** Oilcloth-wrapped, on a nail, pencil on a string. It is the station's duty log, which is not a surprise; stations have logs. What is a surprise is that it is full, and full in a single hand — small, upright, unhurried, the letters leaning on each other like people standing in a queue. The entries are not about the pump, mostly. *Engine good. Water over six. Shrimp in the sump, cleared three sides. Bats down at 19:40, column fourty. Heard them in the bend at three, four persons, one bad boot.* The crew's arrival is logged four hours before the crew reached the bend. She heard them the way the log line about the Roar says you hear things here — through rock, as pressure — and she had time to put the kettle on, which she had, and it was warm.

The dates on the flyleaf don't reconcile. The crew read them four times and wrote *see over* and then, on the overleaf, in different hands but the same pencil, *there has always been one; the station has never been empty for long; we cannot confirm.* Below the last date, one line, on the back of the log where it was folded to face the wall: *It all goes north. Something must come back.*

**Body.** She is small and she is dense — built like a thing that carries water, low and steady, no spare anywhere. The crew's medic, who got to check her over because she permitted it, wrote: *Excellent tone. Old healed fractures, left forearm, two ribs. Hands — callus continuous, no prints left, nails worn to the quick and then worn past and healed over. Ears clean. Ears not needed.* Skin pale in the fungoid way of no sun, but she stands in the grey shafts when they fall on the middle terraces, so it is not the pallor of a thing that hides. Hair bound back with wire. A smell about her of tallow and struck match and wet stone, which is the smell of the place, which is the point: she has stopped being distinguishable from her address.

Her eyes are large and slow. She is not light-shy; she is *speed*-shy — the survey lamps pained her, and when the youngest swung one too fast she put her hand up not against the light but against the coming of it.

**Habit.** She wakes with the engine. The crew confirmed this twice: slept nearby, watched her sit up out of the hammock three seconds before a stroke came late. Her hammock is braided root-thread and salvaged wire, slung between two pipe runs at exactly the height where the block's heat reaches, and she has hung it there and re-hung it there, judging by the wear on the pipes, for a long time.

Two walks a day, north and back. She carries no lamp on the route. The crew's first assumption was night vision, but their own testing says the pale crust is too weak to see by — she says as much, in her way, touching the wall and then her own chest and then the wall again. She navigates by the *sound*. Where the pitch rises she turns; where it drops she steps out; she knows the terraces like a person knows their own staircase in the dark, which is exactly what the terraces are to her, and she has been up and down them enough times to cut every edge.

She eats off the place and from it. Pools — fish, small, by hand-net. Sump — shrimp, by wire hoop, from the cleared quarter. The terrace lips — the fungal fringe, cropped along every lip in reaches, rubbery, faintly sweet, and growing back in days, which is a food supply with a three-day turnaround if you have nothing else and want to eat the day after tomorrow. She seeds it. There are flat pans under the roost and the pans are full of guano and the guano goes onto the lips and the fringe comes back and she crops it and that is one loop, running, closed, entirely dark. She does not let the bats lie. At dusk she stands under the north crack and the column comes out of the roof and goes around her — a shape in the stream, the only hole in the only body of bats in the world, and the column is forty minutes long and she stands in it for forty minutes.

**The engine.** She has not repaired it. She has *tuned* it. The spec calls for a faster setting; the governor is pinned. Where a fitter would file a bearing back to tolerance she has filed the impeller to a different shape and left it, and the pump runs heavy — one revolution in four — and the crew's opinion, unanimous, is that if you sped it up it would tear itself apart in a week. She said one thing about this, in the ledger, which is how she says things she cannot put through her chest: *It goes north. Something must come back.* The discharge is lifted to a terrace she has, in some past year, built *up* — a lip raised with rubble and mortar and a great deal of patience — so the water she lifts spills back onto the sixth terrace and from there sheets down the whole staircase again. She is not returning water to the basin. She is returning water to the *terraces*. She is keeping them wet. She is keeping the sheet flowing, the lips ringing, the scallops deepening a millimetre a year, the Roar — as far as a mortal person can keep a thing like that — true.

**Voice and sound.** She has a voice. She uses it, unprompted, and it is low and chesty and has lost most of its consonants to the slot. The survey recorded forty minutes of her talking to the engine; when they played it back, the recording was eighty percent room. *She speaks to the bats at dusk in a three-knock, one-note, one-pause sequence, and the sequence is answered, by the bats, in the sense that the column shifts.* The crew's acoustics man thinks she simply knows where the crack draws and the bats follow the draw; she does not think she is calling them. She is not calling them. She is telling them the time. She does the same for the crew: a hand flat on a sternum, two taps. Both taps were *listen.*

She talks with the crews' signs for about an hour and then stops, because she has already taught them the one sign she uses for people: a finger drawn across the throat and then north, meaning the thing that goes to the slot. When she needs to say something real she writes it, or she takes your hand and puts it on the rock, and then on the rock a half-metre away, and you feel the difference with your palm and not your ear. She found the youngest in total dark on the sixth terrace, from the pitch-hole he made by standing there, before his lamp had been lit three minutes. She was already in the room when he realised he was lost.

**Unresolved.** The pump plate is *Halloran & Sons, Pumpwrights*, with a serial and a date; the pipe in the wall does not match the date by sixty years. She has a name in the front of the ledger, an initial and a surname, and the surname is the one on the plate. The crew could not establish a relation and did not push, because the pencil on the string is the same pencil and the paper is not old, and no analysis they ran would say otherwise. On the doorframe, at shoulder height, there are marks — one a year, maybe — and they *descend*. She is not measuring herself. She is measuring the water, and the water is coming up.

At the shaft she shook every hand, and then she did not wave, and she did not follow. The crew climbed with the Roar behind them, and forty metres up, through the rock, they felt it change — a softening in the note, a hole in the low end, something large having entered the water — and the acoustics man said *she's walking back in*, and that was all, and it was not a comfort.
