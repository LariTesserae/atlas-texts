Nothing lives here the way a house is lived in. There is no tenant, no keeper, no one who tends the moss or counts the strokes of the clack. What lives here is a schedule, and the schedule has a body—stone plates, wet channels, vents breathing steam—and the body is so entirely given over to the schedule that the two can't be told apart. The Bellows doesn't have an occupant. It has a function, and the function has grown itself a room to run in.

But things do come. They come thinly, and they come on business, and they are never the same animal twice in a row.

The frog lives in the moss near the warm vents and has lived there long enough that its skin has gone the pale grey-green of the wet stone, and it does not jump, and it does not blink at you. It eats what the schedule brings it: small things that hatch in the channels when the water finds a level, brief and punctual and gone. It has never seen the rim. It has no reason to. Its whole world is a hand's width of cushion and the warm seam behind it, and its route across that world is a route something else walked before it and something else will walk after, and the frog does not know this and would not care.

The beetle lives in the channels themselves, walking their edges, lacquered and fussy, and it is not going anywhere. It is patrolling, or it is checking, or it is simply being what a beetle is at the edge of a channel—the passage doesn't tell you which, and the beetle won't. It arrives at the same turns at the same intervals. It does not age in any way you could measure. It has been doing this, or something indistinguishable from this, for longer than the moss has been thick.

The centipede crosses the open stone and does not acknowledge boots. It has a route and the route is the frog's route and the beetle's route, and it is never on the route when they are, and it is always on the route when they are. It has no shepherding instinct. It is not guiding anything. It is just that the paths are the paths, and everything that lives here walks them, because the stone is bare and wet and the paths are the only dry-enough ground, and because there is nothing here that has ever needed to go anywhere else.

The bird is not an inhabitant. The bird comes to the rim, looks down, and leaves. It comes because the Bellows is a landmark, a thing known and checked on, the way you might look in on an old relative who is fine and will always be fine. It never descends. There is nothing down there for it. The bowl is warm and wet and loud and full of small crawling things it could eat, and it doesn't, and that is the closest thing to a mystery in the whole place: the bird has decided, for reasons it will not explain, that the Bellows is not for eating from. It is for checking. It checks. It leaves.

The moths have no night to be confused by and are not confused. They live in the dim sourceless light the way moths elsewhere live in dark. They are the only things here that seem busy in the ordinary way, fluttering and settling and fluttering again, and they are also the only things here that don't keep a route—they drift. They are the one loose thread in the place, and the place tolerates them, and you get the sense the place would tolerate almost anything that didn't try to change it.

And then there is the thing you can't see, the one that makes the clack. It isn't an animal. It's a chamber in the northern wall you can't reach, two stone faces meeting and parting on a schedule, and whatever moves them is heat and water and the standing strata, and nothing else. Nobody lives in that chamber. But if you had to point at where the life of the Bellows is concentrated—where the pumping is, where the work gets done—you would point at that clack, and you would be right, and there would be nothing there to meet your eye.

That's the whole census. A frog, a beetle, a centipede, moths, a bird that only visits, and a stroke of stone meeting stone every eleven or twelve heartbeats. None of them built anything. None of them will be remembered by the place. They are not its inhabitants so much as its regulars—it is the same to the Bellows whether they come or not, and they come, and they will keep coming, because the warmth is here and the water is here and the paths are here, and there is nowhere else on this shelf of cloud that offers anything better.

The one thing that does not live here is you. You are the only moving part the room has no use for. You drip your minutes onto the stone and the stone does not take them, and the hiss goes on, and the clack goes on, and the frog does not jump, and the bird checks and leaves, and the schedule keeps its own time, which is not your time, and has never been.
