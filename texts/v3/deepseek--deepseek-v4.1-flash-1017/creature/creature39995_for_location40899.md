The people of this place are, in a way, a fourth species in the treaty. They are not many. A few hundred, perhaps, scattered across the plateau's folds and hollows. They are a people who have been *made* by the place, shaped as surely as the wind has twisted the shrubs. You see it in the way they move: a slow, deliberate economy of motion, every step placed with an awareness of what lies underfoot—the slick moss, the hidden root, the warped board. They don't hurry. Hurrying is for the lower valleys.

Their bodies have adapted. They are, on average, shorter and stockier than the lowland folk, with a low center of gravity, built for climbing and for balance on narrow catwalks. Their hands are a particular thing—broad, thick-fingered, the knuckles enlarged, the skin permanently calloused and stained with the dark green of lichen and the rust-brown of iron oxide. They have a habit of touching things as they pass: running a palm along a pipe to feel its temperature, pressing a hand against a tree trunk as if checking a pulse. It's not a conscious gesture. It's how they read the world.

Their faces are weathered, wind-burned, with the fine, permanent squint of people who live under a sky that reflects rather than shines. They don't look at you directly, not at first. Their gaze drifts to what's behind you, above you, to the state of the canopy or the color of the steam venting from a nearby joint. They are always, on some level, assessing the system.

They dress in layers of felted wool and oiled canvas, dyed in the dull, beautiful colors of the plateau: the grey-green of the moss, the deep amber of the sap, the rusted red of the old iron. Their clothes are patched and repatched, not from poverty but from a deep-seated belief that a thing is not finished until it has been repaired at least twice. A child's coat is handed down and added to, becoming a palimpsest of the family's history. They wear no ornaments, but their tools are personal, worn smooth by decades of use, their handles carved with small, intricate patterns that are less decoration than record—a tally of seasons, perhaps, or a map of the specific pipes a plumber is responsible for.

Their language is low and slow, full of long vowels and pauses. They speak in a kind of shorthand, referring to things by their function or their state rather than their name. A pipe is "the hot one" or "the one that ticks." A tree is "the grandmother" or "the one by the cistern." They don't say "the central plant is running well." They say "she's humming sweet today," and everyone knows what that means. Their conversations are punctuated by shared silences, during which they simply listen to the place. A newcomer might find this unnerving. To them, it is the most natural thing in the world. You cannot talk over the sound of the plateau. You must wait for it to make room for you.

Their work is the maintenance of the world. There are the plumbers, of course, the most respected caste, who crawl through the root-masses with their wrenches and their replacement valves, their bodies contorted into the tight, dark spaces where the ancient wood meets the modern pipe. They are the doctors of the system, feeling for the flush of hot water, listening for the tell-tale hiss of a leak. There are the arborists, who are not pruners but diplomats, negotiating with the trees. They read the health of the great trunks in the color of the leaves, the sound of the hum, the direction of the new growth. When a branch must be cut, it is done with a kind of ritual apology, and the wood is never wasted; it becomes a beam, a tool handle, a child's toy. There are the moss-tenders, who cultivate the terraces of edible lichen and the small, bitter tubers that grow in the shadow of the roots. They are the quietest of all, their hands always in the soil, their eyes always on the slow, patient growth of their crops.

And there are the children. They are the strangest, the most unsettling to an outsider. They are born into the hum. It is the first sound they hear, the lullaby that puts them to sleep. By the time they can walk, they can read the pressure of a pipe by the sound it makes. They play games on the catwalks that involve identifying which engine is turning, which sluice is opening. They don't need to be taught the rules; they absorb them like the moss absorbs water. The most important lesson—don't let the pressure drop, don't let the fire go out—is not a rule to them. It is simply the way the world works. A child of six can tell you, with absolute certainty, the difference between the hum of a healthy tree and the hum of a sick one. They will tell you, in their flat, serious voices, that "the grandmother is sad today," and they will be right.

They are not a religious people, not in any way you'd recognize. They don't pray to the trees or the machines. But they have a profound, unspoken reverence for the system as a whole. They believe—if that's the word—that the plateau is a single, living thing, and that they are a part of it, as essential as the moss or the pipes or the roots. They believe that their continued existence depends on the health of the whole, and that the health of the whole depends on their constant, careful attention. They are not masters of their world. They are its custodians. They are the hands that tend the fire, the ears that listen for the hum, the eyes that watch for the first sign of rust. And they are, in some strange, quiet way, content.

Listen. You can hear them now, if you're still. The low murmur of a conversation on a terrace above. The clank of a wrench on a pipe. The soft, rhythmic sound of a moss-tender's hoe. And underneath it all, the hum. The deep, vegetable, mechanical hum of a place that is thinking its slow, mineral thoughts. The people are its thoughts made flesh. They are the plateau's way of tending to itself, its way of keeping the treaty. They are not the protagonists of this story. They are the chorus, the maintenance crew, the dreamers in the machine.
