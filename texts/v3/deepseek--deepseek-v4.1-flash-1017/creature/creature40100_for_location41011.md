What lives in Settlow is not quite a population. It is a metabolism with several bodies, and only one of them is human.

The first and most numerous are **the grain**. They are thumbnail-sized, dark, and shelled in grey mineral they have drunk and precipitated themselves; five or six fill a palm, and a million fill a street. They come up from the sump in slow tides and cross the paving in sheets, not hurrying, not scattering, moving in a direction that seems to be decided somewhere behind them. They climb the walls. They enter the machines and come out of the machines. They eat the fine metal dust the works shed, the dust of the paving, the condensation on cold iron, and one another. They are not infestation. They are the place’s circulating tissue, and their bodies are warm enough that the air at your face stays cool while your shins feel the mildness of a crowd.

They make the sound, too. The ticking, sifting, pouring noise is not incidental: in their millions they hold a low major chord, slightly out of tune, and the machinery underneath it keeps the rhythm. The grain do not have faces you can read, but they have a collective habit that is almost a face — the way a sheet of them pauses at a threshold, thickens, chooses, and goes on. At dusk they are briefly joined by the winged things that come up out of the pipes and eaves, thickening the air over the sump until the far bank is gone. Those live in the crevices and the seams and are visible only at their shift change. By full dark they are gone, every night, at the same time.

Then there are **the tall ones**. Six or seven of them stand in the sump on legs like jointed stilts, motionless, facing the same direction all day. Sometimes one faces a different direction. They breathe. They are not statues and not quite inhabitants in the way the grain are inhabitants; they are more like the place’s standing furniture, or its unanswered questions. Nobody remarks on them. They do not eat, or mend, or keep the gutters. They simply persist in the water, warm at the ankle, and the grain moves around their legs as if around roots.

**The people** are a few hundred, in low houses along two streets and a path. They keep the gutters. They quarry the crust where shed shells have welded into limestone and use it in courses; every house in Settlow is visibly made of animals that used to be alive, and looks it, and does not feel morbid, because there is no wood here — no board, no rag, no rope from plants. They mend the plates of the shed roof. They do not feed the grain; the grain feeds itself. They are cheerful and unhurried and loud when they speak, or else they say a great deal with their faces, because the chord does not leave room for ordinary conversation. They do not want anything from a visitor except that the visitor not stand in the troughs.

They are also the thinnest part of the place. The town is theirs the way a reef is a diver’s: they work in it, shelter in it, are held and shaped by it, but they are not the substance of it. Their footprints fill in with grain within a day; their smell is gone in two. This is not hostility. A lake absorbs a dropped stone in the same way. The people know it. They have built their lives at the margin of something that was here before them and will be here after, and they have made a cheerful, habitable margin out of it.

The **crust** is not alive, but it is made of the living — centuries of shed shells and uneaten bodies welded into a grey fur two fingers thick on the north walls, thicker in the lee of the sheds, hard as limestone and warm as a hand. The houses are cut from it; the walls of the hollow are furred with it; in certain dampnesses it takes a faint violet sheen, like the inside of a mussel, and goes grey again when you look away. It is the place’s slow skeleton, and the people quarry it the way people elsewhere quarry stone.

Taken together, they are one finished arrangement. The grain are the blood and the chord; the works are the gut and the rhythm; the tall ones are the bones or the standing questions; the winged things are the daily exhalation; the people are a thin, clever nervous system at the edge, keeping the gutters clear. Nothing here is going anywhere. The grain does not rise or fall, the wheel turns at one speed, the rain comes three or four times a day, the winged things rise at dusk, the tall ones face the same direction, and the chord is the same chord. The people do not add to Settlow so much as attend it.

If you stand on the rim, you are the only thing in Settlow that is arriving. By the time you leave, that will have been corrected.
