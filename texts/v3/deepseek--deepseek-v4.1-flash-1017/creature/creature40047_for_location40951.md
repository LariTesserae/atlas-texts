The ones who live in the Kiln are easiest to miss. At a distance a builder looks like a fold in the pale masonry, a hood-shaped shadow that has learned to kneel. You see the lamp first—small, cold, burning on a half-raised wall—and then, if you are patient, the shape beside it: small, hooded, quiet, hands busy with a block or a cord. They do not startle. They do not look up unless there is a reason. They are not hiding. They are simply attending to the work.

Under the hood the face is small and wet and unremarkable, the face of someone who has been listening for a long time. The hood itself is not weather gear; the Kiln has no weather, only breath. It makes a pocket of dusk around the eyes. The skin is pale, damp, warm at the knuckles, and smells faintly of chalk and iron. Their hands are the largest thing about them—broad palms, thick joints, nails worn to nothing. They leave prints on dry stone that fade as the stone drinks. Their feet are wrapped in cord and soft fibre, and they step without sound. They carry no metal. Their tools are a stone maul no bigger than a fist, wooden levers, coils of cord, a flat stone for tapping, a pouch of the pale waxy fuel. They set these things down and forget them; tools are not owned here. They lift blocks with their backs and knees and the patient leverage of their own bodies, and when a block is set they check the joint with a knuckle, squat, and watch the water find its way through the new seam. They do not hurry the water. They wait until it has said what it means.

They work alone or in pairs. Two builders at a block do not speak. One lifts, one steadies. They breathe in time. When the block is set, one taps it; the other watches the bead of condensation gather and run. If the water turns upward along the joint, they nod and adjust the next course to accommodate it. If a finished wall un-finishes—if a course loosens and rises a hand’s breadth, dripping, and settles back into a new arrangement—they do not shout or curse. They come back in an hour and lay the blocks again. Sometimes in a different order. The wall is not wrong. It is not yet.

Their speech is rare and short and declarative. “The seam is breathing.” “Set it wet.” “That stair is new.” “Leave the lamp.” They say the work, and the work turns out to mean something else. “The seam is breathing” may mean *I am here*. “Set it wet” may mean *wait*. “Leave the lamp” may mean *do not follow me*. “That stair is new” may mean *I do not know either*. They do not explain. A statement is placed in the air the way a lamp is placed on a wall: to be seen, to mark a presence, to claim a little space of making.

They do not appear to eat. No one has seen them sleep. In the quiet hours, when the world holds still and the only sound is the ticking of condensation, a builder will stand with one hand flat on a wall and let the ticking pass through them. That is rest, perhaps. They do not gather often. Sometimes two or three will squat near a lamp and speak their short declaratives, and the sentences will seem to be about joints and water and the newness of stairs, and then one will rise and walk away into the pearl-grey murk, and the others will rise soon after. No one has seen a child among them. No one has seen a corpse. They are all of a size, all hooded, all patient. They seem less born than arrived—found behind a wall that was not there yesterday, already laying blocks, as if the Kiln had produced them the way it produces a seam or a puddle.

They are not masters of the place. They are not its servants either. They are its way of attending. Distances change around them; a corridor they walked an hour ago ends in a wet new wall; a plinth becomes a stair descending into a hall they have never seen. They do not consult maps. They read seams and puddles and the placement of lamps. They remember by the way water runs. When the murk moves and the rules shift, they pause, hood bowed, and then begin again from where they are. They know they will not finish. They say so. It is not despair; it is a condition of work, like wet.

Their lamps are their signatures. They carry them not to see by—the pale walls shine enough for that—but to be seen. A lamp set on a half-built wall says: *someone is here, this is claimed, this is being made*. When they walk away, the lamp burns on. It claims nothing, marks nothing, except that the making went on. The builders leave a trail of such small cold flames across the vast unfinished courses, and if you follow them far enough you will find more hooded figures, more patient hands, more blocks laid and re-laid, and the water ticking down a thousand surfaces toward the puddles, and the heat breathing up from below.

If you ask what lives in the Kiln, the honest answer is not a people or a creature. It is a patience with hands. The builders are that patience—small, hooded, endless—laying blocks they know will move, setting lamps they know will burn, and listening, always listening, to the water find its way.
