The Verdant Cleft is not inhabited by a population so much as by a negotiation. People live there, yes—but so do the green, the water, the machines, the things in the peripheral dark, and the canyon itself, which is still deciding what shape to take. The residents have learned to share the conditional tense.

## The Cleftborn

The people born in the canyon are damp in a way that never quite dries. Their skin carries a faint mineral sheen at the cheekbones; their nails are ridged like wet slate; their eyes have taken on the color of lamp-flame seen through mist. They do not find the blue-grey dusk gloomy. It is simply the light they were issued.

They learn early that depth is a suggestion. A lamp that looks near may be far; a staircase that seems to end may continue behind a fold of rock. Children play games of stair-count and depth-guess, shrieking when a doorway they trusted turns out to be a painted panel on a wall. They can hear the slow grinding of the canyon—the walls adjusting by fractions of an inch—and they call it *the settling*, or sometimes *the adjustment*. It is not a bad sound to them. It is the sound of the place remaining a place.

They are practical in the way of people who live inside a maintenance problem. They patch, brace, re-carve, re-rope. They know which staircases have names and which are only described by the sound they make underfoot. They do not stare at the flowers. They learn to look slightly away from anything too green, because attention feeds it, and a vine that is watched too fondly will remember your window.

Their names often come from the sounds they heard first: Latch, Seep, Girder, Vess, Clatter, Drip. This is not because their parents lack poetry. It is because the canyon is already speaking, and the children arrive into the middle of its sentence.

## The Arrived

Not everyone was born here. The Cleft is full of people who came expecting to leave. Engineers, debtors, factory hires, runaways, couriers, people following a road that seemed shorter on the map than it turned out to be. They built provisional rooms and then reinforced them. They stacked buildings on buildings. They told themselves the scaffolding was temporary until the scaffolding became the architecture.

Arrival is a process here; some never finish it. The Arrived keep curtains over their windows and boots drying by the door. They learn not to argue loudly, because arguments make fog, and fog makes the stairs treacherous. They learn to laugh when the laundry is wet, because laughter brings a momentary dryness. They measure time by condensation and shift-whistles and the brief midday shaft of sun that falls like a coin into the canyon floor.

They have a saying: *Anything not maintained is already gone.* They mean buildings, ropes, relationships, the path down to the lower pools. They are not a sentimental people, but they are a courteous one. Courtesy is load-bearing here.

## The Riveted

Higher up, where the factories are cut into the canyon wall, live the shift-crews—the ones the lower terraces call the Riveted. They are visible through the open walls as silhouettes against green-tinged flame, moving among pistons and gears and conveyor systems that process materials no one below can name. They come home with soot in the creases of their knuckles and a vibration in their bones that never entirely stops.

They speak less than the people below, or they speak in hand-signals and short bursts. The machinery has taken some of their hearing and given them instead a fine sensitivity to rhythm. They know when a gear is about to seize by the change in the floor under their feet. Some have replaced joints with salvage; their bodies are as provisional as the buildings. They do not discuss the shapes glimpsed behind the structures. They leave that to the stair-keepers, and they leave the offerings on their way to shift.

They are not masters of the machines. They are in a working arrangement with them. The machines make heat and noise and the low vibration that never quite stops; the canyon makes damp and green and the slow grinding; the workers keep both from deciding too suddenly what they want to become.

## The Stair-Keepers

The oldest custom in the Cleft belongs to the stair-keepers—the aunties and uncles of the lower terraces, the ones who know which staircases are safe after dark and which should be avoided without being named. They leave offerings at certain niches: bread, boiled roots, small metal charms, a cup of seep-water. They do not call this worship. They call it *courtesy*.

They teach children to avert their eyes from the green, to leave the goats their salt, to listen for the adjustment before they sleep. They keep ledgers—not of disappearances, exactly, but of *borrowings*: a glove taken, a spoon returned three days later warm from some heat no one can identify, a lamp found lit that was empty when left. They say of the large shapes in the peripheral dark: *They were here first. We are the weather.*

The stair-keepers are the closest thing the Cleft has to memory. They remember when a certain pool was dry, when a certain staircase went by another name, when the plateau rim seemed close enough to touch. They do not try to ascend. They keep the lower terraces polite.

## The Uncounted

The things that share the canyon are not often seen directly. They occupy the gaps: behind lamplight, under staircases, in the seam between rock and foundation, in the moment after a sound when the echo should have arrived but didn’t. The residents call them nothing at all, or they call them *the Uncounted*, and even that is said with a slight turn of the head, as if the words should not be aimed too precisely.

They are large in the way a shadow is large. They are patient. They do not threaten; they simply occupy. They accept the offerings left at the niches and sometimes leave mineral gifts in return—a vein of something bright in a wall that was plain yesterday, a stone shaped like a hand. Some say it is the Uncounted who adjust the canyon walls at night, that the slow grinding is their settling, their housekeeping. Others say they were here before the city and will be here after, and that the whole clamor of factories and staircases is a brief weather passing over them.

The people do not name them because naming is a kind of claim, and no one wants to claim the Uncounted. They share the space with a patience that suggests ownership.

## The Green

The green is a tenant too. It is aggressive, suspicious, and not entirely without intention. Ferns erupt from cracks; moss carpets surfaces that were bare stone a decade ago; vines descend from the plateau rim and pry the canyon wider year by year. The residents know it grows faster when observed. So they prune by touch, with shears and breath and averted eyes. They hang mirrors angled away from the walls. They let moss take roofs but not doorframes. They do not compliment the flowers.

There are green-widows—people, usually older women, sometimes men, who tend the overgrowth without looking at it directly. They work in the blue dusk by feel, trimming what has grown too close to a window, coaxing a vine away from a support beam, speaking to the plants in low voices that are not quite apologies. The green is not a garden. It is a neighbor that has not agreed to terms, and the canyon is the room it shares with everyone else.

## The Beasts and the Water

Birds nest in the machinery, their songs interspersed with the sound of gears. Something like goats—too many joints, too flexible—moves up and down the cliff faces, grazing on fern-covered walls. Children leave them salt and dare each other to count their knees. Insects swarm in the damp air, their movements forming patterns that some of the older residents read as signs, though they will not say what the signs mean.

The water is another inhabitant, or perhaps a mood of the canyon. Pools appear where no water should accumulate. Channels flow in directions that contradict expectation. The seep-readers know the water’s habits; they map the pools by name and temperament, and they know that moisture responds to feeling. Arguments create fog. Laughter brings dryness. A household in grief can make the stairs weep for a day. The people have learned to live with a weather that is partly their own.

## The Witnesses

What lives in the Verdant Cleft, finally, is a people who have accepted that they are not building a city so much as keeping one from becoming something else too quickly. They are maintenance crews and offerers, factory hands and green-widows, children who know the sound of settling and old people who know which staircases should not be named after dark. They live alongside tenants that were there first and will be there after. They do not expect the canyon to resolve itself.

At midday, when the sun falls in a narrow shaft and the fog thins, they pause. The place seems to hold its breath. They know, in the way of people who live inside a question, that being seen is part of what holds it together. So they put small stones in doorways—not for luck, but as witnesses. They look, briefly, at the full height of the stacked terraces and the impossible green and the water threading down through it all.

Then the shaft closes. The blue-grey dusk returns. They pick up their tools and go back to keeping up.
