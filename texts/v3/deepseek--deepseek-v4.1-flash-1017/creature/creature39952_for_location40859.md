The first residents you notice are the ones with no names. A blind fly the size of a seed is not a species here so much as a condition of the air. They are born in the wet mats around the sumps, and they spend their whole lives inside the dome — there is no outside for them, and no reason to want one. The warmth is here. The moisture is here. The food is here, which is mostly each other and the mats and whatever the water brings. A fly will drift maybe forty feet in its few days, up a warm column over the boiler housing and back down, and never once leave the sound of the pump. When it dies it falls, and the water takes it, and the water's own population eats it. The grainy look of the lamplight is them. The Kettle's air is not empty; it is inhabited at the density of dust.

The water is inhabited more densely still. The shrimp-things crowd the cooling outflow until the pipe wears a pelt of them, pale and finger-length and blind, and blindness is no hardship because the water is a map they read with their bodies — temperature, chemistry, the slick of algae on warm stone. Watch one and it will work a patch of wall in a slow figure, mouthparts going, then move exactly one body-length and begin again. It is a small patient machine of wet tissue, and when it molts the others eat the molt. Under the ledges the flat whiskered things eat the shrimp, and are seen mostly as a correction in the green underlight: a shape that was there, and then the water has a vacancy in it. The economy is a loop and it closes. The mats feed the grazers, the grazers feed the flat things, the flat things die and feed the mats. Nothing in it is wasted and nothing in it is in a hurry.

The large ones keep to the edges, and you will mostly not see them. They are the only residents with caution in them, and caution is a kind of knowledge — they are the only animals in the Kettle who understand that there is a *you*. They have learned the engine's rhythm and they move in the troughs between its thuds, and they have learned which of the three bulbs is dead and which two cast shadows worth trusting. When you come down the gallery they will already know, and they will have gone wherever it is they go, behind the pipework, into the boarded hole, into the older workings where nobody follows. They will come back when the pump has been thudding alone for a while. They are not watching you. They are simply the only ones here who have anywhere else to be.

The creeper is different from all of them, because it is the only thing in the Kettle that is trying to get somewhere. Each patch is a hand's breadth, widely spaced, and each one is colonizing a single drip — where the water runs, it spreads; where the water fails, it stops. It is nearly white, having nothing here to make it green, and it lays itself along the warmest seam it can find, following the pipe's heat and the lamp's light in equal measure, which means it is following two different promises toward the same place, and will never arrive. In a hundred years it will have crept as far as the raw eastern wall and it will pause there, because the wall does not drip yet. It is the closest thing the chamber has to ambition.

Then there are the machines, which are residents too, and the only ones whose will comes from outside. The engine has a rhythm, and the rhythm is the floor of everything; it is fed and watered and it does not stop, and the pumps are its lungs and the boiler is its stomach. The belt that slips and catches, slips and catches, is its tic, its small unresolved complaint, and no one has fixed it because it still catches. And on the gauge glass there is a red line, hand-painted. Somebody came down the incline gallery with a brush and a tin and made a promise on the glass. That person is a resident in the way a farmer is a resident of a field — present mostly in the marks they leave. They come down, they read the glass, they feed the boiler, they listen to the belt, they go back up. They are the only one who leaves and the only one who knows the red line is a wish, not a fact. Everyone else in the Kettle lives entirely inside the consequences of that wish.

And the Kettle itself — is it a resident? It is a place in the middle of becoming itself, half finished and half raw, and the nearest thing it has to a mind is its patience. The drip is its landlord: the drip sets the rent, the drip decides where the creeper goes and where the shrimp gather and when the seep will soften the sheared rock. In a hundred years the creep will have covered the eastern wall and the pumps will have settled into their final rhythm and the chamber will be what it is becoming. Now it is embryonic, and everything in it — the swarm, the shrimp, the flat things at the ledges, the engine, the hand that paints red lines — is content to be slow. It is very hot, very wet, very loud, and very full of life that does not know or care that it is underground. It keeps its own counsel. It is not yet finished, and it is not in a hurry.
