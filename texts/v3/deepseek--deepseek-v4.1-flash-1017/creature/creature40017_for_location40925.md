At first the honest answer is not a *who*. It is the Knot itself.

The town is the inhabitant. One continuous plant-body, felted and grafted and scored into chambers, a single living mass that has been persuaded into architecture and then left to keep growing. It lives on bedrock gas and grit and the slow recollection of its own dead. It draws the heavy vapor in through its pores and moves it through hollow fiber in four-hour tides, and the whole structure swells and settles, swells and settles, and the drone is its breath resonating in a hundred chambers tuned by their own growth. It has no eyes and no need of the idea of a surface. Its senses are pressure, chemistry, strain, the wet crack of a floor deciding after a decade to give a millimeter. It seals what is broken with pale scar tissue warmer than the wall by a degree. It grows toward closure. It is not haunted; it is inhabited by its own slow metabolism, and it is finishing.

And then, in the spaces between chambers, the almost-none: the last people.

A few. The *almost* in “almost no people” is the population. They are scattered far apart, one warm presence in a barn-sized hollow, then forty paces of dark and drone before the next. They are not a village anymore. They are a residue of hands.

They are upright people. They do not lie down. The gas pools low, knee-high, sweet and oily, and to lie down is to breathe it; so they sleep propped in fiber slings at chest height, or standing against a wall with a cord under the arms, and they wake without having fallen. They are thin and dust-floured and resin-stained at the palms and knees. They smell of tar and overripe yeast. Their eyes have very little work and perhaps very little left; there is nothing to see, and they have never needed the concept of a lamp. They read the town by the drone and by the grain of the felt under their hands. They know a chamber’s shape by how it answers a footfall. They speak little; speech is a thin thing in that sound. They tap on taut cords, feel vibrations through the floor, and know each other by the way the dark changes when someone moves.

They carry stone blades and bone wedges and fiber rope and fire-hardened points — not to build, not anymore. They score a constriction that has grown too tight. They prune a swelling. They tie a cord around a limb that will swallow the knot in a century. They walk certain paths, and the walking is the maintenance: a path walked every day stays open; a path not walked closes in thirty years. They are the reason there are still paths. They know the Knot is closing, and they do not fight it. They attend it. They take the pale sterile growths that swell in sealed chambers, and the slow amber resin, and they are spare with both.

They are not ghosts. Their palms are warm. They have names, or had them; the names have worn off the way the town’s name has worn off, because there is no one left to call across a distance. There are no children running in the galleries. The last generation is old, and it is not replacing itself. What lives there now is a long habit of care in a body that is closing, and a handful of upright, lightless people who have become the Knot’s caretakers and its residue at once.

So what lives in the Hearth-Knot is a double thing, almost one thing: a vast blind plant-town breathing its four-hour breath in the dark, and the last few people who keep one or two apertures open by walking through them, not because anyone is coming, but because walking is what they are for.
