No one lives there, if by “one” you mean an animal with a face. There is no fly in the mist, no beetle in the rubble, no bird under the sealed roof. What lives there is a pale, wet, collective thing: a grove packed into fresh stone, and the water schedule that drives it.

Call the inhabitant the grove, though it is less a garden than a single hydraulic organism assembled from many plants. Its body is slabs of dripping moss, an inch thick and furred like the pelt of something that never dries. Its veins are creepers that have filled every crack and made the fractures look deliberate. Its hands are broad soft leaves layered so tightly you cannot slide a palm between them. Its pipes are the hollow stalks, pale and jointed, standing in clusters like the stops of an organ and half full of water. Its mouths are the bladder-plants along the floor, snapping shut and popping open on the pulse. Everything about it is wet and catching: hairy, waxy, cupped, tilted toward fog and vent and condensation. It has grown toward water instead of light. It is cream and bone and a green so deep it reads as black.

The grove’s world is forty seconds long. It does not experience the room as a place so much as a rhythm. Pressure builds somewhere above, in fissures too narrow to enter. Then something gives, and the room is struck: water through the fist-width vent, down the chute behind the boulder, out of a dozen holes at once. Mist jumps. The hollow stalks take the surge and shriek in their different pitches, a chord made by the length of each pipe and how much water it holds. The bladder-plants clap shut. The loose rubble lifts and resettles. The whole mat of vegetation overhead moves as one membrane, drawing air in and pushing it out through the seam, so the room exhales and the leaves rub and the sound comes from everywhere. Then the pressure drops, the vents suck air, everything gulps, and there is a long minute of dripping and ticking before the build begins again.

So the nearest thing to a keeper is the jammed boulder, shifting a hair in its seat, and the nearest thing to a heart is the pressure behind the north wall. The plants are not passengers in this. They are the machinery and the product at once. The moss holds water; the creepers hold stone; the stalks tune the noise; the bladders register the pulse. It is a factory with no worker in it, making pale green in the dark and keeping its din packed under the fallen wedge.

The grove is also young and temporary. It is not an ancient thing that has settled into a niche. The stone is still sharp; the floor is still chips, not sand. Small collapses happen constantly. A fin sheds, a channel silts and cuts a new one, a basin overfills and abandons the plant drinking from it, a shelf of moss is undercut and hangs over a hole that was solid last month. The vegetation surges into every new opening and is drowned out of every old one. The grove is probably younger than a person. It will be choked or washed out or buried within a few decades, and somewhere else in the same fracture another room will open and begin to knock.

That is what lives there: not a creature but a settlement of plants and water, a blind wet engine on a forty-second cycle, entirely uninterested in whether anyone finds the way in.
