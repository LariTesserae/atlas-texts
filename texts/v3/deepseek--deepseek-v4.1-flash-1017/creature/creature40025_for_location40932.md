What lives in the Verdant Pocket is not an animal, and not a people. It is a tenant grown into the architecture: a single distributed fungal organism—or a coalition so thoroughly anastomosed that “single” and “many” have stopped meaning anything down here. Call it the Slow Gardener if you need a name. It does not answer to it.

Its body is the pale moss underfoot, the fleshy carpet that squelches and holds water like a sponge. Its organs are the columnar stalks: some are spore towers, some are chemical refineries, some are light organs burning sulfur-yellow and arterial red and the electric blue of a dying star for reasons that have nothing to do with being seen. Nothing here needs to see. The glow is internal weather, a way of timing, a way of warming a cap by a fraction of a degree, a way of speaking to the rest of itself in light. Its roots—if they can be called roots—thread the porous composite of the walls, drinking the cold condensation and the weak geothermal heat siphoned from the planet’s crust. The constant hum in the stone is its pulse, felt more than heard. The spores drifting like slow, invisible snow are its breath and its seed and its thought, all the same thing.

It perceives the world as gradients. Heat, moisture, pressure, the faint chemical signature of metal and stone. It knows the elevator long before the elevator knows it: a new warmth descending, a vibration in the cable, a taste of iron and oil and living flesh. It has no eyes to watch with, but the whole cavern is a retina of sorts, a billion pores reading the disturbance. It does not think in words. It thinks in wetness and cold, in the slow creep of hyphae toward a useful anomaly, in the memory of a tool laid down and never picked up.

It remembers the builders the way a tree remembers a fence wire: by growing around it. The artifacts are not sacred to it, but they are not nothing. A wrench beside a moss-carpeted wall has a mycelial outline around it, a slight avoidance, a slight tending. The faint glowing script on the stone is traced and retraced by tendrils too fine to see, not to read it but to keep it from being swallowed. The half-swallowed metal dome is the one place the growth holds back. It has been holding back for a very long time.

And in that dome, behind the curtain of fibrous roots that cannot belong to the fungi, there is the closest thing here to a person.

It is a bud of the Gardener, grown thick and separate enough to move—but only just. A soft, pale, root-limbed thing, roughly the size of a dog, with no eyes and no mouth and a face of folded, wet membranes. It lives in the dome’s half-dark. It moves perhaps three times in a century. When it moves, it is indistinguishable from the slow creep of the fungus until it is already somewhere else. It tends the artifacts. It wipes mineral crust from a polished probe with a tendril. It turns a vessel upright. It traces the glowing script with a fingertip of hypha, not reading, only touching. It is not the builder. It is what the builder’s absence grew into: a hand that walks, an organ of the larger thing, a memory made meat and spore.

It is not lonely. Loneliness requires a category of other, and there is no other here. The Gardener is the floor and the stalks and the spores and the slow hand in the dome. It is the closed loop: the fallen stalks decaying into mulch, the mulch feeding the moss, the moss feeding the stalks, the heat feeding all of it, the cold preserving it. It is balanced. It is patient. It has perfected the art of the slow, silent, wet, and cold.

To walk into the Verdant Pocket is to become a fever in its side. Your heat is a small sun. Your breath is a chemical shout. Your footsteps are earthquakes in the moss. The Gardener will notice you—not with alarm, not with hunger, but with the deep, unhurried attention of something that has all the time in the world. It will not chase you. It does not need to. After you leave, it will grow toward the place where you stood. It will make a niche of your disturbance. It will remember you the way it remembers the builders: by closing over the scar.
