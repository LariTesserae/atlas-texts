What lives there is a dense, warm, breathing knot in all that cold. Not one person—many, though they would not count themselves many. They are the city’s soft strata, the reason stone is stacked and water is kept, and they are as much a part of the place as the springs and the wind.

They are not large people. The plateau has compacted them: short, broad, deep-chested, with thick torsos and strong legs for stairs and slopes and long walks behind the herds. Their hands are wide and hard, knuckles cracked by cold, nails worn flat from stone and bone and sinew. Their faces are weathered into a permanent squint against the sharp light, and their skin carries the dry shine of people who live in wind and grease their hides against damp. Their hair is braided or bound back with bone pins, and in the cold they wear layers—hide over hide, fur inside hide, hoods pulled close, leggings wrapped and tied, boots stuffed with dry grass or wool. They smell of smoke, animal fat, wet stone, and the clean cold of the springs.

They live packed together, and they like it that way. A household is not a house so much as a cluster of rooms and passages and shared walls, with hide curtains for privacy and warmth coming from bodies and small fires and the simple fact of being close. Children learn the paths between the knots of the city before they learn to count: which stair leads to which cistern, which roof sheds rain into which channel, which crevice holds the birds that must not be disturbed, which hollow stays dry enough for sleeping. Old people sit in the lee of walls and remember the springs—when one ran faster, when one went thin, which herd path shifted after a hard winter. Memory here is a form of water-keeping.

They are patient in a way that is not softness. Nothing rewards hurry on the plateau. A wall is patched, not rebuilt. A tool is mended, not replaced. A roof is propped, re-lashed, weighted with another slab. The people are accumulations too—of marriages and adoptions and debts of help, of skills passed sideways and downward, of quarrels that are lived around rather than settled. They do not tear down what can be built over. Their houses read like their lives: strata of care, each layer answering the one beneath it.

Water is the center of their work. They are spring-keepers before they are anything else. They clear the channels, line the cisterns with clay, watch the seepages, know which pools thaw first and which must be kept from fouling. They speak of water in the same low voice they use for the herds, and they do not waste it. The small pale fish in the cisterns are left alone—not sacred, exactly, but understood: they live in the water, and the water is not only for people.

The herds shape their year. The heavy shaggy animals move across the plateau in long slow lines, and the people move with them or wait for them. Herders go out into the pale distance with hide shelters and bone hooks and slings, and those who stay tend the springs and the stone and the dense small city. The herds give hide and bone and meat and horn and the long leg-bones that become lintels and pegs and door-frames. The people do not own the herds so much as attend them, and they know the predators that follow—lean, quiet things that come in the night and are respected, not hated. Doors are pegged. Night is watched. The city is not a fortress against the plateau; it is a careful arrangement within it.

And they are not alone in the city. Birds nest in every crevice, under eaves and in the fissures of walls, and their droppings streak the stone white and grey. Small furred things live under floors and in cracks. Beetles gather in damp hollows. Spiders hang in corners. The people tolerate this the way they tolerate weather—with repair and adjustment and occasional violence, but mostly with the understanding that density means company. Children know the birds. The birds know the children. The city is a shared den.

Their speech is low and spare, because the wind is always there and because there is no need to fill silence. They name the winds, the springs, the herd paths, the frost lines. They measure time by the movement of animals and the running of water and the slow deposit of stone upon stone. They are not mystics. They know water runs downhill and cold takes heat and living things need shelter and one another. Their strangeness is only the combination: warm in the cold, dense in the vastness, wet in the high dry country, old in the young sharp rock.

They are the keepers of the knot. They hold the cisterns and the walls and the paths, and they hold each other, and the Cold Anvil holds them—pale and sharp and quiet, going on.
