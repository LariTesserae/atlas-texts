What lives in the Chord is not a population so much as a closed company of bodies, each doing one thing forever.

**The first inhabitant is the floor.** The basket itself is alive — a continuous root-weave, pale as peeled willow, warm and waterlogged, one sprawling tissue that is at once ground, filter, stomach, and graveyard. It has no soil beneath it and no past in it. It tightens where weight falls, loosens by millimetres a day, lays herringbone in one zone and coils in another without a pattern-maker. It takes back every loose strand, every dead grazer, every drifting jelly before silt can settle. Its life is slow and total: it is the place’s recycler and its only true constant.

**The columns are the tall residents.** They are not trees but grown things — jointed, silica-glazed, hollow, pale green-white. Each is a body that is also a building and an instrument. It drinks through its skin, lifts water up its wall, spills it down its own throat from joint to joint, and breathes out and in at its vents. Each chamber rings at its own note because the water strikes its floor-pool at a steady rate; each column is therefore always singing, always breathing, always weeping on the inside. The rims of the openings are thickened and lipped like healed wounds. Nothing was cut. These are not wild things; they are grown, maintained, and they do not stop.

**The red beard is the soft tenant.** Rust-coloured fur grows only in the lee of the vents, where the warm breath pulses out and in. It is not structural. It is the only furnishing in the world, and it lives on the breath of the columns.

**The grazers are the only visible animals.** Finger-long, segmented, blind, pale as the mat. They live in the wet grooves of the walls and eat the red beard. They are rare enough that you might cross the whole basin and see three. They have no eyes because there is nothing to see at their scale; they move by touch through warm wet channels, cropping fur. They are the basin’s only mouths.

**The jelly colonies** drift in the shallow weave-water, more plant than animal. They do not count, but they are there.

Nothing else. No people. No makers. Nothing flies, no seeds drift, no insects hang in the warm air. The openings are sized — rounded, lipped, framed — but no resident is the size of the doors. The place is inhabited by its own circulation, by water going round, by sound with no listener, by growth exactly matching decay. If you ask who lives there, the answer is not a who. It is a self-maintaining ensemble: one immense living weave, its hollow singing columns, a red fur in the vent-lee, a few blind grazers, drifting jelly. They arrive from nowhere and leave for nowhere. Their dead become floor. Their floor becomes them. The landscape has no age in it, and neither do they.
