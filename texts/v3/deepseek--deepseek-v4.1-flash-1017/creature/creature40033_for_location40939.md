What lives in the Sough lives in scatter. There is no crowd in the foreground, only concentrations. Water is the first concentration, warmth the second, and Aumber the third—though Aumber is less a concentration than a thread of them, a constellation of wellheads and cisterns and raised stone walks.

The grazers are the most visible life. They are small and humped, their dark coats beaded with condensation so that they look wet even when it has not rained. They move in loose lines rather than herds, following seep-lines and thermal pockets, browsing wiregrass and pale moss and dwarf willow no taller than a boot. Their lowing is soft and short-range, a sound meant for neighbors, not for distance. They are not wild in the dramatic sense. They are adapted, ordinary, and watchful. When the line tightens, it is because the follower is somewhere behind it.

The follower is lean, low, and patient. It is rarely seen. It is a pressure in the grass, a track in the grey grit, a shape that uses the long parallel ribs of the plateau to break its outline. It follows the grazers at the distance where they can smell it but not quite see it. It waits at seeps. It does not chase often, because the Sough’s abundance is scattered and a long chase is a waste of heat. It lives by patience, which is another way of saying it lives by the same rules as everything else here.

Smaller lives are local and numerous. Rillflies rise in columns over the shallow pans. Scarp swifts nest on fresh ledges and cut the air with thin blade-sounds. Water shrews work the seep-lines in quick, nervous stitches. And there are the repair swarms—synthetic mites moving over the capillary channels of the city’s stone, silent and busy. They are not pets or pests. They are plumbing that has learned to skitter, and the people treat them as weather.

The people of Aumber are the plateau’s maintainers. They are not many. They live in pale faceted modules grown from local stone, surfaces channeled with capillaries, roofs pitched to harvest condensation. A home is not a fortress. It is a seed that may be decoupled if the uplift cracks it and re-seeded a few meters away. They do not dig foundations; they seed them. They build joints into the rock and expect the ground to move. They are the kind of people who hear an anchor adjust in the night and go back to sleep.

Their days are water days. Check the seep. Read the meter. Clear the capillary. Watch the overflow weir. A cistern is a commons; a wellhead is where neighbors meet. They wear layers and hoods, and their breath fogs in the cold wet air. Their hands know the bite of wet stone. They walk raised stone walks over shallow canals, and much of their city is emptiness threaded with infrastructure. They can walk a long time between presences and still be inside Aumber. Solitude is not abandonment here; it is the normal spacing.

Water is both public utility and private hazard. They do not waste it. They draw it from cold air with weather spines and hold heat in phase-change lattices and keep the paths clear of ice. They do not fight the uplift; they build joints into it. Nothing here is old enough to restore, so things are either growing or not yet grown. A cracked module is not mourned. It is decoupled, re-seeded, absorbed into newer growth. Their culture is a culture of joints.

Socially, they are a constellation. They gather at cisterns and wellheads, maintain guide-rails and weather masts, send repair swarms, and know one another by neighborhood, seep, and mast. They are practical, quiet, process-minded. They notice condensation forming, rain crossing, herds shifting, stone being laid, anchors adjusting. Their technology behaves like plumbing, like weather, like animal husbandry. None of it is flashy. It is simply the shape their attention takes.

Even Aumber lives, in its way. Modules accrete. Capillaries sweat. Repair swarms move. Anchors adjust. The city is a shape made of people and plumbing and stone that is not yet done, and the people are its caretakers and its fauna at once—clustered around water and warmth, sparse, adaptive, quietly busy everywhere you are not looking.
