## The Sarnish

They are not many. You could put them all in the meeting house and still have room by the door, and they would not fill it noisily. Perhaps eighty, perhaps fewer; the number does not matter to them, and they do not count it. They live scattered across the slope in the same generous way the houses do, so that on most streets you will meet more water than people: a gutter running, a trough brimming, a cistern cover beaded with condensation, and then, eventually, a figure coming down through the fog with a bucket or a bundle of sedge or nothing at all.

You know them by their walk first. They go up and down the stairs with a short, patient stride, weight kept over the middle of the foot, hands free or one hand trailing the wall. They do not hurry on the cambered streets. They have learned the exact degree of slant that wet slate will forgive. Their boots are soft and much-mended, and they make almost no sound on stone, which is why the sound of them carries so far: a dry, deliberate tap, tap, tap coming out of the white, arriving long before the person does. When they pass you they nod. That is the whole of it, usually. Not unfriendliness. A nod is a complete sentence in Sarn.

Their bodies are made by the place. Broad hands, cold-red at the knuckles, nails kept short. Faces that are not weathered in the sun-and-wind way of lowland farmers but pale and slightly damp-looking, the skin smooth where it is not lined by looking down at work. Shoulders a little forward. Eyes that flick to the ground, to the gutter, to the state of your boot-leather, before they go to your face. They are strong in the way of people who carry small loads often rather than large loads once. None of them is in a rush. None of them is idle, either. The two things look similar from outside.

They dress in wool and oiled cloth, undyed or dyed to the colour of wet stone, lichen, peat. Layers, because the fog is cold in a way that does not announce itself. Hoods. Fingerless gloves. A piece of iron pinned at a cloak where the cloth has begun to go. Nothing is ornamental except by accident — a row of horn buttons cut from the same antler, a patch of blue thread in a grey sleeve because blue was what was to hand. They smell of wet wool, woodsmoke, goat, and the clean mineral smell of stone that has been rained on and is now drying.

They are generalists. The same person will mend a slate, draw water, cut sedge, settle a goat, and sit on a committee about the north gate in the same week, and think none of it remarkable. There is no trade they call sacred and no work they consider beneath them. They know materials intimately: which slate rings true, which timber has weathered long enough to be used, which lime mortar is still biting, which hinge wants a pin and which wants to be left alone until spring. They carry few tools and borrow them constantly, and every tool goes back on its hook. This is not a virtue they admire in themselves. It is simply what is done. A borrowed awl returned to the wrong hook would be a kind of scandal, and it would be spoken of for years, quietly, in the way of a thing that has been settled but not forgotten.

Their speech is low and spaced. They use fewer words than lowlanders and leave longer gaps between them. A conversation at a trough may take ten minutes and contain four sentences. They have precise words for kinds of wetness, for the stages of fog, for the sound a cistern makes when it is nearly full. They are not poetic about these things. They are practical. A good morning is a morning in which nothing needs a second look. A bad morning is one in which the bell note comes back wrong off the cloud. They will tell you this and then drink their tea and look at the wall.

They are not solemn. This is important. They have a dry, delayed humour that arrives after you have stopped expecting it. A man will mend a hinge all afternoon, say nothing, and then observe that the hinge has outlasted two of his uncles and may be waiting for a third. The table will laugh — a short, breathy laugh, quickly over — and then someone will pass the bread. They laugh at weather, at goats, at visitors who ask where the view is. They do not laugh at breakage. Breakage is not funny; breakage is work.

Children are few and are not the centre of the world, though they are watched. They are given the end of a job before they are given the job: hold this, watch where the water goes, carry the short nails. They learn by proximity and by imitation, and by being told, gently and repeatedly, to put it back as they found it. They play quiet games in the gutters, racing sticks through the drains, listening to the cistern covers for the different hollow notes. They know the goats by name and the goats know them by pocket. By the time they are tall enough to reach the bell rope they have already absorbed the town’s chief law, which is not written and not often spoken: do not improve what is already at rest.

Old people are not set aside. They sit where the work is and do the parts that require less standing: sorting nails, splicing rope, watching the fog, telling the children which stone in the wall is the one that moved last winter and must not be moved again. When someone dies, the house is not emptied. The roof is maintained. The door is closed. After a while someone moves in, and the dead person’s tools are on the same hooks, and their repairs are still in the walls, and that is the memorial. They do not make much of grief. They make much of maintenance. The two are not unrelated.

They are not poor, though they have little. The granary is full enough. The goats give milk. The sedge is cut and stacked. They are unacquisitive rather than deprived. A new thing is a disturbance; a well-mended old thing is a satisfaction. They will spend an afternoon on a hinge because a hinge is worth an afternoon, and they will not spend an afternoon on a decoration because a decoration is not. They have no word for scenery and do not look up when something passes overhead in the cloud. It is not that kind of place, and they are not that kind of people. They are interested in what holds.

They keep animals, and the animals keep them. The goats are the only inhabitants who are openly curious, and they are tolerated, indulged, and occasionally sworn at. The dog belongs to everyone and no one and sleeps where the warmest stone is. The chough on the bell cot is a visitor and is treated as one: noticed, not pursued. There are things in the fog that are never seen, and the Sarnish do not speculate about them. Speculation is a lowland habit. Here, if it does not need mending, it does not need naming.

If you stay, they will feed you and not ask why you came. They will give you water from the trough and a place by the hearth and then they will go back to what they were doing. If you stay longer, they will find you a job — something small, something with an end you can see — and they will watch how you put the tool back. That is the test, and it is the only test. Pass it and you are, in a quiet way, one of them. Fail it and they will be polite to you forever.

What lives in Sarn, then, is a people who have made a compact with a wet mountain: we will not hurry you, and you will not surprise us. They are the town’s maintenance made walking. They are not happy in the way visitors mean happy, and they are not sad in the way visitors mean sad. They are at the point of the curve where it flattens — hands busy, voices low, water going around, the bell making its flat note into the cloud and coming back changed. They live there the way the moss lives in the joints of the paving: not because it is the best place in the world, but because it is the place that holds the water long enough.
