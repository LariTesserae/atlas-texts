What lives there is not one thing. It never was.

Start with the things that were made. The station crews are the easiest to name, so begin there and let that ease you in.

Take Wen. She is tall and narrow, the way people get when they spend years walking on soft ground and have to keep their weight moving or sink to the ankle. Her boots are cut low and laced tight to the shin. She has a lantern that straps to her forehead and a second one at her belt, because there is no light that is not carried or made, and you learn to carry more than you think you need. She has been at the Harrow pump station eleven years, which is longer than most. She keeps a log in pencil because the damp kills ink pens, and she writes in a tight small hand, and she has rewritten the same list of maintenance tasks so many times that she no longer reads them, only moves her pencil down the page to remember what order the day goes in. She knows the sound of the Number Two pump the way you know a relative's cough. When the pitch shifts by a quarter tone she is already reaching for the spanner.

Wen is not lonely, though she is alone for weeks at a time. The marsh is too loud for loneliness. Loneliness is a quiet thing and Kettlemoor has no silence to put it in.

The crews at the unattended stations come and go. They are small teams that arrive by boat or by the pipe roads, stay to fix what has failed, and leave. They sleep in bunks that smell of mildew and old fuel, they eat food that comes in tins, and they hang their wet clothes on lines strung between the boiler pipes to dry, which they never quite do. They learn the local rules faster than they expect to: keep your lantern on your person at all times, never walk a channel you cannot see the bottom of, trust the herons, because the herons know where the ground is firm. They do not stay long. Some of them come back. A few, like Wen, stop leaving.

Then there are the others. The ones who were here before the engines, or who arrived alongside them and were never on any crew list. The marsh makes its own people.

You will find the Vaulters in the drowned hollows, where the water table sits close enough to the surface that every step presses water up around the boot. They are thin, shorter than you'd expect, and they wear layers of fungus-cloth that they grow themselves on frames of reed and wire. The cloth is pale and soft and it holds warmth. Their faces are broad and their eyes are wide and their pupils are enormous, because there is no day here, and an eye born into darkness learns to take every photon it is given. They have no word for "sun." They have a word that means the warmth from below, and a word that means the blue glow of the fungus beds, and a word that means the red of the vent mouths, and when they talk about light they use whichever one applies. They keep the old knowledge of the place—which sinkholes are deep, which springs run hot enough to scald, which reed choirs mean rain is coming. They trade with the station crews, fungus-cloth and lamp-eel oil for brass fittings and glass and wire. The trade is quiet and irregular and both sides pretend it is more casual than it is.

The Vaulters are not the only ones. There are the reed-walkers, who follow the channels and never sleep in one place two nights running, and who sing to the frogs in a register the frogs answer to. There are the sump-keepers, who live near the automated stations and make themselves useful without ever being hired, and who consider a boiler tower their property in a way the company has never successfully disputed. There are people who came from somewhere else and went into the marsh and did not come out, and who are not dead, and who are not quite the same as they were. Kettlemoor is a place that changes what it takes in. The water is warm and mineral and it gets into everything.

And then there are the things that are not people at all, but that the Vaulters will tell you are somebody.

The marsh hogs are smart. Not clever the way a dog is clever, but smart the way a person is smart, and they know the industrial schedule. They know when the outfall at the Harrow station will run warm and they gather there, and they know when the crews will be out with the long poles, and they go elsewhere. They root through the peat and they find things—brass fittings dropped a hundred years ago, glass bottles, the bones of whoever was here before—and they leave them in piles at the edges of the reed beds, and the Vaulters collect them. No one is sure whether the hogs understand the trade or whether they are simply tidying up. Wen thinks they are tidying up. She has seen a hog move a fallen spanner out of a channel and set it on a rock where a crew member would see it.

The blind herons stalk the shallows on legs like thin iron pins. They have no eyes and they do not need them. They feel the water with their feet and they taste it with their beaks and they hear the small sounds of everything that moves beneath the surface. They are patient in a way that feels like judgment. The crews learn to read them: a heron standing still and facing one direction means the ground is firm that way. A heron moving means something is moving that you should know about. The Vaulters say the herons are the oldest residents and that they were here before the rock cooled, which cannot be true, but the Vaulters say it with a straight face and you learn not to argue.

The lamp eels coil in the spring pools, where the vent water is hottest. They are long and dark and they glow faintly, a low blue-green that pulses slow and even, and they coil in tangles that look like something written. The Vaulters harvest a few each season, for the oil, and they do it with a respect that borders on apology. The eels do not seem to mind. The eels do not seem to mind anything. They have been here a long time and they are not in a hurry.

The steam bats stitch the air above the fens in patterns that the Vaulters read the way a sailor reads a sky. The bats follow the warm air, and the warm air rises from the vents, and the vents are where the ground is thin, and the ground is thin where the water moves, and so the bats' flight is a map of what you cannot see. The crews have learned to glance up. The crews have also learned not to rely on the bats too much, because the bats have their own business and sometimes they are just flying.

And beneath all of it, under the moss and the peat and the black water, there are the burrowing crayfish, which are small and plentiful and which tick against the roots of the fern trees in a sound like rain on a tin roof. They are the bottom of the food chain and they do not care. They are the thing that everything else eats and they have been eating and being eaten for as long as the marsh has been wet.

The reed choirs are what you hear first and last. Frogs and insects and things that are neither, all of them making a sound that is continuous and metallic and loud, a chorus that does not stop when you stop listening. The sound is not song the way a bird's song is a song. It is more like a machine running. It has a rhythm and it keeps time with the engines, and the Vaulters will tell you that the chorus changed when the first pump station was built, that it learned the beat, that it has been keeping it ever since.

The engines themselves are alive in a way that the crews do not talk about directly. A pump that has run for thirty years without stopping has a personality. It has good days and bad days. It develops a rattle that means nothing and a hum that means something, and the crew member who has been with it long enough knows the difference in their bones. Wen talks to Number Two. Not out loud, not usually. But she thinks at it when she walks past, a kind of wordless acknowledgment, the way you nod to a neighbor. The engines are another kind of animal, and they are part of the marsh now, and the marsh has made room for them.

Kettlemoor is loud and dense and old and active, and it is in its own state, and what lives there knows this. Wen knows it. The Vaulters know it. The hogs know it, in whatever way a hog knows things. The herons have always known it. The marsh takes what it is given and it grows over it and it makes it part of itself, and the things that live in it do the same. That is the whole of it. That is what lives in Kettlemoor.
