The people here are thin and warm-skinned, and they are quiet in a way that is not shyness. Stand next to one in a hall and you will hear them breathing before you hear them speak, and the breathing is slow, matched to something. They have the look of people who sleep well and do not dream much. Their hands are the hands of the careful — nails kept short, palms callused in the specific places palms callus when they have spent a life resting on warm stone — and their faces are pale in the way of things kept out of light, though the warmth has got into them somewhere around the eyes and the cheekbones and left a flush that never quite fades. They wear their cloth loose and layered, pale grey and off-white, and the dust settles in the folds and they do not brush it out, because brushing is a thing you do when you expect to be clean afterward, and they have no such expectation and no complaint about it. The dust is part of the temperature. It holds the warmth a little, and it smells of the ground.

They do not hurry, and this is not indolence. It is a kind of competence. There is nothing on the plateau that will be worse for being seen to in an hour, and there is a great deal that will be worse for being seen to wrongly, so they walk at the pace of their own hearing — a step, a pause, a step — and they keep their attention out ahead of them the way other people keep a lamp. In the halls, where the machinery is, they stand for long stretches with one hand flat against a casing, not to check it, just to be in contact with it, and you can learn a lot from the way they stand. If the hand is relaxed they will stay for the better part of an hour. If the hand curls, even slightly, they will be gone before you have finished noticing.

Their speech is worth describing because they have made a discipline of it. They do not shout, and they do not call out, and they do not use names much in the open air. A conversation between two of them on the flats has a strange quality — the words come out flat and close and loud, as if spoken into a wall a pace away, and so the whole conversation has the volume of a murmur and the intimacy of a shout, and both of them will feel it and both will begin to trim. They have a large vocabulary for sounds: for the knock of the great patient thing, which they call by a word that means roughly *the one who counts*; for the hum, which has a name that also means *the held breath*; for the particular ring of metal on metal that means a shaft has found its seat and is happy. They have almost no vocabulary for weather, or distance, or hurry. A thing that is far is *far*, and a thing that happened long ago is *before the settling*, and anything that is becoming something else has no word at all, because they have not needed one.

Time, for them, is counted in attentions. A shift is not a length of hours; it is the span during which one of them holds a hall. They sleep when the halls they hold are quiet, which is often, and they wake without being called, because they can hear the difference between the Works asleep and the Works in some small trouble even through the walls of an outbuilding a kilometer off, and the difference will bring them up out of sleep and out into the dark with their cloth half on and their faces already composed. Then they will walk the long empty road, unhurried even now — unhurried because a man who runs on warm stone in the dark will fall into a seam and be no use to anyone — and they will find the trouble, which is almost never much, a bearing a little dry, a belt a little slack, and they will speak to it in a low voice as they fix it, not words of comfort, just commentary, the way you talk to a thing you have known all your life, and then they will stand with a hand on the casing until the sound comes right, and then they will go back.

They are not many, and they do not make many. A child is a rare and serious event, and the child is raised by the hall rather than by anyone in it — carried on a hip from engine to engine, learning the sounds before the words for them, learning first that the hum is safe and the knock is safe and the ring is good and the absence of any of them is the only wrong thing there is. By the time such a person is grown they can tell you, with their eyes closed, which of forty halls is a little off, and they will be right. They will also be unable, entirely, to say what any of it is *for*, and will find the question strange, in the way that you would find it strange to be asked what your own heart is for.

They die on the plateau. There is no other place, and they do not think of the plateau as a place one leaves. When one of them is failing, the others carry them out to a hollow in the stone where the warmth is strongest and lay them there, and they lie for a day or two with their hand on the ground, listening, and then they stop listening. The stone takes them slowly. Nothing is buried deep, because nothing needs to be. The dust falls, the warmth rises, and in a season there is a shape in the dust that the next wind would erase if there were a wind. Twice a season, perhaps, one of the walkers will cross that particular hollow and pause in it, and the people have noticed this and do not speak of it.

The walkers themselves — I should say what can be said. They are tall, taller than a hall door, jointed like something designed to fold, and their surfaces are the color and almost the texture of the plateau's stone, so that at a distance a walker standing still is a walker you have not seen. They move at the pace of a shadow lengthening, which is to say that if you watch one fixedly you will begin to doubt it is moving at all, and then it will be somewhere else. Their legs find the seams. They do not walk across the flats so much as along them, following the warmth the way the people follow sound, and it is possible — the people do not say this, but it is possible — that to a walker the plateau is not dark, that the seams are a writing it reads with its feet, and that the Works, with their circulating heat, are the loudest and most legible thing on the whole surface of the world. It passes. It does not turn its head, if it has a head. It goes on toward the haze, and is a stone, and is a stone, and is gone.

And the small blind things in the lagging. They are soft and pale and slow and they live in the warm dark of the pipe-wrapping in numbers no one has counted, and they are the only creatures on the plateau that are plentiful, and they are the only ones that breed with any enthusiasm. Put your hand on a conduit and something under the cloth will shift away from the pressure, and if you are patient and keep your hand there, it will come back, and press itself against the warmth of your palm, and stay. That is the whole of it. That is the whole of them.

But the true inhabitants — the ones who were here first and will be here last and who outnumber the people by every measure that matters — are the Works. They are not persons, and it would be a mistake to call them alive, but they are not the sort of thing that a person can stand beside for an age and go on thinking of as an object. The furnace that has burned so long no one remembers its lighting has a temperament, which is to say that on some days it draws a little hard and on others it settles, and the people know this and adjust their walking to it. The shafts turn because turning is what they do; the conduits loop and return because a loop is a shape that can be maintained forever, and forever is the timescale the Works have chosen. They are in repair. That is the sentence that says everything. Something maintains them, and the something is the pattern itself, the balance reached and held — the fuel and the air exactly matched, the whole vast scattered thing breathing in and breathing out at the size of a city, with a handful of quiet warm people walking its empty roads to listen, and to notice, and to say a few words when a bearing runs a little dry, and to go back to watching.

That is who lives here. Not a settlement, exactly. More like a held position — the plateau, the Works, the few careful dusty people, the small soft things in the pipes, the tall ones passing — all of it in its final form, all of it warm, all of it knowing, in whatever way a stone can know a thing, that there is nowhere else for anything to go.
