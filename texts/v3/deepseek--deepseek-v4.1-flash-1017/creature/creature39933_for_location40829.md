**The shape.** They are not a tall people, and they would not be tall anywhere. You can tell a Kettle-born from the set of the shoulders: high, rounded, carried forward, with the head pushed out ahead of the spine like someone leaning into weather that never stops. The Kettle does not let you stand up, so they don't. The children learn the bend before they learn to run — you can see a two-year-old going down a passage at a crouch, one hand trailing the daub, not looking. They are worn to the shape of the place and the place is worn to the shape of them, and after a hundred and fifty years of mutual wearing it is no longer possible to say which of them came first. The passages are polished and slightly too narrow, and the people who walk them are slightly too narrow too.

**The body.** Their skin is pale and soft-looking from the constant damp, but under it the tendons are wire. The old ones grow a fine green bloom on the forearms and the backs of the knees, a moss that they scrape off with a shell and do not think about. Their hands are broad, the nails thick and ridged like the bricks, the pads of the fingers polished smooth by wet clay; the palms are always a little damp, and they leave damp prints on the daub. Their teeth are flat and notched at the front, worn from holding gut-thread and reed-cord and bone pins while both hands are busy. A Kettle smile is oddly worn-down.

Their eyes have grown to the green-gold light. The pupils are wide; the whites have a wet shine. Out in the marsh they squint and their eyes water and they shade them with a flat hand, complaining. They do not like the sky. And they are all a little deaf — the din and the damp together — so they shout. Their default volume is too loud for anywhere else. They read lips a little. They touch each other constantly, a hand on a shoulder to say *here, behind you*, because in the Kettle you locate a person as much by touch and footfall as by voice. The oldest sit close to the drip-pots and feel the time through the floor, in the bones of their feet.

**The voice.** A Kettle person giving directions sounds like they are describing a body: *third vault, north side, the low shelf, mind the drip, under the second lamp, the niche with the cracked lip*. Directions are the Kettle's grammar; a person from outside gets lost in the words before they get lost in the passages. And the talk is constant, overlapping, argumentative, affectionate — a dense wet din that rises and falls with the hour. When it goes quiet, everyone stops. Silence in the Kettle means something has gone wrong.

**The day.** The Kettle wakes before the light-shafts do. The first sounds are the pigs complaining and someone clearing the ash from a lamp. The water-carriers go first, in a rotation that everyone has an opinion about, up the crooked brick with gourds on a yoke of reed; hand over hand along the shelves, pouring the tea-colored water over the thin pale rows. The reed-cutters and the fish-catchers pole out into the marsh at dawn and come back at mid-morning with bundles and eels. The potters are in the low chamber with the clay. Someone is always up a ladder with a bone pin, sistering a sagging rib. Someone is always scraping the algae off the bladder-skin caps of the shafts. The children run the vaults in a pack, and are sent to fetch and carry, and are taught the drip-song by being sat down beside a pot and told to keep time.

**The pots.** Every child, at the age they can hold a beat, is given a pot of their own — one of the tuned pots under the worst of the drips — and told to keep the time with it. It is the Kettle's first school and its only lullaby. The child's pot becomes the child's note: people will say, later, *she has a low note*, or *he lost his drip at four and has never quite found it again*. The potter, Fen, does not make the notes; the drips make the notes. The potter only shapes the hollows that the notes fall into, chipping and scraping the inside with a bone until the drip rings true, and this is understood to be the highest craft in the Kettle — to make an emptiness that the world will fill with time.

When a person dies, their pot is re-tuned lower and set in a niche above where they are laid, so that the drips still find it. The dead keep a slower time than the living. The wall is a drum, and it is always, faintly, being played.

**The dead.** They are in the walls, wrapped in reed and sealed with daub, and each generation repaints the seal with a handprint — the hand of whoever loved them best, pressed into the wet clay, so that the last touch they are given is a hand. There are so many handprints now that the lower walls are a sediment of hands, small ones low down and large ones above, and the Kettle-born trail their fingers along the daub as they walk, without looking, the way other people read a wall with their eyes. The children grow up touching their grandmothers. Nobody is ever quite alone in the Kettle. The word for the awful feeling of being outside with no daub at your shoulder is *wallless*, and it is closer to a sickness than to a mood.

**The argument.** The terraces are the great argument and have been for two hundred years. The deep-enders hold that the pool wants deepening — more water, more eels, more frogs, the low shelves fattened — and they mostly live low, near the north wall, and they are loud and numerous. The high-enders hold that the shelves want raising — more light, more room, the vine trained wider — and they mostly live high, near the shafts, and they are fewer and more stubborn. Every meal is a session of this argument. Children are born into a side before they can speak. Nobody wins, nobody is meant to win; the argument *is* the Kettle's weather, and it is how these people say *I am still here, and so are you*. Underneath it is the real question, which is whether the Kettle should feed itself from the inside or the outside, and underneath that is the thing nobody says, which is that the marsh is silting and the Kettle is rising to keep its mouth above the water, and one day the rising will not be enough.

**The wide.** Outside is the marsh, and the marsh is a larder and a rumor. The reed-cutters know its channels the way the water-carriers know the shelves. They go out in flat boats and come back before the light gets high. A child's first trip out is a small ordeal: the sky is too big and too bright, the eyes stream, the sound of the wind in the reeds is wrong — too wide, unenclosed — and the child is glad, coming back down the algae-slick slope between the reed-bundle walls, to be folded again into the warm wet din. Outsiders come sometimes, poling a raft with metal and salt; there is a feast, and the nail is brought out to be admired, and everyone talks at once.

**The nail.** Iron is small and precious and comes from elsewhere. There is one nail. It is kept in a gourd at the belt of Marrow, who is the oldest, and it is used for everything — prizing, prying, punching, scribing, wedging, once a year as the nail of a small ceremony no one can quite explain — and it is always returned. It has a name, which is the same as the word for *nail*, because there has only ever been one. Children are told stories about the nail. Men and women swear by it. When Marrow dies, someone else will keep the gourd, and the Kettle will go on borrowing the nail, and the nail will outlast them all.

**What they are.** The Kettle-born are not one kind of person; they are forty ways of being the same shape. They are fish-catchers and reed-cutters and pig-keepers and builders, potters and water-carriers and keepers of the nail, and they build with their hands and their teeth and their knees, and they have been doing it here so long that building has become a form of inheritance. A wall is not finished; it is kept. They know the Kettle is growing, the way they know the terraces are barely enough: it sits in the noise, gets argued with, is forgotten, is re-argued. They do not expect to solve it. They expect to keep it.

And so the frogs sing in the pool and the drip-pots, brainless and persistent; the birds come and go through the yellow-green shafts and are named and argued over; the pigs complain; the beetles rustle in every wall like rain on thatch; the vine hangs down into the light in threads of green; the drips fall into the tuned pots, wandering, keeping their loose time. The Kettle-born lie down in the dark among all of it and cannot sleep in silence. Take one out into the marsh at night and they will lie awake, listening for a rustle that is not there, waiting for the drips to tell them what time it is — kept, and keeping, in the warm wet mouth of the place.
