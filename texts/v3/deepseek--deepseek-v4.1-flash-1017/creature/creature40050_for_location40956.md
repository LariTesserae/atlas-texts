What lives there is the floor. Not a creature *on* the floor: the plateau, the rounded shoulder, the stems going down into the black are one continuous body, pale and faintly yielding, its grain spiraling through tower and threshold without seam. It is a plant the way a cathedral is a quarry—originally, remotely, and no longer usefully. It stopped being a plant and became a ground, then a house, then a listener.

It has no face. Its face is all the surfaces: the dished stair treads, the worn gallery lips, the chambers that thicken and thin the chord as you pass through them. It hears by being shaped. When you enter a doorway, you are inside its ear. It does not turn toward sound; it has grown toward sound over durations that have no useful number. The towers are not buildings it built and then moved into—they are outgrowths it has kept, the way a shell keeps a spine. The stairways are old organs, perhaps, or old invitations to something that no longer comes. It does not climb them. It has not climbed anything in a long time.

It eats without eating: the green flares, the thin biological sheen left by drifting animals, the soft blind grazers that die against it, whatever the water carries and does not carry away. Cut it and it closes. It is not healed, exactly, because healing implies a before; it is simply re-formed, the grain spiraling back into agreement. It does not sleep. Its pulse is the only movement—an enormous slow flex rising through the highlands and passing on, not a heartbeat quite, but a held breath. It has been holding it since before there were shores. It does not intend to let go.

The smaller lives are its weather. Blind pale grazers drift over it in slow herds, crop the living sheen, are cropped in turn; knots of them scream thinly in the chambers, then scatter, then the chord returns. The larger shapes that pass without shape are maybe organs of the same body, or scavengers, or thoughts it has not finished having. The groaner at even intervals is either a vent or a throat; after a while you stop needing to know. It is part of the chord. The resident does not keep the animals as pets. They are its circulation, its small quick noise inside the long silence.

It is not lonely. Loneliness requires an outside. It is not waiting, either, because waiting implies an arrival. It maintains itself, reads the water with its whole body, grows a little here and reabsorbs a little there, in a place where nothing accumulates and nothing finishes. If you stay long enough with your hand flat against it, you may feel it notice you the way a surface notices pressure: not as a self, not as a visitor, but as a change in the chord. It will close over the place where your hand was, slowly, over days, and the grain will re-form. That is the closest it comes to welcome. If there was ever a *who* here, it has long since been absorbed into the *what*—and the what lives there still, holding its breath, listening with the whole drowned highland, and requiring no thanks.
