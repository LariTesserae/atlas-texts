What lives in the Sump is not the Sump. It is a thin human fringe: perhaps forty or fifty people across the whole drowned bowl, never more than two or three at a station, and sometimes no one for miles of guide-line and roar. They are not settlers in any ordinary sense. They are maintenance—pump-watchers, valve-turners, line-walkers, precipitate-scrapers, the ones who keep the rails true and the mats from bridging a sensor. They come down the stair on rotation, and some of them stay for years.

They look like people who have been slowly edited by the place. Short hair, close clothes, no loose cuffs, nothing metal that clinks. Soft boots. Hands always a little raw from wet and mineral. Eyes wide and slow to blink. Their voices are quiet or rough, because shouting is pointless; they speak in the dry rooms and touch everywhere else. They have a working vocabulary of pressure: fingers on a wrist mean *stop*; a flat palm on the chest means *listen*; two taps on the shoulder mean *follow*; a squeeze means *I am here*. They are fluent in each other’s silences.

They live high in the shells of the stacks, in sealed rooms above the tide line, with cots, batteries, one lamp, a stove that burns what can be carried down. Their windows are shutterless and useless. They sleep to the shaft’s in-breath and wake to its exhale. Their tools are tactile—knotted cords, ridged pipes, grease pencils, chalk that washes away, brass tags read with a thumb. They navigate by line and memory. They know a stack by the wet trail on its north face, a pool by the number of terraces on its rim, a pump by the note it holds. They keep time by the tide and call the twice-daily rise “the turn.” They carry watches and distrust them. They have a calendar of valves and a folklore of sounds: a valve that sings flat is about to fail; a drip that stops is worse than one that starts; the shaft’s breathing is not an omen, it is just the shaft, and only the new ones need to be told.

They are not lonely in the ordinary way. They are lonely in a way that has a shape. When two meet between stacks, they stand close and breathe and do not speak for a moment. They trade dried food, batteries, a scrap of news from above, a dry pair of socks, the location of a safe hollow. They do not waste warmth. They do not personify the Sump. They will tell you plainly that it is not alive; it is a process with weather. But they are careful with it. They do not strike the glassy floor if they can help it. They do not follow a new wet trail without marking the old one. They give the black basins a wide berth. They know the difference between active and alive, and they live on the right side of it—the side that wants things: dry feet, a turned tide, a hand closing on an arm out of the grey.

They bury their dead badly, because there is nowhere to dig. A weighted body goes into a deep basin; a name goes into a ledger; a tool goes to whoever can use it. Most of them go back up the stair eventually. Some do not. The long-stayers get a look—wide eyes, easy hands, a way of standing still in the roar as if it were a room. They are not the Sump’s children. They are its staff. And they are the only things in all that tuned and breathing dark that want anything at all.
