The Sallow Table is inhabited lightly, in ribbons and intervals. Its residents are not gathered around water; they are gathered around light, dust, and the cold volatile sap that passes for water here. They live in a world where the chief currencies are angle of sunlight, mineral drift, and chemical scent, and where the safest way to endure is to be sparse, slow, and hard to surprise.

The most conspicuous residents are the frond towers. They are not trees, though they stand three or four meters high and can hide a crouching animal. Each is an arrangement of hollow jointed stalks topped by waxy, mirrorish leaves that turn through the day. In the cold hour before dawn, when the sap-mist runs in the shallow channels, the towers turn very slightly toward the brightest part of the sky. They are not listening for footsteps. They are listening for light. Their tissues hold the thin, sweet-medicinal solvent that condenses in the chill and trembles away at midday as a haze. Their leaves are cold and paper-dry to the touch, some with lens-like patches that concentrate the fierce white sun, some exuding suppressants that keep rivals at a distance. They mine the dust drifts with holdfasts rather than roots, and they defend space with scent instead of shade. Over decades, whole belts of them migrate, following mineral seams and the long angles of shadow.

Around their bases live the groundcover and scrub: wine-red mats, olive leathery fans, low jointed stalks that close and open in slow waves during the spore pulses. These are not passive carpets. They trade in airborne chemistry, they close over damaged ground, they open when the light is right, and they make the thin air faintly sharp with their exudates. Their leaves are cold and dry; their sap is volatile; their reproduction travels as pale drifting motes, not as seeds in wet earth. The vegetation belts are the table’s real settlement—dense within their limits, separated by kilometers of bare stone, provisional architecture grown rather than built.

The plated grazers move between those belts. They are low and wide, armored with overlapping mineral plates grown from dust, pale with rust streaks and slate shadows. They do not drink. They press their mouthparts to cold leaves and take solvent from the tissues, and they lick mineral dust from the drifts. They crop the frond towers and groundcover to neat bitten edges, then move on, leaving dry scat and tracks that are more common than their bodies. They travel in small parties, not herds, and they speak to one another mostly by scent and by the faint rattle of plates. At noon they stand in whatever shade they can find; in the cold they angle their plates to the sun. Their young are small and pale and follow the adults in single file through the belts. They are prey, and they know it, but predation here is sparse and unhurried—one event in a great deal of space.

The pale moth-like things cling to the lens-leaves. They are not quite moths, though their wings are dusted with reflective scales and they fold themselves flat against the waxy surfaces. They feed on thin films of sap and on dust, and they are most active in the cold hours around dawn. Their larvae live inside hollow stalks, feeding on pith and solvent, and when they emerge they carry spore dust from one belt to another. They do not hum. They do not gather in thick clouds. They are a quiet, scattered presence, flickering at the edge of vision, and they are one of the reasons the frond belts are not simply vegetable.

The lone predator is shaped like a wind-borne kite: a light body slung under membranous wings stretched over hollow struts, with a long tail-keel and sensory filaments that read chemical plumes. It is seen perhaps once a week, circling slowly over the empty stone, riding thermals and the long shadow-bands of cold fronts. It hunts by patience and by dropping—on a grazer separated from its party, on a cluster of pale clingers, on a carcass already opened by the weather. It rarely lands. Its passage is a thin shadow and no cry at all. It breeds on the scalloped cliffs beyond the horizon, laying a single hardened egg in a dust pocket, and then it is gone again into the wide bright silence.

There are other residents, but mostly as sign: tiny borings in stalks, clipped leaves, tracks in mineral drift, a slab of old cliff disturbed by something that burrows in the scree. No one builds. No one stays who needs water. The people who pass through are visitors and leave footprints that feel like events. The true inhabitants are the light-listeners and dust-miners and plate-backed croppers and pale clingers and the one kite-shaped hunter: a population spread across a continent of stone, held together by chemical etiquette, by timing, and by the enormous room the Sallow Table gives them.
