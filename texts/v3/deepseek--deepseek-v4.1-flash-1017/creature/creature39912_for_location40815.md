The people who live there are not tall. They are compact and broad, low to the ground, as if the plateau had shaped them for slipperiness rather than wind. Their skin is the color of the stone after rain—dark, with a faint wet sheen that never quite leaves it—and their feet are wide and splayed, their toes long and careful, their palms thickened into gripping pads. They keep their hair clipped close or bound up in fiber; loose hair would only drip into their eyes. They wear wrappings of beaten plant fiber, moss-felt, and rope, and they smell of wet basalt, bruised fern, and the sour smoke of small, grudging fires. There is no leather about them, no bone ornament, no horn or hide: there are no animals to give such things.

They are people, but the plateau has edited them. Their eyes are large and dark and slow to blink, made for diffuse light. Their voices are low and close, pitched to carry a few paces and no farther, because the warm wet air swallows sound. They do not shout. When the water is loud, they touch—fingertips to a wrist, a palm to a shoulder—to hold attention. They have a habit of pausing in the middle of speech to listen to the channels, and a stranger may think they have forgotten the conversation. They are checking the town.

They are keepers more than builders. They do build—stacking the black stone without mortar, raising low walls and flat-pitched roofs—but most of their labor is repair. Each household has a reach of channel it tends, a set of seepages and basins it knows by name. They clear the narrow cuts with wooden wedges and stone mauls; they reset fallen stones; they cut new channels when the water insists on a new path. They harvest the swollen roots of the broad-leaved shrubs, fern croziers, the pale flowers as a sharp condiment, and a dark moss they press into cakes and dry over stones. They cut shrub wood sparingly and dry it under overhanging slabs, and they twist rope from fibrous plants. Their tools are stone, wood, and rope. They are patient with them. A wall may take a season; a channel may take a generation.

They live in small kin groups, scattered through the sounder buildings. The half-ruined houses are not cleared away; they are allowed to become part of the drainage, their broken walls guiding water down toward the lower basins. Children are few and watched closely, taught early to walk on slick rock with their weight low and their hands free. Old people remember the names of channels that no longer run and the places where water once gathered. Decisions are made slowly, in long quiet councils, by those who know the upper slopes. Wealth is a clear channel, a dry sleeping shelf, a patch of moss that comes back every year.

They have no beasts in their stories. No birds, no insects, no fish—their language has words for the sound of nothing moving, and for the particular silence that follows a drip. They do not think of themselves as lonely, because they have no flocks to miss, but the loneliness is in them anyway, a shape they have not named. They sometimes sing, but low and short, more like humming to the water. They leave pale flowers at springs. They walk the channels together at long intervals and listen. They tell of the First Fracture and the Long Pause, of pressure deep under the stone and water learning its way out.

From a ridge, you might mistake them for shrubs or standing stones until one lifts a hand. They are not masters of the plateau. They are its careful tenants, wet-handed and low-voiced, keeping the water moving and letting the stone stay sharp. They live in the long pause, and they have made a life there.
