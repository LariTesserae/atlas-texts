What lives in Glisten-Reach is not a population so much as a long, quiet collaboration between a people and a stone. They are small, pale, and unhurried—wet-chalk skin without pigment, narrow bodies quick in the joints, built for slipping through rounded passages and crouching for hours beside a seep. Their hands are their most developed feature: broad-palmed, fine-fingered, the tips worn to a smooth, almost polished thickness by shell tools, acid-etched stone, and the endless business of guiding water. Their eyes are large and dark, but they are not a staring people; sight is only one sense among several. They feel the city’s hum in their teeth and the small bones of their wrists before they hear it.

They speak little and listen constantly. Close speech is low and clipped, pitched into the same register as dripping water; a shout would be a kind of vandalism. Across a district they converse by striking quartz-veined pillars with resonant stones, sending patterns through the stone that answer back from other pillars. Children learn to tap before they learn to orate. They learn the city as a second body: which turn of a channel rings hollow, which pillar is true, which changed note means a crack overhead or a stranger in the deep ways. They do not call their home Glisten-Reach. Their name for it is a chord, and their name for themselves is a shorter phrase struck on the nearest stone.

They are artisans of patience. A person is born into a family hollow, learns to read the seep, and spends decades coaxing a new chamber from the rock—etching with acid harvested from blind isopods, polishing with drip and shell and time. They do not mine the stone; they persuade it. A room is lived in while it is being made, and children continue the smoothing after the maker’s death. Property is less a thing owned than a responsibility inherited: the wall your grandmother coaxed into shape, the channel your uncle taught to sing. Their tools are hardened stone, shell, gut, and water. Their technology is leverage, filtration, and exquisite restraint.

They measure time by the slow rise and fall of the subterranean river, a cycle of perhaps forty years. Within that long pulse they have no days, no seasons, only the river’s breath and the city’s shifting notes. Births and deaths are marked quietly, by a new patch of polish or a newly tuned pillar. They are conservative in the deepest sense: not fearful of change, but unwilling to hurry it. A changed echo is a serious event. A stranger is a changed echo. They will know you are coming before they see you, by the way the hum bends around your body.

Other lives are few and mostly kept. In deep wet pits they husband blind, armored, isopod-like scavengers, pale things that scuttle through crevices; their digestive chemistry is harvested with careful, reverent hands. The creatures are livestock and colleagues, the city’s slow chisel. Motes of luminescent dust drift on the air, not alive but part of the weather of light. The people themselves are the only true agents of change: small, wet-skinned, resonant, and patient, a civilization made out of listening to stone and answering in drips.
