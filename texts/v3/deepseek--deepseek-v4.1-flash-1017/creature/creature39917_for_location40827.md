The ones who made the marks are not builders. They are **levelers**, and they live so low to the world that you may mistake one for a hummock of moss until it sighs and stands.

They are roughly person-shaped if a person were made to kneel perpetually: long in the torso, short and powerful in the limbs, with broad splayed feet that do not sink because they spread the weight instead of fighting it. Their skin is the color of wet bark and has the texture of a leaf that has been rained on for years — slick, slightly cool, never quite dry. Their hands end in gripping fans rather than fingers, good for roots and weed-stems and the edges of sunken stones. Their eyes are small, set low and wide, and they have a second lid that comes down when they go into the water. They do not have a profile you would recognize at a distance. They have a *silhouette* only when they choose to stand, and they choose that rarely.

They are not fast. Nothing here is. They move at the pace of a thought you are still deciding to have, and they are almost impossible to surprise, because they feel the ground with their whole bodies. A footfall two ponds away is a rumor in the water. A stranger stepping onto the mat is a pressure, a wrongness, a small event that travels.

They live in the dry pockets — the rain-shadow hollows under the big fallen trunks, the powdery places that have stayed powdery for a hundred years — and they live *in* the wet as well, because there is no real separation. A family group might have three or four favored hollows linked by channels they know by touch, each hollow kept just clear enough to sleep in, each one with a flat stone or a packed-earth shelf at its center. That shelf is the important thing. It is not furniture. It is a **decision**: here, the ground will be level; here, things will not roll; here, you can put down a basket of fibers and know where it is in the morning. In a country where water has no direction and every surface yields, flatness is a kind of wealth.

They make the dressed stones. They make the raised platforms. They make the posts. Not as ruins — as maintenance. A post is set upright because uprightness is rare and useful: it marks a path across a featureless green, it measures the water level, it says *this hollow is ours and it is still here*. The stones are reset when the muck swallows them. The platform is re-packed when it softens. The work is slow and unending and almost invisible, which is why a visitor sees only “ancient arrangements” and not a people quietly keeping their world in order.

Their bodies carry the place. Moss grows on their shoulders if they sit too long, and they let it, and then they brush it off with a palm when it begins to itch. Their breath is low and warm. Their voices are not loud; they speak in taps and hums — knuckles on a root, a palm flat on water, a low throat-sound that carries better through the wet air than a shout. They have names for trees and hollows and rot-stages, but not for years. They measure time by whether a post still stands, whether a fallen trunk has begun to soften at the crown, whether the pale flowers have opened on the vine they planted when someone died.

They eat what the plateau gives without hurry: larvae scooped from the layered ponds, fungus that fruits on the soft dead wood, the starchy hearts of certain rubbery stems, small clinging things picked off the undersides of leaves. They set traps of woven vine and leave them for days. They tend, in a loose way, the ponds near their hollows — not draining them, not damming them, just nudging: pulling out one choking weed so another can breathe, dropping a seed-head into a seep, keeping a channel from closing entirely. They are gardeners of water that has nowhere to go.

They are few. A group is maybe eight or twenty, and the groups are spread thin, because the plateau is vast and the flat places are not. They meet at the posts. They exchange news by touch and by the position of stones. They do not fight over hollows, because a hollow is only useful if someone maintains it, and there are more hollows than people. They are wary of the large presences — the things that make the low horn at night, the things that leave canals where their feet have been — but they are not afraid of them in the way you would be. They simply know where not to be, the way you know not to stand under a leaning trunk.

The dead are sunk into the duff, weighted with stones, and in a decade the place has made them into a low mound with a flat top. The people use the mound. They put things on it. That is not disrespect; it is the only kind of permanence available. Water does not leave, so neither do the dead, exactly. They become part of the closed hand.

If you stay long enough, one will eventually come to look at you. It will not be a dramatic arrival. A hummock at the edge of your vision will resolve into a back, a shoulder, a wide wet hand testing the water. It will crouch there for a long time, reading you. Then it may leave a flat stone on the mat between you — not a gift, exactly, but a question of levelness. If you sit still, and do not loom, and do not make any sudden upright thing of yourself, it may decide you are tolerable.

And if you sleep too long in one place, it will brush the moss off you before it goes. Politely. The way you would brush a leaf from a table.
