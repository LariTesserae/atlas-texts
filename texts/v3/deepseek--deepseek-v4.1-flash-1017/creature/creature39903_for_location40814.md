Whatever lives in the Loud lives in the plural, and mostly without faces. Start with the ground, because the ground is a resident. The mat is not soil; it is a composite animal-thing, root mass and silt and shed husks bound by white filament, and it lives by being walked on and by mending itself afterward. Every step rings through it like a plucked string. It is young, continuous, and not quite separate from what stands on it. The filament threads the roots and the leaves and the bodies of the animals; it is the basin’s connective tissue, its repair crew, its faint blue-white lighting system. When it works at architectural scale it becomes the town: the pale accreted towers, the grown bridges, the staircases that arrive overnight at rooms with no doors. That builder is not a person or a colony with a face. It is a patience with a construction schedule. It lives by adding surfaces nobody has asked for. It does not want occupants. It wants more floor.

Above and inside that, the **chorus**. Insects and near-insects, amphibian-jointed things, jumping invertebrates that travel in sheets and land like thrown gravel. They are small individually and enormous collectively. They live in overlapping pulses that synchronise and break and synchronise again, so that the roar has grammar instead of just volume. They do not flee the noise; they are a large part of it. A chorus here is less a population than a distributed organ — a thing made of many bodies that keeps time with the lift and the water and the light.

Then the **hull-beasts**, the large ones you feel before you see. Slow dome-shelled grazers built of the same white ceramic as the filament, moving perhaps a kilometre a week. They live at the pace of the basin’s rising floor. Their footfalls arrive through the mat an hour ahead of them; a rising figure in the roar announces them. They are walking architecture, half animal and half maintained object, and the filament probably repairs their shells while they sleep. They graze the weed down and leave trails that close behind them.

The **cloud-fliers** are a dusk resident: thousands of them coming over as a single descending tone, a flock that behaves like one note. They live in the high haze and arrive as weather. The **pool-things** are the opposite — residents known only as disturbances in the weed, a moving absence in green water, a shape that is never quite there when you look. They are the reason still water is not trusted.

And then there are the **crews** — the humans who work the edges and spend seasons down here. They are the only residents who know they are visitors. They come back with flattened, careful speech, as though they are still picking words out of the noise. They learn to walk in time with the chorus so their footsteps vanish into it. They learn that a shout carries four kilometres and that the basin answers. They learn not to stand on ground that has started to sing. They keep maps that are worthless, because the place is younger than every survey of it.

Under all of them is the **Lifter**: warm, slow, patient, far too large, never seen. It lives as pressure and low chord, as a floor rising a few centimetres a year, as channels opening and closing like something breathing. It is the largest inhabitant and the least negotiable. It makes the land new, which is why nothing here has had time to round off, why the banks are raw and the mud faces clean, why the oldest thing you can stand on is a mat.

So the population of the Loud is a lattice that mends, a chorus that is also the weather, grazers like slow bells, a dusk tone with wings, disturbances in the weed, a builder indifferent to tenants, and a handful of humans who have learned to be quiet in a loud place. They are all tenants of the same rising floor. The only rule they share is the one the ground teaches: do not stand on ground that has started to sing.
