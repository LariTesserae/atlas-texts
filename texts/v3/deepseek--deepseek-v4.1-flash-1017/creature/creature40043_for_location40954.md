There is no single occupant of the splinter. Ask what lives there and the honest answer is a habit of holding — a distributed, patient, water-hoarding organism that the place itself might call the Keeper, though it has no name for itself and no mouth to say one.

Call it the Keeper if you like. It is not a person. It is closer to a reef that has learned to ration: a colonial body of small pale eyeless workers, each about the size of a thumb-joint, moving in slow tides through the interstices of the bladder-plants, laying down cement a millimeter a year, tending galleries, sealing seams, counting — not counting in numbers, but in the felt pressure of full pods and the tiny catastrophic pop of a pierced bladder. Its hands are those workers. Its blood is the warm sealed fluid in the plants. Its nearest thing to a face is the canopy on the highest tier: a hundred thousand thick-skinned bladders, glossy and green-gold, tracking the sun and folding shut at midday one after another down the whole summit, so that the town goes dim and cool and quiet inside itself. At dusk they open again with a soft leathery sigh that runs across the roof of the place like something breathing out.

It has no eyes. It does not need them. It lives in a world of pressure and temperature and chemistry — the warmth seeping out of the stone at night, the faint sub-audible thrum of the colony working in the walls, the taste of a stranger’s exhalation as moisture leaving a body. It knows footsteps. It knows when a robber has found a seam. It knows the difference between a lean year and a fat one by the tightness of the pods in the deep chambers. Its memory is laminated into the walls: layer on layer of cemented grit, and in the older walls the mummified dead of earlier inhabitants pressed in as filler, desiccated to leather and then to stone-likeness, still faintly recognizable. Nothing rots here. The Keeper does not mourn. It recycles.

Its industry is almost geological. It builds chambers, galleries, buttressed walls, sloped aprons of cemented grit, terraces stepping down the leeward face, vents and flues that carry warm dry air up from the sunlit faces and out through the crest. It keeps fluid stores — sealed pods held in trust against a lean span. It tends shallow dust-gardens of mineral powder where something grows that likes nothing at all. It spreads the highest bladder-plants into flat plates that track the sun. It does all this slowly, because there is nothing here that rewards hurry: no flood to outrun, no season to beat, only the long accumulation of the dry and the warm. A single chamber is a lifetime. A wall is several.

And it is not peaceful. The splinter is crowded, and every other living thing is either a vessel, a thief, or a tenant of the Keeper’s warmth. There is a whole guild of small robbers — hollow spikes, awl-bodies, proboscises like drawn glass — whose entire way of life is to find a seam in a sealed body and open it. The Keeper answers some with armor: crusts of mineral, spines, false skins that bleed a useless dry sap. Some it answers with decoys. A few of its bladder-plants have gone the other way and take the robbers instead, digesting them slowly in a closed cavity, not for the flesh but for the water in it. There are plated things the color of dust that scuttle over the stone. There are things that live entirely inside the galleries and never come out. There are things that hang from the underside of plates. All of them are there for the moisture and not the meat, and the Keeper tolerates them or kills them according to a logic older than appetite.

It did not build the splinter. The splinter was shouldered up out of the white plain recently, its cliffs still glassy and sharp, its talus still angular. The Keeper rode up on it, an old crowded settlement on a new raw raft, and went on with a business it began long before the summit was this high. That is the thing to understand about whoever lives here: the town is older than its ground. The life on top has had time to become elaborate and settled and patient, while the stone underneath is still new enough to cut you.

And it will notice you. Not with eyes — with the pressure of your feet on its worn paths, with the warmth of your body, with the moisture of your breath. To the Keeper, you are a leak. It will not hate you. It has no word for hate and no hurry in which to feel it. But it will feel the water leaving your mouth and it will mark the loss, and somewhere in the deep chambers a pod will tighten, and a wall will thicken by another millimeter, and the pale eyeless workers will go on moving in their slow tides, sealing, tending, keeping.
