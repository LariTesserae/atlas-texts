No one lives in the Murmur Sink.

That is the first correction the place makes. Then, after you have stood in the dim long enough for your eyes to give up on detail and your ears to stop flinching, the second correction: something does. Not a person. Not a species, exactly. A standing population — a multitude of small pale bodies whose loops intersect so tightly that the sink behaves like a single inhabited thing. If you try to pick out one representative resident, you will keep failing. The first body you see will be a wet trail. The second will be a tick of shell against stone. The third will be a green pulse moving along a seam like a slow infection of light.

You will start giving them handles, because you need them. The sink does not.

The **wet carpet** is the easiest to find. Soft-bodied things, pale as the inside of a mushroom, with too many legs and no clear front. They gather along the weeping seams in slow-moving sheets, not marching so much as *seeping* — each body pressed close to the next, leaving a shine on the stone that other things follow. They crop the fungal crusts and the mineral films. They are the sink’s grazing layer, and they are so numerous that a single seam can look like a length of wet rope until it moves. They have no eyes worth the name. They have bristles, and damp, and a mouth underneath. When the rain pulses come, they do not scatter. They tighten into knots in the low basins and wait it out, and when the water drains they spread again, leaving fresh trails in the same places as before.

The **stone-shells** are harder to see until you know what you are looking at. They sit in the shallow basins and under the rubble, coiled or plated or simply lumped, the color of the sink’s own walls. Their shells are built from the water — mineral laid down in thin layers, so that a shell is less a house than a slow record of the seam it lives in. They open when the damp is thick and close when it thins. The ticking you hear is partly them: shells settling, shells touching, shells adjusting to water that arrives and leaves on its own schedule. They do not chase anything. They filter. They wait. They are the place’s patience made small and hard.

The **lantern specks** are the only light the sink makes for itself. Tiny, soft, clustered in patches on the shaded walls, they glow a sick green that pulses in time with the low hum. Not bright enough to see by — never that — but bright enough to mark the loops. When one patch brightens, another answers across the bowl, and another, and the whole dim sea of air seems to breathe. They are not signaling to you. They are keeping spacing, or mating, or doing something older than either; the effect is a map of where the population is dense and where it is thin. If you hold your hand near a patch, the pulse will continue exactly as before. You are not part of the circuit.

The **paperwings** are the sound that blurs the air. Dry, fragile, many. They rise from the rubble and the seams in loose columns and follow fixed lanes through the greenish dark, and their wings make a papery hiss that never quite stops. They do not seem to rest. They die in flight, or they die against the walls, and their bodies fall into the basins where the wet carpet and the stone-shells take them back into the system. The population does not thin. The lanes do not change. If you watch long enough, you will see the same turns repeated, the same crossings, the same brief clustering over the same wet patch — not because the individual flyers remember, but because the pattern is the thing that persists, and the bodies are how it persists.

The **keeners** are the far wall’s voice. You will hear them before you ever see them: a high rising and falling note, scheduled, answered, scheduled again. Individually they are tiny — a grain of rice with a hollow spine, a mouthpart that rubs, a chamber in the shell that resonates — but they gather in vertical seams by the thousand, and the seam becomes an instrument. They do not move much in a lifetime. They do not need to. Their call-and-answer is the most rigid loop in the sink, and when you listen to it you understand why the whole place feels scripted. The keeners are not warning anyone. They are running a program. The program is old, and it is working.

Between these — and under them, and inside the wet seams, and in the thin film on the rubble — are the others. The **cleaners**, small and quick, that find dead bodies before they accumulate. The **borers**, that live in the soft stone and leave pinprick holes. The **clingers**, that hold to the undersides of rubble and open like tiny flowers when water passes. The **hummers**, if that is what they are, whose low throbbing fills the bottom of the sound — not one voice but thousands of small bodies ventilating, rubbing, vibrating against damp stone. None of them are large. The largest would fit in a closed hand; most would fit in a thimble. They are not impressive one at a time. They are not meant to be.

What lives there, in the end, is not a who. It is a standing wave of small pale bodies, each one replaceable, each one locked into a loop that the sink has been running since the floor gave way. They are born and they die, but the population does not boom and does not crash. Reproduction is replacement, not growth. Predation is constant and small and reciprocal. The damp is enough. The dim is enough. The cold is enough. The swarm is not hostile; it is not curious; it will not notice when you climb back out. It will simply continue — clicking, chittering, whistling, glowing, dripping, settling — exactly as it was before you came down, exactly as it will be after you are gone.

Ask who lives in the Murmur Sink and the honest answer is the sound. The bodies are how the sound keeps itself going.
