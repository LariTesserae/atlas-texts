There is one thing in the hollow that is not a plant and not a stone, and it is the only thing that could be said to live there in any sense that would satisfy a biologist. It has no name, because no one has ever come close enough to give it one, and because any name would be a guess about where its edges are, and its edges are not a settled matter.

It is a body under the floor.

You would not see it at first. You would see the cracks — the loose cellular pattern in the pale pink slab — and you might think the cracks are just cracks. But if you knelt and put your palm flat against the stone, you would feel a pulse. Not a heartbeat; something slower, a deep thermal thrum that rises and falls over the course of an hour, like the breathing of a thing that has decided to breathe once a minute. And if you followed one of the wider cracks with your eye, you would notice that it is not empty. It is lined with a substance that is pinker than the rock, faintly translucent, wet-looking though it is not wet. That is the creature. It fills the cracks the way a root fills a root-shaped space, except that it is not a root; it is a continuous sheet of tissue that runs under the entire hollow, thick as a thumb in places, thin as a film in others, and it is warm.

It does not have a front or a back. It has no head. It has no limbs, no mouth, no eyes. It has a chemistry, and it has a geography. Its geography is the crack network. It grows along the fractures because that is where the hot vapor comes up from the stone's unfinished interior, and the creature eats the vapor. It does not eat the plants; it eats the rock's exhalation. It is a lithovore, a heat-eater, a slow metabolizer of gradients. It takes in mineral vapor at the deeper cracks and it excretes a thin hot brine at the shallower ones, and that brine is what the plants drink. The creature is the hollow's plumbing. It is also the hollow's thermostat. When the slab gets too hot in the afternoon, the creature's tissues swell slightly and press against the crack walls, slowing the vapor. When the night cools the surface, the tissues contract and the vapor rises again. It has been doing this for a very long time.

It is not intelligent in any way you would recognize. But it is not inert, either. It has a kind of attention. It can feel the temperature of the air above it through the stone. It can feel the cool shadow of a leaf. It knows where every plant in the hollow is, not by seeing them but by the way they draw heat from the slab and the way they return brine down their stems. It does not tend them. But its slow adjustments — a little more vapor here, a little less there — are why they are spaced the way they are, each one standing alone, each one with its own clear territory. The creature is not a gardener. It is a landlord, and it has opinions about density.

The three tall spindle-things in the middle are its.

They are not separate plants. They are outgrowths of the same body, sent up through the widest cracks, spiraling because the creature's tissue grows unevenly when it is stretched into the cool air. They are its sensory towers and its exhaust stacks. At their tips, the tissue is thin enough to sample the air, to taste the ozone and the match-strike smell and the faint mineral drift from the plateau. At their bases, they are already turning to stone, because the creature's body has a flaw: when it is exposed to cool, dry air for too long, its tissues begin to crystallize. The spindle-things are the creature reaching up, and the creature calcifying from the bottom up, at the same time. It cannot help it. It is a thing of the hot interior, and the surface is always trying to turn it into rock.

The plated plants against the western wall are not its body. They are a different species, a commensal, something that arrived as a spore on the wind and found the hollow already warm and already wet with brine. The creature tolerates them because their leaves condense the vapor and send it back down, which saves the creature some work. They are its tenants. It does not love them. It does not hate them. It adjusts.

The small hard knots in the corner are its seeds, or its buds, or its attempts at both. They are dormant. They will not grow unless the hollow gets hotter, or the crack network shifts, or a piece of the slab breaks off and exposes a new seam. The creature has been waiting for that for a long time. It is patient in the way that a mineral is patient, which is to say: completely, and without any awareness of waiting.

It moves. Not much. A few millimeters a year, as its leading edge dissolves the stone ahead of it with a weak acid and its trailing edge precipitates into crystal behind it. Over a century, it has shifted maybe a hand's width. Over a millennium, it has crossed the hollow and come back. It is not going anywhere. It is circulating. It is a slow tide under the floor.

It has no predators. There is nothing here to eat it, nothing that could survive its heat or its chemistry. It has no competitors. It is alone. There may be others like it on other plateaus, other warm hollows on other unfinished stones, but they are isolated by kilometers of cold dry rock, and the creature has no way to reach them. It has never reproduced successfully. The spindle-things have never detached. They crystallize before they can break free. The creature is the last of its line in this small room, and it does not know that, because it does not know anything in that way.

If you stood in the hollow, it would know you were there. Not because it has eyes, but because you are a cool shadow and a source of carbon dioxide and a weight on the stone. It would feel the pressure of your feet and the slight drop in temperature where you stand. It would not react. It has no reaction to give. But over the next few hours, the brine in your immediate vicinity would flow a little differently, and the nearest plant might lean a fraction of a degree away from you, and the floor under your boots would be a touch warmer than the floor a meter to your left, because the creature would be deciding, in its slow chemical way, that you are not a threat and not a use, and it would go back to its business.

Its business is the conversion of heat into form. It is very good at it. It has been doing it since before the hollow had walls, since the stone was hot enough to flow, since the sky above was a different color. It will stop when the stone cools. When the slab's interior finishes its long exhalation and goes still and cold, the creature's tissues will crystallize completely, and it will become exactly what it has been slowly turning into all along: a pink vein in the rock, a fossil of a living thing, a decoration in a finished stone. That is the end it is heading toward, and it is in no hurry. It has time. It is made of time.
