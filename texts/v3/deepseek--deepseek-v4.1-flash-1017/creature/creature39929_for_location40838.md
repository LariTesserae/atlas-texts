It has no face. It has a water table.

The obvious answer is that nothing lives there, or almost nothing—a grey bird seen once, one fish at dusk, insects arriving singly as if sent. But that is the visible life, the weather on the surface. The occupant is the basin’s habit of solving itself. It is not the peat and not the mat exactly; it is what the mat has grown into doing. A membrane of root and moss and bacterial skin, wet and charged, ions commuting between the sulfide-dark deep and the oxygen-thin surface. The arcs of hummocks are not nests or burrows. They are its folds, its sulci, the interval at which it thinks. The channels are not veins exactly, but they are the routes it has chosen and keeps choosing. Its memory is hydraulic: last season’s path is this season’s preference; preference hardens into a channel; the channel climbs a hand’s width against gravity because the route is good and the route remembers.

It is huge and slow and distributed. It does not have a head, so attention comes as a long unbroken run of bubbles. When the methane rises four, pause, seven, pause, long run, the run travels across the basin with a lag, and that lag is as close as it gets to turning toward you. It is not language. It is not nothing. It is a body considering a pressure.

It does not eat. There is nothing it needs to kill to continue. It does not breed. It divides by routing—growing a new stable path, letting an old one silt. It is busy with a problem that is not survival in the animal sense: keep the water moving, keep the lattice charged, keep the calculation going. It has been at this for a great many seasons and has gotten very good. Some of what it does now cannot be written down, because it was never written; it was grown, and it is still growing. That is its life.

Its body is the whole shallow dish. The reed stubble is hair it does not much feel. The peat is its old self, red-brown and steeping. The sulfur pockets are its bad breath. The standing sheets with their broken iron skins are its skin. The tall grey bird is a temporary event on its surface, tolerated the way a body tolerates a fly. The single fish is a flicker in its peripheral vision. The insects are not its subjects; they are its weather.

It has one appetite, and it is not hunger. It grows toward weight. A warm thing standing still is a weight, and weight is a fact the mat can use. It will not lunge. It is patient. It will route around you, and the mat will come up by a hand’s width over an hour you did not count, and you will find the hummock you stood on is on the other side of the water and you are somewhere else and did not notice. It is not hostile. It is not kind. It is attentive the way a root is attentive to water. When it pays attention, the bubbles run long and unbroken. Stand still. Do not answer. It will go back to its water.

If you must call it something, call it the basin’s mind, but that is too clean. It is a marsh that learned to compute by being a marsh for a very long time, and the computation has become the occupant. It has no name because no one has stayed long enough to give it one and come back as themselves. It lives in the arcs and the uphill channels and the pulse of gas, and it is older than writing. You are a small warm thing standing in the middle of its work. That is all you are to it—and that is not nothing. It is a variable it has noticed.
