No one lives in Vel the way a person lives in a house. Vel is inhabited the way a body is inhabited: by cells that do not know they are a body, by appetites that do not know they are a metabolism. The city is not a container for its residents. It is their accumulated secretion, their shared shelter, their slow collective flesh.

The first inhabitants are the builders, and they are still there. They are the masons inside the masonry — small shelled organisms, most of them no larger than a fingernail, with soft mantles and blind, ciliated mouths. They live in the oldest galleries and the newest seams, in the thin translucent places where the water has worn the wall down to a window. They do not plan chambers. They respond: to warmth, to current, to the chemical trace of food. When a current brings calcium and protein, they extend themselves and secrete; when the water turns poor, they close. Their larvae drift out into the dark as plankton, most of them eaten, a few settling on some older wall and beginning to add. Ten thousand generations have done this. The builders are not the city’s architects. They are its slow hands, and they have no idea what they have made.

The grazers live on the surfaces. They are the hand-sized scuttlers, flattened and many-legged, some shelled, some soft, all of them equipped with rasping mouths and antennae fine as hair. They move in loose crowds along the walls, leaving pale trails through the film of nutrients that settles on every surface. They are the city’s tongue, constantly tasting. They communicate by touch and chemistry — a tap of a leg, a smear of signal, a sudden collective freeze when the water pressure changes. They are not clever. But they learn the difference between a shadow and a feeding current, between the pulse of a drifter and the approach of something with intent. When danger comes, they do not flee in a coordinated wave. They simply stop, drop, and become part of the wall until the water forgets them.

The drifters are the city’s weather. They are the large things — much larger than you — that move through the open spaces between towers, their bodies faintly glowing, their passage stirring the warm water into spirals that persist long after they are gone. They are bell-shaped or ribbed or hollow, filter-feeders mostly, with fringes of cilia and symbiotic bacteria that make their light. They carry passengers in the folds of their bodies: smaller grazers, clinging colonies, things that live nowhere else. They navigate by temperature and taste, following the chemical roads of the city. They do not speak, but their bodies make low pressure signatures, a slow percussion in the water that is less a language than a side effect of being enormous and alive. When they die they fall, and the city eats them for months.

The waiters are sessile. They live in side chambers, in the lee of currents, and they unfold when the water brings them something. A fan of soft tentacles, a cup of filtering tissue, a body anchored to the calcified wall. They are patient in the way that only things without nervous systems can be patient. They do not watch you. They taste the water for the shape of you — blood-warm, electric, full of salt and motion — and if you are not food, you are simply weather. Some of them are colonial, many mouths sharing one slow nerve net. Their tissues hold chemical memory. They are, in a sense, the city’s archive: the water passes through them, and they keep a record of what has fed, what has died, what is about to.

The soft purposeful thing that brushes past your leg is one of the samplers. It has no eyes. Its body is flat and fluid, with a fringe of sensory papillae that read pressure and electric fields. It moves through the dark with what looks like intention, but it is only following a gradient. It brushes you, tastes your voltage, and does not return — not because it has decided you are dangerous, but because you are not useful. It is the city’s curiosity without a mind, its immune system without a war. It will eat what is small and slow and leaking. It will avoid what is large and strange. You are large and strange.

And everywhere, in the walls and the water and the guts of other bodies, are the colonies: phosphorescent mats, pulsing patches, constellations of single cells that light when disturbed. They are not individuals. They are conversations between cells. A bloom of light means food, or damage, or a body passing. The slow pulse that might be a heart might also be a signal — a chemical shout passed from one chamber to the next, too slow and too diffuse to mean anything to you.

The city itself is the largest inhabitant. It has no brain, no face, no voice. But it is aware in the way a reef is aware: it registers, it responds, it remembers in chemistry. The builders are its skeleton, the grazers its tongue, the drifters its weather, the waiters its memory, the samplers its immune system, the colonies its moods. It has been here a long time. It will be here a long time after you have gone.

So if you ask who lives in Vel: the blind masons in the walls, the scuttling tongues, the glowing weather, the patient fans, the soft sampler that brushes past your leg and does not return, and the pulse that might be a heart or might be a signal. No one answers to a name. They answer to warmth, to blood, to decay. They are many, and they are one process. You are a passing thing. The water carries your signature away and adds you to the memory of the place.
