What lives there is not one thing. That is the first thing to understand, and the large pale shape at the back will let you understand it slowly.

At first you think the occupant is that shape—the slow heavy shift in the undergrowth. It is real. It is roughly the size of a sheep, maybe larger, though size is hard to judge because half of it has been given over to the floor. It is a salamander the way a fallen trunk is still a tree: same lineage, different commitment. Its skin is the color of wet bark and liverwort, patched with the same faint luminescence as the growths on the walls. Its head is broad and flat. Its eyes are two dull seeds set under a shelf of skin, and it does not blink, because blinking is a kind of change. Its mouth is a long seam. Pale feathery gills hang at its neck and stir slightly in the wet air—not urgently, just enough to show they have not forgotten what they are for. Its limbs are folded under it. It does not come closer. There is no closer. It has been in that spot so long that moss has grown up its flank and the mat has accepted its weight. When it shifts, the whole hollow seems to adjust around it, a mattress taking a sleeper’s turn. It eats what comes within reach, slowly, without heat. It is not waiting for you. It is not waiting for anything.

But it is not the owner. The owner is the density.

The real population is everywhere and almost invisible until your eyes give up on color and start seeing texture. The finger-length salamanders are the most legible: pale, wet, with tiny hands, sliding between liverwort plates like coins into a slot. They do not scatter when you move. They pause, consider you without eyes that can consider, and continue. Fear is a response to change, and nothing here changes. The beetles are soft-bodied, their wing-cases like wet clay, and they crawl over your boots as if boots were only another surface of moss. The midges hang in still columns, dense as loose stitches in the air. They do not whine. They do not bite. They are there to be breathed. Inside the moss there is another population entire—mites, worms, larvae, things with no names you would recognize—and in the standing water another, and on the walls another, and in the wet air itself, probably, another. Every surface is a tenancy. Every hollow is occupied. The pale lights are not just fungus; they are the visible edge of a habit of glowing that has been going on so long it has become the local idea of light.

The thing that lives there, in the end, is a single crowded life that has learned not to be separate. It has no voice. It gave up sound as a bad habit long before you arrived. It does not want you dead. It wants you still. It would make room for you by making you part of the room. If you stayed, the wet would enter your clothes; the moss would find the seams; the salamanders would investigate your boots; the beetles would test the salt on your skin; the large pale thing would shift once, settle, and accept you as one more warm spot in the mat. You would not be eaten all at once. You would be inhabited.

So the occupant is the whole packed chapel of life that has nowhere to go and no wish to. The large pale thing at the back is only its oldest thought—patient, wet, silent, complete, and entirely uninterested in whether you leave.
