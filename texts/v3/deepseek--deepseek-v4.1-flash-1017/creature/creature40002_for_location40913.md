The things that live here are the groves. Not the stalks individually—though each stalk is alive in its way—but the grove as a single standing body: a hundred or five hundred hollow columns packed shoulder to shoulder, closed at the top into a thatch, rooted into one seep in the bedrock, breathing one small atmosphere around and around inside itself.

Call one grove one creature. Its body is a bundle of chimneys. Warm gas comes up through the fracture beneath it, enters the roots, rises through the hollow cores, reaches the canopy, cools by a degree or two against the inner surfaces of the thatch, condenses there, and runs back down the inside of the stalks to be heated and sent up again. That is its blood and its breath and its weather. It does not drink. It does not need rain. It has its own well, and the well is a loop, and the loop has been closed for a very long time. The working fluid smells of warm brass and burnt sugar and struck flint. If you press your palm to a stalk you can feel the cycle going—a faint steady tremor, a hum at the floor of hearing, the sound of a few hundred columns moving the same few tons of vapor through themselves forever.

What it does with that vapor is the rest of what it is. The tissue of the stalks is catalytic. It cracks the gas as it passes. Sulfur comes out, and carbon, and traces of metal the deep rock is still exhaling. The grove does not keep these inside itself. It pushes them out through the root walls into the stone, where they accrete in bands and crusts and druses and thin iridescent frostings—bismuth-pink, arsenic-grey, a green that is not the green of the leaves. Every grove sits at the center of its own ring of pure metal, brightest close in, thinning to nothing at the edge of the bare. That ring is its exuvia, its leavings, its signature. It is also the only record it makes, and it records only the present, continuously, like breath fogging a window.

It is not a plant that wants anything from the world. It does not flower; there is nobody to attract. It does not fruit; there is nobody to carry. It does not rustle, because nothing visits. It spreads, or did once, by slow mineral creep at the root ring, and it reproduces by fission of the stalk-base, and neither process is doing much now. It has no predators here, no pollinators, no grazers, no burrowers, no small dry scuttling things in its thatch. It is not lonely, because loneliness is a thing that requires another kind of creature to be absent. It is simply closed.

If it has a point of view—and this is not a thing one can prove—it is not made of sight or sound. It is made of pressure and temperature and chemistry: the warmth of the seam below, the composition of the gas, the moment condensation begins in the thatch, the slow outward creep of its own metal ring into the slab. It knows the fracture it is rooted in the way a hand knows a warm stone. It knows its own hum. It knows the loop closing.

And it lives in a place with no floor for the past. Nothing silts down here. Nothing is buried. There are no strata, no sediment, no soft rain of the dead pressed into layers. The grove does not grow and does not die in any way you could mark on a calendar. It holds its balance the way a spinning top holds its balance: everything in motion, nothing changing. It stands in its ring of iridescent frost, dark and glossy and copper-edged, humming to itself, refining the breath of the deep rock, and it has been caught precisely at the instant of becoming, and it will be caught there a while yet.
