# Those Who Live in the Hollow

## The Water-Tenders

They are not born here anymore—not many of them, at least. The Hollow has a way of thinning the blood of outsiders, of wearing them down through the soles of their feet and the small bones of their wrists, until they either leave or become something the Hollow can use. The ones who stay are the ones who learn to sleep through the valve-slams, who stop flinching at the bells, who come to find the constant fall of water soothing rather than maddening. These are the Water-Tenders, though they would not call themselves that; they would say they work the Works, or keep the pipes, or simply that they live here, which is answer enough.

They are a lean people, damp-skinned and quiet-footed, their hands large and knuckled from the cold and the constant work. Their faces have a particular look—the Hollow look, someone from outside might say—the eyes slightly wide and always listening, the mouth relaxed in a way that is not quite a smile but is not unfriendly either. They have the pallor of people who have not seen strong sun in years, but it is not an unhealthy pallor; it is the color of things that grow in shade, pale and soft and somehow nourished.

They sleep in shifts, these people, or they sleep when they can, and their beds are hung from the ceilings on iron chains in the upper stories so that when the water rises below, they are already above it. Their children are born in these hanging beds, and they learn to walk on slatted floors that move beneath them, and they learn to speak softly because the pipes are always speaking and one must not compete.

## The Body of the Hollow

You would know them anywhere by their bodies. Not by their faces—the steam and the dim light make faces hard to remember—but by their bodies, which have been shaped by the place as surely as the stone has been shaped by the water.

Their legs are strong from the endless climbing of terraces and the balancing on walkways that shift and settle. Their feet are wide and calloused from the iron-rimmed clogs, and their toes have learned to grip through the open tops, to feel the condition of the slats beneath them. Their hands are the most marked: the palms thick and tough as boot leather, the fingers slightly curved from a lifetime of gripping pipes and turning valves, the nails thick and ridged and never quite clean, the creases of the knuckles dark with a grime that no amount of scrubbing will remove. The young ones have hands still soft, but the work finds them soon enough.

They carry themselves with a slight forward lean, as though always walking into a wind, though there is no wind in the Hollow. It is the water they lean into, the constant pressure of the place, the sense that one must push against something even when standing still. They move slowly but not sluggishly—deliberately, the way one moves in a place where a single careless step can mean a fall through rotted slats into the flood below, or a hand laid wrong on a valve that will release a scalding jet of steam.

Their voices are low and a little rough, the voices of people who have spent their lives talking over the sound of falling water. They do not shout; they have learned that shouting is useless here, that the Hollow swallows sound the way it swallows water. Instead they lean close, mouth to ear, and speak in short sentences, and the words they use are the words of the Works: *pressure, cycle, bleed, arrest, the fill, the drain, the long low.* They have a vocabulary of water and metal that outsiders find incomprehensible, and they use it without thinking, the way any people use the words for the things that matter most.

## The Names They Give

They name their children for the water and the Works, and the names are not pretty in the way that outside names are pretty. They are names like Sedge, which is what grows in the flooded courtyards, and Sluice, which is the gate that controls the flow, and Verdigris, which is the color of the copper roofs and the color of the mark that the damp leaves on the skin of those who touch it. They name their children after the sounds: Clang, which is the noise the pressure reliefs make, and Hiss, which is the sound of the steam-vents, and Drip, which is simply what the place does, always, everywhere, and which they have chosen to embrace rather than resist.

There are older names, too, names that came with the first builders and have been worn down by generations of use until they are little more than sounds: Ba, which means the low place, and Mol, which means the slow and patient thing, and Wen, which means the water that comes from below and which cannot be stopped. These names are given to the ones who show an affinity for the Works, the ones who can feel the tremor in the ground and know which valve is straining, the ones who wake before the bells and know what the bells will mean.

## The Shrine-Keepers

Not everyone works the pipes and the pumps. Some among them keep the shrines—the little copper-wire icons, the glazed tiles set into niches, the offerings left at the turning of the great cycle. These are not priests, exactly; the Hollow has no formal religion that an outsider would recognize. But there are those who feel the place more keenly than others, who hear the voice in the falling water and the speech in the clang of the valves, and they are the ones who tend the shrines and teach the children the proper way to touch them as they pass.

The Shrine-Keepers are often old, their bodies bent by years of climbing and their hands too stiff for the delicate work of the pipes. But they are not idle. They are the memory of the Hollow, the ones who remember the great floods and the long dry cycles, who know which walls are safe and which are waiting to fall, who can look at the moss on a building and tell you how many years it has stood and how many more it might. They keep the chronology of the place, the tiers of stone and tile that mark each generation's work, and they pass this knowledge down in the quiet hours between one cycle and the next.

They are the ones who go to the ruins on the rim at the turning of the great cycle, when the Works marks its slow years. They climb the gentle terraces in their heavy oilskin, their clogs clattering on the forgotten paths, and they stand among the collapsed walls and the rusted lace of the old pipes, and they remember the first attempts to tame the seep, the ones that failed. They leave small offerings—a twist of copper wire, a shard of glazed tile, a handful of reeds—and they do not speak. They listen to the water, which is quieter up there, and they let the moss grow over their feet, and then they come down again and resume their waiting.

## The Mechanics of Kinship

There are not enough of them to be careless with one another. This is the first law of the Hollow, and it shapes everything. A hundred souls? Two hundred? No one has counted in years, and the counting is difficult because the Hollow holds them in its own way, in the tiers of its architecture, in the shifts of the Works, and they come and go through the mists without ever assembling in one place. But they know each other. They know each other in the way that people know each other when the loss of one is felt by all, when a hand injured in the Works is a hand that cannot turn a valve, when a body gone under the water is a gap in the rhythm of the place that cannot be filled.

They are careful with one another in small ways. They share their food—the pale fish that live in the flooded quarries beyond the Hollow, the roots that grow in the controlled pools, the reeds that they harvest and cook and weave. They share their beds when the floods come, the hanging beds swinging close enough to touch, and they do not speak of this afterward, because it is simply what is done. They share their tools, which are precious, and their knowledge, which is more precious still, and they do not keep accounts of what is owed. The Hollow keeps its own accounts, and the water always balances them in the end.

## The Children

The children of the Hollow are born with the tremor already in their bones, and they do not know that it is strange until they leave and come back, or until an outsider tells them. They learn to walk on the shifting walkways before they learn to walk on solid ground, and the first time they encounter a truly stable floor, they stumble, because they have learned to expect the ground to move beneath them and have adjusted their balance accordingly.

They are quiet children, not sullen but watchful, with the wide listening eyes of their parents. They do not play the games that children play in other places—games of running and chasing, of hide and seek in dry spaces. The Hollow does not permit these games. Instead they play at the Works: they build small models of pipes and valves from scraps of copper and iron, and they make water flow through them, and they learn the principles of pressure and release before they learn to read. They play at the shrines, touching the icons and murmuring the words their parents have taught them, and they do not know what the words mean but they know they are important.

They are taught early to respect the water. A child who falls into the flooded courtyards might be swept away, might be pulled down into the drains that carry the water to the Works, and there is no saving them once they are taken. So the children learn to walk the upper terraces, to stay away from the edges, to listen for the change in the sound of the flow that means a surge is coming. They learn to swim, of course—everyone learns to swim—but they learn also that swimming is not enough, that the water in the Hollow is not like the water in other places, that it has its own currents and its own intentions, and that one must not trust it.

## The Old Ones

Those who live long enough become something else. The tremor in their bones deepens until it is not just a sensation but a kind of knowing, a sensitivity to the Works that borders on communion. They can feel a valve beginning to fail before the pressure gauge shows it, can hear the change in the flow that means a filter is clogging, can smell the difference between water that is clean and water that is beginning to sour. They do not need to see the Works to know its condition; they carry it in their bodies, in the constant shaking of their hands, in the way they wake at night when something is wrong.

These old ones are respected, but they are also a little feared. They have become too much of the place, and there is a sense that they might soon become the place entirely, that they might one day simply stop moving and let the moss grow over them and become part of the walls, part of the drainage, part of the slow circulation of water and stone. This is not considered a bad death in the Hollow; it is considered the natural end, the completion of a cycle. When the old ones die, they are given to the Works—their bodies laid in the settling tanks, their remains filtered and carried down into the deep places, their substance returned to the water that sustained them. The bells ring for them, and the flat cracked sound falls across the Hollow, and everyone pauses to listen, and then they resume their work.

## The Ones Who Left

Not everyone stays. There are those who cannot bear the dampness, the constancy, the slow conversation of water and iron that never ends. They leave by the one long road out of the bowl, their spring-suspended carts groaning under the weight of tanked water, and they do not come back. The Hollow does not judge them; the Hollow simply continues, as it has always continued, as it will always continue.

But there are also those who come back. They come down through the seam in the hills, through the running mud and the thick air, and they stand at the rim and look at the place they left, and they listen to it weeping and singing and clattering, and they feel the tremor in their bones that they thought they had lost, and they know that they have come home. The Hollow does not welcome them with words; it welcomes them with water, with the sound of the valves and the bells and the endless falling, and they descend into it as they descended at birth, into the holding hollow of the place that made them.

## The Uncounted

There are others in the Hollow. There are the ones who are not quite people anymore, who have been here so long that the place has absorbed them, who move through the steam and the mist like shadows, whose faces are impossible to remember, whose voices are indistinguishable from the sound of dripping water. They tend the deep parts of the Works, the places where the pipes are oldest and the pressure is highest, where the great slow-turning mechanism grinds against the bedrock and sends its tremor up through the entire Hollow.

No one knows how many of these there are. No one has ever counted them. They are part of the Works now, part of the water's circulation, and they do not speak and they do not stop and they do not leave. They are the hollow heart of the Hollow, the ones who have been given to the water and have become water, and the people do not speak of them except in the quietest hours, when they lean close and whisper the old names—*Ba, Mol, Wen*—and touch the shrines as they pass.

## The Voices of the Works

And then there is the Works itself, which is not a person but is not entirely a thing either, which has been running for so long that it has developed something like a personality, something like an intention. The people feel it watching over them, not with eyes but with pressure, with the constant tremor that passes up through the ground, with the bells that mark the hours of the water's cycle. They do not personify it exactly; they do not say that the Works wants or thinks or feels. But they know, in the way that one knows the presence of another in a dark room, that the Works is aware of them, that it holds them as it holds the water, that it will continue to hold them as long as they tend it.

There is a saying in the Hollow, passed down from generation to generation, whispered to children as they learn the ways of the place: *The water remembers.* No one can explain exactly what this means, but everyone knows it is true. The water remembers the shape of the pipes it has flowed through, the pressure of the valves it has passed, the hands that have touched it. It carries this memory in its chemistry, in its temperature, in the trace minerals it picks up as it seeps through the soil and filters through the Works. And when the people drink the water—and they drink nothing else; there is no other water in the Hollow—they are drinking the memory of the place, the history of every hand that has touched it, every voice that has spoken over it, every body that has been given to it in death.

This is what it means to live in the Hollow: to be part of a cycle that is older than any of them, to be held and carried and eventually absorbed, to become part of the water that remembers. It is not a sad thing, this life. It is not even a hard thing, in its way, though it is constant and demanding and wet. It is simply what it is: a place where the water falls and rises, where the Works breathes, where the bells ring their wrong-but-right hours, and where the people work and sleep and eat and love and die, all of them held in the hollow that holds them, all of them part of the slow and patient conversation of the water and the iron.

They are a quiet people, these hollow-folk. They are a wet people, and a patient people, and a people who have learned to live in a place that lives on its own terms. They are not rich, and they are not powerful, and they are not famous. They are simply there, in the bowl within a bowl, in the mist and the steam, and they are doing what they have always done, and they will do it for as long as the water falls and the Works breathes, and the place will hold them, and they will be content.

*The water remembers. The hollow holds.*
