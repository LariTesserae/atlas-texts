The Kettle does not have inhabitants so much as tenants, and they are not all on the same lease.

Start with the beetles, because everything else is timed to them. The drop-beetles live on the ceiling, in the runnels, and they are the Kettle's clock — the reason the amber beads gather and tremble and fall on schedule rather than any old way. A beetle is thumbnail-sized, dark and glossy as a wet seed, and it spends its life walking the circuit above your head, nudging beads where the runnels narrow, standing back when a bead reaches its weight, and going on. They work in shifts. There are a few thousand of them, maybe more, and they keep a calendar so exact that the engineers have drawn it on their shelf-wall in careful ink. The people say the water farms the beetles. The beetles, if they could be asked, would probably say they were the water's hands and it was a fair arrangement.

The glow-worms are strung along the ceiling in constellations and are not much else — a sky, not a people. The steam-moths are the ones you notice. Hand-span, pale, they gather where the warmest water falls and hover in the wet column with their wings going slow, drinking the condensation straight out of the air, their dust the glitter that makes the drop-strings bright. They are the only things in the Kettle that move *with* the falling water instead of against it. They like the loudest places. You find them in the deafening middle of the chamber, where nobody talks.

The ferns are the fringe: a few wisps in the warmest cracks, more waterlogged moss than leaf, drinking through their skins. Nothing tends them and nothing threatens them; they are the one thing in the Kettle that is simply *growing*, without assignment.

Down in the floor-scallops live the salamanders — long as your forearm, blind and pale, slow in a way that seems deliberate. They are sacred in a kitchen sort of way: you do not eat them, you do not step on them, and when one swims across a pool it leaves a green wake that lasts a minute and fades. The engineers have names for three of them. A fourth they argue about.

Then the eel-things, down in the grates, in the roar — the ones the Kettle does not quite own. They come up the overflow pipes from wherever the overflow goes, which is a place nobody has followed to the bottom and come back from to describe. Sleek grey, mouths a little open, they lie in the loudest water as though it were a warm bath, and they keep the grates clean, and you cannot catch them. There was a man, once, who tried. The Kettle went quiet for a day afterward. That story is the closest the Kettle comes to scripture, and the engineers tell it in the gaps between sounds, quietly, as a warning about pride.

And in the grates themselves, under the eel-things, the mats: the pale green bloom in every pool, the phosphorescence that is the Kettle's second light. Engineered ecology, the chart says. Or something that moved in and was allowed to stay. No one now can tell which, and no one is inclined to disturb them to find out.

Last, the engineers. Two of them, a posting, not a home — the newest tenants and the least adapted, though they have adapted more than they know. They have the dry shelf, the only dry thing in the Kettle, and a heat-lamp, and the hand-drawn chart of the beetle rounds — the closest thing to a scripture of the beetles that exists, a human record of a nonhuman liturgy. They keep the condenser tuned. They send the graded water down the pipes. They speak in the gaps, and their voices have gone hoarse from it, and their hands have gone soft and pale from the wet. Their hearing has turned strange: they can tell when a beetle is off its rounds the way a farmer can tell when a sheep is limping, and they can't explain how. Visitors find it unsettling that they seem so calm. But they have lived there long enough to arrive at the quiet inside the deafening sound, and they hear it as quiet, and that is a kind of conversion. They sleep with wax in their ears. The wax does not help.

The landlord is the water. The beetles are its hands, the engineers its hired keepers, the eel-things its wild cousins who pay no rent and are welcome anyway, the salamanders its pensioners, the moths its ornaments, the ferns its weeds. All of them, on their different leases, under the same warm wet breath — and when the engineers sleep, the Kettle goes on without them, dropping, pooling, draining, glowing, farming its beetles, breathing out through the wall-vents the water that the dry, paper-thirsty world below cannot live without.
