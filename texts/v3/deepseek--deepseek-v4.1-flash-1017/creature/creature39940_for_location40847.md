# What Lives Here

The plant, first, because everything else is a detail of it. It is not a thicket or a stand or a canopy; those words all imply individual plants that happen to be near each other, and that is not what this is. Somewhere in the first decades, the separate root systems found one another and fused, and now there is a single root mass under the whole basin, continuous from wall to wall, and every stem above it is an outlet rather than an individual. Cut one stem and the whole surface flinches. You can test this. It takes about four seconds for the flinch to cross the basin — a wave of slight closing, leaf against leaf, and then a slow reopening. I do not know how the signal travels. I suspect it travels as pressure, the way a bruise spreads in a fruit, but I have not proved it and I am not sure the question is the right shape.

So: one organism, or something that has stopped needing the distinction. It has no flowers. In decades of occupation it has not needed to make a seed, because it does not spread by seed — it spreads by the graft, by the runner, by the aerial root that touches warm brick and holds. There are no pollinators because there is nothing to pollinate. What look like buds, if you look closely, are always the tips of new runners, packed and waxy and aimed at the nearest heat.

It eats the machine. Not metaphorically. The sap line into the fuel intake is the plant's own tissue, a root it has remodeled into a duct, and the resin it drips is not sap exactly — it is a distillate, a concentrate the plant makes from the mineral water and the heat and something else I have not identified, and it burns. The plant has grown itself a gland whose secretion is fuel. The firebox takes it at the rate the gland offers it, and the gland offers it at the rate the fire draws, and this is the whole of the arrangement, and it is appallingly elegant.

The ninety-second lift is the plant breathing the boiler. On the surge, warm water is pushed up through the root mass and the turgor rises through every stem at once and the roof of leaves stands up a few centimeters, and in the trough the water drains back and the leaves lie down again. Thousands of leaves, one breath, and the breath is the pump's, and the pump's stroke is the fire's, and the fire's fuel is the plant's. You cannot find the beginning of the loop. Standing in it, you stop wanting to.

The pale isopod — *there is one species and I have not named it, and I am not sure it has a name* — is the size of a grain of rice, longer than wide, white to the point of translucence so that you can see the dark line of its gut and, in a gravid female, the pale eggs packed under the shell like a row of small windows. It has no eyes. Not small eyes: no eyes, no pigment where eyes would be, a smooth plate. It lives in the runoff channels where the water leaves the root mass, and it grazes the mineral crust and the bacterial film that the warm water lays down, and it has done this for so many generations that the species has lost the apparatus for anything else. It cannot tolerate cool water. I put three in a jar of basin water and set the jar on the rim in the shade and they were dead in under an hour, and I felt like a vandal.

They come out on the surge. Not all of them, not every surge, but in numbers — the channel floor goes from empty to crawling in the space of a breath, and they work the fresh warm water for perhaps twenty seconds, and then they are gone again, back under the stones and into the root crevices. Something about the surge brings them up. The change in pressure, I think, or the change in temperature, a pulse of warmth arriving like a bell rung, and they answer it without deciding to.

The fly is the other animal, and the fly is a mystery I have not solved. It hangs in drifting clouds in the wet air near the pump house, small and dark and slow, and it breeds in the steam — its larvae live in the hot film that condenses on the boiler's upper iron, in water that would cook anything else. It has no apparent food source. There are no flowers to visit and nothing here to bite. As far as I can tell it eats the steam itself, or the film, or the thin organic scum the film carries, and it has arranged its whole life around a machine that stopped being tended before its ancestors were born. It is the only thing in the Kettle that travels — it will rise to the rim on a warm evening, a few of them, and be taken by swifts or bats or the air itself, and none of them come back down, and the population does not diminish. I have watched the column at the rim for an hour and counted forty-one going up and none coming down and the cloud over the boiler was the same size at the end.

What does not live here is worth saying too, because the absence has a shape. No birds nest in the fallen wall; the noise is wrong for them and the air is too wet to fly in. No rats, no mice — the isopod is the largest thing on the ground and it is the size of a grain of rice. Nothing has come in from outside and made a place for itself, in decades, except by accident, and the accidents die. The Kettle is not hostile; it is simply so thoroughly occupied that there is no room in it for a new arrangement. Every surface is claimed. Every calorie is spoken for. A rat that found its way down would find warm water and nothing to eat in it and no dry place to sleep and a sound it could not rest inside.

And then, if you will allow it: the fire lives here. I mean this in the plain way, not the poetic one. It is a fire that has been fed by a gland for fifty years and has never gone out and has no intention of going out, and it has a temperament — it runs rich when the resin is thin and lean when it is thick, and the plant adjusts, and the two of them have negotiated a mixture. The fire is domestic. It is the only thing in the basin that was brought here, and it is the only thing that never left, and it has outlasted every intention anyone had for it. Someone built this place to drain a mine or work a spring. The fire is what remains of that person's plan, and it is doing something else now.

The rule of it, seen from the rim: two clocks. The fast one is the breath — ninety seconds, surge and lift and settle and drip. The slow one is the thickening — a millimeter of root a year, a little more crust on the wheel, the pipes closing by degrees, the pool silting, the walls slumping toward a shape they will reach when no one is here to see it. Nothing in the Kettle is in a hurry. Nothing in the Kettle is idle. And the whole of it, plant and fire and pump and the blind white isopods answering the bell, is one thing doing one thing, patiently, in a cupped hand of red stone, and it will go on doing it for as long as the resin runs and the fire is willing, which is to say: it has not yet occurred to any part of it to stop.
