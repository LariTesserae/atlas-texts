The people of Vaeless are few enough that you meet them as events rather than as a crowd. Call them Vaelessans if you need a word; they rarely do. They are human-shaped, rain-soft, ordinary at first glance—until you notice the tempo. They stand as though the ground has agreed to hold them. They walk without hurry but not without purpose; each step is placed, not drifted. Their voices are low, and because the wide pale streets carry sound farther than they should, low speech is not shyness but architecture. A question may sit between you for three breaths before anyone answers. The silence is not empty. It is the room the city has given.

They dress in the colors of the basin: undyed grey, reed green, the brown of wet bark, cloth that dries fast and does not shine. Sleeves are loose, hems short enough not to drink the rain. Many go barefoot on the warm-grey streets, which take the water down and give back a pleasant warmth. Their skin has the damp-napped look of the air; their hands rest easily; their eyes are dark and patient and do not dart. They smell of mineral water and warm stone and clean cloth dried indoors.

They do not labor in the way a town labors. The indefinite tech does the daily work, and the city keeps itself, so the people’s work is attention. They keep rain calendars—not to predict the weather, but to notice it. They walk the low ridges and trace the old shorelines in the pale rock, the way other people read the rings of a cut tree. They know which fig the walkway bends around, which well-court catches the morning light first, which corridor is longer inside than it should be. They do not prune the green back. They do not test the walls. They are amateur geologists of their own young land, water-watchers, hosts of small rooms.

The well-court is their public square. They arrive without appointment, sit on the moss-soft rim, and put their eyes on the water when the conversation runs out. They do not swim; the water is for the sky and for silence. The few children wade—loud, public, known to everyone and claimed loudly by no one. Adults do not hush them sharply. They lower their own voices, and the children learn the city’s quiet by osmosis. Hospitality is a shallow bowl of water, a place to sit, and no demand that you fill the quiet.

They accept the city’s strangeness as you accept stairs. A corridor twelve paces long where three should be is not a marvel; it is simply the house’s manner. A room larger than its skin is not a trick; it is where they keep the winter stores, or nothing, or a chair for someone who may come. They leave unused rooms unused. The emptiness is not a failure to them. It is courtesy—space kept for the city’s own generosity. They treat Vaeless the way a town might treat an old clock that has never needed winding: not sacred, not exactly alive, but not to be poked.

They share the basin with the waders at the well-courts, the small unbothered birds, the busy non-biting insects, and the horned grazer out on the floodplain. The grazer is avoided by custom, not fear: people give it the long way around and do not name it loudly. The green is not a garden but a neighbour, and the people have learned to walk around it because the city already does. They are not masters here. They are tenants who have read the lease and found it gentle.

What lives there, then, is not a mysterious race. It is a sparse, soft-footed, slow-spoken people shaped by room and rain and a city that asks nothing of them but courtesy. They are the least busy large animal in Vaeless. Their chief art is not building or mending but attending—letting the water hold the sky, letting the corridor be long, letting the fig keep its corner, letting the silence carry. They are mid-sentence too, and they seem content to be.
