The first thing you notice about the people of Oveth is that none of them stand still. Not nervously — it's slower than that, almost tidal. A pan-raker listening to a foreman will shift her weight from one foot to the other every minute or so, a small deliberate rocking, and if you watch the whole yard you see it happening everywhere at once, forty people swaying gently on their own feet like weed in a current. They do it without noticing. Standing still on the Sud means the crust begins to take an interest in you, and the people here have adapted by never quite giving it a surface to work on.

They are wet. This is the second thing, and it takes a lowlander longer to stop finding it strange. Oveth cloth is loose and pale and always damp; they dip their sleeves and their head-wraps in the warm threads before they go out, and the evaporation is the closest thing to air conditioning the plateau offers. Nobody in the town is ever dry, and nobody is trying to be. A visitor's first instinct — to find shade, to wipe the face, to get out of the wet — reads to them as a kind of panic. They will offer you a soaked cloth and be puzzled when you flinch from it. The cloth is the kindness.

Their skin has gone the color of the pans. Not burned, exactly — bleached and reddened in patches, the backs of the hands and the cheekbones roughened, and everywhere a faint pale rime in the creases, at the hairline, in the seams of the knuckles, that is not sweat but what the sweat has left behind when it went. Their hands crack and heal and crack again in a slow cycle and they've stopped treating it as an injury. Their hair goes stiff and pale early. They smell of sulfur only if you're new; after a week you stop registering it, and after a month you would find a lowland town unsettling for its emptiness of smell.

They drink constantly — warm water, flat and faintly mineral, drawn off the condensers and rationed by the canteen. It's the only thing on the plateau they don't have in surplus. They drink it in small amounts all day and they drink it at blood temperature and they will tell you, seriously, that cold water makes them thirsty.

Oveth is a town you walk, and the people are walkers — long unhurried crossings between the yard and the wellheads, boots crunching morning crust that has gone gelatinous by the time they come back. You learn to read them by their routes. The rakers keep to the terraces and move in lines, working a pan down and back in a rhythm that looks agricultural. The well crews travel alone or in twos, following pipes, and they navigate by numbers — they will say *eleven* and mean a wellhead a kilometer off in the fog, and they will know they are close because the throb in their feet has changed pitch. Some of the older ones can name a pump by its shudder the way a lowlander names a bird by its call. Ask them to point the way and they will look at you with mild pity and point at the ground.

They talk little, and when they talk they talk close. Sound dies in wet air; a conversation at ten meters is a rumor. So Oveth has built its language for short distances — the low voice, the face near yours, the hand on the shoulder to begin. And for the long distances it has the other thing: the gestures. Two fingers flicked at the wrist for *come*. A flat palm pressed downward for *wait*. A tap to the wellhead number on the belt. Visitors take a while to realize the people in the yard are talking across forty meters of open crust without a sound, and that the conversation is going fine. Children learn it before they learn to read. They also learn not to shout, and there is a particular softness to Oveth voices, a habit of speaking just above the mist's floor, that you can hear in a room full of them — a whole canteen murmuring like rain on a roof.

The veils follow them. This is the thing the town will not quite say out loud and clearly believes. A veil drifts on its own heat and its colony's business, but it also turns toward breath and to the warmth of a body standing in the column, and a person walking out to the far wellheads in the noon opening will often pick up two or three of them, hanging a little behind and above, trailing their filaments, keeping pace. The Oveth attitude to this is neither fear nor sentiment. It's the attitude of a farmer to weather that has been around a long time. You don't walk through a standing flock if you can go around; you don't brush them off your shoulder because there's nothing to brush; and if one settles over your face on a bad day you go home, and you don't talk about it at dinner.

The children are the only loud things in Oveth and they are not very loud. They have a game where they stand still on the crust, perfectly still, and see who can last longest before the ground starts to crust their boots — the winner, and there is always a winner, gets to be the one who is scraped off. They chase the crawlers, which is easy, and they are given the armored shells when the crawlers shed them, and the shells go pale and soft in a pocket and are thrown away and collected again. Mostly the children go out in the mist in the morning the way children everywhere go out in snow: to be in a world that has erased its own landmarks overnight.

The dead are in the sinter field beyond the calcining sheds, and it is the one place on the Sud where things grow. A body laid on the warm ground is not buried, because there is nothing to bury it in, and it is not burned, because the plateau would only take the smoke back. It is left, and the vents do the rest. Water runs over it and lays down its load, patient as a raker, and after ten years there is a shape under the mineral, and after forty the shape is a mound, banded rust and cream and copper, rounded like everything else here — and it is bigger than it was. Oveth people visit these. They know the stones by their bands. A grandmother is a lump of sinter with a shoulder in it, growing a millimeter a year, and in a place where every other surface is being dissolved and re-formed and taken away, this is the only accumulating thing, and the town finds that comforting in a way it doesn't try to explain.

What they are, in the end, is the plateau's most concentrated solution of itself — brine made into a working body, dosing itself all day with warm mineral water, crusted at the seams, talking softly at a range of six meters, reading the ground through its feet. They were not born to this and it is not a long history; a few generations, a company town that became a place. But the Sud has done to them what it does to a pipe left in the open — it has begun, gently and without malice, to make them part of the mineral order of things. They know it. They have arranged their clothes and their routes and their quiet around it. And on a still noon, when the mist opens and the town's whole nervous system of pipe and terrace is visible at once and the veils are standing in their columns and the light is bleaching everything to the same pale coral, the people out on the causeway look less like occupants than like one more band of grazers — slow, wet, patient, browsing a warm chemical world for what can be got from it, and asking the ground no questions it hasn't already answered.
