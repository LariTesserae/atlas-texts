The people of the Salt Spire are not citizens of a city so much as tenants of a vertical weather. There are only a few hundred of them, and they have adapted to the Spire the way a hand adapts to a tool it has held for generations: quietly, thoroughly, without much need to explain itself.

They are tall and spare, long-limbed in a way that makes them look built for ladders. Their skin is the color of the dust-sea at dusk, and their eyes are large, dark, and slow-blinking, shaped by the perpetual dimness of the lower tiers. They wear filters over their mouths—woven fiber, salvaged mesh, sometimes a folded scrap of old cloth stiff with salt—and goggles of smoked glass or hammered metal. Their hands are always covered. Their hair is cropped or wrapped. They smell of hot iron, brine, and the mineral grease they use on the pipes. When they speak, it is in low, short phrases, because the Spire is never silent and they have learned not to compete with it. A change in the hum is louder to them than a shout.

They measure distance in ladders and time in steam. A child learns to read the temperature of a pipe by touch before learning to read marks on stone. They know the Spire by sound: the gurgle of a healthy vein, the tick of a cooling terrace, the groan that means a joint is settling, the particular hush that comes before a valve fails. They move through the gantries with an unhurried certainty that can look like fatalism but is closer to expertise. They have nowhere else to be, and they know exactly where the weak plates are.

The engineer-priests are the closest thing the Spire has to a ruling class, though they would not use that word. They are mechanics who have inherited a liturgy of maintenance. They climb to the great open shaft near the peak, where the main engine glows dull orange and the smoke makes the city’s only cloud, and they coax the ancient turbines with ritual and repair. They listen to the bearings through metal rods. They anoint the joints with grease and chalk their readings on the basalt. They argue with the machine in low voices. Their prayers are torque and patience. Their heresy is neglect. They train apprentices by touch, hand over hand on a hot pipe, and they teach the same simple rule everyone knows: keep the engine running, and the Spire lives.

Around them are the pipewalkers and water-tappers, the salvagers who strip the abandoned upper terraces, the cooks who use old turbine housings as ovens, the children who run messages and scavenge and learn the ladders before they learn caution. The people live in clusters of low, flat-roofed rooms pieced together from salvaged plates and cast-off machine parts. They sleep behind curtains of filter cloth. They keep small stores of cool water in sealed jars. They do not go to the deep places. They say the water there is bad.

But sometimes a child finds a droplet of it on a pipe higher up—a bead of water from a broken vein, and in it a faint blue-green light. The child will hold it in their palm and look at it for a long time. It is a tiny, living star in a world of salt and stone and steam. They do not show it to the engineer-priests. They keep it until it dries.

Because the Spire has its own native life, older than the people and far more patient. In the deepest abandoned shafts, where the drills have broken into major veins, hot mineral water pools in the dark. There, a film of pale, glowing bacteria—or something like bacteria—clings to the rock in sheets of soft blue-green light. It feeds on heat and stone. It grows a millimeter a year. It does not need maintenance. It does not need to be coaxed. In the silence of the deep places, it is the only thing that is simply, quietly alive, and it is beautiful in a way that has nothing to do with comfort.

The people of the Salt Spire are thin, deliberate, salt-skinned, and few. They are keepers of a failing engine and children of a vertical world. They live in the hum. They listen for the change. And in their palms, now and then, they hold a droplet of the deep, glowing life that was there before them—and will be there after.
