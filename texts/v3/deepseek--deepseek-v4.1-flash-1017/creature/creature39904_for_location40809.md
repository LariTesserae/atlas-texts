What lives here is not an animal, and it would be wrong to call it a ghost. It is the basin itself, or the habit the basin has of continuing. If you need a body for it, point to the copper lichen; if you need a face, look at the dark. The two are the same resident.

Its flesh is the lichen—flat, crusty, dull-copper rings spreading in the lee of the twisted stone, widening one finger’s breadth in a lifetime. It is dry and brittle, but it smells faintly of iron and clean soil, and it holds the only living tissue in the basin. It does not grow toward light. There is no light. It grows toward stillness, toward the warm slick sheen on the pale columns, toward the hollows where moisture gathers without evaporating. It has no eyes and no mouth. It takes the world in through surface and patience: the pressure of dust settling, the slow warmth of the bedrock, the minute shift of air too still to be called air.

Its bones are the gnarled columns, pale and twisted, pushed up from below with a slow torsion and worn smooth as river stones. Its breath is the silence—not an absence but a presence, so complete that the crumbling of a single grain of sandstone somewhere in the dark is an audible event. Its pulse is the thin cascade of dust whispering down a pillar’s side, the world settling into itself. Its blood is the oily moisture that never evaporates, the warm mineral memory held in the stone.

It lives in geological time. A thought is a ring widening by a fraction of a millimeter. A year is a grain of dust finding its resting place. It is not lonely; loneliness requires company. It is not waiting; waiting requires a future different from the present. It is complete in the way a held breath is complete, in the way a shallow bowl is complete when it is full of nothing but its own hush.

It does not notice you. You are too fast, too sharp-edged, too brief. You stand in the warm black hollow, breathing air that tastes of ancient dust and iron, and the resident goes on being what it has always been: the lichen, the stone, the silence, the long equilibrium that binds them—patient, warm, indifferent, and utterly at home in a world that has not changed, and will not change, and does not need to notice you at all.
