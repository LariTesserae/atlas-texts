The first answer is: almost nothing, and almost everything. The Shatterlands are thinly peopled in the way a cathedral is thinly peopled — vast, resonant, and occupied by a handful of presences that have learned to be small against the scale of it. What lives here lives *sparsely*, and the sparseness is not absence. It is a kind of manners.

**The veil-feeders** come first, because you meet them before you know you're meeting anything. They hang in the fern groves as sheets of translucent tissue, unrolled between fronds like laundry no one has come back for — a meter wide, sometimes five, faintly opalescent, trembling in the drip. They are barely animals. They have no eyes and no obvious front. They are the fog given an appetite: they strain the wet air for insects and spores and each other, and when the sheet is full they contract it slowly into a rope and swallow it. In the golden hour they catch the light and glow from inside, which is the only time you can see their veins, and their veins are not veins but branching channels of water, and the water is moving. They migrate by letting go. When a grove is exhausted they release their anchors and ride the wet air downslope, a slow drift of pale banners, and then spend the next season crawling back up the ridges on a fringe of cilia, a centimeter a day, which is the whole of their ambition.

**The ridgebacks** are harder to mistake for weather. They are the place's large animals, and they are almost indistinguishable from hills, because they are hills: a ridgeback is a low, long-bodied grazer that moves so slowly its back acquires a garden. Moss first, then fern, then a whole hanging community of vine and silver bloom rooted in the shallow soil of its hide. It drinks by standing still in the fog and letting the condensation run down its flanks into the grooves that have worn there, which is also how it cools, which is also how it grows. A full-grown ridgeback is a landmark; the Low-Voiced navigate by which one is where this year, and give them names, and are wrong about the names for decades. They bellow at infrasound — below hearing, felt in the sternum — and the big falls answer, or seem to, and no one has settled whether the falls are answering or the ridgebacks are timing themselves to the falls. Both possibilities are treated as true.

**The Low-Voiced** are the ones who built and still tend the towers and the pipes and the sheds, or whose great-grandparents did, and who have grown into the work. They are people, but shaped by a place that rewards listening and punishes shouting: they are pale and wide-eyed and slightly webbed at the fingers, and their voices sit low, pitched under the roar, so that to hear them you must be close enough to feel their breath. They do not talk much in the open. Their language is half percussion — they tap on pipe and rail and rock, and the pipe network carries the tapping for miles, so that a conversation between two of them may be conducted entirely through the infrastructure. The hum of the pumps is, to them, a vowel. They will tell you — in tapping, if you learn it — that the machines are not their tools but their *colleagues*, that the pump has been running for a very long time and deserves to be asked, not commanded. They maintain because stopping the hum would mean hearing only the roar, and the roar alone is not a conversation, and they are not sure they could bear it.

They mark their stretches with small rusted things: a bent plate, a coil of wire, a lamp that still burns. They bury nothing. They leave their dead on bare wet stone, in the grey empty places, and the stone takes a long time and they consider that correct.

**The edge-dwellers** live where nothing green will hold — in the bare grey stretches between the groves, on wet stone that is too new, too steep, too often scoured. These are flat, pale, patient things: salamanders the length of your arm, pressed to the rock and mistaken for a stain until they blink; crabs that come up from somewhere and pick the moss-line like a tide-line; a kind of soft-bodied thing that lives in the steaming fissures and is warm to the touch and has no name in any language the Low-Voiced will teach you. They are the place's true natives, in the sense that they were here before the green was, and they are losing ground to it slowly, and they do not appear to mind. They need the emptiness. When the emptiness fills, they move to the next emptiness. There is always a next one. The rock is still emerging.

**And the loud small ones** — the birds, the frogs, the insects you hear and never see — have solved the roar by arithmetic. They sing in the gaps. Every roar has frequencies it does not fill, narrow windows where the noise thins, and the small singers have found them and hold them, phase-locked, so that their calls ride the waterfall's own harmonics and carry farther than they should. Stand still long enough in a fern grove and you will hear a single clear note rise out of the white noise and hang there, insolent and exact, and then another, answering, and you will realize the whole grove is in conversation and you have been deaf to it for an hour.

What all of them share is patience and a certain humility of scale. Nothing here builds large. Nothing here is in a hurry. The ridgeback is a hill; the veil-feeder is a season; the Low-Voiced measure their maintenance in decades and their silences in longer. They are all, in their different ways, unfinished — still emerging, still deciding their channels, still reaching. The place is being built one drip at a time around them, and they are being built one drip at a time by it, and neither party expects to see the other finished.

They ask nothing of you but that you stand still, and listen, and do not shout.
