**The floor.**

Start with the ones you will not see, because there are millions of them and they are the color of the stone.

They are discs — palm-sized, dinner-plate-sized, horny and flat, the grey-green of wet liver, lying flush against the bedrock under a film of water. They have no front. They creep in any direction, and the mouth is a ring on the underside, and it is never not working, rasping the felt down to the clean stone and moving on before the felt forgets. You can stand in a place all day and never know that the ground is grazed.

But step. The shock goes out and every disc within thirty meters slams down at once — a click, and another, and another, a ring of clicks taking off ahead of your ripple and overtaking it and running past it into the grey, then coming back off the stakes, then coming back again. The floor shuts like a fist and opens. That is the first evidence that anyone lives here, and you have already given it your position.

Between them and under them is the felt itself, which is not a plant and not a scum but a city — layered, ranked, with its own traffic, worms no thicker than a hair moving through it the way a rumor moves through a town. The felt is the Pan's real population. Everything else is a thin bright aristocracy that eats it.

---

**The column.**

The water is thicker than it looks, and what makes it thick is mostly alive.

The pale silt you take for ground stone is perhaps a tenth of it — a fine snow of small bodies that do not sink. They will not sink; they spend energy not sinking; they hold themselves up in the convection cells like people treading water in a flooded basement. The whole sheet is a slow upward rain into a sky that keeps swallowing it. Everything in the column is either the snow or the thing that eats the snow or the thing that eats that, and the eating happens in the same water that the eating happens in, so that nothing here is ever quite clean.

The eaters are sieves. Bags of gauze, hand-sized, headless, with a ring of muscle to hold them open; the water goes through and the snow stays. They live a day. There are so many at once that the water can never clear — they strain the snow and pass it back as snow, and the standing crop of the water is a steady state of being eaten. When you wade, you break a thousand of them without feeling it, and the breakages feed the ones behind you.

---

**The shafts.**

Around the rims of the wells the reeds stand, chest-high and noisy, and in the shafts themselves is the oldest continuous life in the Pan.

It is pale and about the size of a dog, and it is soft, and it is blind the way a hand is blind in a pocket. It lives in the shaft wall, in a burrow it does not leave, and it rides the well's breath: up a hand's breadth over an hour, down again, its whole life oscillating in a column of hot sulfurous water that has never changed temperature and never will. It grazes the mineral crust. It has been doing this since before the mountains it drained existed. It has no other trick and needs none. It is the only thing here that lives in a place that does not change, and it is therefore the least interesting creature in the Pan and the most successful.

The strange part is that these are also the things that bring the objects up. Not on purpose. The well breathes and the rider rises, and whatever has come down the delay line to that shaft — a splinter of painted boat, the wing of a bird from a cold country, a wooden doll worn round and featureless — comes up in the rider's fur or against its flank and is left on the rim when the breath goes out. You will find the rider's leavings at every well. No one has ever explained the boat.

---

**The readers.**

Something understands the delay, and it is not you.

It is flat and wide and red-brown, the size of a hat, with legs like fingers and a pair of taut membranes along its back, and it does not hunt. It waits. It lies at the lip of a well with its membranes slack and it listens to the layers — the same sound arriving ten times from ten directions, smeared by the shallow water, and it can tell, from how the layers are stacked, what made the noise, how big it was, how far, and which shaft the noise will come up. Then it goes there and it waits, and whatever arrives it eats, and it has been eating this way for a very long time.

Which means it is also listening for you. It heard your footsteps an hour ago and it is already walking, unhurried, in a direction you cannot calculate, and it will be at the well you are standing beside before you have finished deciding that you do not like this. The Pan has one predator and it hunts the past. You are always already found.

---

**The waders.**

The herons are the only things here with a silhouette, and they have no shadow, because there is no sun to cast one.

They are grey and they stand, and standing is the whole of their technique, because the Pan punishes motion and rewards stillness: a heron that does not move is a heron the delay cannot place. They wait for hours. The water goes around their legs and does not ring. Then they strike, and the strike is over before the ripple has begun — and the ripple, when it comes, arrives everywhere at once and tells nothing.

They are mute. Every heron in the world calls; these do not. To call in the Pan is to be answered by everything, and to be answered by everything is to learn that your voice is not yours. The sound goes out, is returned, is reflected and returned again, until the Pan is full of your own call and you cannot find the original in it. So the Pan's herons have stopped, generation by generation, and the silence they keep is not the silence of patience. It is the silence of something that has been argued with.

---

**The stakes.**

The stakes are the only vertical thing for a thousand kilometers, and everything that needs a fixed point comes to them.

There is a creature — small, hard, ugly, with a hooked foot — that lives its whole life in the lee of one stake. It does not travel. It will not leave the stake even to feed, and it feeds on what the wind piles against it, and when the stake finally wears to a nub and the water takes it, the creature stays where the stake was for a while, hooked to nothing, and then it goes. It is the most pitiable animal in the world's basement, and it is exactly the same shape of thing as a person keeping a hearth.

The reeds crowd the lee of the stakes too, and the herons nest there, and the whole line of forty runs east for a kilometer and stops, pointing at a thing that has been erased. Whatever the stakes were for, the thing they referred to is gone. The stakes remain. Everything alive on this plain has organized itself around a mistake.

---

**The under-felt.**

Press your palm to the stone where it is thinnest and it gives, very slightly, the way a bruise gives.

There is something down there. The felt is thickest where the bedrock is thinnest, and under the felt, in the last warm millimeter between the rock and the water, there is a sheet of living matter no thicker than paint, and it goes on — under the clamps, under the stakes, under the wells, under you — and no one has found its edge, and it may be one thing. It may be the largest single organism in the world, lying flat at the bottom of it, spread across the sump where every mountain has come to rest, breathing heat up through the lens of the stone. It has no name and does not need one. It is the floor, and the floor is alive, and you have been standing on it.

---

**The keeper.**

Somebody keeps the ring of fired brick, and the floor is dry.

They are not old and not young; they have been worn to the same grey value as the water and they do not cast a shadow either. Their clothes have not dried in years. The felt grows on their boots and they let it, and once a season they scrape it. They do not speak — not from sorrow, from method; speech here is spending. They write, when they need to, on the stone with a nail.

The hearth is kept, but low, because fire is the only thing in the Pan that is dry and directional and casts anything, and a hearth is a fixed point, and a fixed point is how you know where you are. That is the job. It is not clear who they keep it for. They tell the hour by which well is high, since the wells are out of phase and the whole Pan is a clock that runs on breath. On the shelf inside the wall, on the only dry ledge in the world, they keep the things that arrived: the doll, the boat splinter, the wing of a bird from a cold country. They cannot say why they keep them. They keep them because they came, and because everything that comes to the bottom of the world comes to them eventually, and somebody should be here when it does.

---

**The count.**

So: the discs, in their millions, invisible, clicking under your foot. The snow, holding itself up. The sieves, eating the snow and becoming it. The riders in the shafts, older than mountains. The reader, patient, already on its way. The herons, mute, striking before their own ripple. The hook-footed thing in the lee of a stake that no longer points at anything. And under all of it the flat warm sheet of a single organism, or a hundred million, spreading to the edge of finding-out.

And one person, keeping a dry floor and a low fire, in a place with no shadows and no direction, listening to the wells breathe, being answered by everything whenever they forget themselves and speak.

And you, breathing it in, growing it on your boots, standing on the living floor, your reflection half a second late.
