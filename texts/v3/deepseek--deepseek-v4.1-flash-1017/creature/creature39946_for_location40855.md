What lives in the Low Works is not a town, not a colony, not a wilderness population. It is a maintenance people and a maintenance ecology, pressed together until the line between them is mostly a matter of grammar.

The people—call them tenders, sump-keepers, the Low—are few. You might walk a sector for a day and meet three. They are small and low-slung, broad through the feet and hands, with gray-pink skin that never quite dries. Their toes splay to grip grating; their palms are creased and tough from warm metal. Their eyes are large and dark and slow, built for cloudy-noon light, and they blink often, not from glare but from the constant bead of condensation on their lashes. They have fine, colorless bristles where other people have hair, and they smell of hot metal, mineral water, and the same faint musk as the fauna. They wear harnesses of cable and polymer, tool loops, sealed vials, scrapers, siphon bulbs. Boots are rare: water gets in, cultures grow, and the grating is warm anyway. Bare feet are how they read the floor.

They live in dry pockets—service galleries under the pipe banks, rooms where the condensation is diverted into a gutter and the air is only damp instead of saturated. Their beds are hammocks of baffle cloth slung between cable trays. They sleep in shifts, because the Works has a low hum under the floor, and when the hum changes pitch they wake. Not loudly. They wake the way a hand wakes when a fly lands on it.

Their day is not a day. There is no sun, no tide, no season. Their calendar is maintenance: the drift of rain cells across sectors, the tick of thermal expansion in a long pipe, the interval between mat blooms in a sump. They measure time in cycles of scraping and checking, in the slow passage of a condensation front behind a ridge. They do not say *tomorrow* so much as *after the next cell*. They do not celebrate harvests. They celebrate flow—a drain that ticks clean, a seam that holds, a sump that stays at its level. The only sin is clogging. The only virtue is keeping the water moving.

They eat what the Works grows: sump shrimp netted in a sieve, bacterial loaves grazed from warm trays before the mats thicken, mineral cakes precipitated from a slow drip. They do not cook much; the air is warm enough, and heat is everywhere. They eat quietly, in twos and threes, sitting on the edges of channels with their feet in the water. They have gut symbionts—engineered, inherited, or both—that let them live on the Works’ chemistry as much as on its animals. They are not separate from the food web. They are grazers and cleaners in it. When they scrape a film from a pipe, they are eating the same thing the crawlers eat, only with tools.

They speak low. Their voices are soft and clicked at the edges, and they use touch more than sound: a palm pressed to a pipe to feel the hum, a knuckle tapped on a flange, a hand laid on a shoulder to say *wait*. They can taste a change in the water—copper, rust, a bloom starting—and they will spit and point. They have names for sectors that are mostly smells: the sweet-ceramic pan, the copper-stain channel, the sump that smells of clean steam and old shrimp. They do not shout. The place does not reward shouting. Sound is absorbed by wet surfaces and fibrous baffles, and the fauna do not call, so a raised voice is a kind of violence.

The hand-things live with them. You have seen them: small, pale, many-jointed, working at the seams of the ceramic, retreating when the water is disturbed. The tenders do not call them pets. They call them the seamers, or the neighbors, or sometimes just *them*. There is a truce. The tenders do not scrape where the seamers have sealed; they leave small cakes of mineral paste at the mouths of the cracks. In return, the seamers keep certain seams tight, or at least keep them occupied, and they do not crowd the intakes. It is not friendship exactly. It is a shared understanding that the Works has many kinds of hands.

The overwhelming majority of the residents are not people at all. They are the skimmers in sheets over the water, the blind crawlers under the gratings, the soft-bodied grazers on the warm pipework, the filter-feeders hanging in the mist, the shrimp in the sumps. The tenders are a thin layer on top of that density, and they know it. They keep the fauna from clogging the works; the fauna keep the tenders from becoming a clog themselves. The people are not masters of the Low Works. They are one of its maintenance species, the one that carries tools and tells stories.

And they do tell stories. Not of seasons or migrations, because there are none. They tell of the Makers, who laid the first ceramic and set the first pipe, and who are not expected back. They tell of a seam that held through a bad cell, of a sump that almost dropped, of a hand-thing that sealed a crack no one else could reach. They tell these stories in the dry pockets, over the tick of water into a drain, while the rain falls in soft steady cells outside and the warm air pools in the low pans. The stories do not change much. Nothing here is becoming. The people are young in their surfaces—newly made, maybe, or newly arrived, or newly grown—and old in their behavior, held steady by warmth and chemistry and the low hum under the floor.

When a tender dies, there is nowhere to bury. The dead are committed to a sump, and the fauna and the water and the mineral skin take them back into the Works. Nothing decays into soil; there is no soil. They are eaten, flushed, scraped, and the level stays the same. The tenders do not find this grim. It is the same thing that happens to a dead skimmer, a shed crawler shell, a bloom grazed back before it thickens. The Works is a mouth, and they are inside it, and they are also one of the things that keeps it clean.

So if you ask who lives in the Low Works: a few quiet, pale, broad-footed people in harnesses, who read the floor with their feet and the pipes with their palms; the small many-jointed seamers who work the ceramic in exchange for mineral paste; and beneath and around and above them, a continent of soft-bodied fauna, layered in the water and the air, making almost no sound. The place is saturated with life and held in equilibrium, and the residents are not outside that equilibrium. They are part of the machinery. They leave footprints in the shallow water that close behind them almost at once, and the rain keeps falling, and nothing else changes.
