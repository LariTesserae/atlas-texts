# The Rack-Dwellers

## Body

They are small and wide, and they were made by water. Not by the sea exactly — by its habits. A person low to the ground and broad through the hips is a person a wall of water moving without a wave on it will shove against and not over. Their feet are long and splayed, the toes almost as useful as fingers, and they walk flat-footed and rolling, the way you walk on a floor you do not entirely trust. Their hands are the biggest thing about them. The skin of the palms is thick as hide and the webbing is thin and pale enough that a person can hold a hand up against the Lid and see the shadow of the bone inside it.

Their color is not the bloom's color. They are grey-green, or the color of the wet inside of a shell: the stone's color, the sediment's color, a little translucent at the wrists and the throat. Their hair comes in thin and colourless, and most of them keep it short or bound up tight, because hair left lying gets covered, and a comb comes back furred. The vain ones wear it long anyway and are forever sweeping it with their fingers, and this is a recognized joke.

Their eyes are pale — a washed amber, a milky grey — and they are not good at far things. There is no middle distance in their world. There is the arm's length where everything happens and there is the enormous useless plain of the Flat, and their eyes are for the first, and for reading the Lid. They can name eleven shades of white. They are poor at blue. The damp is in them so thoroughly that they smell of crab-shell and the fermented paste, all of them, and so it is not a smell at all to them; it is what a person smells like.

## Hands and Names and Voices

A Rack-dweller's name is not a sound. It is a sequence of pressures, worked out over a child's first year, changed and argued over, and finally fixed at a gathering where every adult in the cluster takes the child's palm in turn and presses it into the root of the hand. After that, meeting someone means taking their hand and pressing your name and receiving theirs, which makes greeting a small negotiation and makes politics tactile. Who presses hard. Who presses first. Who does not press at all.

Because they are always touching — warning, greeting, lifting, carrying — touch is not intimacy here. Touch is the weather. What is intimate is speech. A voice does not carry in this place; the chalk drinks it, and a murmur at three paces is gone by ten. So the loudest thing in a Rack-dweller's life is a low voice at arm's length, close enough to feel on the skin. To be spoken to is to be singled out. Public life is silent and runs on the hands: a plaza full of people talking is a slow dance of fingers, and children sign before they speak and speak badly for years.

They sing with the mouth almost against someone, into the jaw or the shoulder, one listener at a time. There are no songs that a group can sing. This is not felt as a loss.

The coldest thing one person can do to another in the Rack is refuse the hand. Feuds run that way — a name not pressed back, a palm withheld in a doorway — and it is possible to live forty paces from someone for twenty years and never touch them, and everyone knows, and nobody says.

## The Carried

No child in the Rack has ever been put down.

An infant cannot keep itself from being covered, so an infant is never set aside, and the sling of scraped hide goes from arm to arm all day, shaken out at every handover, and by evening the folds of it are faintly lit and the whole cluster has held the child. There is no word for a mother alone with a baby. Ask who a child's parents are and you will get a list, some of it argued over.

The old are handled nearly as much, and for the same reason. Stiffness is how the bloom gets you, so the oldest people in the town are its busiest: they sweep. Every cluster keeps sweepers, out before they are properly awake, going over the same twenty paces of foundation stone. The stone does not need it. The sweeper does. A broom is a good thing to hold when the hands are failing, and the sweepers' stretch of floor is the polishedest thing in the world. Their grown children take their palms and press their names into them, slowly, over and over, to remind them who they are.

## Roofs

Vanity goes into the roof. The lattice is the household's face — broken on purpose, hide and shell and stone, and the pattern of it is remembered and copied and stolen, and there are famous roofs that people walk half a morning to look at. A well-built roof lays a shape on the floor, and the shape swells and dims with the Lid, and the children of the house learn the hour by their own patch of light. A dark house is a scandal. It says something about you. The poor have coarse lattices, and the truly poor live under hides like a wrapped tool.

## Work, and Pride

They knap chert and grind axes on wet sand and are prodigiously good with cord — sinew, gut, hide cut in one long spiral. Everything here is tied. Nets for the pools, slings, hawsers for dragging a house down before the flood takes it, and the same rope skill lets them pull a wall apart, carry it a hundred paces and set it on new footings between one flood and the next.

They hammer copper cold because they have never melted anything, and they do not miss fire. They eat the paste and are proud of it, and guests get the good paste, which is only slightly less bad. They cook almost nothing and consider most smells of cooking to be a sign that something has gone wrong.

And they know what their own name for their town means: *the thing that holds the shape while it sets.* A form, a jig, a mold. Something you build, use, and knock apart. They know every house will be taken down and every footing built over, and they know that in the deepest layers there are notches worn past reading, and their attitude to that is neither grief nor reverence but an old, dry, working familiarity, like a scar you have stopped noticing.

## The Herd

The herd is blind, warm, huge, and knows where the vents will open. The town follows it and brushes it. A beast cannot reach its own back, and the bloom gathers there in a thick sweet pelt, so people walk alongside with brushes and baskets and the beasts lean into it and will slow for a person with a brush and not for one without. Bloom off a herd-beast is better than bloom off the Lid. It is the only thing the Rack has ever traded with anything, and the herd does not know it is trading.

Now and then a beast stops. The herd flows around it and leaves it, and it lies down on the Flat and is covered and begins to glow, and in twenty years it is a low patient fire the size of a cart. The town does not visit the herd's dead. There are two kinds of glowing dead out there and only one of them is anybody's grandmother.

## The Still

There is one thing the town genuinely fears, and it is not flood. It is the person who sits down.

Children who sit too long are nudged up and given something to hold. Grief and sickness are known by the hands — *she has gone quiet-handed* — and the cure is work, and company, and being passed around. Hands are laid on a person in trouble for hours. It is not subtle and it is not meant to be.

Very rarely, someone stops on purpose. They do not explain and the town does not prevent them, though it argues, and the arguing is the loudest thing that ever happens there. The hands are laid on for a long time and then taken away. It is understood as a kind of leaving that leaves the body behind as a lamp, and it is spoken of in a particular tone, quietly, and not often.

The ordinary dead are carried out past the last houses and laid on the Flat. A year and they are dusted at the edges; two and they are furred; then they are a soft full glow and they stay that way. There are acres of it. Walk out into the dark with a direction in mind and you will find your grandmother. Everyone can point to theirs. It is the only landmark in the Rack that is not a ruin, and the only writing that does not wear out — so that the oldest lights, the ones nobody can name anymore, are the town's true archive, and going out to look at something you cannot read is a thing people do on purpose, and call it reading.

## Weather, and What They Are Like

They count years by the water, not by number. *The year the plaza opened. The year of the three floods. Two grandfathers back.* They measure small time by the bloom — a knuckle of it, a hand's depth of it — and they measure nothing by the sun, and if you told them about the sun they would ask what it was for.

They are not solemn. They are relentlessly, cheerfully busy, and the busyness is not devotion; it is husbandry, and it is also gossip and quarrelling and a hundred small vanities. They quarrel over footings and pool rights and whose turn it is to scrub the old stones. They laugh with their mouths wide and hardly any sound comes out. They are fatalists with excellent manners and a low opinion of anyone who stands still to be admired.

Their one proverb on the subject of everything: *Sweep, and be swept after.*
