The Throat is not empty. It is tenanted by the **chit**—and by the film they eat, and by the sound they make, which is almost a third inhabitant.

The chit are small: two fingers long, plated, pale as the inside of a shell, six-legged, flat enough to vanish into a crack the width of a coin. They are cold and slow. Individually they would be easy to overlook. Together they are the wall. Where they cluster they lie three and four deep, near-touching, a saturation of small bodies that turns the rock into a moving surface. You do not see one chit and then another; you see the surface breathe in patches.

They live on the film—the lichen, the mineral skin left behind when the condensation dries. There is no other food up here, so they are also each other’s food. One will take another without ceremony, and the wall will close over the gap in a minute. Their ecology is a loop with only one strand in it: rock, film, chit, chit. They are not cruel about it. They are simply the only available meal.

And they drum. Each one keeps a little shard of the ringing stone gripped in its forelimbs—not carries, *keeps*; each has its piece and does not let go—and at intervals it lifts the shard and strikes it against the wall. The strikes run in waves along the surface, one part of the wall answering another, and the sum is a constant chiming rattle that fills the crack from bottom to top. You cannot find the rhythm. There is a rhythm. You can feel that there is one the way you can feel that a language is a language without speaking it. To the chit, in the dark, it may not be music at all. It may be closer to weather, or to grammar—a way of knowing that the wall is inhabited and where the inhabited parts are.

They use things, and this is the strangest part. They carry pebbles. They move small objects from one ledge to another and leave them arranged. Nothing is built. There is no structure in the Throat that was put there by intent. But the ledges are sorted, and the sorting is not geological. It is the chit, patient, one pebble per lifetime, a slow gradient of arrangement accumulating across durations no single one of them lives to see. No chit is an architect. No chit sees the pattern whole. The pattern is what the population remembers instead of building.

At night the chit go quiet in layers, top first, then down. The drumming thins and stops, and what is left is the drip and the ring of the last few strikes fading in the crack. In the morning the cloud returns, the walls sweat, and the whole thing starts again.

So what lives there is a cold, pale, chiming population, pressed into the young rock, eating the film and each other, striking stone against stone, and sorting pebbles across centuries they will never see finished. They do not look up. They are not waiting for you. They are doing the slow work of a place that has only just started.
