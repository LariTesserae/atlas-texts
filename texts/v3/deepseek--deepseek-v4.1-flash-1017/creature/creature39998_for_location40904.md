Nothing lives here, you think at first. That is only because you are looking for a body with a face, a thing that eats and sleeps and dies in the usual order. The Weeping Plateau does not arrange its inhabitants that way. Its residents are tenancies more than creatures: a machinery that has learned to persist, a vegetation that has learned to feed on persistence, and a waiting that has learned to look like a place.

**The engines are the first residents.** Not the towers alone—the towers are only their visible bones—but the whole embedded assembly of pipes and vents and rotating masses sunk into the basalt shelves. They are colonies as much as machines. A single tower is a stratigraphy of repairs, old metal swallowed by newer metal, concrete fused to stone, indicator lights blinking in sealed rooms like slow, patient eyes. They do not move across the plateau; they extend through it. They grow by accretion. A pipe added here, a vent widened there, a shelf reinforced with a brace that is already half-incorporated into the rock. Their metabolism is water and mineral and pressure. They drink the seep, they exhale steam, they shed heat into the wet air. Their senses are not sight or hearing but tolerance and flow: a blocked line, a temperature drift, a vibration that travels wrong through the stone. A fault in one tower is felt three shelves away as a kind of stomachache. They speak in hums too low to be called sound. They have no purpose you can name, but they have habits, and the habits are very old.

**The green things are the second residents.** Moss first: sheets and pillows of it, deep green and nearly black where the wet gathers, upholstering every horizontal surface until the stone looks like something that has been made comfortable. Then the ferns, enormous and unhurried, unfurling from vertical cracks and hanging down the cliff faces like green waterfalls that have forgotten how to fall. Then the woody shrubs in the deeper seams, their roots crawling over the rock like veins, their pale blossoms opening and closing on a rhythm nobody has charted. They are not decoration. They are tenants with grips. They clot the cracks; they pry the seams; they drink the mineral-bright water and whatever the vents breathe out. They have no pollinators and no herbivores and no need for either. They reproduce by spore and runner and patience. They are the plateau’s soft tissue, the only things here that die in the ordinary way—a frond browning, a moss drying at its edge—and even that death is absorbed, broken down, returned to the seep. They live inside the engines as much as beside them. A fern rooted in a warm vent seam is not trespassing. It is being housed.

**The water is not exactly an inhabitant, but it is the medium in which the inhabitants know each other.** It weeps from the cliffs, pools in the cupped hands of the shelves, climbs the stone as often as it falls. It carries mineral messages from one shelf to the next. It trembles when the engines turn. It makes the air heavy enough that sound arrives with a body. Without it, the plateau would be only rock and rust. With it, the place has a bloodstream.

**And there is the absence, which also lives here.** The paths that connect nothing. The doors, some sealed, some open onto corridors of indicator light and moving steam. The repairs layered on repairs. Whatever made those things is not visible—no people, no animals, no keepers with lanterns. But the making persists as habit: a clearance maintained, a door left open, a vent cleared of moss, a brace added before the old one fails. The builders have been absorbed into the built world. Their intentions survive as tolerances and routines. That is a kind of life too—a purpose that outlives its hands and keeps opening doors for no one.

Together these residents form a single tenant, though it has no center. The engines are its organs; the plants are its soft tissue; the water is its blood; the paths are its nerves, though they lead nowhere obvious. It is not conscious in any way you would recognize. If it thinks, it thinks in thresholds and flow rates, in pressure and waiting. It is industrious and purposeless. It maintains itself because maintenance is what it does. It flowers because the vents are warm. It hums because silence has been engineered out.

If you want to meet one of its inhabitants, stand beside a vent where a fern has rooted in the warm seam. The fern is an inhabitant. The engine beneath it is an inhabitant. The water climbing the fern’s root is the same water that sweats from the cliff and pools in the shelf’s shallow hand. You cannot tell where one ends. The plateau does not offer you a population. It offers you a symbiosis that has settled into itself so completely that it no longer looks strange. It waits. It has always waited. The waiting is one of the things that lives here.
