**The holt-thing**

You will probably never see one whole, and it would disappoint you if you did. The animal is soft, the length of your hand, the colour of the inside of a wrist, and it has no eyes and no shell and no speed whatsoever. What you see, if you kneel at a broken wall and let your eyes adjust, is the lip of a cell: a ring of wet tissue, pale rose going grey at the edge, opening and closing at about the rate of a sleeping breath.

That is the front end. The rest of it is the wall.

Each one sits in a chamber it has made and will not leave. It has a siphon it can push out into the channel-water and a mantle that lays lime, and it does the two things alternately, all its life, which is a long one. Draw water, sieve the fine brown floc out of it, close, and lay down a course along the front edge of the chamber — a fraction of a millimetre of chalk, wet and soft, going hard as it cures. Then extend. Then draw again. The animal advances through the mound the way a slow worm goes through cheese, always at the leading edge, always sealing the room behind it, and the rooms behind it fill and harden and become, in a century or two, the fabric. The thing you are leaning on is not its house. It is its shed work. The living population is always at the newest, wettest surface, and the deep chambers are empty, and the city is a shell that its inhabitants have been leaving, generation on generation, for longer than the shell is thick.

They are warm. Not much — two or three degrees above the air, the heat of very slow burning, peat oxidized in a wet dark. In the cold season you can see it: the mounds smoke, faintly, a thin steam off the south faces at dawn that is gone by the time the light is properly up. Stand close to a wall in winter and it is like standing near an animal.

**The colony**

An individual is not quite the unit. Where two of them meet, the tissue joins through pores in the lime, and a wet film runs between them, and through that film passes chemistry — slowly, at the speed of diffusion through a sponge. An alarm takes hours to cross a mound. Cut a seam and nothing flinches; but by evening the siphon rate has changed everywhere on that face, all of them drawing a little less, a little more often, as if the whole accretions had decided something. Nothing has decided anything. There is no place in a holt-thing where a decision could be made. There is only a great many of them, joined, responding at the pace of a wet thing to a change in the water.

This is what the closeness is, the thing you feel in the narrow passages when both walls are warm and you are certain, without evidence, that you are not alone in the room. You are not. The walls are population. There are more of them in arm's reach of you than there are people in a market, and every one of them is asleep in the only sense it has — open, drawing, laying, closed — and none of them has ever been in a hurry.

**The making**

They bud. A colony under good water puts out daughters into the space ahead of itself, and the space ahead is whatever the water has left open: a slumped roof, a rotted seam, the lee of an older buttress. Where a channel scours, nothing settles. Where a channel slows, they settle, and then the channel is slower, and then it is a pool, and then it is a chamber with a floor of lime. This is the whole of their engineering and it is not engineering. It is a preference, repeated. Wet enough, warm enough, slow enough — lay lime.

Fragments travel, too. Bits of tissue torn off in a thaw drift in the channels and die in most of them and settle in a few, and a new mound begins as a grey smudge on a stone that nobody planted. This is how the Tarnholt is continental: not by marching outward but by seeding every wet hollow in it at once, and by the fact that the marsh is always making new wet hollows.

And the weirs. You will have noticed them before you understood them — low crescent dams of reed and lime, hundreds of them, holding the water at a level. A colony that happens to grow across a flow holds more food; a colony that holds more food grows faster and seals the gap; the ones that grow the other way are scoured and starve. Repeat that for ten thousand years and you get crescents, exactly the right height, in exactly the right places. The builders wanted the water at that level in the way that water wants to run downhill. There is no plan in it. There is an exactness in it that you could mistake for a plan, and that mistake is the most interesting thing about the place.

**What dies**

A holt-thing dies when the water leaves it — when a seam above it opens and the chamber drains, or a terrace slumps and the siphon is left sucking air. Then the tissue goes grey and then brown and then the moss comes.

The moss is the second population, and it is not scenery. It arrives on the dying wall within a season, first as a green bloom in the seam, and it does what moss does: holds water against lime, breathes out acid, mines the chalk for the slow grey lace of itself. Where it works, the wall goes soft. Where the wall goes soft, the water gets in. Where the water gets in, a hand's width of channel widens, and a chamber becomes a hollow, and the mound slumps into the marsh that made it. Nothing is being won here. The marsh is rising and the city is sinking and the two rates have been nearly equal for so long that they have the look of an agreement.

**The others**

Almost nothing else lives here, and that is the strangest census in the world, because the place is so full. But fullness is not liveliness. The population is sessile and slow and does not move and therefore does not count, the way the crowd in a wall does not count.

What counts: the grey birds. They walk. They have given up most of flight, and their legs are long and their wings are short and their bodies are the colour of the wet stone, and they pick along the weirs at a pace that suggests they have all the time in the world, which they do. They nest in the dry chambers — the only dry chambers, the ones high on the south faces where the steam goes and the seam has held. They are the only quick thing in the Tarnholt that you will see.

And the prints. Five toes, no claw marks, a partial web, and a long drag where something was carried. Fresh every morning in the mud of the same channel, going the same way, and you will never see what makes them, because whatever it is is better at not being seen than you are at seeing, and because it is not interested in you. It is, so far as anyone can tell, the only thing here that wastes motion.

**You**

You are the anomaly. Dry-skinned, quick, four metres of voice and then nothing. You are the fastest thing that has ever come onto this plateau, and you will be gone in a season, and the Tarnholt will register you the way a body registers a splinter: slowly, and by closing around the place where you were. The channel you waded will silt. The chamber you slept in will be reoccupied, and its lip will pulse again at the rate of a sleeping breath, and the lime will go down over the ash of your fire — there is no fire here, but you take the point — a fraction of a millimetre at a time.
