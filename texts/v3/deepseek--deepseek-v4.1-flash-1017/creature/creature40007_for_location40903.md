**The Loft-Born**

The people of the Loft are short and broad and built low to the wind. If you are tall, they come up to your sternum; if you are not, they still seem to take up more room than they should, because they stand close and move in packs. Their legs are short and their feet are wide, toes splayed, soles like horn, and they go barefoot on the warm stone because the hum is easier to read through the feet. Their hands are the most obvious adaptation: broad, blunt-fingered, nails thick and ridged, palms always a little damp from the cloths they carry folded over one shoulder. Their skin is a matte grey-olive, fined with pale down that catches the vapor; they are warm to the touch and do not shiver easily. They smell of wet stone, moss, woodsmoke, and the sweetish musk of too many bodies in too small a stack. In the bowl’s grey-gold dusk they look like something carved from the stone and then warmed by it. Their voices are clipped and hard-consonanted, full of stops and knocks, and they call across the terraces by striking a lintel with a bone hammer rather than shouting. They touch the stone as they pass—fingertips on a doorjamb, palm on a warm wall—as if checking a pulse.

They wake when the hum thins, before the bright smear of sun resolves. The first work is the cloths. Overnight they have been pressed to the shaded faces of rocks, and in the morning they are wrung into a common cup. By custom the oldest and the youngest drink first; the strongest last. Children learn the wringing before they learn to read the hum. To spill is a small crime, and the punishment is not a beating but a cloth and a patch of stone: you press until you have taken back what you lost. They do not wash in water. They scrape with moss and warm dust and anoint their skin with crushed insect fat. They chew the upper moss for moisture and lick the stone where it sweats. Their bodies are miserly: they sweat little, their urine is dark, their breath comes out wet. They are not thirsty in the way you are thirsty. Thirst, to them, is a thing that happens to outsiders and to the very old.

The terraces are a household’s first inheritance and its first debt. They must be weeded, packed, patched, kept from slipping. The moss is not a crop exactly; it is the foundation, and they shear it like fleece, never taking from the living mat, only the upper growth, which is dried and ground and eaten as a flat grey cake or woven into cloth. They keep insect colonies in hollow lintels, smoke them at intervals, take wax and grubs and leave the queen. They eat shrub fruit, bird eggs, crickets, beetle larvae, the soft inner bark of shrubs, and a great deal of moss. The furry climber is not eaten by custom; it is a commensal, a household guest, and children learn its moods before they learn the names of the upper walks. The birds on the lip are not hunted; their screams are a wind warning. The trees are few and old and each one is named, and the rules about them are among the few things here that are not practical: you may take fallen wood, you may sit under them, you may not cut them, and you may not climb them unless you are the tree-warden and the tree is sick. Metal is scarce, soft, and scavenged; a good knife is an heirloom. Cloth is felted moss-fiber, sleeveless and knee-length, with a shawl for the wind and knee-pads for the terraces. They keep rain-catchers on the roofs, not because they expect rain but because a roof is a thing that should have one; the catchers fill with dust and insects and are cleaned.

A household is a stack of dry-laid stone cells. The lowest is storage and animals; the middle is sleeping and work; the top is a walkway, and the walkway is also a roof, and the roof is also someone else’s floor. You are always under feet and over heads. Doors are low and screens are cloth; privacy is a curtain and a silence, not a distance. They sleep close, in piles when young, in pairs and threes when old, because warmth is cheap and the bowl is loud and being alone is a thing that happens outside the lip. A few hundred people live here, and everyone knows everyone’s name, gait, cough, and the particular clatter of their ladder. They argue constantly and loudly, and the arguing is not aggression; it is how a dense place lets off pressure. They are generous with food and stingy with cloth. They touch to pass, forehead to forehead in greeting, hand on shoulder to make a point. Their names are short and hard—Tek, Mar, Sull, Venn—and a name is not a private thing.

There is no chief. There are people who are listened to: the cloth-keeper, who says when the wringing is done; the hum-listener, who says when the pitch has changed; the tree-warden; the moss-keeper; the one who counts the children. Authority is a rota and a reputation, not a crown. Decisions are made by those who do the work, in arguments that last as long as the work does. They have laws, but they would not call them laws: do not spill; do not cut the living moss; do not take the last of a colony; do not eat the climber; do not strike the lip after dark. The dead are walled into the outer face, upright, facing in, so they keep their place in the stack.

They do not think of the warm stone as a god. They call it the Underwarm, and they tend the terraces as one tends a fire: not with worship, with maintenance. The hum is the place being the place, and they can tell a good hum from a bad one. A bad hum is a change in pitch, a stutter in the note; it means the under-stone is shifting, and the hum-listener will walk the lip for a day with her hand flat against the rock. They date events by such things: the year the upper walk gave way; before the last good hum; the season the moss went grey. They are not being poetic. That is the resolution at which time is legible. They have no seasons worth the name and no years they count. They do not wait for anything. The mountain is not a ruin to them; it is a house. They know the word *river*, but it has drifted: it means a thing that is imagined, a story you tell children about water that runs away and takes things with it. They find the idea funny and obscene.

If you stay, you will be given to a household and a cloth-keeper will watch your hands. She will be short and wide and grey-haired, maybe forty, maybe sixty—it is hard to tell, because they age late and slowly and their faces are weathered evenly. She will carry a bone hammer at her belt and a folded cloth over her shoulder. She will tap the lintel twice before she enters a room, and when she laughs it will be a short, hard sound, like a stone knocked once. She has never seen a river. She knows the pitch of the hum in her knees. She can name every child on the upper walks and every insect colony in her corbel. She is not a mystic. She is a maintainer. She will offer you cloth, not a cup, and she will watch to see whether you wring it carefully or waste it. If you spill, she will forgive you once. If you spill again, she will think you a child or a fool, and she will not be wrong.

The bowl is not only theirs. They live inside a thicket of other lives, and they know it. Insects are in the moss by the thousand per square meter; they are in the walls, in the corbels, in the hollow lintels, in the food, in the air. The people are used to them. They farm some, tolerate all, and read the noise. A change in the insect chorus is a warning the way a change in the hum is a warning. The furry climber lives in the trees and is not eaten; it is a guest and a barometer. The birds ride the updraft along the lip and scream, and the screams are useful. The moss is the blanket and the foundation. The people are one layer in a reef, dense and loud and layered, never alone and never out of earshot of something alive. They do not think of themselves as separate from the place. They are the place’s answer to itself.

They are thriving at an ending, and they do not experience it as an ending. The mountain wore down to a stump and stopped; the town was built and rebuilt on the same terraces until the walking surfaces are worn into troughs and the door-sills are dished by feet; the moss is deep; the trees are old; the hum comes up through the bones. They are not tragic. They are loud, practical, touchy, generous, stingy, and patient. They measure things in collapses and hums, and they keep the cloths wrung and the terraces packed and the names of the dead in the outer wall. If you stand on the lip at evening and feel the warm stone underfoot and hear the bowl below you dense and loud and old, you are feeling what they feel every day: not a height, but a home that happens to be an ending, and it is thriving.
