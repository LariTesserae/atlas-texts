What lives in the Sump is not one thing. The first answer is a town — a couple hundred people spread thin across a black bowl, each house alone on its poles, each walkway stopping deliberately short of the next. The second answer is that the town is the smallest part. The real population is the ceiling, the water, the fine continuous ticking, the living lamps, the pale sacs that are harvested like fruit and are not quite fruit. The people live among all of it, and they have become the part that builds and cuts back and covers its mouth.

They are not an ancient people of the Sump. The Sump is too new for that. Some of them remember the tearing and the wet enormous thud when a wall came down; everyone remembers a different shape of the walls. They are first-generation keepers of a collapse, tenants of a place that is still falling in. Their hands are the chief tool: rope-burned, clay under the nails, wrinkled from standing water. They carry bone hooks and poles and gourds. They have a few metal blades worn to slivers and re-hafted many times, heirlooms and jokes. Everything else is withy and hide and twisted fiber and daubed clay. They are a people of small repairs, and they know that a repair is never the last one.

They are quiet — not by law, by habit. The Sump teaches it. There are no choruses here, no calling across the bowl; the loudest thing is water dripping and trickling and lapping at the poles. So the people speak low, and they listen more than they talk. They hear the Sump as texture. A break in the ticking is an event. A change in the dripping means a wall is thinking about coming down. They do not sort the world into plant and animal, not in any way that matters to them; they sort it into what is ready, what is dangerous, what is in the water now, what must not be touched until the water falls. Ask them whether the gray flesh in the ceiling is plant or animal and they will find it funny and slightly rude. The question misses the point of supper.

Their economy is the ceiling and the shallow water. The ceiling mat grows down, and they cut it back — that is the first duty and the image of all duties: keep the roof from becoming the floor. In the mat live the pale sacs and curled bodies rooted in the fiber, soft gray flesh that opens when touched. They cut them down with bone hooks and eat them. The staple is also a hazard: once a year, for two weeks, the pale sacs open and release something into the air in numbers past counting, and everyone stays indoors with their lamps low and their mouths covered and waits for it to be over. They do not explain this to outsiders in a way that takes. It is not a festival. It is weather with a season.

Below, the water is a calendar. After rain it rises through the broken rim, and the buried things come up, and the grazers follow, and the things that eat the grazers follow, and the whole population of the Sump moves in a slow pulse. The people move with it. There are hours when you do not go on the water. There are channels you do not dredge. Nobody wrote the schedule down; everybody knows it. Their prohibitions are not moral so much as temporal and spatial: not *don’t*, but *not now*, *not there*. They dredge channels to move the shallow water where they want it, and set stake-and-brush weirs to catch what the pulse brings, and they are not at the top of anything. They are in the pulse too.

Light is the strangest livestock. Almost no daylight reaches the floor — only a green-brown murk enough to make shapes, not colors — so the real light is animals. The people keep drifting luminous things alive on wet fiber in jars and gourds, hang them at doorways and along the walkways, carry them on poles. The light is low and uneven and breathing; it moves, it dies, it is replaced. At night the town is a scatter of small wandering glows across a black bowl, and a great many things in the water and in the ceiling notice them. The people know this. Light is a need and a risk, and they are its keepers, not its owners.

They are not masters of the Sump. They are its pruners, its water-movers, its lamp-keepers, its rebuilders. When a wall slumps they hear the change in the dripping first, then the long tearing, then the thud and the water slapping the stilts; then they shift a house or two and rebuild the walkway somewhere else. They are attached less to sites than to relations — to the schedule, to the walkways, to each other. Nothing here is finished, and they have made of that not a tragedy but a maintenance schedule. They live in a hole that is still falling in, in the dark, in the wet, among an enormous number of small lives that do not announce themselves. They speak low. They cut the ceiling back. They carry the light.
