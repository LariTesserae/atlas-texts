The one that belongs here is the horned wader, though it would not answer to that. You meet its print before you meet it: three broad, splayed holes in the mud, each filling slowly with cloudy seepage, each already crusting at the rim with that pearly-green scum. The print is not deep. The ground resents being pressed and closes slowly behind the foot, so the track looks less like a footprint than a wound that has begun to remember itself.

The wader is low and heavy and long, a sliding ridge of back that parts the vegetation without hurrying. It has three legs, not in any arrangement that would look balanced on dry land: two forward, one trailing, so that its gait is a slow rolling tripod from one soft collapse to the next. It never lifts its feet high. Each leg ends in a broad, wrinkled pad that spreads under its weight like a hand pressing into something warm. The hide is slick and grey-green, not scaled and not quite skinned, patchy with algae in the creases and crusted at the joints with pinkish salt. Its horns are not weapons. They are swept back and knobbed and furred with the same soft absorptive algae that hangs from the stems, and they push the thorn tangle aside so the body can follow.

It is not one clean animal. It is a walking island. Flies move in wedges around the wet folds of its eyes, which are small and deep-set and mostly useless in the dim green light; it reads the marsh by pressure instead—by the crackle of salt crust, the give of mud, the density of the fly cloud, the slow tide it carries in its bones. Iridescent beetles lodge in the seams of its hide. A clay-shelled lizard climbs the warm groove behind its horn and stays there for days. When it stands in the shallows, the round grey-haired fish-things gather around its legs and push through the silty water without swimming, and the pale blind worms come up through the mud to feed where its feet have been.

Its mouth is wide and soft and patient. It crops the sweating leaves and the resonant reeds, but mostly it waits for the pods to split their seams. It takes the seeds wet and whole, and later, in the warm cloudy seepage of its footprints, it leaves them—along with everything else it carries—to crust over and begin again. Its breath is the bubbling sigh from the deeper muck to the east, the long exhale that sounds every few minutes as if the ground itself were thinking. Its voice is a low resonant hum, almost below hearing, that comes up through the hollow reeds and becomes one more strand in the wall of sound. When it moves through the trees forty paces away, you hear the slow sucking footfalls and the wet rearrangement of fibre, and then it is gone into the green again, not hiding, only continuing.

It lives at the edge of drowning, as everything here does. The water holds most of its weight; the mud takes the rest. It has no hurry in it. It has been hot and wet and loud and dim for a long time, and the wader has been part of that same note for just as long—patient, enormous, half-hidden, leaving nurseries behind it with every step, breathing out into the broth, and never waiting for anyone to see it.
