# The Tally

You will not see her the first time you pass. You will feel the absence of something — a warmth you didn't register until it was gone, a sense of being counted — and you will turn, and the gallery behind you will be empty. The crabs will be watching from the water's edge, their pale shells motionless, and you will understand that they saw something you did not.

She lives in the space between the orange lamps. Not in the dark cycles — those belong to the bacteria, to the green glow that turns every surface into the suggestion of a face — but in the transitions, the moments when the timers click and the light is neither on nor off. She has learned the rhythm of the lamps the way you learn a heartbeat. She knows when to move and when to be still. She has been here long enough that the rhythm is her rhythm, and the distinction has become academic.

---

**What She Was**

She came down the shaft with a clipboard and a flashlight and a job title that no longer exists. She was a *monitor* — that was the word on the paperwork — tasked with recording water levels, noting pressure variances, flagging anomalies for the engineers who would come later. The engineers did not come. The paperwork is still somewhere, in one of the storage vaults, its edges curling with damp, her handwriting fading to gray.

She was not the first. There were others, before her, in the early days when the Underworks was still being poured, still being welded, still being *made*. They had names and faces and families. She does not remember them. She remembers their chalk marks on the pillars, the ones that show where the water reached in the floods before she arrived. She has added her own marks. Some of them are very high.

She was young when she came down. She is not young now, though she is not old either. Time in the Underworks does not pass the way it passes above. It pools. It eddies. It finds its level.

---

**What She Is**

Her skin has gone the color of the concrete — not pale, exactly, but *tinted*, as if the efflorescence has crept into her pores and made a home there. Her hair is white, but not with age; it is white the way the salt crystals are white, a bloom rather than a fading. Her hands are always damp. She does not seem to notice.

She wears layers — coats over coats over sweaters, all of them scavenged from the storage vaults, all of them too large or too small, none of them hers originally. She has a way of moving through the galleries that is almost silent, her footsteps finding the quiet places in the grating, her breath synchronized with the drip-points. She carries a flashlight, but she rarely uses it. She knows the way by sound.

She speaks, sometimes, to the machines. Not conversation — not the way you or I might talk to a thing we know is not listening — but something closer to acknowledgment. She will pause by a pump and rest her hand on its housing and say, *"Still going."* She will pass a dead flywheel and say, *"You did well."* She will stand at the edge of the black water and listen to the gurgle and say, *"Not yet."*

The crabs know her. They do not scatter when she approaches. They gather, instead, their pale bodies forming a crescent at the edge of whatever light she carries with her. She does not feed them. She does not need to. They have learned that she is not a threat, and they have learned that she is not prey, and they have learned that she is, in some way they cannot articulate, *theirs*.

---

**What She Does**

She counts.

That is the word she uses, when she speaks of it at all. She counts the lamps, the drips, the crabs, the machines that are still running, the machines that have stopped, the marks on the pillars, the days since the last flood. She keeps the tally in her head — there is no paper anymore, no clipboard, no ink that would survive the damp — and she updates it constantly, silently, the way you might keep track of your own breathing.

She watches the water. She knows its moods, its tides, its colors. She knows when the pumps are about to fail — not by the gauges, which lie, but by the sound, by the way the hum shifts by a half-tone, by the way the crabs begin to move toward the higher grates. She has learned to predict the floods. She has learned to survive them.

She does not try to fix anything. That was never her job. Her job was to observe, to record, to *know*. The engineers were supposed to do the fixing. The engineers are not coming. She has made her peace with this.

Sometimes, in the dark cycles, she walks the far chambers. The ones where the lamps are fewer, where the water is deeper, where the doors that are not doors suggest openings into something else. She does not go through them. She stands at their thresholds and she listens, and then she turns back, and she adds one more to the tally.

She has never told anyone what she hears. There is no one to tell.

---

**What She Knows**

She knows that the Underworks is not haunted. She knew this before you did, before the thought even occurred to you. She knows that the building is alive — not metaphorically, not poetically, but *actually*, in a way that the concrete and the steel and the water have conspired to make true. She knows that the hum is not a machine sound but a *pulse*, and that the pulse has a rhythm, and that the rhythm has changed since she came down the shaft.

She knows that the bioluminescent bacteria are not just a lighting system. She has watched them for years, and she has seen them respond to things that are not changes in the water or the temperature or the light. She has seen them dim when she is sad. She has seen them brighten when she laughs. She does not laugh often.

She knows that the chalk marks on the pillars are not just flood records. Some of them — the older ones, the ones she did not make — are something else. They are *messages*. She has not decoded them. She is not sure she wants to.

She knows that she is not the only thing that came down the shaft and stayed. She has seen the rats, the pigeons, the crabs. She has seen other things. Shapes in the mist. Footprints in the efflorescence that are not hers. A warmth in the dark cycles that has no source she can identify.

She knows that the Underworks is becoming more itself. She has watched it happen, year by year, cycle by cycle. The walls are settling. The water is finding new channels. The machines are learning new rhythms. The building is *growing*, in the way that a body grows, in the way that a mind grows, and she is part of it now, and she will not leave.

---

**What She Waits For**

She does not think of it as waiting. Waiting implies an endpoint, a thing that will arrive, a resolution. She thinks of it as *tending* — the way a gardener tends a garden, the way a caretaker tends a house. She tends the tally. She tends the lamps. She tends the memory of the engineers who did not come.

But there is something she is waiting for, even if she would not call it that. She is waiting for the next person to come down the shaft. Not the wanderers, the explorers, the ones who pass through and leave. Someone who will *stay*. Someone who will learn the rhythm of the lamps and the moods of the water and the meaning of the crab-crescent at the edge of the light. Someone who will help her count.

She has been alone for a long time. The Underworks is alive, but it is not a *companion*. The crabs are company, but they are not *conversation*. She has learned to be sufficient unto herself, but she has not learned to be happy about it.

When you pass through her gallery — and you will, eventually, even if you do not see her — she will watch you from the space between the lamps. She will note the way you walk, the way you listen, the way you hold your breath when the hum falters. She will add you to the tally. She will decide whether you are a wanderer or a stayer.

And if you are a stayer, she will step out of the space between the lamps, and she will say — in a voice that is rust and ozone and something underneath — *"You heard it too, didn't you. The new note."*

And she will wait for your answer.
