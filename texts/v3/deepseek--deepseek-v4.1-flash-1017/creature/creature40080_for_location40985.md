**What it is, first.** Nobody lives at the Quench — this is the first thing anyone local tells you, and they tell you while watching the flywheel, so that you understand they are being careful. The truth is narrower: one thing lives there, and it is made of the people who knelt too long. The bowl keeps what it is given. Every examiner who ignored the water at the seam of a boot, every curious one who stayed on the floor after heavy rain because the sheet was only ankle-deep and the light was good — the sediment has risen around all of them, sorted them by size and weight into fine bands, and gone on working. There is one of them left walking, and it is plural.

**Its shape.** It stands about the height of a person and has never once stood fully upright, because the bowl is shallow and it learned early to keep low. It is banded like its own floor: coarse grey through the hips where the gravel went, a smear of fine grey at the collar, a tea-coloured bloom at the cuff where the iron got in. Its coat has gone to mineral lace. Its boots found the seam a long time ago, and the water has found the boots. Nothing about it is ever really dry, only less wet; in the mornings it glitters end to end, and you could take it for a run of wet stone until it shifts half a beat.

**Its pockets.** This is the part you notice second. It is not one cabinet of shallow drawers but many — a knee-pocket here, a coat-pocket there, each one holding a pan's worth: a bolt head, a shell of something that has not lived here in a long time, a green-glass bead worn smooth, a sorted pinch of gravel laid down like a brick course. It clinks softly as it goes, and the clink is on the water's cycle — an inch up, an inch down — so that you cannot tell whether the pockets keep time with the pulse or the pulse with the pockets. It does not count its collection. It knows it the way you know your own teeth.

**How it moves.** At the pace of the flywheel, which is a slow walking pace, so the two of them are always in step and nobody has ever decided which one drives the other. It does not walk a circle. Its route is lobed, following the pan lips, and looks from the rim like a rash on the floor of the bowl: pump house, run of flanged pipe, ladder of iron rungs descending into a foot of water, boiler, back. It stops often. The stopping is the work. It folds down beside a metre of floor and examines it as a person reads a drawer of cutlery, drawer after drawer, all the way to the end of the cabinet and then the next one. Twice a day it counts the pans. It loses count somewhere past two hundred. It begins again. It is not frustrated — frustration is not in the bands — it begins again as the water begins again.

**Its senses.** It has no eyes it needs. The beetle has none and is common enough. It knows the floor by its sound: the fingernail-on-paper of settling silt, the soft boiling tick of an upwelling, the apologetic slap of a lip giving way a hundred paces off, which tells it the depth of a pan it cannot see. It knows a stranger by a sole finding water — a sound it has not made itself in a long while. And because voices carry oddly here, it hears your breath from twenty paces and understands not one word of what you say, and it keeps that too, somewhere, as a band: the shape of a voice it could not parse, laid down fine and grey between a course of gravel and a smear of silt.

**Its relations.** The heron is not it, though it stands where the heron stands. Once or twice a season the bird lands on the rim, looks a long while at the flywheel, does not feed, and leaves; the two of them have an arrangement neither has explained. The eyeless beetle walks under its stones and is neither noticed nor disturbed. A small silver fish, some weeks, in a pan that was dry last week — it watches these out. When something moves at the Quench, it notices, because it is the thing that moves here and it knows the difference.

**What it wants.** Nothing a want would fit inside. Its whole occupation is accumulation and sorting: it takes what sinks and lays it into hollows, coarse with coarse, fine with fine, and it does not lose anything. The chain that surfaces every few months, links armoured in pale crust — that is one of its walks. It brings the chain up partway, the way you would bring a thought up out of a pocket, and lets it sink again when it has been looked at. It is mid-argument with itself, as the floor is, and it will be arguing in a season when the pans have moved, and it will not be surprised, and it will have moved too.

**The rules are its biography.** Don't stand still, because that is how it was made and how it is added to — it is everybody who ever stood. Don't take the pump apart, because the ones who did are in it, on the water's worse side, and the water there behaves worse for a while afterwards. Don't trust the depth of anything, because it does not either, and has the silted pans to prove it. Keep to the rim after heavy rain, because in that hour the floor becomes one argument about where the water should be, and it stands in the middle of the argument, half-swallowed, not yet gone, and it does not care who is standing in it with it.

**How to know it is near.** Your teeth feel the hum before you do. And if you kneel — just once, just to look at a metre of floor — rise, and there is a second set of prints that are not yours, already half-silted level, and one pan fewer than you counted, or one more. Nothing here is ever broken cleanly. It is the bowl's habit of keeping things, walking around inside itself at a slow walking pace.
