The people are the quietest thing in Tallow, and the hardest to notice.

You understand this before you understand them: the mill and the marsh have taken all the room that sound needs. They speak in a register pitched just above the moving water and just below the engine, and their voices have the flattened, carrying quality of people who have learned to be heard through noise without raising. Twice a day the engine stops — eleven minutes at the shift change — and for those eleven minutes you can hear the whole town talking at once, a brief startled aviary of a few hundred people, and then the chuff resumes and they go back under.

They are wet-handed. That is the first physical fact of them, the thing you notice at a handshake. The reed cutters have the elephant reed's signature on them: fine parallel scars on the forearms and the backs of the hands, and they wear long sleeves in the heat because of it, and the sleeves are always soaked to the elbow. Their hands are stained a deep tea-brown from tamarind tannin, so dark in the creases that it looks like a glove they can't remove. They smell of the town, concentrated — wet paper, hot iron, and under that the green bitter note of crushed reed — and a Tallow native down the Outflow in a trading village is known by nose before face.

The mill people are a different smell. The digester crew carry the digester: sweetish, rank, unmistakably alive, a smell like a held breath let out. They are the only people in town who are ever truly dry, because the digester runs hot, and they stand too close to it in the cold hours the way other people stand near a fire. The press operator is a woman of about fifty who has run the same squat engine for twenty-six years and can diagnose it by ear from the far end of the shed, and does, and is usually right. And everyone hangs sheets. That's not a job, it's a weather event — when the lines fill, whoever is free goes to the lines, and they work inside those translucent curtains of wet paper with the light coming through them, so that for an hour a day the whole population is standing inside a lamp.

They treat the finished paper with something that looks like superstition and is actually arithmetic. It is the only dry thing they make. They wash and dry their hands before they touch a finished sheet; they never set a stack down on bare ground; they stack it on tamarind offcuts that have been kiln-dried and kept dry in a shed that nobody is allowed to open in the rain. A visitor reads this as reverence. It's not. It's the ordinary care of people who make one clean, flat, dry thing in a world that refuses all three, and who know exactly what it costs to keep it that way for the four days it takes to reach the buyer.

The children are the ones who notice the town. They are taught the lean of it as topography — the standard curriculum, the same as the names of the channels — and they can tell you, with the confidence of experts, which buildings have drifted since the last correction and by how much, because drift is the only news there is. They play on the shims. They learn to walk the boardwalk without looking down, which is necessary, and then they learn to walk it without looking down *and* step over the frogs, which is the real skill, and they acquire it around six. They are the ones who see what's changing, because the old people have stopped seeing it and the middle-aged are too busy. There is a girl of about eleven who keeps, in a notebook, a running list of every vine she has pulled from the mill's north wall, dated, and her mother thinks this is a phase, and it is not a phase, it is the beginning of a career.

The old people argue about straightness. There's a man who will tell you, at length, that the third column of the drying shed was true when he was a boy, and there's a woman who can recite, from memory, every correction made to that column in forty years, and the two of them have been having this conversation for a decade and neither will yield. This is what history looks like here. Not events. Drifts and corrections, held in the body, disputed.

The cats are a fiction. Everyone knows this. There are perhaps five of them, fat and slow and profoundly unbothered, and there is nothing for them to hunt in a town where the ground is either water or boardwalk, and they don't hunt, and the town keeps them anyway — not for mice, because there are no mice, but because a town is supposed to have cats in the way a town is supposed to have a well. They are a superstition of ordinariness. The frogs are the actual population. Frogs in the channels, frogs in the axils of the bladder ferns, frogs on the boardwalk at night in such numbers that the path seems to be moving. The dusk sound of them is not a chorus, it's a surface, something you can put your hand against. Nobody thinks of them as animals. They're neighbors, and they're stepped around without a glance. The herons are different — four or five of them, known individually, one with a crooked leg, standing in the Outflow for hours in the patient, watchful way that the town privately respects because it is the only other thing here that also just stands and watches. And the small brown divers nest in the mill's vents, in the rust-orange corrugation where the steam keeps the metal warm, and the mill workers have learned not to clean those particular vents, and don't, and would not explain why if you asked.

The living timber is an inhabitant too, and nobody in Tallow would find that sentence strange. The coppiced columns are individuals. Somebody's grandfather trained that one — squared it, pruned it, shimmed the brace — and thirty years on it is still alive, still leafing from the knees, still holding the roof. A few of them have real names. A tamarind flowering on a load-bearing column is an event that people mention. The leaves sprouting from the undersides of beams get picked or don't get picked not by rule but by judgment: is this where the weight is, is this where the damp sits, will this hold. The moss on the roofs is managed the way everything is managed — by hand, in the drier week, leaving a third, and the third that's left is a decision somebody made and can defend.

The boatmen are the only people who leave. One day down, two days back, poling against the current the whole way home because the channel won't take an engine. They come back with salt and news and a look in the eye that the town recognizes and doesn't comment on — the look of a person who has been dry for two nights. The returning poler is met at the rim by whoever is free, which is usually everyone who can be, and the unloading is the closest thing Tallow has to a public occasion.

And at the margin, the outermost houses, the negotiation is daily and unspoken. The bladder ferns don't attack; they simply continue. Stop pulling for two seasons and they come up through the floor — not violently, just lifting the boards and plating the ground and then the wall, patiently, as though it were the obvious next step. So everyone pulls as they walk. A vine goes as you pass it. It's not a war and nobody calls it an accord, but the accord is what it is, and the margin people live inside it, and when the dusk comes down and the frogs start and the mill's steam column goes gold and the insects rise, the whole town goes inside at once, without discussing it — not from fear, but because the day has a shape and the shape is done.
