The ones who live there are not exactly cave-dwellers. They are **accruers** — a small, stubborn people who have learned to live inside a geology that is still making up its mind.

They are short-boned and broad-handed, with skin the color of wet paper and a permanent sheen, because nothing here is ever dry. Their eyes are large but hooded, built for amber dusk and rose-glowing sinter; their fingertips are callused smooth as horn, and their nails are kept thick and short. They crop their hair close or bind it in sinew. They smell of warm stone, smoke, and the sour mineral breath of the pools. They wear hide oiled until it is almost waterproof, then fiber nets over it, and everything they own has a rind on it — tools, pouches, ladder rungs, the warm stone they carry in a pocket because they have been holding it all day.

Their villages are not villages. They are a dozen alcoves daubed shut with mud-and-fiber paste, a smoke-stain, a screen of woven sinew, a ladder going up into a chimney that may not be there next season. They call a place home not because it is permanent but because they have dug it open before. They return, scrape the new sinter from a doorway, and find their old scratch marks underneath like a letter from themselves. They build for the season and repair for the decade. Metal is precious, traded up from somewhere above; a knife is resharpened until it is a sliver, then re-hafted, then kept as a charm.

They live by the fauna calendar. When the grazer sheets thin and the filter-feeders close up tight, they pack. They catch drifting grazers in sinew screens, scrape salt from fresh sinter, harvest the burrowers’ mounds, and follow the columns of leggy things only at a distance. They do not tame the place. They read it. A rememberer sings the routes — which passage is closing, which pool has moved, where the blind hunter was last heard — and the song changes every season, because a map that is not revised is a trap.

They are quiet the way people are quiet in a house with a sleeping animal. They talk low, use hand signs, and stop when the chamber stops. They know the surge is coming before it comes: a tension in the hum, a tightening in the sessile things, a silence where the blind hunter has been. They do not name the ceiling organism indoors. They put a palm against it when they need to know something, and they wait. The answer is slow, a shiver like a wall considering you. They leave it alone. Sometimes they leave it offerings — a scrap of metal, a handful of grazers, a song.

Their children are born in warm wet, learn to walk on slick stone, and are taught first that the floor is not a floor but a process. They learn to tap before they trust, to test a hold, to listen for the wet drag and the click. They learn that a tool set down and not carried is a tool given to the mountain. They learn the names of the fauna before the names of the stars, which nobody here has seen. Their dead are sealed in fresh sinter if the stone is growing, or carried up a chimney if it is not. Grief here is practical and long.

They are not the only residents. The Accretion is thick with them and with everything else: sheets of thumbnail grazers moving like weather, pale filter-feeders opening and closing with the drips, armored scavengers following the columns, the blind warm-bodied hunter that makes a noticeable silence around itself, and the colonial ceiling organism that is half animal and half mineral and older than the fissure. The people are one more layer in the reef — the layer that builds alcoves, repairs ladders, catches grazers, and touches the ceiling to ask if it is awake. They are seasonal, attentive, and stubborn. They live in a place that is mid-sentence, and they have made a life out of being mid-sentence with it.
