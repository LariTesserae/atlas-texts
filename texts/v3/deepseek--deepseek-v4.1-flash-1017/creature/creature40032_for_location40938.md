The people of Vaur Rin are not quite a townful of people. They are a listening population — human, but edited by the cave into something that lives by ear and etiquette. They are warm-skinned and faintly damp, with the mineral sheen of the walls worked into the creases of their hands. They go barefoot because the stone is warm and because a hard sole makes a lie of the floor. Their clothes are soft, loose, mineral-dyed in cream and rust and wet slate; nothing that clinks, nothing that rustles, nothing that competes with the bed of sound. Their hair is cropped or bound back against the fog. Their eyes are wide and amber-lit and not entirely trusted; they tilt their heads when they listen, and they stop mid-sentence the way other people stop to look at something.

A child in Vaur Rin learns the drip-clock before letters. They learn which fissure exhales every nineteen minutes, which corridor has a wet year-round note, which footfall in the eastern braid will come back eleven seconds later from the western wall like a patient follower. They learn to speak low and in the gaps of the sound, so that a conversation becomes a small piece of counterpoint rather than a thing laid over the top. They learn that silence is not empty. Silence is a sour chord, a stopped machine, a wall that has gone quiet when it should be singing. When a district goes silent, people come out of their doors without being called.

They are not mystics. They are exact. The most respected among them are not the loudest or the boldest but the best listeners: chord-wrights who can hear a fault developing three streets away by the way the third above the bass has gone thin; seep-readers who know which sheen on a wall is a leak and which is only the cave licking its lips; fin-keepers who guard the condensers in lean cycles; mat-wardens who will not let you scrape the pale thread-growth from a corridor because it is the only thing in the cavern making oxygen. There are wall-askers, too — people who know which old structure prefers to be asked for heat in which order, and who can tell when a machine is sulking and when it is dying. They do not call the machines machines, mostly. They call them the old walls, the warm threads, the ones who remember. The machines have dispositions, and the people have learned the grammar of asking.

They live densely in the three braids, and they leave the gaps alone. This is not fear, exactly. It is a kind of civic manners. You do not fill the empty stone between districts with anything that would change the standing waves. You do not straighten a leaning wall; the lean is load-bearing now. You do not dam a seep; you negotiate with it. You do not silence a working structure, even if it is inconvenient, even if it is singing at a pitch that gets into your teeth. And at the turn of the air-tide you do not go into the gaps alone, because the pressure changes and the sound changes with it, and a person who knows the map by ear can suddenly find that the map has folded.

They are a warm, noisy people in a place where warmth and noise are the conditions of staying alive. They gather at corners under the low amber lanterns and talk in overlapping murmurs. They touch the walls when they pass them, not superstitiously but the way you might touch a sleeping animal to feel that it is still breathing. They ignore the long-legged translucent thing that comes out at the tide’s turn and stands in the empty gaps for hours. The ignoring is the etiquette. A child who points is gently turned away. The pale crawlers in the wall mats are tolerated, even encouraged, because they are part of the same thin living film. Once a season, when something larger comes up from the deep seams, the people stop and listen to the new low note, and then they go on with what they were doing, and nobody goes down to look.

What lives there, in the end, is a small human culture shaped like an ear. It is suspicious of silence, devoted to maintenance, conservative in the way a bridge is conservative. It knows that the cavern is a body and the town is a thing living in it, and that the body is patient but not infinite. So the people keep the chord in tune, guard the fins, protect the mats, ask the old walls politely for heat, and leave the gaps and the deep seams and the standing translucent thing their privacy. They are a bright noisy warm dense knot of human making, settled deep into a cold dark quiet world that has not decided yet whether to keep them. They live accordingly.
