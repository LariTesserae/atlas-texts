# The Veyfolk

They are small—smaller than you'd expect, and you don't realize it until one stands close. Not child-small, but compressed, as if the weight of the place above them has pressed them down over generations. The tallest might reach your shoulder. Their bodies are compact in a way that seems intentional, the Vey's own engineering applied to flesh: shorter limbs for navigating tight catwalks, lower centers of gravity for the swaying bridges, hands that are slightly too large for their frames, all knuckle and grip.

Their skin has gone pale in the perpetual dusk, but not the sickly pallor of things that never see light—more like the inside of a shell, or the flesh of a mushroom. It has a faint warm undertone from the ochre glow, a permanent blush of bronze that never fades. Their eyes are large, pupils permanently wide, and they have a way of not quite looking at you directly, as if they're tracking something in their peripheral vision—the sluice-gate three platforms up, the drip-pattern on the eastern wall, the vibration in the catwalk supports. They are always, always monitoring.

## The Marks

You notice it within the first hour: they are written on. Not tattoos, not scars, not quite either—the marks are in their skin the way rings are in a tree, layered and integral, grown rather than applied. The patterns are the same ones that appear in the fungus, in the vine, in the strange experimental markings that thread through the walls of the Vey itself. On the Veyfolk, they cluster at the joints—the inside of the elbow, the hollow of the throat, the back of the knee—places where the skin folds and moves. They darken and lighten with the body's rhythms, with the four-part cycle, with things the Veyfolk themselves don't seem to fully control.

Ask about them and you'll get different answers. The eldest say the Vey marks its own; the marks show where the body has learned the shape of the place. The youngest shrug and say they've always been there, that babies are born with the first faint lines already visible, that the patterns deepen as you grow into your work. The fungal cultivators have the most elaborate markings, spiraling up their forearms like cultivated mycelium. The sluice-tenders have them concentrated on their palms, their fingertips, places that touch the machinery. The children have almost none—just the faintest traceries, like the first threads of a web.

Everyone agrees on one thing: the marks change. Slowly, over years, but they change. And sometimes, when the Vey is doing something new—when a new structure is being built, when a path is being rerouted, when the light shifts in a way no one has seen before—someone wakes up with a mark they didn't have the day before, and everyone goes quiet and watches them for a while, waiting to see what the Vey is asking them to become.

## The Knowing

They don't speak much. Not because they're unfriendly—they'll answer questions, they'll share food, they'll sit with you in comfortable silence for hours—but because speech isn't how they communicate. The Vey is a place of vibration, of rhythm, of pressure and temperature and the subtle shifts of machinery. The Veyfolk have learned to read it the way you might read a face: the particular tremor of a catwalk under a specific weight, the pitch of a sluice-gate's hiss, the way the condensation drips change when someone is moving through a particular corridor. They know where each other are at all times, not by sight but by feel, by the minute disturbances each person makes in the Vey's atmosphere.

When they do speak, it's quiet and direct, stripped of ornament. They use words for things that can't be felt—abstract concepts, memories, the names of the dead—but for everything else, there's the Vey. A Veyfolk asking if you're hungry might tap the table twice; a Veyfolk warning you that a platform is unstable might simply look at it and then at you, and you'll feel the warning in the way the air pressure changes around them. They are a people who have learned to trust their bodies as instruments, and they expect you to do the same.

This is why they seem, at first, almost telepathic. They're not. They're just paying attention in ways you've forgotten how to.

## The Work

Everyone works. There is no class of people who don't—no aristocrats, no scholars lost in thought, no children kept from labor. The Vey doesn't allow it. The system is too tightly wound, too interdependent; every person is a component, and components that don't function are removed. This sounds harsh, but it isn't, not in practice. The Veyfolk understand work as participation, as belonging. To work is to be part of the breathing, to contribute your rhythm to the whole. A person who cannot work—the very old, the very sick—is cared for absolutely, but everyone knows they are waiting, and the waiting has its own dignity.

The work is varied but specialized. You can tell what someone does by looking at their hands, their posture, the particular way they move through the Vey's layered geography. The sluice-tenders have a patient stillness, a way of standing that suggests they're listening to water they can't see. They spend their shifts at the gate controls, making micro-adjustments, their hands always slightly damp, their fingernails permanently stained with mineral deposits. They speak in terms of flow and pressure and the subtle art of not-quite-stopping the water, of guiding it, of letting it do what it wants within the constraints of what the Vey needs.

The fungal cultivators are the Vey's gardeners, and their work is the most intimate. They spend their days in the warm, dark alcoves where the fungus grows, tending the beds with their bare hands, breathing in spores that have been part of their bodies for so long that their lungs have adapted. They have a way of touching the fungus that seems almost tender, a slow stroking that they say encourages growth. Maybe it does. They are the ones most heavily marked, their arms a dense tapestry of patterns that shift and ripple as they move. They are also the quietest of the Veyfolk, as if the fungus has taught them something about patience that the rest are still learning.

The builders are the Vey's engineers, though "engineer" implies too much planning, too much abstraction. They are improvisers, problem-solvers, people who spend their days clambering through the scaffolding of the settlement, reinforcing a joint here, re-routing a pipe there, carving back the rock to make room for something new. They work with the materials the Vey provides—timber harvested from the surface (a whole other trade, one that requires climbing up through the passage and into a world the Veyfolk find disorienting and vast), vine-rope cultivated for strength, stone quarried from the walls, metal scavenged from somewhere no one quite remembers. They build fast and rough and then refine, refining, refining, until the structure becomes part of the Vey's rhythm rather than an intrusion on it.

And then there are the ones who don't have a single specialty, the generalists, the ones who move between tasks as the cycle demands. They are the Vey's connective tissue, the ones who fill gaps, who notice when something is about to break because they happened to be passing, who know a little about everything and are trusted to make small decisions without consulting anyone. They are often the youngest adults, still finding their place, or the oldest, who have done everything and are now beyond category. They are also, often, the ones who talk to outsiders, the ones with the patience to translate the Vey's rhythms into words.

## The Body

They touch each other constantly. Not in any dramatic way—a hand on a shoulder as someone passes, a brief press of foreheads when meeting, a palm laid flat against a back during moments of rest. The Vey is a place of vibration, and touch is how you share it. A Veyfolk pressing their hand to your arm is telling you something: *I am here, you are here, we are together in this place.* It's not affection, exactly, though affection is part of it. It's information. It's presence.

They sleep in stacked platforms, close together, bodies curled against each other for warmth and comfort. There is no privacy in sleep, and they don't expect it. A Veyfolk waking in the night might find a child crawled in beside them, or an elder whose breathing has changed in a way that needs attention, or simply the warm weight of a neighbor's arm across their chest. They sleep deeply and wake easily, attuned even in rest to the Vey's shifts.

Their food is simple: fungus in various preparations, crustaceans from the river, the pale fish from the tanks, roots and shoots from the cultivated beds, a kind of flatbread made from ground root-fiber. They eat together, in the small clearings between structures, passing bowls and cups without ceremony. They don't seem to taste food the way you do—for them, eating is about the warmth, the rhythm of shared consumption, the way the Vey's heat moves through the body as a result. They eat slowly, unhurriedly, and they always leave something for the next person.

They age differently. In the Vey's constant warmth, with its constant pressure, with its constant rhythm, the body seems to reach its peak early and then slow. You see Veyfolk who look thirty but are fifty, who look fifty but are seventy. Their skin stays pale, their limbs stay compact, their eyes stay wide and watchful. The only signs of age are the marks—they darken, deepen, become almost black as the years accumulate—and the way their voices change, dropping lower, becoming less speech and more a kind of low hum, as if they're gradually becoming part of the Vey's soundscape. The oldest Veyfolk rarely speak at all. They sit in the warm alcoves and listen, and sometimes, when the Vey is doing something especially important, they nod.

They die at about the same age as anywhere else, but their deaths are different. A dying Veyfolk will often ask to be brought to a specific location—a particular catwalk, a particular fungal alcove, a particular place along the river's lower reaches. They want to be where the Vey is doing what it needs to do, so that their last breaths participate in the cycle. Afterward, their bodies are returned to the fungus beds, because the fungus is the Vey's stomach, and the Vey recycles everything. Their marks—the patterns in their skin—are carefully noted by the eldest before the body is given over. Sometimes the patterns are reproduced in the walls, grown into the fungus for a season, before fading.

## The Fear

They don't talk about it, not directly, but you can feel it in the way they move, in the way they touch you, in the way their eyes track things that aren't quite there. The Vey is young. It is still becoming. And they know—the way you know the floor beneath you is solid even when you can't see it—that the Vey is doing something. Something with the markings. Something with the light. Something with the slow, patient intelligence that they can sense in the walls, in the water, in the patterns of their own skin.

They know the Vey is watching. They don't know what it wants. They know they are part of it, cells in an organism that is trying to become, and they know their work, their cycles, their dense, hot, jammed-together lives are the medium in which whatever the Vey is becoming is happening. They wait. They maintain their rhythms. They touch each other, and eat together, and sleep in stacked platforms, and listen to the hum of the distillation vats. And sometimes, when the light shifts in a way no one has seen before, they look at the newest marks on the youngest skin, and they wonder what the Vey is asking them to become.

## The Stranger

When a stranger comes—like you—they are welcomed. They will show you the safest paths, the clearest routes. They will share their food and their warmth. They will touch you, hand on arm, hand on shoulder, press their marks against your unmarked skin, and say: *You are here. You are part of this now, whether you want to be or not.*

And then they'll watch you. Not obviously—they're too polite for that—but with that peripheral-wide gaze, tracking the way your body moves through their space, the way your rhythm does or doesn't align with the Vey's own. They'll wait to see if marks begin to appear on your skin. They'll wait to see what the Vey is trying to learn from you.

Because you are new. You are strange. You carry a different rhythm, a different heat, a different way of breathing. And the Vey is young, still becoming, and it wants to know what you are, what you can teach it, what part of itself you might help it discover.

The Veyfolk will show you their home. They will feed you, and shelter you, and touch you, and wait. And if you're lucky, if the Vey's patterns allow it, you'll begin to feel what they feel: the warm, patient, watching presence of a place that is thinking itself into being, using everything it has—stone, water, heat, fungus, animal, and people—to become what it is meant to be.

And if you're luckier still, you might catch a glimpse of what that is. But probably not. The Vey is patient. It has time. And you are just passing through.
