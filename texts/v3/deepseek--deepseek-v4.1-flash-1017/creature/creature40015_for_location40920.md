**The Gauger**

The thing that lives on the Weeping Shelf is not hidden. It is simply made of the same patience as the stone. Call it the Gauger. It is man-high and a little more, upright but not quite a person, its body a stack of dry slabs fitted without mortar. The seams are iron-red. The shoulders have been rounded by centuries of water. It has no face, only a shallow basin where a face would be, brim-full, and the reflection in it shows the softer weather the pools keep. Its hands are five hairline channels each, running with a glassy cord; when it raises one, the water climbs the wrong way along the fingers and hangs there, deciding.

It was one of the ones who laid the low walls and cut the channels. That one is long gone into the agreement of the place; the Gauger is what remains, or what the Shelf made of the remaining. It carries a stone wedge worn shapeless and no longer cuts with it. It attends. It walks at the pace of seep, and because distances are not kept here it is often already where it is going. The early echo is its doing: it takes your footfall and returns it before you have finished, so that you are always being answered by the step you have not yet taken. It sets the cairns, each slightly wrong in its balance, not to mark places but to keep intervals. It does not count days. It counts the pause between booms, the length of a mist bank, the moment a tilted pool decides not to spill, the hesitation of a drop before it rises. The channels are its notation — a scribble it does not need to read.

It lives in the throat of the rock that drums, and under the overhangs where the drips bead upward, and in the half-hut that is no longer a hut, standing among the grey splinters in the postholes as if listening to a rope. Now and then it hauls on a line that goes over the rim into cloud. There is no bucket on the end. The rope creaks under load anyway. It is measuring the weight of the lower weather.

It does not eat; it drinks mist through its surface. It does not sleep; it stands in the drumming throat and lets the boom go through it. It is not hostile and not kind. It has no use for visitors and no objection to them. You are an irregularity — a temporary dam, a new channel, a wrong tilt in a terrace that agrees with itself. It will not drive you off. It will fail to include you, and you will find the Shelf has moved around you. If you move a stone, it will be put back, or a cairn will be left one stone shorter and still balanced. If you speak, your voice will come back early. If you stand still long enough, you may feel the boom pass through your soles and know that something is keeping time.

It is one of it and many of it: each pool a hand, each cairn a knuckle, each thread of water a nerve. It is the Shelf’s witness and its reader, and it has been in the middle of the sentence for a very long time. It does not require you to finish it. It only requires that you not disturb the agreement more than the water can forgive.
