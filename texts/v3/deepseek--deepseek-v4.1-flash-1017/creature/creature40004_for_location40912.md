What lives in the High Separation is not one thing you could point to and call a body. It is a scatter of pale colonies—patches the size of a palm, grey-white threads with a faint pink beneath, felted into the caulking seams and the roots of the bolt heads and the perforated lip of the cable tray. No leaves. No roots in earth. No water in it or near it. It holds to the room the way frost holds to glass, except that frost is a thing happening to water and this is a thing happening to sound.

It is alive the way a tuning fork is alive after it has been struck—except it does not fade.

Call one patch a creature if you like, but it is better understood as a settlement. The threads branch and rejoin; the mat thickens into itself; there is no head, no mouth, no organ you could isolate and say *here is where it decides*. Its whole surface is its ear and its stomach and its hand. It has no eyes because there is nothing to see but four steady fixtures and bare plate. It has no need for eyes. Its weather is pressure. Its daylight is the standing wave.

It eats the roar.

Not metaphorically, or not only metaphorically. The threads take the vibration and spend it—fold the room’s huge, unchanging pressure into whatever slow chemistry is available to a thing that lives on motion. Cup a patch with your hand and the sound under your palm softens by a degree you feel in the wrist. That is the creature feeding. Where it is thickest, the wave is weaker by a percent or two: a dead spot in the pattern, a small soft place in the roar. The engineers measured that once and then stopped. The plant’s tone has a tolerance. The growth is inside the tolerance. It has always been inside the tolerance.

It is not a plague. It is an equilibrium. It grows exactly as thick as the sound allows—no thicker—because past a certain thickness its own softness starves the threads beneath. It damps the thing that feeds it. So it stops. It has been exactly that thick for as long as the room has existed, which is not long, and also forever. There is no bloom and crash in its life, no season, no swarm. There is only the slow arithmetic of patches set out according to some private logic of nodes and seams, each one a small negative of the noise.

It does not drink. It does not nest. It does not visit. It has no parasites and no predators, because nothing else lives here. No flies circle the fixtures; no rats work the insulation; no birds rest on the stacks outside. The only living things in the High Separation are these pale mats, and they are not alive in any way that would reassure you. They do not seem to die. They do not seem to be born. They accrete where the wave is right, and then they hold.

Its senses are strain and shear. It feels the room as a continuous deformation—the low groan carried in the plant’s bones, the mid shriek off the duct, the fine high ringing off the perforated sheet at the crown of the head. It does not hear these as separate sounds. It lives inside their braid. A change in the wave would reach it as hunger or as surfeit, as a leaning, as a slow ache in the threads. But there is no change. The tone does not drift. The light does not flicker. The cold does not shift. The creature’s world is a condition, not an event.

And it is patient in a way that makes patience look like panic. At the rate the threads take sound, it will need a hundred thousand years to make this place quiet. It will get there. It is not in a hurry. It is not going anywhere. It is not spreading, not thickening, not changing—only working its slow arithmetic against the noise, one palm-sized patch at a time, turning the plant’s enormous unbroken voice into a faint pink under grey-white threads.

If the tone stopped, the room would be destroyed. So would the growth, though more quietly. Silence would be starvation. But the tone has not stopped. The stacks go up into air that is barely there; the works are louder than weather; the growth sits in the thin cold and eats. That is what lives there: a distributed, deaf, patient thing that has no name, no face, and no hurry—a soft place in the roar, exactly as large as the roar allows.
