The Kiln Gardens have no single resident. What lives there is a household of specialists—small, damp, patient things that have learned to make a living off warmth, mineral water, and the slow metabolism of a machine that never quite stopped. A human moving through is a temporary event. The permanent population is quieter and more distributed.

The most numerous inhabitants are not animals at all. They are the sulfur-eating mats on the wet brick: single-celled things growing in sheets and slicks, giving off just enough greenish light to see the next three steps. They are the foundation of the place’s visible life. Where they thicken into a carpet, pale stalked colonies rise toward the pipes—plant-shaped but not plants, topped with little nodding bells open at the bottom. They filter the air. They are cool and slightly slimy to the touch, and they close when disturbed. They do not bloom; they simply persist, nodding in the slow exhalations of the corridors.

With them live the fungal residents: hard shelf-growths on the walls, gray and pink and cream, layered like wet pages. They prefer the hottest, wettest rooms, where water pools and the pipes radiate. Dry, they are brittle; wet, rubbery. They smell of bread and then of something sweeter and less pleasant underneath. They are not decorative. They are tenants, breaking down what the moisture and heat bring them.

The water is inhabited too. Not dangerously, but persistently. Pale things swim and crawl in the standing pools and the channels cut into the floor—small enough to be seen only when they catch the green light, with too many legs or no legs at all. They are part of why the water tastes mineral and alive. In the ankle-deep sumps lives something the size of a hand, shelled, patient, grazing the fine white silt. It moves slowly and does not care about you.

Insects are individual, not swarming: a papery-winged flyer, a wall-crawler, a thing that will crawl over your hand and teach you not to mind. Larger animals are rare and shy. One is cat-sized, and you will mostly know it as a shape moving away from you and a sound like a wet cloth dragged over stone. Another nests in the upper pipes and might be a bird, or might be something that evolved into the shape of one. Every few hours it calls—one low note, not a song. It sounds like a question, or an answer, or neither.

And then there are the people. They are not residents, exactly. They come down from the red plateau and the dry country beyond, quiet and purposeful, carrying skins and vessels. They go to the deepest, hottest rooms for water that has passed through the whole machine and come out changed—warm, mineral, useful. They have their own worn paths, their own marks on the walls, their own small repairs: patched insulation, grey cement smoother and harder than the old mortar, offerings left in certain rooms. They do not stay long, and they will not show you their routes. But they still come. Someone still checks the bolts on the iron stair. The Gardens are not abandoned and never were.

The nearest thing to a landlord is the heat itself—the deep source still feeding the pipes, still burning or ticking or circulating below the accessible corridors. It is not alive, but it behaves like a host. It keeps the rooms blood-warm. It condenses water on the vaults and sends fat drops into the floor cups. It has made a habitat out of its own old purpose, and the residents have arranged themselves around it: the mats where the condensate runs, the bells where the air moves, the fungi where the heat is strongest, the shelled grazer where the silt collects, the bird-thing in the upper pipes, the cat-shape in the gaps, the people at the edges, taking water and leaving repairs.

So the Kiln Gardens are inhabited by a slow, layered tenancy: microbial light-makers, air-filtering bells, shelf fungi, pale water-things, a handful of shy animals, and the quiet human visitors who know the worn paths and keep some of the bolts tight. Nothing there is trying to help you. Nothing there is trying to hurt you. They are all simply living in the warm, wet, settling rooms of a machine that has become a world—and doing so without needing anyone to tell them it is time.
