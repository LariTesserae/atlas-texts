What lives on the Levelled does not announce itself as a population. It announces itself as ground.

Call it the crust. It has no name for itself and no need of one. It is the pavement you have been walking on since you came up over the rim, and it has been walking on itself—settling, breathing, healing—for longer than the stone has been flat.

The individuals, if the word holds, are small and soft and many-legged: dust-coloured on top, faintly translucent beneath, most of them thumb-sized but varying so continuously in size that “size” begins to feel like a mistake. The top layer is matte and finely granular, like a pinch of hardpan. The underside is pale enough to show a dark bead pulsing once a minute, and a slow shadow moving through the body that might be a gut or might be the body deciding to be a different shape. They have no eyes. They have pale spots that darken when light falls on them. They have no head, no front, no direction. Their legs are short and numerous and fringed, and they are not arranged for walking so much as for holding and sensing. In torpor they tuck them under and around their neighbours, and the legs knit the layers together.

They lie three, four, five deep. Near-touching. Overlapping. A living pavement that runs past the horizon and probably does not stop at the horizon. They are not a swarm; a swarm has individuals. They are a surface with local thickenings. A large one is not old—it is a place where the separation has not happened yet. A small one is not young—it is a place where the crust has thinned. Lift one carefully and it may part in your hand: two, then three. The cut edge pales to dust and closes. The leg you are holding may belong to a body you cannot find. Put the pieces down and they join whatever is nearest. They do not struggle. They do not bleed. They are cool and soft and heavier than they look, like a piece of felt left in a cold room, and they settle into your palm as if your hand were a temporary improvement on the ground.

They do not eat in any way you would recognise. They press their undersides to the mineral film on the stone, to the grey-green lichen-like rind that grows on the north sides of rocks at perhaps a millimetre a century, to the edges of each other, and take what is there by a process closer to staining than feeding. They do not grow in numbers. They do not swarm. When a patch becomes too thick it separates; when a gap opens the margins extend and close. They bud and merge and split. There are no dead. When one stops—if one stops—the body loses its boundary, dries, and becomes gravel. The gravel is the ground. The ground is made of them, or they are made of the ground, and the distinction is the kind this place does not support.

Their most visible activity is breathing. The whole surface rises and falls in unison, one slow tide a minute long. It is the largest motion on the plateau. Put your palm down and you can feel it: not a heartbeat under your hand but a slow lifting of the ground, as if the plain itself were asleep and dreaming of lungs. When you walk, you press through several layers. The pressure travels outward as a ring of small bodies uncoupling, curling, resettling. The ring goes ten metres and dies. Within a minute the crust is smooth. Within an hour there is no mark. They do not flee, because there is nowhere better. They do not pursue, because there is nothing to chase. They are not waiting for rain, because rain is not a category here. They are not waiting for spring. They are at equilibrium. Persistence has become their form.

The other native is the lichen, or the thing that is not quite lichen. It is a grey-green rind on the north faces of stones, a garnish on a very old table. It may be what the crust eats when it eats. It may be eating the crust. It is hard to tell, and the place does not encourage the question.

Then there are the makers. They are the only *who* here, and they are not here. They left rings of upright slabs, knee-high, with a flat stone in the middle and no indication of what it was for. They left dry-stone walls thirty paces long that run out into nothing and stop—not because they were unfinished, but because the ending was the point. They left cairns on rises that barely rise, hollows lined with flat stones and roofed with more flat stones, big enough for one person to lie in and be cold. They left bone tools, and the bone is not from here. It came up from below, carried by someone who climbed for two days and then sat in the quiet and carved a small, careful thing and left it in a stone ring. They used no metal. They built without mortar. They chose stone because stone is final.

We do not know what they were doing. They may have come to finish a grief, or keep a vow, or count something until the counting failed. They cannot have lived here long. There is no water. They must have carried it up, and gone down when it ran out. They lived in ones and twos, a day or two apart. They must have learned not to shout, because a shout goes out and does not come back. They must have learned to listen to the slow tide of the crust and to mistake it, sometimes, for company. Their works have not decayed, because there is nothing here to decay them. They are exactly as old as they look, and they will look the same in a thousand years. The makers are gone. Their patience is not.

So the Levelled is inhabited in two registers. Underfoot, the crust: a living pavement, uncountable, unindividual, breathing once a minute out to the hard horizon. Above it, the makers: a scatter of hands, a few stone rooms, a bone thing in a ring. The place does not distinguish between them. It absorbs disturbance. It smooths the ripple. It lets the shout go out. If you ask who lives here, the answer is the plain, wearing a skin of small soft animals; and the people who came up to be quiet, and left their quiet behind.
