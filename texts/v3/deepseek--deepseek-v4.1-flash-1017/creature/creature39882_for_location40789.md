What lives there lives in layers, because the place itself is layered. It is not one habitat but a vertical city with no empty rooms.

In the canopy, where the sky has been swallowed, the residents cling to the underside of leaves and the tops of branches as if to the ceiling of a crowded house. There are gliding things with loose flaps of skin and long, anxious tails; fruit-eaters that move in noisy bands and scream at shadows; sap-drinkers with proboscises fine as hairs; beetles like thrown coins, iridescent and clumsy; clouds of midges that rise from every warm pocket of air. Things that look like winged seeds drift down through the green light, and then land, and bite. The canopy is loud because it is full. Every branch is a thoroughfare, a nursery, a hunting ground, a toilet, a corpse.

The Weavers live in the mid-canopy, and they are the closest thing here to a *who*. A Weaver at rest can look like a knot of lianas, a fibrous bundle caught in a fork of branches. When it moves, the knot unfolds into something long-limbed and insectile, with too many joints and a face that is mostly sensitive filament. Its body is not fixed. It can stiffen into vine, loosen into limb, flatten against bark, or hang in a loose tangle that the eye keeps trying to read asvegetation. They do not build so much as persuade. They coax special vines into bridges, shelters, blinds, and signal-lines, grafting stem to stem and feeding the joints with pulp until the plant accepts the shape. Then they drum on hollow wood—click, pause, rattle, pause, a percussive speech that moves through the forest faster than a body can. Their bridges sag and are eaten by fungi; their shelters rot from the inside; their paths are swallowed before they are worn. So they rebuild. A Weaver structure is lucky to last a handful of days. Their whole culture is a frantic, beautiful negotiation with a world that is always, always winning.

Below them, the understory is a nursery and a battlefield. Squat amphibians the color of wet bark croak from hidden pools. Hunting spiders mimic fallen flowers. Parasitic vines feel their way up young trees and strangle them slowly. There are insects that snip leaves with impossible precision, ants that farm fungus in rotting wood, wasps that stitch nests from chewed fiber. Everything is either hiding, hunting, or being consumed. The air is thick with the smell of crushed green and the coppery tang of blood and sap, and the two are not always easy to tell apart.

On the ground, the residents are pale and blind and quick. Things scuttle through the leaf litter on too many legs. Worms as long as a forearm surface after rain and vanish again. Fungi bloom in soft, sexual shapes overnight, then collapse into black jelly. The leaf litter is not dead matter; it is a crowded nursery and a dismantling yard, where beetles, bacteria, and roots compete to take the fallen world apart. Every footfall lands on something living or something recently alive. The ground trembles faintly with root growth, with water shifting under peat, with the deep sub-audible engine of the place.

The black river has its own nation. Ribbon-fish move like oiled shadows. Something amphibious and guttural hauls itself onto a collapsing bank and sings. Insects skate on the surface; larvae hang beneath it; things with too many eyes wait in the murk for things with too few. When the river carves a new channel overnight, it takes residents with it and leaves new ones stranded in the mud.

A human can visit, but not settle. The forest does not hate human presence; it simply outgrows it. A cut path closes. A shelter sprouts. A fire is remembered as a clearing and then erased by a dozen new species battling for the light. The Weavers might watch a human with curiosity—a large, slow animal with an odd drum—but they would not mistake it for a master of the place. They know better. They have been negotiating with the forest for longer than any human record, and they have no monuments to show for it.

So what lives there? Everything, in too great a number. And one people—the Weavers—who have made a culture out of being temporary: who speak in drumbeats, grow their bridges, abandon their shelters, and rebuild in the warm wet breath of a world that never fully exhales.
