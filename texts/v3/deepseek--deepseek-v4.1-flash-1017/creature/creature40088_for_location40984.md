# The Conderfolk

Conder is a town of short, dense people, built like the town itself: low, layered, hard to knock over. Altitude takes height the way it takes breath, so a Conder body spends itself on width instead — barrel chests, thick necks, short legs that climb stair-lanes all day without complaint and refuse to run. They walk with one hand out. On a wall, on a rail, on the shoulder of whoever's ahead. It is not affection, or not only; it is ballast. The paths are wet stone and the drop is close and the fog eats the edges of things. An outsider can be spotted at fifty paces: they touch a wall and then look at their hand.

Their skin is pale, fog-colored, never quite reached by the sun, and their hands are wrinkled at twenty — gully hands, puckered and soft and always a little cold, the fingertips worked over by years of wet stone. Their voices are low. In the fog a shout comes back at you from the wrong direction, so the Conderfolk talk quietly and listen long, and a conversation between two people on opposite walls of the gully sounds like one person thinking aloud. They last, mostly. The damp gets into the joints and stays, and by sixty most of them creak, but the ones who last, last. The old are moved down. In Conder the young take the cold upper rooms and the old go down into the warm houses near the seeps, and it is the closest thing the town has to an inheritance: you spend your life climbing, and at the end the mountain is allowed to warm you.

Conder keeps time by water. The gauge-house stands where the channels converge, a squat stone box with brass float-meters inside and a ledger in oilcloth, and the ledger has two inks — one for the flows, one for the dead. The woman who keeps it is called the Reader, and her name is Tev, and children learn it before they learn their own, because *Tev says* is what their mothers say instead of *the meter says*. Tev rings the bell when the flow crosses a threshold. Tev writes the morning's beading in the ledger. Tev holds water-rights of exactly nothing — by law the Reader's hands stay out of the troughs, so that no one can say she tilted the scale — and so she keeps the only dry room in Conder, a small hot box at the top of the gauge-house with a stove and a chair and a window that fogs. People come to stand in it. She lets them. They stand in the dry and steam gently and say nothing, and then they go back out into the weather, and it is the closest thing the town has to a church.

Everything else is lattice and family. The Vanes hold the north ridge, where the fog lies thickest and a good night wets the mesh to a heavy gray nap. The Orrs hold the Long Lattice, two hundred strides of strung fiber across a gully mouth, mended so many times it is more knot than mesh. The newer families hold the scaffolds on the western shelves, which drip in a good year and stand dry and mocking in a bad one. Everyone counts everything, always, in drips per hour. A dowry is stated in liters per fog-night. A feud is a disputed half-meter of channel. Children can hear a rate before they can read one — can stand under a lattice with their eyes closed and tell you whether it is wetting fast or slow or sulking — and this is thought a more useful accomplishment than letters, and is taught first. The town's constant sound is the hiss of fog against mesh and the mutter of the pumps, and the Conderfolk can hear a torn panel by the change in its hiss.

The lace-men and lace-women climb the frames at night, when the fog is thickest, silhouetted on the ridges in their harnesses with spools on their belts, and the sound of them mending carries down into the town like someone very patient playing a harp badly. They know every gauge bird's nest in the mesh and mend around them. The birds are small and brown and tolerated, because they eat the moths.

A Conder house is a wet house. Floors slope to a drain; roofs are collectors; gutters are channels; every surface above knee height is a surface water touches, and everything below it is something the water has been at for years. Beds are raised on stone legs. Boots are never dry and nobody expects them to be. The smell of the town is wet felt — the mill at the bottom fulls it in great gray sheets, and half of Conder is wearing it — and wet wool and wet stone and, near the seeps, the iron smell of the warm cracks, which people call the mountain's breath and have long stopped noticing. The mill turns the pumps that lift water back up the terraces and the hammers that full the felt, and hums, and never stops. The bath-houses are fed from the exhalations: three of them, the oldest called the Long Soak, where the water comes out of the rock already warm and smelling of iron and old coins. The old sit in it for hours. The children are scrubbed in it until they are pink, which is the only time anyone in Conder is pink. The three greenhouses on the north wall stand over the hottest seeps and they steam; there are tomatoes in there, and peppers, and one lemon tree grown from a seed somebody's grandmother carried up from the low country. The greenhouse families are pale and mild and smell of green things, and the town treats them as slightly sacred and slightly indecent, the way a wet place treats anything that wants sun.

Conder children are quiet, sure-footed, damp at the knees. They race water-beetles in the rock basins and lose them. They learn knots before letters. They are allowed anywhere in town except the deep seeps, and they are told the Cropper will crop *them* if they go too far up the gullies — a lie that keeps them close, and which no parent is entirely sure is a lie. At twelve a child is given their first lattice: a square of mesh on a frame no bigger than a window, angled to the fog, to be mended, read, and recorded. The first drip a child catches off their own lattice is a small ceremony. The family comes. The drip is measured. The number is written down.

The moths come to the lamps that burn at all hours, pale and soft and heavy with the damp, and beat themselves against the glass until dawn or death. The mill eels live in the millpond and nobody knows how they got up here; they are fed, not eaten, and the miller's children name them every year and give them the same names. In the warm seeps live the flat blind things the town calls embers, unhurried, feeling their way along the stone, and the bath-house keepers keep them in the cisterns because they clean the walls, and nobody eats them, because they come out of the warm water and the warm water is the mountain's, and you do not eat what the mountain makes warm. Children catch them anyway. They die in jars. There is a small penance for this and it is not enforced.

And up in the deepest gullies, where the moss is thickest, the Cropper browses. Something large and slow and low, furred with a pelt that holds water and grows moss in it, so that from a distance it is a boulder that has moved. It crops the ferns in a neat line and leaves prints that fade by afternoon, and it is seen perhaps twice a season and never photographed, because the light is dim and it is patient and it does not want to be. The lace-men find its hair in the upper mesh. Some say it is the last of something larger. Some say it is the mountain's tongue, come up to taste whether the water is going where it should. The town does not mention it to outsiders.

When a Conder person dies they are carried up to the highest warm crack and laid in, and the mountain takes them down into its motion, and their name goes into the ledger in the second ink. Once a year, on the wettest night, the Reader reads the names aloud in the same voice she reads the flows, and the bell is rung once for each, and the town stands in the fog and listens to itself being remembered.

The geologists came once, and wrote that the place had stopped arguing with the sky, and left. The Conderfolk, who had never started, did not understand the compliment.

Nothing in Conder is in a hurry. Everything in Conder is moving — the bead, the drip, the run, the soak, the warm rise, the fog — and so are the dead, who are in the water now, going down through the cracks and up as vapor and onto the mesh and into the troughs and into the ledger and into the Reader's voice on the wettest night. The mountain remembers where the water should go. The town remembers who it was carrying.
