**The winterers.** They're not a tribe and not a colony — they're a season-job, a few dozen people who climb the pass in the third week of the hard freeze, before the snow shuts it, and come down again when the ice above the basin starts calving in earnest. Some families come every year for four generations; some come once and never again; a few come up and simply stay down, meaning they stay up, meaning they don't go back, and everybody knows what that means and doesn't say it. They are ordinary people — the same stock as the lowland towns, no long bones, no special lungs — which is the point. Anybody could do it. Almost nobody does.

They bring everything they need on their backs because nothing up there can be made except stone, hide, and fire. Wood is the precious thing: every beam and pole and ladder-rail in the Kettle was carried up at some point in the last two hundred years, and they are carried up again when a surge takes one, and they are carved all over with the marks of the households that have held them — not names, mostly, just notches and hands and the little boat-shape that means *this came up with us*. Beams get inherited. A beam is a better thing to leave your daughter than a ring.

**The work.** They are drainers and ferrymen and gutter-keepers, and that is nearly the whole of it. Every shelter is a boat turned upside down and set on stone piers — a raised floor, a lip, a gutter, a chute that sends the water where they've decided it should go this week. They rebuild the chutes constantly. The channels move; the chutes must move with them; the whole rim-town is a negotiation conducted in wet stone, and it is layered — this year's repair on last year's repair on the year before's — so that the walls of the oldest shelters are banded like the inside of a tree, and you can read the bad years in them the way you'd read a scar.

Their tools are short: hook-staffs for testing depth and pulling a person out, stone mauls, bone awls, scrapers, and rope. Rope is the true craft. Rope is money up there, rope is manners, rope is the thing you give a new household and the thing you are buried in. Everyone carries a coil over the shoulder and everyone knows how to tie a line to a person in the water in under four seconds, and they practice this the way lowland children practice letters.

They dress in hide over hide, waterproofed with fat until it stinks and then until it stops stinking, and they cut the glare with slit-plates of horn strapped across their eyes — the old ones wear them even at dusk, out of habit, and look like they're always squinting at something a long way off. They keep their hair short and their hands greasy. They are, all of them, extremely good at getting up.

**Manners.** They don't stop the water. This is not a philosophy they'd articulate; it's a habit so deep it's stopped being a rule. You don't dam, you don't still a pool to see into it, you don't swim in the deep water, and you don't name a surge before it has happened — surges are named afterward, and the naming is a small ceremony, and the name is usually stupid and funny, and this is how the Kettle is thanked.

Speech up there is close and low and mostly hands, because the basin eats sound and because the birds have taught them that chattering carries. At dusk the whole rim gathers on the high shelf and the names are called, and the count has to come out right. If it doesn't, ropes go out, and no one eats until it does. That is the entire legal system.

The government of the Kettle is one person with their hand in the water. Usually an old one, usually someone who has wintered there longer than they've wintered anywhere else, and their whole office is sitting at the edge of a warm runnel for an hour before dawn with their forearm submerged, feeling the pulse — the low human hum of pressure moving under the basin — and saying, at some point, *now*, or *not yet*. They can call a surge a minute or two ahead. A minute or two is everything. Nobody argues with them because nobody has ever had a reason to, and when they die they are replaced by whoever has sat beside them longest, which is how you'd choose a bell-ringer.

**Eating, and the neighbors.** They eat the shrimp-things — the pale crustaceans that mat the vent-edges so thick the stone looks alive — boiled by the vent itself, in baskets, which is the one luxury: free heat, always. They eat the grass seed out of the runners' caches when the runners have been extravagant, and they eat the birds, which are stringy and hoarse and taste of the water. They do not eat the pale shapes in the deep pools, and this is a rule with no explanation attached, which is the strongest kind.

The runners live in the shelters as much as the people do. They cache in the wall-courses, they steal the boot-linings, they are underfoot and unafraid and treated with a grumbling tolerance that is one part pest-control and one part affection. Children are given the job of counting them, which is a joke, because you cannot count them. The birds nest in the crevices above the waterline and clatter all night and are never quiet and are never, ever singing, and after a winter up there a person finds the lowlands unbearably loud and also unbearably silent, both at once, and can't explain it.

**The old ones** who stay up. They aren't abandoned, exactly. There's a shelter above the highest recorded surge, on the dry rim, and it's kept stocked and it's kept swept, and whoever has decided to stop coming down moves into it, and the others visit, and bring them shrimp, and ask them what the water's doing, because a person who has lived inside the hum for forty years can feel things the listeners can't. When they die, they're given to the water below a vent, or, if the family prefers, carried down in the thaw — and the people who carry them down are considered to have done a hard kindness, and the people who give them to the water are considered to have done the other kind.

That's the population of the Kettle: a few dozen drainers, some rope, a lot of shrimp, a hundred runners under the floor, birds that sound like two stones knocked together, pale things in the deep water that are not to be followed, and one old person with their arm in a runnel, deciding. The Kettle itself is the last of them, and the winterers are quite clear that they are guests — rent paid in gutters, in labor, in getting up early — and that the thing they call the Kettle by the same word they call the place was there before the beams and will be there after, humming, courteous, thorough, and not stopping for anybody.
