The one who lives there is a solitary keeper—human in shape, but shaped by the room until the room seems to have grown them. They are smallish and broad-handed, with a permanent slight stoop from ducking under the central pillar and the bone trellises. Their skin has the pallor of things kept under green glass: not sickly, but washed thin and luminous, as if the lamp had slowly bleached the ordinary colors out of them. Their hair is kept short or bound back with cordage twisted from the tree’s woody pods. Their hands are the clearest record of the place—knuckles thickened, palms calloused, nails cut blunt, the creases stained grey-green from hydroponic water and dark with bronze polish.

They dress for work, not for company: a sleeveless tunic of pod-fiber cloth, a stiff apron of laminated leaf-material, soft shell-soled sandals or bare feet that know exactly where the concrete turns slick. They smell of warm mineral water, wet concrete, crushed waxy leaves, and the faint tinny sweetness of bronze. Their voice is quiet and a little hoarse, not from disuse exactly, but from being used mostly to mutter counts, to soothe a sticking valve, to answer the dome when it groans.

They are a gardener and a mechanic in the same body. Their day is not marked by sunrise—there is no sunrise—but by the fan’s great rhythmic *whoosh*, by the slow accumulation of condensation, by the leak that must be checked, mopped, paned, and watched. They wake with the pressure in their teeth and knees. They know the dome’s moods by sound: the healthy thrum, the fretful tick of a seal, the deeper moan that means a cable is straining against a current. They can hear a change in the ventilation before they can name it. They move through the round room in habitual arcs, never crossing it straight, checking the vines, turning the fruit pods, wiping the pale leaves free of dust, sweeping water into the gutters, hanging tools back in their exact rows.

They are not lonely in the ordinary human way. They have the sea for company—a vast, warm, breathing presence pressed against the glass, never silent, never still. The keeper talks to it sometimes without meaning to, the way one talks to weather. They are patient with it and a little wary. They know its patience is larger than theirs. When they sleep, it is under a cloth over the eyes, because the lamp never dims, and they dream in the fan’s rhythm: intake, exhaust, intake, exhaust, the slow pulse of a small bright lung sunk in the sea.

They are tender toward the meager plants and unsentimental about them. The vines are coworkers; the squat tree is a slow, stubborn companion whose pods must be harvested for cordage. The keeper’s affection shows in maintenance—in clean water, trimmed roots, polished tools, a bronze pan set exactly under the daily leak. They are the kind of person who trusts a worn workbench more than a promise. Their idea of wildness is the immense, patient pressure outside the glass, and their idea of home is a noisy, warm, orderly room where every surface is made and every process is watched.

If they have a religion, it is upkeep. If they have a conversation, it is with the structure: the hiss, the thrum, the groan, the leak. They are the resident the place selected for—a quiet, salt-smelling, methodical creature of bright isolation, half gardener, half engineer, wholly at home in a machine for living that floats inside the living sea.
