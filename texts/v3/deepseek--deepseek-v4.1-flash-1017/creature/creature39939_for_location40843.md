They are not many — a few hundred, maybe fewer, with the count shifting as people come up from the plain and others climb out for good. They call themselves Kettlers, and they say it with the flatness of people who have decided not to be embarrassed by where they live.

They are a damp, warm, close-living people, and the first thing you notice about them is that they are never quite dry and never quite comfortable about it. Their skin has the powdery white bloom of salt in the creases of the knuckles and at the hairline. Their hair goes stiff at the ends. They wear oiled canvas and salt-stiffened serge, and they keep a second set of clothes sealed in a chest against the day they need to be dry for an hour — that chest is a small inheritance, passed down. Boots are thick-soled and unlaced indoors, because the floor of the lower galleries is warm enough to be felt through leather.

Because the wet is in pockets and the dry is in pockets, they live a two-sided life. A Kettler works in the damp — the condenser sheds, the sumps, the drip channels, the furnace floor where the air is like breath — and then climbs, by ladder and catwalk, to a dry room high in the cavity wall, where a fissure leaks a thin stream of hot, parched air out of the rock. They sleep there, on rope-strung cots, and wake with their mouths tasting of stone. The dry rooms are the only privacy most of them get; they are also where the sick are kept, and where the dying are taken, because a wet death and a dry death are different things and the Kettlers have opinions about which is bearable.

Work is small and exact. There are no great engines here: piston pumps thudding two floors down, heat exchangers ticking as they shed their scale, gauge glass glowing green and amber, sodium lamps staked out on wire. A Kettler's day is measured not by the sun but by what the pipes are doing. They know the pumps the way a farmer knows weather. Some of them can tell by the vibration in a catwalk rail which pump has cycled. A valve sigh carries farther than a shout.

Their hands are their reputation. The pipe-runners, who lag the bundles with cloth and chase the new leaks, are the ones people trust with anything; the scale-scrapers, who go along the wet metal with brass blades, are quicker and more solitary and smell of vinegar. The sump-divers are few and are treated with careful politeness, because sump work is dangerous and the sumps are where the town's secrets sink to. And the condensers — the families who own roofs — are the closest thing the Kettle has to gentry. A condenser family owns a catchment of corrugated iron or ceramic tile, the gutter that feeds it, and the trough it drains to, and their politics are the politics of whose roof the ceiling rain visits. A cold pipe crossing a warm updraft is a claim, a dowry, a grievance. More marriages have been made over a drip line than over anything else.

They are water-proud and water-parsimonious in the same breath. They can taste where a cup came from — which ceiling, which shed, which sump it was pulled off — and they will offer a guest a cup the way other people offer wine, naming the roof it fell on. They wash in thimblefuls. They are not a filthy people; they are a careful one, and carefulness has begun to look like fastidiousness.

Food is brought down from above — sacks of grain and pulses hoisted through the shaft, salted mutton, dried fruit — and bought with water, salt, and the foundry's surplus heat, which the plain above uses to warm hothouses. Locally they eat the pale fungus they grow in the wet galleries on beds of soaked straw, and the salt-moss they scrape from the seams of wet brick, which is bitter and which children hate. There are no animals in the Kettle at all, not a dog, not a rat, not a fly in the sumps. Grain stores are safe and quiet. The consequence is that the Kettlers are the only moving things in the cavity, and they feel it — they are intensely, exhaustedly aware of one another. There is no other life to look at. When someone crosses the floor of the foundry level, everyone's eyes track them.

They do not shout. Sound dies against the dense stacked buildings; the rock knocks it flat. So they talk low and quick, and they knock — on pipes, on railings, on door frames — and the knocks carry where voices don't. A whole code has grown up in it: three on the rail means I'm coming up, two means don't, a long drag on the water pipe means trouble at the foundry. Children learn to knock before they learn to read the gauges, and reading the gauges is the main schooling.

Time is water and machinery. There are no days. They count the drip, the pump cycle, the foundry tap, the condenser scrape. Ask a Kettler their age and they may give it in fills of a particular trough. They have no word for weather; they have words for ceiling rain, for heavy sweat, for a dry week, for the day the ceiling stops and the whole town goes quiet and looks up.

The children are born in the dry rooms and most have never stood under the sky until they are old enough to make their first climb — a rite, half festival, half dread. Some come back and cannot stop talking about the width of the light. Some come back silent and never quite look at the ceiling in the same way again. A few do not come back. The Kettlers are not sentimental about this; they say the plain takes what it takes.

Their dead they salt. There is no ground here that will hold a body without going soft, so the dead are packed in coarse salt in the old foundation pits, in the dry, and left. Salt is cheap here and it keeps. There is a low wall of the salted in the driest corner of the cavity, and the Kettlers bring them water — a cup tipped onto the floor beside them, because it would be cruel to deny the dead the thing the living think about most.

They are not a religious people; the Kettle is too young. Nothing here has had time to soften, and that includes belief. Their customs are improvised, borrowed from wherever their parents came from, patched together the way the town is patched. But there is a habit, when the condensation starts up again after a dry stretch, of stopping what you are doing and listening to it strike the roofs, and that listening is the nearest thing they have to prayer. Heat rises. Water condenses. Everything is arranged to make the two meet where it can be used. And the Kettlers, damp-handed, salt-haired, knocking on their pipes in the perpetual dusk, are arranged the same way.
