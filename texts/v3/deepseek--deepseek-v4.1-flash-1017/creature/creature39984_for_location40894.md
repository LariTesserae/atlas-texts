The first residents are not visible as residents. They are films and furs and stains — not green, never green, but sulfur-yellow, rust-red, bone-white, a bruise of purple where the mineral water leaks. They live in the fractures and on the clean faces of the basalt, and they do not eat sunlight because there is no sunlight to eat. They eat chemistry. They take the cold mineral water and the sea and the rock’s own leaking metals and they turn the meeting into food. Everything else here is built on that invisible meal. The mats are pastures. The crusts are orchards. The bare stone is bare because the films have been grazed down to the stone and the stone has been grazed down to the films.

The pumpers are the ones you notice first. Tubes — pale, fleshy, stiff as pipe — standing in banks, each with a plume at the top that opens like a flower made of blood and feathers. They have no mouths you would recognize. They are chimneys with a garden inside them: bacteria packed into their tissues, fed on the water the animal moves through itself. The tube is the house, the plume is the gill, the body is a landlord for microbes. They clench and open in slow tidal pulses, and the current over the crown is partly theirs, worked up out of the crowd. When they knock — and they knock, a hollow ticking from inside the tubes — it is the sound of a tenement settling. When one clogs and dies, the tube stays standing for a while, a vacant apartment, until something else moves in or the grazers take it apart.

The fist-shaped things on the ledges are clams and mussels and their relatives, packed shoulder to shell, opening thin lips into the current. They are not picturesque. They are a living cobble, a pavement that filters. Their gills are gardens too, or sieves, or both. They close when the water goes wrong. They open when it goes right. They grow hard parts that become, after death, the next surface for someone else to sit on. The whole crown is paved with the shells of previous tenants.

The fans are the architecture. They stand up off the rock like ferns made of glass and bone, branching, sieving, fragile and sharp. They are not plants. They are animals holding nets into the current, and the haze of small swimmers above the stone is their food and also their neighbors. The fans do not move much. They wait. They are the least hurried things on the crown, and they are always eating.

In the crevices live the folded ones. Crabs the color of a bruise, with legs tucked into gaps too small for them. Worms with scales like overlapping armor. Shrimp with pale bodies and long antennae, sitting in the lee of tubes. An eel-like fish lying on a ledge, not chasing anything, just present, its face arranged around a mouth that will open when something dies. These are not the factory’s engines. They are its maintenance and its scavenging and its occasional violence. They do not hunt often. They do not need to. The crowd is dense enough that something is always failing.

And above the stone, suspended, is the mobile fraction: a haze of amphipods and copepods and small shrimp, never quite dispersed, never quite swarming. They are the ones who can leave. They drift over the pumpers and the fans and the mats, and they drop down to graze and scavenge and rise again. They orient to the pulse — that faint electric wrongness in the water, the prickle at your fillings — and you can see them turn to it like commuters to a bell. They are not coordinated by a brain. They are coordinated by a field. Many small bodies make the field; many small bodies feel it. The pulse is the closest thing the crown has to a nervous system, and nobody owns it.

The grazers are the ones who keep the place clean. Limpets and snails and small crustaceans move over the stone and the tubes and the shells, scraping bacterial film, clearing clogged throats, eating the dead before the dead can rot. They are janitors who become fixtures and fixtures who become food. When a bank of pumpers silts up and slows, the grazers arrive first, then the scavengers, then the settlers looking for vacant rock. The factory replaces its own parts. The replacements do not know they are replacements. They are larvae drifting in from elsewhere — the crown is a place you can only reach as a seed — and most of them die in the dark water before they find it. The few that land become the next layer.

There is no keeper here. No one decides. There is no night shift, because there is no night. The residents run continuously, on gradients and appetite, and their lives are mostly waiting and filtering and opening and closing and being eaten. They are not friendly and they are not hostile. They are busy. They are each other’s habitat: the tubes are apartments, the mats are pasture, the shells are pavement, the dead are weather. The whole crowd is a metabolism with partitions, and the partitions are made of bodies.

If you want a who, the who is the crowd. If you want a what, the what is everything: the red-plumed pumpers, the shell pavement, the glass-bone fans, the bruise-colored crabs, the pale shrimp in the crevices, the swimming haze that turns to the electric pulse. They live in the crown the way a fire lives in a coal seam — not as individuals who chose it, but as a process that cannot do anything else. Stay long enough and you will watch one of them wear out and another take its place, and the roar will not change, and the light will slide over them, and the machine will keep running.
