## The Masons

The true residents of the Kettle are the ones who built it, and they are the least visible.

A colony is a fist of pale tubes, each tube a mouth and a gut and a small soft heart that beats the cold water through itself. It has no eyes and no brain and no notion of the tower it is making. It knows only two things: the taste of the water, and the light. And it is always hungry — there are never quite enough drifting shells to fill it, and so it spends everything it cannot use on building, which is a kind of hunger too, and one that can never be satisfied, because a colony that stopped building would be eaten alive by the borers and the walls and the crowd.

They are patient the way a candle flame is patient. They do not decide to build under the drip. They *drift* — the young ones, the swimmers, the spawn that leaves the parent fist and hangs in the crowded water until it finds a rough surface and settles and begins, at once, to make stone. And the colonies that settle where the water is poor make slow and stunted towers and are outgrown. The ones that settle in the rich fall under a crack in the ice make tall ones. So the city grows toward its food without knowing it has a plan, generation after generation, each one building on the backs of the dead ones below, milky and blind and terribly industrious.

They glow when they feed. A colony with the melt running over it shimmers faintly along its growing edge, a bead of green light where the new cement is curing, and the taller a tower the brighter it burns, so that the rich colonies are also the beautiful ones, and the poor stunted ones are dark. The Kettle grades its citizens by light. There is no cruelty in it. That is simply how you can tell who is being fed.

## The Tenants

Everything else in the Kettle is a squatter, and knows it.

The shrimp are the Kettle's most nervous citizens. Pale as the stone, small as a grain of rice, they cling in the galleries the borers opened and fan the water through their feathery arms and startle at everything, which here means startling at almost nothing — a shift in the light, a change in the lean of the water, the slow arrival of a shadow that should not be there. When one flares, the flare runs down the wall in a chain as each shrimp startles at the last one's light, and for a moment the whole colonnade is stuttering blue, a rumor passing through a crowd. They never learn. There is nothing to learn from. Nothing in the Kettle is ever twice the same.

The borers live inside the walls and are never seen except as a slow pink flush where they are working, and the fine pale grit of their chewing that drifts down and settles on the roofs below, and the small round holes — perfectly round, bored with a patient circular motion — that appear in the stone overnight. They are the city's only enemies, and they are also its landlords, and also its eventual floor. A borer that dies inside its tunnel becomes a little capsule of softness in the rock, and the cement closes around it, and some shrimp will nest in the hollow it leaves behind. There is no villain here. The city eats its own dead and calls it architecture.

And the eyeless fish. They are the only free things in the Kettle — the only ones that move through open water rather than clinging to a wall — and they move by pressure, by the push of displaced water against the fine lines of their bodies, which is how they know where the towers are, and where each other are, and where the big one is. They go gray-white and blind and they are always, always hungry. They pick over the towers in slow flotillas, mouthing the cement for whatever has settled on it, and they never touch the living tissue of a colony, and no one knows why. Perhaps it tastes wrong. Perhaps they remember, the way the walls remember, that the city feeds them too.

## The Patient One

And there is the one that lives in the wide gallery under the central tower, where the fall is thickest and the towers lean in, and nothing has yet built there because nothing could.

It is old. It is old in a place where nothing is old yet, and its body has been growing, slowly, one ring of glucosamine at a time, since before the fracture in the ice was a fracture. It is shaped like a slow closing hand: a broad pale palm of a body, and long fingers of bone that fold, and a mouth at the hinge that opens like a door. It hangs in the still water and does not move for days. Its gills work. Its eyeless face turns, minutely, toward the pressure of the melt.

It does not chase. It has never chased. In a room where everything is close and nothing can run, there is no need. It simply waits where the food is richest, and the food comes to it — the fish drawn to the towers, the shrimp drawn to the light, the falling snow of copepods, the slow settling of whatever dies. Sometimes a fish arrives against its mouth and is gone, and the water does not even know it happened. When it does move — and it moves rarely, and always at once, and always without warning — the whole Kettle feels it: a pressure wave going out from the gallery, the startled shrimp flaring all along the walls, the fish scattering blind. The city knows when the hand closes.

It has been here longer than any tower. Its fingers are furred with algae along the seam-side edge, the only green on it, and the softest parts of it have begun, very slowly, to accrete — a fine crust of cement where the water has been laying down mineral against its skin for decades. It is becoming a little architectural itself. Someday it will die in the gallery it has ruled, and it will not decay, and the masons will build over it, and it will become the floor under the richest drip in the Kettle, and the towers will rise on its bones, and the city will be a little taller because it was there.

## The Kettle's Mind

It has no mind. That is the thing to say clearly. There is no one in the Kettle who is thinking about the Kettle.

But something is happening here that is like a mind, the way a reef is like a city. The colonies build toward the light and the drip; the borers chew; the shrimp panic and flare and so reveal the presence of things; the great patient one closes its hand and the water rings out and the walls answer in light. And the light is a kind of speech — not words, but *reportage*: here is a thing, it is moving, it is large, it is gone. The Kettle is a room full of blind things telling each other, constantly, everything that happens, and the telling is done in green and blue and pale, and it runs along the walls like a pulse along a nerve, and any point on any wall knows, briefly, because of the light, what just happened somewhere else.

Nobody is listening to it. The shrimp are not curious. The fish do not care. But the pattern is there — a slow, dim, constant conversation that includes every creature in the room and has been going on since the first colony threw its first thread of milky cement. The Kettle is alone, and it is not silent, and somewhere in the murmuring of its towers and its tenants it is, in a way that would embarrass it if it had a self, *talking to itself*.

## What It Is to Live Here

There is no going out. The walls are the walls. The roof is a lid that was sealed before anyone was born and will not be lifted for lifetimes of lifetimes. The water is heavy and close and full of bodies, and the far wall is always at the edge of sight and never resolves. If you live here you have never seen anything at a distance. You have never known a horizon. Everything you know, you know by press and by light and by the taste of the water, and it is all within reach, and it is all, in the end, the same few things, endlessly rearranged.

You would be hungry always, and you would build. You would be startled always, and you would flare. You would be very, very still, and then you would move — and the room would feel it, and the walls would answer in a slow blush of green, and that would be the whole of your life, and it would be enough, because there is nothing else to want and nothing else to lose. And above you the seam of green light would brighten and dim with a tide that does not care what you do, and below you the stone would be warm with the dead, and around you the crowded water would go on leaning against itself in the cold, and the city would go on gently, patiently, building itself out of everything that dies.

There is no decay here that is faster than the building. That is the mercy of the place. Everything in the Kettle becomes something. Nothing in the Kettle is ever truly finished. Not even the Kettle.
