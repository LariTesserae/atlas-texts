What lives there is the thing below the light: the Sump, the old throat of the shaft, the warm heart of the bowl. It has no name for itself. The only names are the ones you would give it if you saw it, and you will not see all of it at once.

It is huge and soft and patient, a body like a collapsed bellows or a sea anemone allowed to grow into the shape of the bowl. It lies at the bottom, over the old drain, half-sunk in silt. Its hide is the color of wet tile, mottled pale and green, and the weed grows on it as if it were wall. Crabs graze its flanks. Eels coil in the folds where its body has gone slack. It is not one clean shape. It is a colony of lobes and lips and slow muscles, all of them rounded. The pumps have been at it too, in their way: it has grown into the current, streamlined where the water pulls, furred where the water rests.

It has no eyes. It does not need them. It reads the shaft by pressure and vibration—the knock of the pumps in its floor, the slap of the belt, the rain coming down the channels as a thin cold sting at the surface. Its mouth is a ring of soft lips at the center of the down-current, wide enough to take a swimming person if a person were foolish and slow. It is not a trap, but it is a mouth. When it opens, the water sinks into it with a sound like a drain finding its breath. When it closes, the pressure changes all the way up the shaft, and the small hard creatures at the waterline close with it. Its exhale comes out through pores along its sides, warmed by the slow furnace of its body. That is why the water is blood-warm. The pumps keep it moving; the Sump keeps it warm; neither knows the other is doing it.

It eats what the shaft gives it: silt, weed, crabs that wander too near, the occasional silver fish. It does not hunt. It waits. It has a heartbeat you can feel in the stone if you lie on the lip long enough, a slow double knock underneath the pump rhythm. It sleeps in the way of things that have no day: not all at once, but in lobes. One part of it rests while another keeps the current. It has been there longer than the tile has been rounded. It may have been put there by the builders; it may have come up through the drain on its own and found the water already warm. It does not remember, and it does not need to. It knows the rule the way a lung knows breathing.

It is not lonely. Loneliness is a thing with a before and after. The Sump has only the shaft, the knock, the light moving across the water, the rain. It lives there the way the pumps live there: without knowing it is a ruin. If the pumps stopped, it would close, and the water would go still, and it would begin to silt over. If the light went out, it would not notice. If you swam down and put your hand on it, it would not startle; it would only tighten, and the current would tug at you, and you would understand that you were touching the warm part of the mountain. That is what lives there: not a keeper, not a monster, but the slow mouth and warm heart of the place, keeping the water moving because it has no other way to be alive.
