The hollow is inhabited, but not populated. If you look for residents the way you look for animals—quick movement, eyes catching light, something that startles—you will see none. Stay still long enough, though, and the tenders resolve out of the green: a few people-shaped figures, spaced far apart, standing at the coils or moving along the catchment channels with the unhurried purpose of water finding a way down.

They are not many. A dozen at most, perhaps fewer; it is hard to count them because they do not gather. They are short and broad and very still. Their skin is the color of wet bark, with a copper-green bloom in the creases of wrist and ankle where the runoff collects. Their hair is not quite hair—a mat of moss and pale lichen, cropped close, sometimes beaded with condensation. Their eyes are fog-colored and slow to focus, and when they turn to look at you it happens by degrees, like a fern unfurling. They wear almost nothing: belts of braided cable sheath, capes of oiled fern-cloth, cured hide soft with age. Their hands are broad, their nails ridged like mussel shells, and they keep their palms turned up when they walk, not in supplication but because that is how water is caught and read.

They are the keepers of the works, though “keepers” may be too active a word. They do not rebuild. They clear a leaf from a coil lip. They straighten a thread of water with one finger. They wipe silt from a grate and press the silt back into the moss. They walk the same routes at the same slow intervals, and they know each coil by its note—the tiny change in pitch when the drip thickens or thins—and by the ring of moss beneath it, which is sized to that coil’s particular rate. They can tell the time of day by the counterpoint of the field, and they can tell the weather by whether the counterpoint loosens or tightens. They do not need instruments. The valley is instrument enough.

They speak rarely, and when they do it is low and spaced, a sentence sometimes finished after a drip. Among themselves they use a tapped code on wet stone, or they simply adjust a channel and let the water carry the message. They have names for intervals rather than for things: the long pause after the fog lifts, the quickening before rain, the hush when the cable hums. They do not seem to distinguish between tending the apparatus and tending the forest. To them the coils are a kind of root, the moss a kind of gasket, the pale moth a kind of weather.

They eat little—fungus from fallen timber, fern starch, the clean condensate from the highest coils. They do not hunt; there is nothing to hunt. They leave the great pale moth alone, not out of reverence exactly, but because it is the only thing in the hollow that moves without their permission, and they have learned to let that be. When one of them dies, the others carry the body into the deep fern and let the moss take it. There is no marker. A favorite coil, its moss ring slightly thicker, is marker enough.

Whether they built the works or the works built them is not a question they would understand. They are what remains of the crew, or the crew’s descendants, or the crew itself slowed to the pace of the place—no one there would draw the distinction. They do not remember a before. They know the maintenance as inheritance, the routes as language, the drip as a clock that is never quite the same twice. They have settled into agreement with the apparatus and the forest, the way everything here settles, at the pace of water finding a way down.

If you stay long enough, you will mistake one for a mossed post until it turns its head. It will not be surprised. It will hold out a hand, palm up, and wait for a drip to land. Then it will nod, as if you had said something correct, and go back to listening.
