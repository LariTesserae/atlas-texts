# The Current-Walkers

They come when the thrum changes.

Not the great current itself—that ceaseless, bone-deep roar is the constant of their world, the heartbeat they have known since before memory—but the smaller currents, the eddies and backflows that curl around the drowned peaks like fingers testing the surface of a thing. The current-walkers feel these shifts through their skins, through the delicate fronds that drift from their elongated bodies like the kelp itself, and they stir from their patient gatherings among the stones.

They are tall, these beings, taller than seems possible for a creature of the deep. Their forms are elongated, attenuated, stretched by generations of existence in a world where movement is measured in the slow pulse of water rather than the quick twitch of muscle. Their bodies are translucent, faintly luminous in the jade twilight, their internal structures visible as dim shadows within their flesh—a latticework of what might be bone or cartilage, but softer, more flexible, like the structure of a leaf. They have no eyes. Instead, their faces—if faces they can be called—are smooth, featureless domes, sensitive to pressure, to the faint chemical signatures that drift through the water, to the vibrations of stone and silt and the slow breathing of the kelp forests.

Their limbs are many, and long, and delicate. Not arms and legs as a surface-dweller would understand, but something more like the trailing fronds of the bronze kelp, prehensile and sensitive, capable of gripping stone with a gentleness that leaves no mark, of weaving through the dense thickets with a grace that seems almost like dancing. Each limb ends in a cluster of fine, hair-like tendrils, and these tendrils are how they taste the world—the minerals in the water, the age of a stone, the slow decay of organic matter settling into the ooze.

They wear nothing. They need nothing. Their bodies are their garments, their tools, their homes.

---

The current-walkers do not speak. They have no mouths, no lungs, no need for breath. But they communicate in ways more subtle and more profound. When they gather—and they do gather, in the sheltered valleys where the current softens and the kelp grows thick—they press their long bodies together, their translucent flesh touching, and through that contact they share something. Not words. Not images. Something older, more fundamental: the memory of pressure, the taste of a particular mineral spring, the sensation of a stone's surface worn smooth over centuries.

They remember the before-times. Not with the clarity of history, but with the dim, persistent ache of racial memory, passed from body to body through the long generations. They remember when the water was not here. They remember when the sky was open and the light was harsh and the air moved in great, violent gusts that they could not understand. They remember the slow, creeping terror of the rising tide, the way the water climbed and climbed and did not stop. They remember the ones who built the stones.

Those ones are gone now. The current-walkers do not know where. They do not wonder. The wondering is not in them. But sometimes, when they gather around the ring of rough-hewn basalt or press their tendrils against the sheared-off top of a cylindrical tower, they feel something. A resonance. A faint echo of the hands that shaped the stone, the minds that conceived it. The current-walkers do not mourn. They simply... remember. And in remembering, they honor.

---

They move with the current, not against it. This is the fundamental truth of their existence, the law that governs every motion, every decision, every slow drift of their lives. To fight the current is to die—not quickly, not violently, but slowly, worn away by the endless pressure, ground down to nothing. So they yield. They flow. They find the paths of least resistance, the eddies and backwaters where the great river-in-the-sea grows calm, and there they make their homes.

Their homes are simple. A depression in the ooze, lined with softened kelp fronds. A hollow in the side of a cliff, worn smooth by centuries of water and now claimed by a single family, or what passes for family among their kind. A cave, perhaps, in the deeper places where the light does not reach and the darkness is absolute—though these are rare, and the current-walkers who dwell there are strange, even to their own kind, their bodies grown pale and their senses sharpened to a painful degree.

They do not build. They do not shape. The stone ruins fascinate them, but they do not seek to emulate them. What need have they for walls and towers? The water is their wall. The current is their tower. They are already sheltered by the world itself.

---

Their lives are slow. Their lives are long. A current-walker might live for centuries, drifting through the jade twilight, tending to the kelp forests, gathering in the sheltered valleys to share the pressure-memories of their kind. They do not age as surface-dwellers age. Their flesh does not wrinkle; it simply... thins. Fades. Becomes more translucent, until at last they are little more than ghosts of themselves, their internal structures faintly visible, their tendrils barely able to grip. And then, one day, they simply stop. Their bodies settle into the ooze, and the kelp grows over them, and the current wears them away, and they become part of the world again.

The young—the few young, for they breed rarely, and only when the pressure-memories grow too heavy, too insistent, demanding to be passed on—are small, no larger than a frond of kelp, and they cling to their parents' bodies for years, absorbing the pressure-memories, learning the taste of the water, the feel of the current. They are curious, these young ones, in a way their elders have forgotten. They explore the edges of the kelp forests, the mouths of the abyssal trenches, the strange stone ruins that dot the landscape. They press their tendrils against the basalt and wonder—not with words, but with sensation—what made these shapes, what hands touched this stone before the water came.

The elders do not answer. The elders do not know. But sometimes, when the young ones return, they carry a new pressure-memory, a new sensation, a new taste. And the elders add it to the great shared memory of their kind, and the current-walkers become, in their slow, patient way, a little more themselves.

---

They are not the only living things in this drowned highland. The kelp forests are alive, and the current-walkers know them as they know their own bodies—every frond, every root, every slow pulse of growth. They tend the forests, these beings, in ways both subtle and profound. They do not farm, not as surface-dwellers farm, but they... encourage. They clear away the dead fronds. They guide the currents through the thickets with gentle sweeps of their limbs. They press their bodies against the roots and share something—a warmth, perhaps, or a nutrient their own flesh produces—that the kelp seems to crave.

In return, the kelp shelters them. Its broad, leathery fronds break the force of the current, creating pockets of calm where the current-walkers can rest. Its dense thickets hide them from the deeper currents, the colder waters that sometimes rise from the abyssal trenches. And its slow, organic sounds—the creak of the fronds, the soft pop of gas bubbles rising from the sediment—provide a kind of music, a kind of comfort, that the current-walkers have no words for but feel in the very core of their being.

There is something else in the kelp forests, too. Something the current-walkers do not speak of, cannot speak of, but know. The kelp is not merely a plant. It is... aware. Not in the way the current-walkers are aware, not with thought and memory and purpose, but with something older, more fundamental. The kelp knows the current. It knows the stone. It knows the water. And it knows the current-walkers, who tend it and touch it and share with it. There is a bond between them, a symbiosis older than the rising water, older than the stone ruins, older even than the drowned mountains themselves.

The current-walkers do not understand this bond. They do not try. They simply accept it, as they accept the current, as they accept the cold, as they accept the slow, patient rhythms of their lives.

---

In the deepest places, where the light does not reach and the pressure is immense and the cold is absolute, there is something else. The current-walkers do not go there. The current is too strong, the darkness too complete. But they feel it, sometimes—a vibration in the stone, a taste in the water, a pressure that is not the pressure of the current but something else, something deeper. Something that has been there since before the water rose. Something that waits.

The stone ruins are close to these places. Not the simple rings and cylinders of the upper plateau, but older stones, deeper stones, half-buried in the ooze of the abyssal trenches. The current-walkers do not know who built them. They do not know what they were for. But sometimes, when the current shifts and the water grows still, they can hear something. A low murmur, a whisper, a voice just on the edge of hearing—not a voice like theirs, not a voice like anything they understand, but a voice nonetheless.

They do not speak of it. They do not wonder. But when they gather in the sheltered valleys, when they press their bodies together and share their pressure-memories, there is always one memory that passes between them, unspoken but understood: the feeling of being watched by something vast and patient and utterly indifferent. The feeling that the drowned highland is not dead, not empty, not merely a place where they happen to live, but a place that has a purpose of its own. A purpose they are a part of, whether they understand it or not.

---

The current-walkers are not explorers. They are not conquerors. They are not even, in the way a surface-dweller might understand it, curious. They simply are. They exist in the jade twilight, in the cold and the pressure and the endless thrum of the current. They tend the kelp and remember the past and touch the stones that others built and do not question why.

But sometimes, when a young one is born, when a new pressure-memory is added to the great shared memory of their kind, they feel something that might be hope. Or might be fear. Or might be something else entirely, something they have no word for, something that exists only in the space between thought and sensation.

They feel the vastness of the world around them. They feel the age of the stone and the patience of the water. They feel the presence of something larger than themselves, something that has been here since before the water rose and will be here long after they are gone.

And they yield to it, as they yield to the current. They flow with it, not against it. They accept it, as they accept everything. And in that acceptance, they find a kind of peace, a kind of grace, a kind of belonging that no surface-dweller could ever understand.

---

They are beautiful, the current-walkers. Beautiful in a way that is alien and unsettling, beautiful in a way that speaks of depths and pressures and times beyond human comprehension. They drift through the jade twilight, their translucent bodies trailing long tendrils like the kelp itself, their featureless faces turned toward the faint light from above, their many limbs moving with a grace that seems almost choreographed, almost intentional.

And sometimes, when the current shifts and the water grows still and the murmur rises from the deep places, they turn. They turn toward the sound, toward the darkness, toward the presence that they feel but do not understand.

And they wait.

They wait, as the stone waits. They wait, as the water waits. They wait, as the drowned mountains wait, patient and indifferent and utterly themselves.

The current-walkers are not the masters of this place. They are not even its stewards. They are simply... here. Part of it. As the kelp is part of it, as the stone is part of it, as the water is part of it.

And that, perhaps, is enough.
