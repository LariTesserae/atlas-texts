Mira Tend is forty-one years old and has never been dry for a full day in her life. She works the deep intakes on the southeast pump, the one they call Old Six, and she has the arms for it: roped with muscle, the skin pale and soft-looking the way skin gets after decades in warm water, the fingernails thick and slightly ridged. Her hair she keeps cropped to the skull, because long hair in the Sump is a nuisance and a hazard both. She has a scar across the back of her left hand where a bearing housing bit her when she was nineteen, and she shows it to apprentices the way other people show photographs.

She sleeps in a room on the fourth floor of a tenement on Catfish Row, which is not a row and has no catfish, and which she has lived in since she was a child. The room is one hundred and forty cubic fathoms of air, more or less, and it holds a stove, a table, three chairs, a shelf of jars, a hammock, and a window the size of a door, and the window looks out on a drowned street and the tall green-lit face of the foundry opposite. She keeps a lamp in the corner by the stove and a second one hanging from a beam, and she trims the wicks more often than she needs to. She likes the yellow. Everyone likes the yellow. It is the only warm thing that isn't also the water.

She is not a talker, but she is not silent either, and when she does talk it is in a low flat voice with long pauses in it, and people lean in to hear her, which she has never noticed. She notices a great many things. She notices that the silt has been coming down heavier on the north side of the seawall this year. She notices that the eels at the outfalls have gotten bolder and one of them has a mark on its flank she thinks she recognizes from three years ago. She notices which of the young ones are naturals at holding their breath and which are struggling and which are pretending, and she teaches the pretending ones the trick of it, which is to stop wanting the surface.

What she believes is a little hard to say, because she would not put it that way. She does not think of the Sump as a place that survived a disaster. She thinks of it as a place that is doing what it does. The sea came up; the district stayed; the pumps keep the water moving; the people keep the pumps. That is the whole of it, and it is enough to fill a life. When the pumps go quiet — and they do go quiet, one or another of them, a bearing or a seal or the slow accumulation of a decade — she feels it before she hears it, and she is already reaching for her wrench before anyone has come to tell her, because the silence is in her sternum and it is wrong, and it needs to stop.

She has a daughter, Teal, who is seventeen and who wants to go up. Not out — there is nothing out there — but up, to the surface, to see the sky, which she has only heard about. Mira does not argue with her. She has seen the surface. She went up once, at twenty-five, on a line with a group of others, and she came back down through the green-gold water and the brass water and the hand of pressure, and the lights came up out of the murk, and she wept into her mask, and she has never been able to explain to Teal what she wept about. Something about the light. Something about the way the district glowed down there like a thing that had chosen to stay. She does not know how to say it. She does not think she will try.

She cuts weed from the intakes four days in six. She sits on the catwalks in the evening and watches the kelp swing. She mends the tar on her window frame twice a year with a hot iron and a pot of pitch, and she does it well, because a badly-tarred window is a drowned room and a drowned room is a drowned life. She has held her breath for seven minutes and eleven seconds, once, on a bet, and she does not consider this remarkable. She considers it Tuesday.
