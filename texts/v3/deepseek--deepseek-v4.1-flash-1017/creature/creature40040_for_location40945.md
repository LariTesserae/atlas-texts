## The Tenant

No person lives in the Throat now. The last hands finished their low wall and their five steps and went out, and the hollow kept the work the way it keeps everything — slowly, without fuss. What lives there is a hand-sized folding thing, and it has no name, because there is no one to give it one and no use for one.

At rest it looks like a knot of pale tissue the size of a spread palm, creased and damp like leather left in a cellar. It has no eyes. Its front end is a blunt wedge with two shallow pits that taste the air, and its skin is grey-white and thin enough that the dark of its gut shows through when it has fed. Six limbs fold under a mantle in a seam that makes it look like a stone with a crack in it. When it unfolds, it does so by degrees — one limb, then another, each foot placed and tested before the weight goes onto it — until it becomes a low, long-legged shape that can span both your hands.

The roar is its daylight. It does not hear it as you hear it. Along its back runs a fluid sac and fields of fine bristles, and the constant pressure of the hollow is a map laid against its skin. It feels the underground water as a slow heaviness in the floor, the wind at the mouth as a faint lean in the column of air, the drip-lines by the way the stone answers a landing. It cannot locate a single falling drop, and does not try. What it reads are differences inside the constancy: a change in the water below, a footstep on a ledge three body-lengths away, the tiny shift when the long-legged insects walk their drip-lines. The roar is not noise to it. The roar is the page.

Its world is small and exact. The third ledge, the wall opposite the green room, the shallow stair, the lip of the shelf — it knows these by texture, by temperature, by the particular dampness of each. It moves in stops rather than flows. It folds when the pattern under its feet is wrong and waits, sometimes for a long time, until the wrongness resolves. It is not fast. It does not chase. It arrives where prey will be, and folds, and lets the long-legged insects come to the drip-line it has chosen.

It eats sparingly. Moss tips from the green room, mineral film scraped from wet stone, the pale walkers of the walls. It drinks condensation from its own limbs. It does not need free water and would not know what to do with a pool. Its metabolism is the speed of the place: slow, cool, patient. It may live longer than the stair will take to wear round, and it will not hurry about it.

The green room is its nursery and its larder. It lays its eggs there, in the damp cushions where the drip reaches, and the young come out translucent and nearly invisible, and most of them go out onto the walls and are not seen again. The ones that return return to the third ledge, because the third ledge is what they know. The green does not spread, partly because the stone will not let it, and partly because the tenant grazes it back to its edges and no further.

It has no call. There would be no point. When two of them meet, they press their front pits together and fold, and that is the whole conversation. They do not guard territory, because there is enough wall and not enough of them. They do not mourn and do not remember faces. They remember routes, drip schedules, the feel of a ledge that has shifted a hair since the last rain.

It sleeps in the curve of the old wall, where the stone fits the fold of its back. It crosses the five worn steps because worn stone is easier than rough. It does not know who wore them. It has inherited their use the way it inherited the shape of the hollow: without story, without gratitude, simply as the way the floor is.

So the Throat is not empty. It is tenanted by a quiet reader of pressure, a folded hand of pale tissue that lives inside one long note and finds in its constancy all the small differences that mean *prey*, *damp*, *cold*, *safe*. It keeps no note of its own. It does not need to. The note is already being kept, and it has learned to live inside it.
