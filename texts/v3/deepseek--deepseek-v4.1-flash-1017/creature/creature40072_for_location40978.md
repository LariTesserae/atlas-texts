They are a people who have learned to breathe water. Not literally—not quite—but their lungs have grown deep from a lifetime of inhaling mist, and their skin has taken on the soft, pale quality of things that live perpetually in shadow. You can see it in the way they move: slow, deliberate, like they are always testing the ground beneath them. They walk with their weight pitched forward, knees slightly bent, arms loose and ready. Not from fear. From long practice. The Sump has taught them that balance is not a state you achieve but a negotiation you conduct, moment by moment, with a world that is always shifting.

They are called the Sumpfolk, though they themselves prefer no collective name at all. They think in terms of families, of boat-crews, of the particular cluster of stilted platforms where they happened to be born. "Where are you from?" is answered with a channel-name, a tree-mark, a description of some nearby landmark that may or may not still exist. Identity here is not a thing you carry with you. It is a place you stand on, and it may wash out from under you before the year is done.

Their bodies tell you what they value. The long, deft fingers are the first thing you notice—knotted with tendon, sensitive at the tips, perpetually stained with rust and pitch and the dark green sap of the alder. These are fingers that can splice a cable in the dark, that can read the grain of a beam by touch alone, that can find the weakness in a hull by running a palm along its length. Their hands are their most precious tool, and they protect them obsessively. Gloves are worn until they fall apart and are then worn again, stitched and restitched. A Sumpfolk with a fresh cut on their hand will bind it with fastidious care, not out of vanity but because a hand that cannot feel the subtle vibrations of a creaking support is a hand that will get you killed.

Their faces are lean, weathered, and watchful. They do not look you in the eye when first they meet you; they look at your feet, to see how you stand on the trembling ground. They look at your hands, to see what you carry and how you hold it. Only then, having assessed your relationship to the world around you, will they meet your gaze. And when they do, their eyes are the most striking thing about them: pale, pale grey, almost silver in the flat, diffuse light, with a peculiar depth that suggests they are seeing something behind you, some pattern in the mist and the dripping branches that you cannot perceive. They have a way of tilting their heads when they listen, as if hearing a sound beneath the sound—the deep, sub-audible hum of the collapsing machinery, maybe, or the slow pulse of the coal-spirit itself.

They dress for the damp, not for fashion. Layers of waxed canvas and oiled wool, patched and repatched with whatever scraps are at hand, so that a single coat might carry the history of a dozen different trades in its mismatched panels. Everything they wear is dark—greys and browns and the deep, wet green of the alder—because color is a thing of the bright world above, and they have no memory of such a place. Their boots are the one concession to solidity: heavy, welted, steel-shod, built by hand from the hides of the mud-larks, which are the only creatures large enough and tough enough to provide them. A pair of Sump boots might last a single season. A Sumpfolk without proper boots is a Sumpfolk who has already begun to die.

They are, by necessity, a people of profound practical skill and profound superstition, and they see no contradiction between the two. The same person who can calculate the load-bearing capacity of a spliced cable with uncanny precision will also refuse to work on a platform whose main support has been touched by the "warm seep"—the luminous, chemically-tainted water that pools in certain depressions. They know exactly why they avoid it—the seep eats through rope and rots wood at accelerated rates—but they also believe, or half-believe, that the seep is the blood of the coal-spirit, and that to disturb it is to invite the spirit's attention. The distinction between chemistry and theology is not one they bother to make.

Their religion, if you can call it that, is not a matter of belief but of observation. They do not pray to the coal-spirit. They do not build shrines. They do not tell stories of its origin or its purpose. Instead, they watch. They have developed an entire grammar of signs, a vocabulary of omens read in the behavior of the world around them. A certain pattern of ripples in a channel means the spirit is stirring. A change in the pitch of the industrial hum means a collapse is imminent. The sudden, unseasonal warmth of the water under a particular boardwalk is a warning to move. They speak of these things matter-of-factly, the way a farmer speaks of weather. "The spirit is awake today," they will say, and then they will go about the work of moving their homes, or reinforcing their supports, or simply not going down that particular channel.

The rituals they do observe are small, domestic, almost casual. When a new anchor is driven into the muck, a pinch of rust is scattered over the site—a propitiation, they say, though they cannot tell you to whom. When a building is abandoned to the water, the last person out will leave a single lamp burning, though it will gutter out within hours. When a child is born, a piece of their umbilical cord is dried and thrown into the deepest sump they can find, a gift to the spirit that made them. These are not ceremonies. They are habits, passed down so long that no one remembers their origin, performed with the same unthinking care as tying one's boots.

They are a community of specialists, though the specialization is loose and fluid. There are the water-readers, who know the channels by the sound of the water moving through them and can navigate the mist-blind waterways by echo and memory. There are the riggers, who scale the alder trunks to lash new platforms into place, working high above the muck on branches slick with condensation, as calm as if they were walking on solid ground. There are the salvagers, who dive into the flooded ruins of the old factories in search of usable metal and wire, holding their breath for minutes at a time, their eyes adapted to the murky, green-lit depths. There are the bell-ringers, who maintain the warning system—a network of salvaged bells and gongs strung between the platforms, rung to signal emergencies. And there are the keepers of the old machines, the great water-wheels and the stamping mills, who coax life from rust and rot with a mixture of mechanical genius and resigned fatalism. No one is only one thing. The water-reader is also a rigger. The salvager is also a bell-ringer. The keepers are also the ones who butcher the mud-larks and dry their hides for boots.

The children are raised in the boats, in the narrow channels, on the tottering platforms. They learn to swim almost before they learn to walk, and they learn to fear the warm water—the seep—before they learn to fear anything else. They are quiet children, watchful, with the same pale eyes as their parents. They play games of balance, walking along the taut ropes that connect the platforms, standing on the very edge of the boardwalks and leaning out over the water until they are at the point of falling. This is not recklessness. It is education. The Sump is a place where a moment of inattention can kill you, and the children are taught to find the line between balance and catastrophe and to walk along it with confidence.

They are not a warm people, not in the way that outsiders might understand warmth. They are not given to displays of affection or long conversations. But they are a people of profound and unspoken loyalty, a loyalty that expresses itself in action rather than words. When a support beam cracks in the night, the whole community is awake within minutes, moving with a shared, unspoken urgency to shore up the failing structure before the whole platform goes down. No one gives orders. No one asks who is in charge. They simply move, each to their own task, a single nervous system responding to a single crisis. And when the crisis is past, they do not linger to discuss it. They return to their boats and their work, and the next time they meet, they do not mention it at all. The loyalty is understood. Words would only cheapen it.

They are, in the end, a people who have made peace with impermanence. They do not build for the future, or for posterity, or for any god. They build for now, for the season, for the next high water. They know that everything they make will eventually be reclaimed by the muck and the mist, and they do not find this tragic. It is simply the nature of things. The Sump is not a prison to them, nor a punishment. It is home. It is the only world they have ever known, and they have learned to live in it with a kind of fierce, cautious grace. They are the children of the damp, the inheritors of the rust and the root, and they have made themselves into something that the water cannot quite wash away. Not yet. Not while there are those among them who can read the signs, and listen to the hum, and move their homes when the spirit stirs.
