The first thing you'll notice is that they are wet all the way through. Not soaked — wet the way a thing is wet that has never once been dry. Shake a Sinker's hand and you'll feel it: warm, and slightly feverish, and faintly tacky, as though their skin has spent so long at the edge of condensation that it gave up on the distinction. Their fingertips are soft and pale and permanently a little swollen, and they do not prune. Put a hand in the cistern for an hour and it comes out wrinkled; put it in for a lifetime and it comes out like theirs, smooth and pinkish and a bit too tender for the work they do with it.

Their feet are the exception. Barefoot is the default — boots rot, and there is no version of the Sink where boots stay dry, and the tanners gave up generations ago. So a Sinker's soles are thick and pale and hard as a cutting board, with a crackle of fine lines across the heel that the natives read, no joke, for signs of age. Children's feet are soft; old feet are like the terrace stone, worked and rounded and grooved. You can tell how long someone has lived in the Sink by looking at the ground under them.

They run hot. Ask one to hold your hand in the cold hour before the false dawn and it will be like holding a cup of something. They sleep uncovered, on the terraces, or in the lee of the low halls where the warm return-water gurgles, and they do it in shifts that follow the engine rather than the sun. Somebody is always awake down there. The Sink does not sleep, so neither does anyone fully; the whole population exists in a rolling overlap of naps, a few hundred people trading wakefulness back and forth like a coal they keep lit.

You will smell them before you can see them well. Mint and machine-oil from the scrub they push through, warm stone, and bread. Always, faintly, bread — because the breaddust, the yellow puff that comes off the bloom in the wet season, is their leaven, and their ovens run on tap-steam, and a district smells of what it bakes.

---

Structurally, they are sound-shaped people.

Their hearing has worn into a strange profile. It isn't that they're deaf — it's that the range has been carved out of them. A Sinker will not notice the bird on the railing three feet away, will not hear a shout from a hundred yards, but will cock their head and say, flatly, *the third pipe's gone sour*, and be right, and be irritated that you couldn't tell. They hear the middle of things: the pipe note, the seam ticking, the vent that's half a beat late, the cataract that has narrowed in the night because a terrace lip is giving way. Their ears are the organ they think with. Their eyes are for reading the bloom.

They don't shout. They can't; the roar eats it. What they do instead, in the older families, is stand very close and read each other. It's a whole grammar. Two fingers on the other's sternum to feel the pitch of the voice through the bone. The eyes on the throat, watching the larynx lift and fall. A clipped half-voice, mostly consonants, pitched to the one gap in the roar the two of them have agreed to occupy. You will see two Sinkers standing forehead to collarbone, looking down each other's throats, holding absolutely still, nodding. They're having a conversation. It is more precise than yours.

The hand-talk, though, the hand-talk is the thing. It's old — older, probably, than the works themselves; there's a root vocabulary for water that grammar has been built on top of for centuries. Ten kinds of drip. Forty ways to say that a thing is *thinning*. A gesture for *the neighbor's been through here* that nobody will teach an outsider until they've lived a year, and that half the natives won't teach at all.

Children learn the hands before the voice. Watch a Sinker toddler at its mother's hip, doing something patient and small and exact with its fingers, and understand: it is already speaking, and in a language built entirely by people who had to be understood without being heard.

---

What they do, mostly, is knock the fur back.

Every surface of the works grows its pale cauliflower crust of calcium and mineral bloom, and every surface must be beaten back by hand, with an iron bar, on a schedule. This is the great labor of the Sink and the great text of it. A tendril of fur a finger thick means a railing left too long. The color tells you whose hands were last on it — a particular crew's bloom runs a little more orange, from something in the water on the terrace they work. When a Sinker looks at a chimney flue, they aren't seeing a chimney flue. They're seeing the last forty tending-seasons, stacked like years in a tree.

Ask one how old they are and you may get a blank look. Their ages are counted in readings.

They are careful with each other in ways that surprise visitors. It's a place where everything is maintenance, and so maintenance is the whole moral vocabulary: a person is *tended* or *neglected*, a friendship is *kept back* or *let fur*, a grudge is a crust nobody's scraped. A man who lets his own ladder fur over is not merely lazy, he is *unreadable*, he has no history, and there is something wrong with him.

They work down. The lowest terraces are the oldest and the most important and the hardest to reach, and the tenders who go down the long ladders into the steam and stay below for days are a caste of their own. They come up blinking, do not adjust — because there is no brightness to adjust to — and speak less than anyone, having less occasion. Some do not come up. There is a kind of retirement in the Sink that consists of simply going down one morning and not returning, and being spoken of, thereafter, in the past tense with mild approval.

---

And there is one thing they will not put into words, though you'll feel it in how they choose to stand.

The warm paths in the scrub. They are marked — a scraping of lime along the edges, renewed every few seasons, and no one builds on them, and no one says why, and if you walk one you'll find your feet are warm and your hands smell of mint. Ask, and a Sinker will tell you about drainage. The drainage does not account for the warmth, and both of you know it, and they will look at you with great levelness until you change the subject, and that look is the whole of the answer.

They don't fear it. They don't worship it. They *track* it, the way they track a pipe that's been running long — with the flat professional attention you give a thing that is neither friend nor hazard but condition. It has been through here many readings of the bloom. It has been through here longer than the ladders have been iron. It makes a path in a wall of scrub, and the path stays warm, and nothing has ever been taken, and nothing has ever been given, and the Sink has decided, in its slow and correective way, to leave a stripe of lime on either side of it and go on knocking the fur off the railings.

---

If you stay long enough, you'll find your own heartbeat has gone a little off its old rhythm, and your hands have learned a few words you didn't teach them, and your feet have gone hard, and you sleep in shifts now, and you wash and it doesn't take. You'll be standing still somewhere with the cataracts braiding white in front of you, and you'll have been there an hour, and you won't mind.

They'll notice, is the thing. The tenders will see your bloom coming in orange, the same as theirs, and someone will hand you an iron bar without a word, and that is the whole ceremony. You live here now. The Sink doesn't rush anybody, and it keeps what it takes, and it never once lets you dry.
