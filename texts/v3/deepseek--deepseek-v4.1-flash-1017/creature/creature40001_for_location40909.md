The thing that lives there does not announce itself as alive. You would have taken it for a valve, a length of conduit, a knuckle of dark rock worn smooth by the water. It was there all along, pressed into the trench wall at the edge of the node-light, and it did not move in any way you would have called movement. It lives at the speed of the place: by pressure, by interval, by the slow note of the pipes changing against its bones.

It is long and low, roughly the size of a person folded at the waist, but its body does not end cleanly. The back is a shell of overlapping plates, the same basalt-dark material as the industrial veins, grown rather than built, cold to the touch and seamless at the seams. The underside is pale and wet, ridged with fine cilia that taste the water for chemistry. It has no eyes. The node-glow is not for it; it reads the world through pits along its flanks that register pressure, and through a lower jaw it presses to the rock to listen. When a pipe’s hum shifts by a fraction, it knows before the silt has begun to settle.

Its limbs are not arms exactly. They are jointed, cable-thin extensions, six or eight of them, ending in splayed filaments that can tease a film of pale silt from a valve face without disturbing the polyps nearby. Two longer tendrils trail back into a conduit and disappear into the machine; whether they are nerves, feeders, or simply anchored harnesses, the creature does not distinguish. It moves by peristalsis and by pulling itself along the pipes, and a single crossing of that chapel-sized pocket may take it days. It does not swim. It does not startle. It has no need for either.

It feeds on the thin bacterial mat that blooms where vent fluid seeps from the joints, and it drinks the diffuse chemistry of the water through the soft underside of its jaw. It can go a long time without eating—years, perhaps, in the way you count years. What it excretes is a fine mineral paste, and where it daubs that paste into a microcrack, the crack closes. It is not repairing the factory in any human sense. It is grooming a body it shares. The tube worms are not its food; they are its weather. When their red plumes lean a certain way, it knows the current has changed. When they stand perfectly still, it waits.

It has a route. Node to node, valve to valve, polyp cluster to polyp cluster. It presses a filament into a port and holds it there for a long time, listening. It clears silt from a sphere-shaped node with slow, deliberate sweeps. It pauses where the hum is deepest and lets the vibration pass through its shell and into the pale flesh beneath. It is not lonely. Loneliness requires a world of interruptions, and this place has none. Its mind is not empty; it is full of gradients and intervals, a slow, pressure-shaped attention that thinks in differences of cold and chemistry. If it had language, it might say: *You are a fluctuation. Be still, and you will pass.*

It is not the only one. Farther along the trench, in other pockets of stone and pipe, others lie half-merged with the walls, their shells indistinguishable from rock until a valve changes pitch and a filament stirs. They are sparse, a handful in the whole cut of the world. They do not meet. They do not need to. The network is their conversation.

So the resident of that place is a keeper that does not keep, a component that is also a creature, a patient dark body in a chapel of stone and flesh. It was there before you arrived, and it will be there after the pressure has closed over your disturbance. It does not watch you. It simply registers you, the way it registers a venting hiss or a current’s shift, and then it returns to its long, slow work of listening to the machine breathe.
