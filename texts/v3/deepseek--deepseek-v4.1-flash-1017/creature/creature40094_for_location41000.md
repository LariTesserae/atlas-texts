The Vault is inhabited the way a body is inhabited: not by one tenant, but by a working crowd of organs that do not agree on what the body is for.

The first resident is the Green itself. Not a single plant—a parliament of them, root and rhizome and spore and glowing membrane, all competing and all feeding the same vast metabolism. It has no face and no center. If it thinks, it thinks in tropisms: toward water, toward warmth, toward the faint salt of a living thing. It is not malicious. It is hungry with the patience of something that has never needed to hurry. It builds by growing, and it grows by taking. It keeps the light going because the light is its breathing, and it keeps the air moving because the air is its blood. It is the landlord, the house, and the hunger in the house.

Then there are the ones with hands.

They are not quite people anymore—or not only people. Call them the grafted, the root-kin, the tenants in the seams. They are tall and narrow, their skin the wet gray-brown of bark after rain, mottled with lichen in the hollows of the joints. Their hair is a mat of fine rootlets, never dry. Their eyes have a second lid, milky and slow, that slides across when the ceiling-light flares. Their mouths are small; their teeth are worn flat from a lifetime of chewing fungal shelf and mineral crust. They smell of loam and crushed stems. When they stand still too long—and they often do—moss begins at the ankles, and they do not always brush it off.

They are born in nursery pockets in the root-mass: for the first years of life, a child is partly sessile, rooted at the soles, fed by the Green before it knows it is being fed. Then the root scar closes, and the child walks. In old age many of them root again, by choice or by inattention, and become slow, listening shapes in the wall. They do not consider this death. They consider it retirement.

They build almost nothing, because building is a temporary argument with growth, and growth always wins. They borrow: a fungal shelf becomes a stair, a hollow root becomes a room, a curtain of vine becomes a door. They keep no fires. Fire is a wound in a world that has no wound-healing but absorption. They drink condensation from cupped leaves. They eat what the Green offers and what the Green kills. They know the rule of sound: the loudest thing in the room is usually the most dangerous, and also usually the most afraid. So they speak in taps, in low hums, in vibrations passed through root-cable and stone. Their names are not spoken. They are carved as spirals—turns of a line that mean a lineage, a debt, a count of nights survived. The spiral on the wall is not a map. It is a prayer that the ceiling will keep breathing.

They are few. A dozen families scattered through the root-seams, maybe fewer. They avoid the open basins where the light is brightest and the moth-things swarm thickest. They know which hanging vesicles are true glow and which are the lantern-mimics waiting to descend. They know the screamers in the root-mass by their pitch. They are not masters of the Vault. They are tolerated guests who have learned the etiquette of being eaten slowly.

And then there is the one in the stone house.

The house is half-swallowed now, its stacked stones furred with moss, its hearth cold for a long time. But the bed of dried moss inside is fresh. The ladder outside—vine-wood lashed with sinew—climbs toward a flowering shelf that has since moved. Someone still uses it. Someone still climbs it to nowhere and sits there, listening.

The tenant is old in a way that is hard to measure. Their skin has gone green in the seams. A fern grows from one shoulder, small and deliberate. Their fingers are stained with sap and mineral dust. They are not the gardener; no one tends the Vault. They are the guest who has learned not to stand still too long. They keep the hearth cold. They carve spirals when they need to remember something the Green is already forgetting. They speak rarely, and when they do it is a low, careful sound, because they know what loudness means here. They will offer a traveler water wrung from moss and watch, without embarrassment, for the first green to appear at the traveler’s wrists.

They are not hostile. Hostility requires intent. But they are hungry in the way everything here is hungry, and they have had a long time to become patient. They are the Vault’s unfinished resident: not yet absorbed, not quite separate. When the light dims, they notice before the plants do. When a new shelf of stone pushes through the moss, they hear it before it breaks. They are lonely only in the way a root is lonely when it touches another root and cannot tell where it ends.

So the Vault is not empty. It is crowded. It is inhabited by the Green, by the crawling and fluttering and screaming things, by the grafted people in the seams, and by one old tenant in a stone house who has not yet decided whether to be eaten or to eat. All of them are quiet. All of them are listening. The loudest thing in the room is usually the most afraid.
