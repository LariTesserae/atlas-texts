The Bell is inhabited by a dry, noisy commonwealth — not a list of species so much as a closed loop with many mouths. Everything there lives by the same arrangement: sap moves, noise moves, growth leans toward the noise, and the dead are packed back into the root-mat until they become growth again. The categories leak into one another. The plants are listeners; the animals are walking vessels of sap; the tenders are the ones who keep the listening from stopping.

**The rooted listeners** are the thickets themselves — the bellows-plants, the cordgrass cables, the lungmoss mats. They are not scenery. They are voluminous, hollow, waxy-skinned inhabitants, taller than a person in the case of the bellows-plants, creaking when the wind leans on them, swiveling slowly on their root-braces like blind heads. They do not turn toward light; they turn toward uproar. A thicket is thickest where the chirpers swarm and the drummers beat. The sap inside them is amber and thick and smells of turpentine and burnt sugar; it is their blood and the Bell’s water both. They are lush by commitment, every surface leathery or resinous, nothing soft, nothing wet, everything patient and swollen and listening.

**The mobile sap** is the animals. The hard-shelled chirpers go up in columns and come down and go up again, and their wings are a pressure on the ear. The drummers — flat, many-legged, hand-sized — find the thin places in the crust and beat on them deliberately, not quite rhythmically, and the ground hands the beat back up through your feet. The gliding kites ride the gyre in long ellipses. The grey hopping herds come through in rushes, a hundred at once, and are gone. They drink sap, not water, and it coats their throats and burns going down. They carry it in their bellies and on their bodies, and they leave it behind in scat and shed shell and the slow seep of their skin, and the root-mat takes it up like felt. Nothing rots there. The dead dry and are collected — by ants, by tenders, by the wind — and are packed into the mat and become the next thicket.

**The carriers** are the slow, enormous ones: walking cisterns of sap, patient as barges, with small animals riding them and drinking from the seams of their hide. They come to the rubbing-posts to be scratched, and the posts are worn smooth and dark with handling. They are not beasts of burden exactly; they are infrastructure with a heartbeat. Around them live the small riders and attendants, the ants and tenders of the dry economy, the ones who tidy the dead into the root-mat and keep the sap moving.

**The tenders** are few, and they are the strangest inhabitants because they are the only ones who know the rule as a rule. They live at the edge of the Bell in low shelters of woven cordgrass smeared with resin against the dust. They wear resin-stiff coats. They carry sacks of bone spiles and rattles. They tap the bellows-plants; they do not cut them. They keep the three harps strung along the western shelf, where the wind is forced through gaps in the rock and makes a constant low complaining chord, and the growth around those harps is the thickest in the Bell. They talk to the thickets out loud, because the thickets are listening, and because a silent tender is a tender who has let a grove die. In the evening they sit at the fire-pit of blackened stones and drum on a flat rock, and the chirpers come down to see, and the bellows-plants turn their blind faces toward the noise and swell.

And when the wind falters once or twice a year and the Bell goes still — when the harps stop and the drummers stop and the whole bowl holds its breath — the tenders stop too. They stand very still in the chalky dust and wait, because quiet is death here, and they are waiting for the gyre to take the rim again and the roaring to come back and the place to remember how to be alive.
