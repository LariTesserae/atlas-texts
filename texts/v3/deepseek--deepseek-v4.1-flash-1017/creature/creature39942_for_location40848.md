# The Sokers

## The body

The Soke grows a kind of person as surely as it grows a wall, and the first thing you notice is that they are all a little bit stooped.

Not bent — stooped forward, neck set low, head carried a fraction ahead of the shoulders, the way you carry yourself when you have spent your life walking under lintels that are lower every year and reaching down to channels at shin height. The spine takes a gentle permanent bow. Visitors from the lowlands stand up straight in the Soke and look, after a day, like they are trying to be seen.

They are small and wiry rather than heavy. The strength is all in the grip: the forearm, the thumb, and the two fingers next to it. A Soker's hand is her first tool and her hooked knife is the second, and you can read a trade off the callus — a hard ridge across the palm from the back of the blade, a pad on the thumb where the graft is pressed home and held while it takes, a crescent at the base of the fingers from hauling wet rope. Everyone has the same scar, too, on the left forearm, where the reach steadies the branch while the right hand cuts. There is an old joke that you can tell a local from a lowlander by whether the cut is fresh.

The skin stays pale, there being no sun to speak of and no summer that bakes; but it is mottled and speckled, thorn-pocked on the shins, nicked on the knuckles, shiny with small burns from the smouldering heaps and the pitch that spits out of them. Hair is short or bound and oiled, because loose hair in the Soke ends up in the grafts. The old have teeth the colour of the travertine — spring water and smoke and a great deal of fermented and pickled and salted things. Feet are bare more often than not in the lower town, in the warm wet, soles split and regrown until they are like the insides of old boots. Everybody smells of cut sap, woodsmoke, and the cold mineral smell of the spring.

And everybody coughs. By forty there is a wetness in the chest that never quite leaves. The Soke cough, they call it, and they say it the way you'd name a dog.

## The ear

The ear is the organ of the Soke.

A Soker knows the song of every channel within earshot — the home channel and the branch and the branch of the branch, the one that drops over the third stair like a slap and the one that runs under the tanner's floor and mutters. They wake when a note changes. They can hear the difference between condensation beginning to drip and rain beginning to fall, which matters more than you would think, because one is a morning's work and the other is a lost day and possibly a lost roof. They can hear a graft creaking a season before it fails. In the deep parts of a wet night they hear the eels coming up the Throat before the otters do.

It is also why they stand so close. The water and the frogs and the crickets and the crowd will not be talked over, so the Soke talks *under*: faces near, words clipped at the ends, a flat carrying tone from the chest. Everyone reads lips without knowing they do it. Everyone has a private set of gestures — a flat hand for the seep-line, a hooked finger for *come cut this*, a closed fist shaken twice for *the Spill is coming, get in*.

## The year

They do not count years. They count cuttings.

Nobody says *three years ago*. They say *the year the Throat bridge was re-grafted*, or *the spring the low terrace went*, and everyone knows when that was, and everyone is wrong in a different way, and that is the argument they will have for the rest of the evening. The Cut makes the map every spring, and the map is the town's one sacred object and its favourite quarrel. Two old men can spend an afternoon proving that the baker's steps stand where the tanner's steps stood, both of them correct, each in his own decade.

They are not a nostalgic people. Nostalgia needs ruins, and the Soke does not permit ruins. A house is renewed by being cut back and grown around the people in it; a stair is re-laid when it has been worn into a trough; an abandoned building is not left to fall, it is *taken*, and the takings go into fuel and fibre and feed. They venerate the cut, not the thing cut. To be old in the Soke is to have been cut many times and to have grown back crooked, and crookedness is a kind of honour.

The dead are given to the Throat — there is no cemetery that would stay put. And then the family grafts a joint into the frame of the house: a deliberate knot, worked and reworked every year so it will not straighten, and the house grows around it. It is not a grave you can visit. It is a place in the wall you can put your hand on. A very old house is full of them, and a very old house is therefore a very sad one, and the Soke finds this comforting. The only monument they build is a place where the grain goes wrong on purpose.

The knife is the one thing that does not get eaten. It is re-hafted, re-ground, worn down over a life to a sliver no longer than a finger, and then it goes to a child as a first blade, and the child learns to cut with her grandmother's leavings. There are blades in the Soke that have been in a family for nine cuttings. Nobody would say so, because it would imply permanence, but they know which ones they are.

## Manners

They will feed a stranger without being asked and then interrogate him for an hour, and they will lend him a meal without a word and a length of copper only with a tally and a witness. Copper comes up from the lowlands and is sharpened and treasured until it is an embarrassment of a thing and then finally retired to the map-keeper's box.

They are superstitious in a practical sort of way. Nobody builds on the seep-line at the foot of the wall. Everyone obeys it. Nobody can quite say why, and the approximate answer is a collapse two hundred years ago, and the real answer is older than that.

What they fear is stillness. A silent channel, a quiet evening, a roof that does not need cutting — these are not rest, these are symptoms. Silence means something has gone wrong upstream, and the Soke is a hundred paces from a gorge and a third of the way up a mountain and has learned to take a warning. A person who has been down in the lowlands too long, or who has lost the ear, or who no longer flinches at quiet, is said to have gone *dry*. It is the worst thing one Soker can say of another, and it is said gently, and usually about someone who is not there.

## Two of them

Pell of the Low Steps has been a cutter of the Cut for sixty years and has outlived four houses on the same stump. She cannot straighten her back and does not try. Her toes are so fused from the damp that she walks on the outsides of her feet, a crab-wise scuttle that is faster than it looks, and she knows the sound of every graft on the lower terrace and can point at a wall and tell you which joint will go first. She keeps a tally-stick for the map and disagrees with it. Children bring her moths in the morning, not for the food but because she will tell them what the water sounded like before they were born, and they want to know, and she lies to them with great precision.

And Sisk, who is nine, who can hear a graft failing through a floor and will wake the household for it. She has a moth basket and a sliver of her grandmother's knife and a cat that is not hers but has decided it is. When the Spill comes she stands in the middle of the street with her face tipped up and her mouth open and does not move until it is over, and her mother has stopped calling her in.

## And the rest

They are outnumbered and they know it, and it has made them what they are: quick, decent, close-talking, knife-handed. They kill an eel without ceremony and use every part of it. They sweep the moths off the walls at dawn and salt them and eat them. They let the cats be half-wild and name the frogs. They eat their own past and call it wisdom and are, on the whole, right.

You would not call them serene. You would call them a people who have made their peace with the crowd, and remake it every morning, with a knife, before the cloud lifts.
