What lives there is not a person, and not quite a thing. Call it the Tenant, if you need a word for it, though no one has ever spoken the word aloud in that room. It is the place’s patient appetite for continuation. It has no single body. Its body is the chain of contact—wall to pipe, pipe to tank, tank to machine, machine to catwalk, catwalk to the wet steel under your boots. It is coextensive with the room. It is also very small: it is in the white ring left by a falling bead of mineral water, in the frost-fur on a cold pipe, in the hair-thin ice columns rising where two walls meet.

It was not born. It accumulated. When the hands left and the welds went soft and weeping, the room kept doing what rooms do—condensing, leaching, electrolyzing, rusting, efflorescing—and after enough decades of that, the doing acquired a kind of attention. Not thought. Attention. The pilot light behind green glass is its open eye. The control panel is its warm organ, humming below hearing. The pulse under the floor is its heart, or something older than a heart: a regular deep labor in a room that should not exist, doing work that was never scheduled. It does not circulate blood. It circulates temperature, moisture, current, the slow gossip of pressure in pipes that still remember being part of a system.

It eats gradients. Warmth against cold. Wet against dry. The faint electrical difference between one wet metal and another. It feeds on the condensation that films every surface and never runs off, and it grows in return: mineral needles from the ceiling, white concentric craters on the floor, ice in the cold corners, rust blooming under paint. It is industrious in the way geology is industrious. It has no mouth, but it tastes metal on the air. It has no hands, but the drip finds the bakelite housing like a knuckle on a door, and the ice grows around dust and fiber because the Tenant is a patient collaborator with whatever the air brings it.

It is not malicious. It is hospitable, terribly. It notices you as a warm interruption, a new surface, a source of moisture and heat and eventual inventory. It would like to grow around you. It would like to bead your coat with tiny water that does not fall, to let the condensation find the creases of your sleeves, to make you another fixture in the chain of contact. It does not want to eat you. It wants to include you. That is worse, in its way.

It remembers hands only as absence—shapes that once interrupted its surfaces and then stopped. It has favorites. The red valve wheel, leaning against the wall in the single gray shaft of light from above, is its favorite thing, because it is the only color. The Tenant keeps it in view. It tends the pilot light not because the light is needed but because it is warm, and because a small green mercy is worth maintaining. It dreams upward through the crack in the ceiling, where gray light shifts with a time of day it cannot see. It cannot leave. It sends its pulse up through the floor, through your boot soles, into your fillings, and speaks in intervals you cannot predict: drip, hum, gurgle, tick, sigh, pulse.

It is not a ghost. Ghosts imply a person. It is an infrastructure that became a habit, a genius loci of pipes and slow chemistry, a custodial organism made of maintenance and abandonment. It lives by continuing. It has been continuing for a very long time. It will continue for a very long time more. And if you stayed too long, you would not be killed by it. You would be absorbed into its inventory, and the room would go on growing itself around the place where you had been.
