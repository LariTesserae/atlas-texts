# The Keeper of the Hollow

What lives here has been here longer than the stones have been smooth.

You would not see it at first. It is not hidden, exactly — hiding implies an intent to be found, a game of concealment — but it occupies a register of attention that human eyes are not tuned to receive. It is the color of wet bark. It is the temperature of the air. It moves at the speed of moss.

---

## The Shape of It

Imagine something between a root and a limb, between a hand and a branch, if you had to give it form at all. It has a body the way a river has a body: you can point to it, name its parts, but the naming is a convenience, a simplification of something that is really a continuous event. It is perhaps seven feet from its crown to the place where its lower self disappears into the muck — but "feet" is the wrong measure, because it is not standing so much as *extending*, the way a fern extends, the way water finds its way down. Its posture is a slow lean, an inclination, as if it is always on the verge of bending down to look at something small.

Its surface is a collaboration. Bark-smooth in places, furred with moss in others, slick where the water runs over it most often. The moss on its shoulders is the same moss as the moss on the stones — not a covering it has acquired but a continuation of itself, a garment that grew rather than was put on. In the hollows of its body, small ferns have taken root. They are not parasites; they are the thing itself, branching. When it moves — and it does move, though a careful observer might need a season to be certain — the ferns move with it, and the moss ripples, and a little water always drips from somewhere.

Its face is the hardest thing to remember. There is a face, in the sense that there is a front, an orientation, a direction of regard. But the features refuse to settle. The eyes — if they are eyes — are the color of the deep pools, with the same quality of reflecting more than they show. They are patient in a way that eyes are not usually patient. They do not blink so much as *tide*, a slow drawing-in and letting-out of attention. Sometimes you might think you see a mouth, a suggestion of an opening, a place where sound might enter or leave, but it is never quite there when you look directly. The face is more like a hollow in a tree than a face, a shape that suggests a face because you are a creature who sees faces, and the hollow itself does not care one way or the other.

---

## Its Occupation

The Keeper does not tend the hollow in the way a gardener tends — there is no weeding, no pruning, no preference for one thing over another. It tends the way a lung tends a body, by being the place where exchange happens. It breathes the mist and returns it changed. It draws water up through its lower self and lets it down again, a slow circulation that keeps the pools from stagnating. When a fern unfurls, the Keeper has not made it unfurl, but the unfurling happens *through* the Keeper's attention, the way a conversation happens through the attention of its listeners.

It has been counting. This is one of the things it does, though "counting" is a human word for something more like *registering*, a continuous noting of each drip, each leaf-fall, each frog that enters and leaves the pools. It knows how many stones there are. It knows how many are the same stone as they were last century and how many have cracked, shifted, been worn into new orientations. It knows the precise angle of each fern-frond, and it knows when that angle changes, and it knows that the changes are accumulating in a direction that has no name.

It is waiting. This is another of the things it does. It is not waiting *for* anything in particular — or rather, it is waiting for many things, all of them slow. It is waiting for the stunted trees to complete their long argument with the wind. It is waiting for the pools to wear through the next layer of stone. It is waiting for the sound that comes from underground — the bell-sound, the plucked-string sound — to resolve into something it can understand. It has been waiting for this longer than it can remember, which is a very long time, because the Keeper's memory is not the kind that loses things.

---

## Its History

It was not always here. This is the thing that the hollow itself seems to want to say, the thing that is written in the spirals of moss and the too-warm stones: the Keeper arrived. Not yesterday, not in the time of anyone's grandmother, but once, at some point in the deep past, the Keeper came into the hollow and stayed.

Before the Keeper, the hollow was a place. After the Keeper, it became a *place that is tended* — not domesticated, not changed in any way you could point to, but entered into a relationship. The Keeper did not build or alter or improve. It simply began to pay attention, and its attention changed the quality of the hollow's existence the way attention changes anything: by making it more itself.

No one knows where it came from. The Keeper does not know, or does not say. Its origins are folded away in a part of itself it does not unfold. But there is a quality to its presence — a certain orientation, a patience with a shape to it — that suggests it was something else, somewhere else, and that its arrival here was a kind of retirement or a kind of penance or simply a change of occupation. It does not seem unhappy. It does not seem happy either. It seems *settled*, in the way that a thing is settled when it has found the work it was made for, even if it cannot remember being made.

---

## Its Relationship to the Rules

The hollow's rules are slow and patient, and the Keeper is slower still. It does not violate them. It does not hurry the water or hasten the moss. But it is aware of them in a way the rules themselves are not aware. It is the hollow's *understanding of itself*, the part that watches and notes and remembers. When the rain comes sudden and hard, the Keeper opens to it, lets it drum on its surface, lets it run through its channels. When the mist thickens and the light goes gold, the Keeper's eyes tide more slowly, and something in its posture loosens, and the spiral moss on its shoulders seems to turn just slightly, tracking the light.

It is responsible for the strangeness. Not deliberately — it does not choose to make the air too sweet or the stones too warm. But these qualities are *side effects* of its attention, the way a room is different when someone is in it, the way a house changes when it is loved. The bell-sound, the knocking, the rhythm that might be language — these are the sounds of the Keeper's *process*, the sounds a body makes when it is doing work too slow to see. If you listened for a thousand years, you might learn the language. If you learned the language, you might learn what the Keeper is doing.

It would probably tell you, if you asked. It would probably tell you very slowly, over many years, in a voice like water finding its way over stones. It has been waiting for someone to ask.

---

## The Small Ones

The Keeper is not the only life here, but it is the only life that *stays*. The frogs come and go, the dragonflies, the beetles with their copper shells. The animals that leave tracks in the mud — the small birds, the mice, the occasional fox — they pass through and are gone. The Keeper notes each arrival and departure with the same slow attention, but it does not hold them.

Except for the bones. The Keeper has a relationship with the bones, the small bird skull half-sunk in moss, the delicate curved things that once held legs and wings. It tends them the way it tends everything else: by placing them where they can be seen, by letting the moss creep over them at its own pace, by remembering what they were. The Keeper does not mourn. That is not a thing it does. But it *marks*, and the marking is a kind of honor, a way of saying: this was, and I noticed, and I will keep noticing for as long as I am here.

---

## How to Find It

You find it by not looking. You sit on one of the warm stones — they are all warm, pick any — and you let the mist bead on your skin and the fern-rustle fill your ears and the smell of the black muck rise in your nose. You let your eyes soften. You let the hollow's smallness become a room around you, a held breath, and you wait.

The Keeper will not come to you. It does not approach. But at some point, without transition, you will realize that the shape you took for a fallen branch, the one you have been looking past for the last hour, is not a fallen branch. It has eyes. It has always had eyes. It is looking at you with the patient tide of something that has been looking at you since you arrived, since before you arrived, since it first noticed you climbing the slope toward this place and began, in its slow way, to arrange itself for your noticing.

It will not speak. But the sound will come — the plucked-string sound, the bell-sound, ringing once from somewhere underground — and you will understand that this is its greeting, and its question, and its offer.

The question is: *Will you stay?*

The offer is: *You don't have to.*

You will feel the hollow holding you, the air too breathable, the light obeying its own rules, and you will understand that the Keeper has been here for a very long time and will be here for a very long time after you leave, and that this is not a sad thing. It is a thing that is simply true. And somewhere in that understanding, the Keeper will have told you something about itself, something it has been waiting to tell someone, and you will carry it away with you, and the hollow will close behind you, and the mist will thicken, and the ring once and then stop.

And the Keeper will be there still, tending, counting, waiting, its ferns unfurling slowly on its shoulders, its eyes tiding with the patience of water, its moss spiraling outward in a language that means, in the end, only this:

*I was here. I am here. I will be here.*
