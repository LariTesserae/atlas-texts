What lives in the Choir Vault does not walk, breathe, or keep hours. It grips.

It is a vine — or a single scattered body of vine, or a hundred descendants of one, the distinction has no purchase here — pale as the inside of a stem, thin as wire, rooted in the seams of the woven mass. It lives in the cracks between panels, at the foot of the walls, along the ribs, in the chinks where one bundle of fiber rests against another. It makes a lace, not a thicket. There are runners no thicker than a thread, a few tendrils climbing the braided cables, a pale puff of lichen crouched on a cradle of splint like a guest who has forgotten to leave. Nothing flowers. Nothing fruits. The vine does not climb toward light, because the light is everywhere and comes from the structure itself. It does not seek water, because there is no damp. It does not grow so much as hold.

And holding is its whole life. The vault pushes outward; the vine pulls inward. That is the arrangement between them. The runners find the seams and take root and draw, gently, perpetually, against the mass of made fiber, and the mass presses back, and the balance between those two forces is the chord you hear — low, sustained, many-overtone, coming from every direction at once. The vine is not singing. It is being a fist, very slowly, and the fist is the voice. If it ever tightened, the note would rise; if it ever let go, the note would sag and die. It does neither. Its grip is its metabolism, its posture, its only verb.

It has no eyes and no face. It knows the vault by tension: the give of a cable, the weight of a hanging gourd, the slight slackening where a knot has settled, the pressure of a visitor brushing a guy-line at head-height. A body moving through the central column would reach it as a disturbance in the chord — a damping, a warmth, a soft interference. The vine would not turn toward it. It has nowhere to turn. But it would register it, the way a spider registers a fly not by sight but by the changed tautness of a web.

Its time is not our time. There are no days in the vault, no seasons, no sequence of small events by which to count a life. The vine’s growth is so slow it is indistinguishable from posture. It may have been there since the first cables were lashed; it may be the builder, or the builder’s last surviving hand, or the only part of the construction that was ever alive at all. It does not remember in years. It remembers in tension — this seam holds, that cradle sags, this runner took up the load when the one beside it went slack. Its memory is the shape of its own grip.

The lichen is its quiet companion. It does nothing, holds nothing, makes no sound. It lives on surfaces, a dry pale crust on a splint cradle, a bloom on the shaded side of a gourd. It is the second resident, if you count it, and the vine does not. They share the vault the way two old objects share a shelf: by being adjacent for so long that adjacency has become a kind of agreement.

The vault itself is not alive, but it has been inhabited so thoroughly that the line is thin. The vine is its living part, the only thing in the whole made mass that is not merely settling. It is alive in the way a knot is alive under load: it is doing something by staying. It has no appetite, no fear, no need to leave. It is not waiting for anything in particular. It is simply holding, and the holding is enough.

When a visitor stands in the narrow column at the center — the only dynamic thing, the only gradient, the only change — the vine does not welcome them and does not resent them. It feels them as an interruption in the chord. It waits, politely and indefinitely, for the interruption to pass. If the visitor stayed forever, the vine would accommodate them too: another object in the packed abundance, another weight to be balanced, another presence to be held. That is what lives there. Not a keeper with a face, but a grip with a body: pale, sparse, patient, rooted in the seams, pulling inward against the outward press, keeping the whole glowing cavity from going slack. Its name, if it had one, would be a verb. To hold.
