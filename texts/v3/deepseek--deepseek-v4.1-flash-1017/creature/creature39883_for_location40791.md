They are, first of all, people of the hand. Not the eye—the eye is the wrong organ here. The small pale eyes, the nictitating films, the way they can face into the fountain-hiss without flinching: all of that is a matter of making the eye small enough to ignore. The hand is what they live by. Every person in the Vault is fluent in touch before they are fluent in anything else, because touch is the only sense that cuts cleanly through the water's constant noise and the cold's constant pressure. Their fingers run long, splayed, webbed to the second knuckle and in some lines to the wrist, and the skin of the fingers thickens in the first decade of life into something like a callus and like a glove and like a hide, so that a hand grown in the Vault looks at forty the way a piece of driftwood looks after the sea is finished with it: smoothed, darkened, ridged with the marks of use.

They cannot speak to each other underwater, or not well. Sound travels too far and carries every meaning too plainly. Instead they have what the ethnographers would call a tactile register—but that's a word from outside. Inside, they call it *closing*, though that's a translation too, and an imperfect one. It means the holding of a hand, the pressing of a palm flat against a shoulder, a knuckle drawn slowly down a forearm. It means grammar made of contact: a question is a certain pressure; an assent is a return of it; a warning is a grip that tightens and does not let go. Two divers can carry on the better part of a conversation this way in total darkness, over the roar of a fountain, while both keep their eyes on the schools they are hunting. A mother will close with her child all day, not because the child needs instruction but because that is what a mother's hand does, and the child learns the difference between the pressures the way a child on the surface learns the difference between tones of voice.

They wear very little. The cold, they will tell you, is not the cold you are thinking of; the water is a uniform temperature and a uniform pressure and the body that lives in it becomes a body that stops arguing. Their skin is a cool gray with a flush of something underneath—the older ones go powdery pale, the younger ones have a faint blue or olive in the palms and along the ridge of the spine and across the arches of the feet, the color of the blooms they harvest. They grow slowly and live long; the old ones are enormous in a quiet, unhurried way, and the very old are said to stop moving almost entirely and simply float in doorways of the interior chambers, hands folded, listening. A child is a small fast thing that swims in loops around the adults for the first eight or nine years, then settles, then begins to work.

They do not swim the way we swim. A swimmer from the surface lifted into the Vault would look like the wrong kind of animal—churning, expending, fighting the water for every foot of advance. The Vault-born move through it as though it were not a substance but a direction. They fold themselves along the current, they bank, they let the long columns draw them around their curves, they ride the outfalls of the fountains which are rivers in themselves if you know where the rivers run. A diver leaving a doorway in the great column called—in their practice of naming things by what they do—the One That Stands Against the Current, will arrive at a harvesting ledge two miles away in the time it takes a surface hour to pass, and never once put out a hand to move. The water takes them. They let it. To resist the water, here, is the moral failure, the thing children are taught to be ashamed of, the way we are ashamed of clumsiness.

Their houses are in the bones of the thing. This is the fact their whole culture is built on, and it is worth being clear about: they do not live in the water. They live in the dry chambers the water has not yet found. Every column has its hollow, every hollow its sealed doors, every door its lock of a type that no one remembers designing and everyone knows how to operate. A family lives in a chamber that may be twenty feet across and thirty feet high or may be the better part of a cathedral, depending on which column the family's ancestors happened to claim. The interiors are warm—the engines see to that—and dry, and bright with little lamps that burn a steadier light than the Lid's, and lined with the things that a family accumulates when a family is patient and doesn't have much to throw away. Netting stretched and dried and folded. Blooms, cured into something you can eat, hanging in bundles from the low curves of the ceiling. Rope, which in the Vault is a kind of writing, because each rope is knotted at intervals with a knot that means a dive, a season, a death. Tools of stone and of the old metal they harvest. A child's first hand-glove, kept. The teeth of an eel that a grandfather killed.

And in the middle of each chamber, in the warm and listening dark: the engines themselves. Not every family has one. But most have at least a wall of one, a panel of buttons and dials and the low vibrating hush of a machine that is older than the family and that the family tends with a tenderness they do not always give to each other. The engine is not a tool, to them. It is closer to what a hearth is, or a saint. It has a temperament. It likes some things and not others. The grandfather's father's grandfather is said to have known a way of tapping the casing that made it run cooler, and that tap was lost when the old man drowned on a dive, and the family has been trying to rediscover it for six generations. They will put their palm flat on the warm metal and not speak. The engine hums its sixty-cycles and the whole column carries that hum, and the family's bodies learn the pitch of it—a person raised in the column can hear when the engine is not well from three rooms away, can hear the difference between a bad hour and the beginning of a bad year.

They eat what the water gives. The schools are the great harvest—they run nets across the necks of the halls where the fish funnel, and the nets fill and the divers haul them in and the fish are gutted and dried and salted and kept. The blooms are the second harvest, gathered with a care the schools do not require, because the blooms do not regrow quickly and the elders who know where they grow are the ones who decide when they may be cut. A bloom harvest is a ceremony. The harvesting dives are timed to the Lid's dimming, when the light comes up and the water is bright and the divers can see the growth-mats whole. The divers go out in a line, oldest leading, youngest trailing, and they work in the faint cold fluorescence with their hands, picking in a slow rhythm that is understood to be the proper rhythm, and the picking of too many is understood to be a failure of character and, ultimately, a variety of theft. There are families that are spoken of badly for having cut too deep into a bloom-mat two generations ago, and the mat has not recovered, and the bad speaking has not stopped.

What is hardest to translate is their sense of the fountains. A surface observer would call it religious, and then would be corrected, and then would stop using the word. But they do not have a word for it either; they have only the practice. The practice is this: when a person passes beneath a fountain—when the falling condensation reaches them and begins to come down on their shoulders and skull—they slow. Not stop. Slowing is important; stopping would be a claim. They slow and let the fountain fall on them for a certain count of breaths and then they go on. Everyone does this. A diver on urgent business will not skip it. A child learns it before they learn to hunt. The old ones will sometimes slow and stay slowed and let the fountain fall on them for a long time, so long that a companion will begin to worry, and then they will come out of it and go on as though nothing had happened, and will not say what they were doing, because there is no saying what they were doing—or rather, there is, but the saying of it is the slowing and the letting, and to put words around it would be to double it, and doubling a thing is how you ruin it.

I should say something of the ones who go down. The Base-divers. Every generation has a few. They are not, as a rule, the reckless ones—the reckless ones die early and are mourned and forgotten—they are the quiet ones, the ones who have been slowed by a fountain and have come out of it changed, who have begun to ask the questions that the world does not answer because the world has not been arranged to answer them. They go down in ones and twos. They go with a rope and a light and the closing-hands of whoever will come with them, and they go past the eels and past the light of the Lid and down into the cold where the water grows heavy and the roar of the fountains dies away and the hum of the engines reaches them only as a suggestion, a remembered thing, the sound a house makes in the ears of a person who has left it. And they come to the edge. The cut place. The place where the columns stop being anchored and simply cease, where the structure comes down out of the dark and ends, and there is nothing below but the water going on, and the water going on, and the water going on.

What do they see there? Ask three Base-divers and you will get three accounts. One says: nothing. One says: columns, continuing, down out of sight, and a faint light that was not the Lid's light. One says: a door, or the place where a door would be, though no one could say what it was a door in. The three accounts are not reconciled. The Base-divers are not asked to reconcile them, because a Base-diver who has come back is regarded as slightly different from a person who has not, and is excused certain habits of speech. They are given more room than they had before. They are listened to at the fire. But they are not, usually, given children to teach, and they are not, usually, entrusted with the bloom-harvest, and there is a way that the community holds them at arm's length, a way that is not unkindness and is not fear and is not quite anything with a name.

The children ask about them constantly. Every child in the Vault, at some age—usually nine or ten, when the settling begins—decides they will go down to the Base one day. Most outgrow it. The outgrowing is not complete and not public; it is a slow putting-away of the idea, a folding of it into a smaller and smaller shape, until the adult has it somewhere in the back of themselves, folded, and takes it out now and then to look at it, and does not speak of it. The ones who do not outgrow it are the Base-divers of the next generation, and they are recognized before they go: the elders see the slowing in them, see the way they linger under the fountains, see the way they hang back at the doorways and listen to the engines, and the elders know, and they do not stop them, because stopping is not something this world has learned to do to its own people.

And the fish—the schools, the great slow rivers of them—do not care about any of this. That is the thing that keeps the Vault honest. The schools ribbon through the columns whether or not anyone is watching, and the eels take the weak fish at the edges of the schools, and the crawling things graze the architecture in their patient tides, and the blooms open and close their pale fronds in the mineral-rich water, and the engines hum, and the Lid sweats and sweats and the ten thousand fountains pour down through the bright and moving nothing, and the people swim among it all, closing their hands, slowing under the falls, doing the work of a world they did not build and cannot explain.

It is a good life, they will tell you, if you ask—and they will tell you with a certain surprise in the telling, because it is a thing they have had to decide, and have decided. The cold, the dark, the roar, the weight of water overhead, the questions that have no answers and the engines that have their own opinions: none of it is comfortable. But it is theirs. The Vault was not made for them and does not know they are in it, and they have made it a home anyway, which is—one of the old ones will tell you, folded against a doorway, hands still, eyes almost shut—which is, in the end, the only kind of home there ever was, anywhere, for anyone.
