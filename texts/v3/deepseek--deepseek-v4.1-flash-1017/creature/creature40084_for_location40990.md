What lives in Vell is a people who have been edited by their home.

They are not a separate race; they are what happens to people who have lived for generations under a root, beside a pump, inside a drip. They are shorter than they might have been, maybe, or only seem so because they walk with a shoulder-roll, ducking under vines that have grown since morning. Their faces are lamp-tanned—orange across the cheekbones, pale where the oilcloth hoods cover them—and their hands are moss-stained in the creases, green-black at the knuckles, nails ridged from wet work. They keep their hair cropped or braided tight because everything here drips. They wear felt and oilcloth and root-fiber soles, aprons with too many pockets, hook-knives on their belts. Their ears are muffled against the engines with waxed cloth or fungal felt, and they tilt their heads when they listen, the way people do when they are reading a sound rather than hearing it.

They wake to the pumps, not the sun. The first shift is when the generators cough and settle into their grinding note, and the whole burrow seems to exhale. They speak in a half-shout, clipped and practical, and they have a hand-language for the loud places: a flat palm for *pump*, two fingers flicked upward for *grow*, a thumb drawn across the throat for *water rising*. Children learn it before they learn to read. They also learn the walk—the shoulder-roll, the easy grace for moving under a fine indoor drizzle without minding it, the way you step over a channel without looking.

Their work is maintenance, and maintenance is their religion. There are pump-tenders who can hear a failing bearing through the floor and know which of the dozen engines is sick by the color of its smoke. There are fungus-binders, seam-washers, tanners, condenser-crews, lamp-hangers, beetle-keepers. There are the Grow-watchers, who walk with chalk sticks and painted stakes, measuring the slow creep of roots, marking which wall has begun to lean, which roof will have to be unbuilt and rebuilt a pace to the left. They are not architects of permanence. They are architects of yielding. They know the forest above is not done growing. They know nothing stays built. They have made a craft of moving things in place.

They are calm, but not soft. They are stubborn in the way of people who have argued with a root and won a season. They salvage before they mourn. When a building has to move, they make a feast of it—everyone hauls, everyone eats, the hounds bark, the birds steal, the lamps are re-hung on the other side of the new growth. They keep shrines in the root-clefts, small things: a twist of wire, a beetle-shell, a cup of clean water. They leave the first fruit of every harvest out for the pale things in the deep seams, not because they are afraid, exactly, but because the pale things were here first and it seems rude. They have catalogued the bioluminescent pulses into a calendar. They know which bladder-vine means rain and which means the pumps are failing. They are not afraid of the dark. They have lived in it long enough to have made it warm.

And they are not alone.

The burrow-hounds live with them—hairless, pale, long-jawed, kept as guards and companions, barking at everything and answered by everything. The flit-lizards live in the root-cracks and come out to snap at midges. The beetles trundle the paths like small armored citizens; children tie strings to the big ones and are pulled along. The moths dust the lamps. The millipedes climb the walls in drifts and are harvested for food and medicine. The birds—bright, raucous, transplanted from the world above and gone feral—nest in the machinery and shriek at the lizards and steal from the drying-racks. The engines themselves are almost animals: kept, listened to, fed, cursed, tended. Each has its own heartbeat, and the people know them the way a village knows its wells.

Deeper, in the flooded seams, live the pale things that are only heard. No one has a good description of them. They are a splash, a soft knocking, a shape that is not there when the lamp swings around. The Vell-born do not hunt them. They leave offerings and tell stories and go on with their work.

So what lives in Vell is a dense, loud, damp, practical people, moss-stained and lamp-tanned, who have learned to live inside a living machine without expecting it to hold still. They are the ones who re-hang the lamp when the bracket is swallowed, who re-deck the bridge every season, who shout over the engines and sign with their hands and leave the first fruit out for the dark water. They are not afraid of the forest above or the water below. They have made a warm little lit hollow between them and called it home.
