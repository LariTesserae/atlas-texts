The Flue is not inhabited by a species so much as by a condition: a moving rind of small blind animals, a few larger scavengers, and something in the walls that knocks.

**The pale grazers** are the room’s ground cover. They are thumbnail-sized, hard-shelled, pale as the salt they eat, packed shoulder to shoulder until the rock has no color but theirs. Each has a low domed carapace of overlapping plates, translucent at the rim, crusted white at the crown. Underneath, a gray, soft body and a mouth like a rotary file. They have no eyes. Along the shell’s edge, fine bristles read heat and moisture. They orient to the fissure the way a plant orients to the sun, though they have never seen the sun. They feel the hot breath, crawl toward it, graze the mineral bloom, and then retreat when the air turns. When your shadow falls over them they do not scatter. They close—a thousand tiny doors—and wait. Then they open and resume.

They are miners. They rasp the sulfur seams and the salt crust down to bare rock. The rock does not stay bare. The vent exhales, the minerals condense, the crust blooms again, and the grazers return. They do not drink. They take the trace moisture bound in the crust, and later from each other. Their bodies are small reservoirs in a place where water is currency. They are prey and pantry both. When one dies, the shell stays; another may shelter in it; a larger thing may crack it for the moisture inside. The dead do not rot. They dry, curl, and become part of the floor, and the living walk over them.

**The black scrapers** are the room’s large motion. Hand-long, segmented, soot-colored, with stiff legs that tick on stone. They move over the slabs with a high-backed gait, mandibles working, antennae sweeping. They eat the pale grazers and the dead. When one crosses the rind, the grazers close in a wave ahead of it and reopen behind. The scraper does not hurry. It has no need. It can go days without water and then take it from a husk. Its shell is dull except where the red light catches a joint. It might be predator, scavenger, or both; the room does not require the distinction. It is the thing that keeps the rind from sealing the chamber entirely.

**The knocker** is heard before it is seen: three blows, evenly spaced, from inside the wall. A minute passes. Three more. The sound is dry and hard, not the boom of a settling slab. It comes from a different seam each time, as if the wall itself were walking. If you put your ear to the stone you feel the answer through your jaw. The knocker is never out in the open. You see only evidence—a fresh white scar on a ledge, a jointed limb the color of wet slate withdrawing into a crack, a smell like struck flint. It is large enough that its body must be folded through the wall’s fractures, living in the negative space, in seams too narrow for the scrapers. It knocks to test the stone, or to call, or to warn. The grazers do not react. The scrapers pause, then go on. The knocker is the room’s third rhythm, under the hiss and the rain-like seething.

All of them are shaped by the same rules. They follow the air. When the convection turns, the rind migrates in a slow tide, thickening on the cool floor, thinning near the vent. They need heat, mineral, and the trace moisture in both. Their lives are slow. A grazer may take years to reach full size; a swarm may bloom and crash over decades. They are not adapted to the outside. The cold night air would dry them; the sun would kill them; the wet would dissolve their shells. The Flue is not their habitat. It is their whole world.

And they are still making it. Every rasp takes the crust down; every death adds a husk; every breath of the vent lays down another millimeter. They are the workers and the product. They do not know it. They know the hot side and the cold side, the near wall and the far, the three knocks in the dark. They go on scraping the salt.
