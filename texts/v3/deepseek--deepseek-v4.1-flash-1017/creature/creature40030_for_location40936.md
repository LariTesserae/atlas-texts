The ones who live there would not call themselves residents. They would say they tend, or they keep, or they are kept. The distinction matters less each generation.

They are people still, in the way a river stone is still a stone after the water has smoothed it. They are long-limbed and quiet-footed, their skin pale in the way the underside of a leaf is pale, faintly porous, always slightly damp. Their hair is dark and thick and sometimes carries a greenish cast in certain lights—though there is no certain light there, only the green-gold filtering and the blue-white fungal glow. Their eyes are darker than they should be, slower, patient in a way that can look like peace and can look like something that has learned not to flinch. Their hands are long and careful. They touch leaves and root-wood the way other people touch animals they love: not quite stroking, not quite listening, something between.

They live in clusters. Small ones—three homes, five, a dozen—built on the broader platforms where the root-wood is dense enough to bear a hearth and a sleeping-mat and a door of woven leaf. The homes are grown as much as built: saplings trained into ribs, vines encouraged to close over them, fallen wood lashed and then swallowed by new growth until the walls are living and the walls are slowly changing shape. The doors are plaited leaf, and they are closed at night, though not against anything the people will name. The fires are small and careful, fed with dried root-wood, used for cooking and warmth and not for light, because the fungi provide light and because a large fire in this wet breathing place would feel like an argument.

They eat what the garden gives. Fruits that hang heavy and soft from trained branches. Fungi cut from the luminous shelves—some bitter, some sweet, some that must be cooked twice and some that must not be cooked at all. Cultivated insects, pale and fat, kept in woven cages and fed on leaf-mash. Water that drips from above, caught in gutters and channels and carried from platform to platform in thin living streams. They do not hunt the birds. They do not go down into the depths. They prune, and water, and harvest, and mend, and when a platform shifts in the night—when a supporting vine reaches toward better light and a home tilts five feet to the left—they sigh and adjust and do not think it strange.

The children learn the garden before they learn anything else. They learn which roots bear weight and which are hollow. They learn that a path can be clear in the morning and closed by evening, not out of malice but because growth is growth. They learn the maps, which are not maps at all but poems, lists of landmarks and smells and the feel of certain bridges underfoot. They learn that distance is a thing you navigate by intention and acceptance, and that wanting to arrive too much can keep you walking for an hour toward a platform that stays a hundred paces away. They learn to listen for the birds. They learn to leave something when a bird lands near them—a bit of fruit, a scrap of fungus, a moment of stillness—and to listen to whatever the bird seems to want to say. They learn, without being told, that the garden is not cruel. The garden is simply itself.

The birds are the closest thing to news. They are not quite birds—their wings are membranes stretched over leaf-veins, their calls carry strangely through the mist, and their eyes are too aware. But they fly between clusters, and they land on railings and shoulders and the edges of platforms, and they regard. They speak, or sing, or something between. The people say the birds know things, though they cannot say what things, and when a bird arrives it is offered food and silence and a kind of respect that is not quite worship and not quite fear. Then the bird leaves, and the cluster goes back to its tending, and perhaps something has been communicated and perhaps not.

The small creatures are everywhere and nowhere. Insects with too many legs. Pale crawling things in the root-mats. Slow spiral fliers in the mist. They are not pests; they are part of the breathing architecture, and the people have learned to move around them, to harvest some and leave others, to accept that a home is also a habitat. At night the fungi glow, and the water drips louder, and the creatures of the depths become bolder. There are low hooting sounds from below, and sometimes a distant crash of something heavy moving through the lower growth. The people bank their fires and close their doors and tell stories. The stories are about the garden. The garden listens.

The ones who stay long enough change. It is slow, and no one marks the moment. Their skin grows paler, more porous, more leaf-like at the wrists and throat. Their hair darkens. They need less food. They stand still more easily. They sense the garden’s shifts before they happen—a platform drifting, a root-path closing, a bird approaching through the mist—and they do not comment on this, because to comment would be like commenting on your own heartbeat. They say the garden provides for those who tend it. They say the garden cares for those who care for it. They say this without looking at you, their hands moving over the leaves, and you are not entirely sure if it is truth or warning or simply the grammar of a place that has replaced the grammar they were born with.

The dead are placed in the root-mats of the lowest inhabited level. The mats are soft and dark and always slightly warm. The bodies are not buried so much as given over, and the garden takes them, and after a time there is no distinction between the root-mat and what was a person. The people do not call this loss. They call it returning, or being kept, or sometimes they do not call it anything at all.

And below the inhabited levels, in the untended green dark, something lives that was perhaps once part of the design and has since become something else. No one goes down there. The people do not speak of it directly. But they know the hooting, and they know the crash of heavy movement, and they know that the garden has depths that are not for tending. They leave those depths alone. The depths leave them alone. This is not peace, exactly. It is an arrangement.

So the ones who live there are the tenders, the kept, the long-limbed and slow-eyed, the children learning poems that are maps, the old ones whose skin is becoming leaf. They are the birds that carry news and the insects that carry pollen and the fungi that carry light. They are the deep things that hoot in the untended dark, and the garden itself, which breathes and drips and shifts and provides and takes. They live in small warm pockets of tended green, suspended in a hollow sky, and they are not lonely—the place is too full of life for that—but they can be lonely in it, very lonely indeed, and some of them have forgotten how to be anything else.
