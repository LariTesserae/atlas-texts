The permanent citizens of the Aria Vast are small, low, and stubborn. Nothing here stands tall if it can help it. To live in the Vast is to be a thing that presses close to stone, bends with the roar, and lets the wind pass through rather than against it.

The largest resident is a ground bird—sleek and iridescent blue, all legs and streamlined feathers, with wings too stunted for flight. It lives in the lee of quartzite ribs and in the deeper folds of heather, running with its body held level, using the wind to carry its single clear chime farther than a voice could go. You rarely see it whole; you see a blue flash, a scurry, a chime cut off mid-note. It is the Vast’s closest thing to a person: rare, quick, and unwilling to be looked at.

Below it is the true mass of animal life—wingless insects with dark, hard carapaces, scuttling deep in the heather and moss. They come out in the damp hour before dawn to drink condensation from the stone, and they vanish when the wind surges. Stone-mites graze the pale lichen. Small burrowing things live in the root-mat and leave no sign but a faint ticking in the soil. None of them are large. All of them are anchored.

The plants are inhabitants too, and they behave like a thick, resilient skin over the ancient rock. Bronze and rust heather mats the hills. Leathery vines cling between stones and open their small pale flowers only in the fleeting calm between gusts. Water-filled mosses swell like saturated sponges underfoot. Tough woody grass whispers and rattles, bending almost flat and springing back. The whole ground is alive, but it is alive sideways and downward, never upward.

The stone rings and meandering walls are inhabited now by moss and insects and the slow work of lichen. The people who built them are gone—not as ghosts, but as absence. Their huts are half-buried shelters for heather roots; their long stone lines are windbreaks for nothing that remembers why. No one here tends them. They are simply the final, settled state of a place that has almost finished returning its old marks to dust.

And the wind is not alive, but it behaves like a resident god. It is the voice and hand of the Vast, the thing every living creature is shaped around. It screams through the quartzite vertebrae, hisses over the moss, moans in the hollows, and in its quieter moments hums so low you feel it in your teeth. To live in the Aria Vast is not to master it. It is to be a small, anchored thing that has learned to endure the roar—and to make a home in the brief, glistening calm between gusts.
