What lives in the Still Basin is mostly a discipline, and the discipline has a few bodies in it.

The keepers are the ones you will meet first, though “meet” is too brisk a word. They do not gather. You become aware of one standing on a landing above you, or sitting at the foot of an engine, or already beside you with a hand near your elbow. They are tall and wrapped — pale, close-layered stuff, not bandages but something between cloth and the city’s ceramic, hooded and seamed so that the body becomes a long quiet shape. You see their hands more than their faces. The hands are bare, dark-veined, expressive in a way their voices are not. They speak by touch: a fingertip pressed to the wrist means wait; a palm laid flat against the shoulder means stay; two knuckles against the sternum means listen. When they do use words, they use few, and slowly, and their voices are low enough that the water seems to take the sound out of them before it has gone far.

They are not priests, exactly, and not engineers. They attend. They walk the dark terraces on no schedule you can learn. They sit beside the engines with their palms turned up and their eyes half-closed, feeling the hum in their teeth. They know where the three grazers are without looking. They will watch you look for the image, and they will be very still, and if you swim too briskly they will take your arm — not unkindly — and hold you until your blood slows and you understand that the basin is not asking for your silence so much as requiring it.

They do not repair anything, because nothing needs repair. They do not clean, because nothing accumulates. Their work is the maintenance of conditions: no ripple, no bubble, no hurry, no bright sudden movement. They are the slow hands of the place. They disagree, when they speak of it at all, about what the basin holds — a calculation, a shape, a face, a message, a thing no one has ever been able to point to — but they keep it sharp anyway, and they have kept it sharp for so long that keeping has become their form of life.

The grazers are the only animals. There are perhaps three in the whole basin, pale and blind and shelled, no bigger than your hand. Their shells look like unglazed porcelain, faintly translucent at the edges; their bodies are soft and unhurried. They move a centimeter a day, maybe less. They do not school and do not flee. If you put your hand near one, it does not recoil; it simply continues, or stops, and the stopping is not fear. They graze the stone for something the stone barely has. They are not part of a system, because there is no system here to be part of — nothing grows, nothing decays, nothing is consumed and nothing accumulates. They persist. The keepers leave them alone. They are like commas in a page that no one is writing.

The engines are not alive, but they are more resident than anything else. They stand at the intersections of the tiers: pillars of something that is not quite matter, or not quite the matter you know, humming at a frequency you feel behind your teeth. They are sinks. They drink motion out of the water and put it somewhere else, and they have been doing it without pause or variation since before the lid went on. Around them the cold is deeper and the silence is more complete. The keepers sit beside them not to operate them but to keep them company, or to keep themselves company, or because sitting beside an engine is the closest thing this place has to a conversation. The engines do not need attendance. They receive it anyway.

The city itself lives as a system. The terraces climb the cleft in pale tiers; the bridges are threadlike; the stairways are cut into the stone. Forty lit windows, scattered, with enormous dark between them. Sealed doors. Shafts that go down into the mountain and come back up. Whole terraces stand unlit and unfurnished, waiting for a population that never arrived or has long since gone quiet. The city does not corrode or pit or accumulate a film. It maintains itself in perfect equilibrium. It is not abandoned, exactly. It is waiting, and its waiting is a kind of metabolism. Somewhere in it the single low tone sounds at intervals no one counts.

And there is the thing the basin holds. That is the most important inhabitant and the least visible. It lives in the water’s stillness the way an image lives in a lens. Every ripple would blur it; every current would smear it; every rising bubble would drift through it and corrupt it. So the city was built, and the engines were set, and the water was forbidden to move. The keepers will not show it to you. You may swim the length of the basin and look, and the water is perfectly clear, and you will see the sharp young rock and the pale tiers and the few cold lights and the three slow grazers and nothing else. But the keepers will watch you look, and they will be very still, and the tone will sound its same note, and the water will not move.

What lives there, in the end, is an old act of attention with a few bodies and machines arranged around it. If you stay too long, you will become slower. They will not ask you to leave. They will take your arm until you understand.
