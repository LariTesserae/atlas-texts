The ones who live on the Foundry Plateau are not its architects. They did not cut the flues or lay the vascular pipes or teach the bladders to close. They arrived, or their ancestors did, and the plateau made a habit of them. They call themselves, when they bother to call themselves anything, **the ones who go up**—though the phrase is less a name than a rule they keep.

They are small and dense, with deep barrel ribs and slow hearts, the kind of body that holds thin air and wet heat without complaint. Their skin is a dark wet umber that goes copper in the underlight and never quite dries; they smell of warm stone, leaf mold, and the mineral tang of sinter. Their fingertips are broad and heat-callused, the pads dulled by a lifetime of testing trunks and vent mouths. Their eyes are large but not for distance—distance is useless here—they are for catching the violet pulse when the cloud-lid stutters. They stand with one palm flat against a trunk, reading the temperature as it rises toward the crown. They speak low and close, mouth nearly at the ear, because a shout dies at four meters. Touch does more work than gesture: a hand on a shoulder means *wait*, a knuckle against the sternum means *the bladder is about to close*, a palm turned upward means *carry it higher*.

They do not live in villages. They live in shifts.

A household is a length of warm pipe, a hollow trunk, a bladder-vessel they have learned to trust. They move every few days, following the vents as they open and close. They sleep in the throats of old flues, wrapped in leaf-leather against the drip. They wake to the pressure tone in the sternum before they wake to light. They eat the pale fungus mats they farm on the warm stone, glider eggs when they can get them, the acid-sweet pulp of the thick leaves, the white sinter crusts scraped from the terraces and boiled down. They drink condensate from the seams of rock, and they carry their water in clay bottles stoppered with wads of fibrous root.

Their work is maintenance. They keep the pipes clear by coaxing the ribbon-things through them—wet clicking running away down the vessel, a sound they listen for the way a mechanic listens for a bearing. They prune the heat-exchanger fans when the plates grow too dense. They tap the grinding mats for the dark greasy metal and carry it upslope in baskets, because everything goes up. They harvest the sinter shelves before the water can build the same shelf over the same shelf again. They know which repetitions are safe and which are a stutter: when the three-lobed vessel appears too many times, when the same fan of plates returns at valley scale, they change route. They have learned that the plateau has one idea and cannot stop applying it, and that this is sometimes useful and sometimes a warning.

They are not miners or farmers, exactly. They are more like gardeners of an industry they did not plant. They do not tame the gliders; they tolerate them, and the gliders tolerate them, and sometimes a glider will carry a message-stick from one shift to another because it has learned that the stick means fruit. They do not herd the mites; they follow them to the freshest fungus. They respect the things that eat heat. They do not go into the hottest vents, and they do not stop a ribbon-thing mid-passage, and they never, ever stand still in the low wet basins where the travertine terraces are building the same shelf forever.

Kinship is by vent and by shift, not by blood. Names are descriptions: *the one who hears the third bladder*, *she who carries the dark metal*, *the child who was born during the great closing*. They have no kings, but they have listeners—people who can feel the plateau’s inch-high pulse through their soles and say when the next rain-column will set down. Children are carried in slings against a warm back and taught the first rule before they can walk: always carry something up. Even a child’s handful of mineral dust goes into the high dry places. When someone dies, they are wrapped in leaf-leather and laid high, because the body will become soil, and the soil will be carried up grain by grain in the bodies of crawlers, and the plateau will keep running its shift.

They do not worship the plateau. Worship would be too simple, and too still. They know it is not alive in the way they are, but they know it is *at work*, and they know they are inside the work. When the light flickers and the bioluminescence comes up from the understory—violet, rust-orange, a slow pulse converging from all directions—they do not run. They keep doing exactly what they were doing. They have a saying: *Don’t be a new thing.* If the plateau has noticed an input, the worst thing to be is an input that moves wrong. So they answer the attention with labor. They go up. They carry. They keep the pipes clear.

They are the plateau’s hands, though the plateau has none. They are its habit of going up, wearing a coat and carrying a basket. And when the cloud-lid dims and the whole warm dripping forest seems to turn slightly toward them, they feel it, and they do not stop working.
