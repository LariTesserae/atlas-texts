What lives there is a single resident, though “single” is a courtesy. It is the Keeper of the wheel: a tall, stooped figure that might once have been the settlement’s last engineer, now grown into a symbiosis of root, fungus, corroded brass, and wet stone. It lives in the room beyond the carved doorway at the end of the sagging catwalk. The doorway is low; it must fold itself to pass through. Its body is bark-slick and dark, segmented like a bundle of cable, its joints wrapped in brass wire. Its left arm ends in a fist of rootlets that can tease a valve open or splice a vine. Its face is a knot of pale fungus around two dim bioluminescent eyes that pulse slightly out of phase with the vines on the walls. Its mouth is not for speech; it is a wet seam that exhales the same sour warmth as the cavern. A rusted valve has grown into its chest, and when it breathes there is a faint, high *skree*.

It is not an animal. It does not hunt, and there is nothing here to hunt. It eats mineral crust scraped from the engine’s housing, pale fungus harvested from the walls, and the warm condensation it catches in a basin of hammered scrap. It drinks by dipping its root-fingers into the pools and letting the water climb. It sleeps standing, or not exactly sleeping: it goes still for long stretches, one hand on the engine, listening through its palm to the great wheel’s bearing.

Its life is the engine. It prunes the roots that creep into the spokes. It oils the bearings with a thick fungal grease. It taps the pipes and listens for the change in note that means a valve is failing. It has kept the wheel turning for longer than it remembers, though the wheel drives nothing now. It turns because the Keeper turns it, and the Keeper lives because the wheel’s heat and slow rhythm keep its roots from stiffening. It does not think of this as purpose. It thinks in drips and spoke-counts and the difference between a healthy hum and a sick one. It is patient in the way the stone is patient, but its patience has intent.

It knows when something enters. The catwalk sings underfoot; the air changes; the roots carry vibration. It will not come rushing. It will stand in the doorway with its fungus-face tilted, its eyes pulsing, and it will wait to see whether the visitor is a thing that grows, a thing that corrodes, or a thing that turns. If you touch the wheel wrong, its root-fingers will close around your wrist with the gentle, immovable pressure of a vine finding a pillar. If you sit still and let the heat settle, it may bring you water in a brass cup it has polished with its own sap. It will not speak. It may tap a rhythm on the pipe: three slow beats, a pause, three slow beats.

It is not lonely, not exactly. It is occupied. It lives there the way the engine lives there: not as a tenant but as a process that has learned to keep itself going.
