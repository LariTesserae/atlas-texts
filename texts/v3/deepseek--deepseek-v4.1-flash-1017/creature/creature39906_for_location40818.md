What lives in the Felt is not one thing but a closed chorus with a town inside it. The humans of Ambersill are only the loudest part, and even they are less like settlers than like a local habit of listening that the mat has grown used to.

They are dry people, dusted at the creases, dressed in quilted fibre that sheds the mineral flour and holds the warmth of the amber veins. Their hands are calloused from rope and scaffold-vine, from braiding and tuning and cutting back growth. They wear ear-baffles shaped to their own heads, not crude plugs but personal instruments — each pair whittled and rewhittled over years until it selects the frequencies its wearer has decided to live with. You can tell a family by the shape of its ears. Children learn early that a shout is not just a sound but a deformation; the resin takes it, and it stays. Ambersill’s people are careful with raised voices, not because they are gentle, but because the mat is always taking notes.

They live inside the ambitions of their great-grandparents. A loom is seeded, sung to, waited for; eighty years later it is tall enough to house the descendants who will bud new chambers off its old ones. The oldest rooms are buried at the base, abandoned under centuries of accretion, still lit by a slow amber pulse and still faintly full of old arguments. People climb between looms on swinging rope-bridges and trained vines. They work in amplifier-galleries cut into the oldest chambers, tuning the recording-strata and hearing back the wind of nine thousand years ago, the footfalls of the previous inhabitants, or — if they are patient and a little unlucky — sounds that have no obvious source. Scholars still argue whether those are records or compositions. The argument is old. The mat has recorded all of it.

Their technology is grown and sung to. An engineer does not build a machine; they find a node, sing to it in the right intervals, and wait. The mat answers in geometry — swelling, chambering, veining — and the answer is read off the cross-section when the grower’s children are old. Some looms are weather-listeners, some are treaties, some are the long chord that keeps the spires from resonating into one another. Some are weapons, or were, and are still grown around because nobody dares stop feeding them. The people of Ambersill are therefore inheritors more than inventors. They keep obligations alive. They do not distinguish plant from computation, because here there is no difference worth making.

Their psychology is shaped by absence. Water is *the thing that isn't*, a word used mostly in stories and mostly as a warning about longing. They do not pine for rain. They do not expect new things to arrive from outside, because nothing has come in or out in an age. They live in a rhythm that has the smooth worn quality of the plateau itself: patient, closed, fatalistic without being sad. They are not trying to be new. They are trying to keep the chord from breaking, to read the strata without waking something, to feed the looms their ancestors left hungry.

Around them, and under them, the rest of the town’s citizens are small and hard and noisy. Dust-mites scuttle and tick in the millions. Spore-fed gliders ride the drift on wide dry membranes and whistle. Tap-beetles pierce the veins and steal bright, and the long grey predators hunt them by listening for the wet click of the wound. Slow grazers crop the felt in wandering herds and sing as they go, so that the mat grows back shaped like their passage. Things live inside the looms. Things live inside the mat. Some things are not clearly either. None of them are pests exactly, and none are pets; they are the soundscape the town is built inside.

And beneath all of them is the felt itself — the true inhabitant, the landlord, the recording medium, the slow computation that is also a plant. It thickens where sound is, thins where sound is not, puts out resonating chambers at the nodes of whatever hums at it. It holds every noise that has ever fallen on it as decay, layer under layer, deeper and slower the older they are. Whether it remembers or composes, nobody has settled. It murmurs to itself endlessly, fibre against fibre, veins pulsing, chambers filling and emptying, a dry rustle like paper in a wind with no source.

At night the bright comes up in the veins and the plain glows faintly under its dust. The tap-beetles tick. The gliders whistle. The looms hum their long chord. The people in their quilted fibre and shaped ear-baffles sit inside the grown rooms of their ancestors and listen to a world that is never quiet, and the mat goes on laying down its layers of everything it hears. They live there by being heard, and by hearing.
