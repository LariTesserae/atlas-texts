The people who live in the Verdant Span are a vertical people, and it shows in their bodies. They are not mountain-folk and not quite city-folk; they are something the shaft has made. They are lean through the chest and strong through the hands, with forearms roped from ladders and grips and the constant lifting of things along narrow ways. They walk with a slight inward lean when they are high up, as if the open center of the shaft were a slope they are always quietly refusing to slide down. Their feet are hard and their shoes are patched and their knees are bad early. Their lungs are deep but careful—they breathe the thin air of the upper terraces and the wet air of the mid-levels with the same slow economy, and you can hear the difference in them when they climb.

They smell of wet rust and crushed leaves and the faint ozone of the processors. Their clothes are practical and layered: oilcloth coats against the condensation, cotton and hemp beneath, goggles pushed up on their foreheads, gloves with the fingers cut out because they need to feel the pipes. The maintenance crews carry machetes and hooked poles. The growers carry seed-tins and grafting knives. The engine-keepers carry wrenches older than their grandmothers and rags that are never dry. Almost everyone carries a rag. It is that kind of place.

Their voices are pitched to carry over the engines. They speak in a clipped, carrying way, not loud exactly but placed, like a stone skipped across water. In the crowded ways they have learned to move without touching, and when they do touch—a hand on a shoulder to pass, a palm against a wall to steady—it is deliberate. They pause to watch the rain. That is not a small thing about them. When the processors purge and the warm water comes down through the open center of the shaft, they stop. Growers lean on their hoes. Engine-keepers come out of the halls with grease on their faces. Children hang over the railings until someone pulls them back. They watch the silver ghost of the waterfall fall and evaporate before it reaches the bottom, and they do not talk much while it happens.

They are a stacked and close people, and they know each other by sound before sight. They can tell from the shift of the engines whether the atmosphere processors are purging, whether the pumps are straining, whether the day crew is about to change. They know which elevator rattles in which way and which catwalk rings under a boot. They know the particular clatter of the bamboo on the upper terraces when the wind is coming from above, and the particular hush of the mid-levels when the spores are heavy. They will tell you the Span has moods, and they are not being poetic. They live inside a machine that is also a jungle, and they have learned to read it the way other people read weather.

They are born into the green. Children learn to climb before they learn to swim, because there is no swimming here, only the sump, and nobody swims in the sump. They learn which vines hold and which peel off the concrete. They learn not to eat the bright fungus on the lower levels and not to touch the pale things that come up from the wet dark. They learn the rain-watch habit early: stop, look, wait. They learn the names of the engine sounds the way other children learn birds. They are allowed to be loud in the crowded ways and quiet in the grow-labs, and they are taught that the trees on the upper terraces are older than their families and should be treated with the same courtesy.

The adults divide themselves by what they listen to. There are the growers, who live by the light arrays and the soil beds and the slow cycles of the terraces, and who carry the smell of green sap and compost. There are the pipe-readers, who know the circulatory system of the place by touch and sound, who can find a blockage by putting a palm against a wall and closing their eyes. There are the engine-keepers, who are the closest thing the Span has to priests—not because they are holy, but because they tend the deep throb that keeps everyone alive, and they speak of the compressors with a familiarity that sounds like affection. There are the elevator runners, the lift mechanics, the seed-keepers, the bird-catchers, the machete crews who cut the green back from the walkways in the growing season. There are the old people who sit near the grow-labs and mend nets and watch the children and remember when a certain catwalk was not there.

They are long-lived and unhurried. They do not rush. The Span has been here for three hundred years and will be here for three hundred more, and they carry that knowledge in their bones. They measure change in rings and rust and the lengthening of vines. They have a calendar of purges and harvests and fallow seasons, and they keep festivals that are mostly excuses to cook the good food and stand together on the broad terraces and listen to the engines slow. When someone dies, they are wrapped in hemp and laid in the deep soil beds, and the orchard trees on the upper terraces are said to know them. Whether that is true or not, the people believe it, and they tend those trees with a particular care.

And there are the others. The Span is densely inhabited by things that are not people and never were. The birds are everywhere—feral pest-control birds, gone bright and bold and noisy, raiding the unharvested plots in the fallow season and nesting in the crane arms. The insects are a constant presence, a layered hum and chirp and rattle, and the engineered pollinators go about like tiny engines with wings. On the mid-levels the ferns and hanging gardens have their own small populations: lizards the color of wet concrete, frogs in the runoff channels, spiders that build webs across the walkways overnight and must be swept away each morning. In the lower dark there are the pale creatures of the sump, leggy and strange and adapted to wet and silence, and the people do not hunt them and do not quite ignore them. They call them neighbors with no lease. The old maintenance robots still do their rounds, their paths gone idiosyncratic, and the children have names for them. And in the deep groves where the trees are not quite trees anymore, where the leaves are phosphorescent and the roots are threaded into the electrical grid, there are things that are neither plant nor machine and are treated by the Span-dwellers with the same practical courtesy they give the engines: leave it alone, learn its mood, do not cut it unless it is in the way.

The people of the Verdant Span are not surprised by any of this. They live in a place that is entirely built and entirely alive, and they have made a culture out of that contradiction. They are practical, watchful, patient, crowded, and at home in the damp. They know the Span breathes. They say so without irony. And when the engines shift in a certain way, and the rain comes down through the leaves, and the light falls in a certain dappled way through the shaft, they stop and look up, because they are the kind of people who have learned to listen to the place that keeps them.
