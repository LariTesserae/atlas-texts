There is no *who* here. No builder, no keeper, no hand. The inhabitants are a multitude, and the multitude is the occupant.

The true population is the drifting snow. It is not snow, of course, but a blizzard of tiny lives and nearly-lives: glass-shelled drifters, bristled scavengers no larger than dust, worm-thin larvae, protists folded into shapes like wet stars, bacteria riding flakes of ancient mineral. They live suspended, never quite settling, caught and recaught in the great slow gyres. Most of them will never be seen as individuals. They are the water’s texture, its thickness, its faint continuous crackle. When disturbed, they spark—cold blue-green pinpricks, a flinch of light running through the dark. They are the place’s real density: not empty water with a few animals in it, but a living soup, occupied everywhere at once.

The larger creatures are rare enough to feel like events. An armored thing like a crab of polished obsidian might emerge from the gloom, its shell black and smooth, jointed legs moving with a patience that looks almost geological. It does not scuttle. It shoulders the current aside, lets the current shoulder it back, and feeds by scraping the fine grey silt with mouthparts like a slow file. Its eyes are two pinpricks of colder light, or perhaps no eyes at all—only pits that read pressure and the faint chemical signature of food. It has no urgency because urgency is a shallow-water luxury. It has been this shape, or something like it, for longer than the stones have been round.

Then there is the ribbon-serpent: translucent, longer than a thought, with a thread of gut visible inside it and a row of faint blue nodes running down its length. It is felt before it is seen—a wrongness in the current, a soft shear in the water, a pressure that passes and returns. It swims by undulating a skirt of muscle, not fast, but with a smooth, inevitable purpose. Its mouth is a pale seam. Its light is a ghost-aura, a faint trailing veil. It does not hunt so much as arrive, and what it eats is often already part of the drifting snow.

And clinging to the chance arrangements of stone are the almost-plants: pale, fungoid growths, reach-like forms, clusters of soft stalks that are not vegetation and not quite mineral. They are sessile and hungry. They live on the chemical whisper of the deep, filtering the perpetual rain, opening slow mouths, pulsing once every long while. They are the closest thing to a forest here, and they are ghostly, sparse, and patient.

These inhabitants do not conquer the water; they consent to it. Their senses are pressure and taste and the tiny electric flicker of other bodies. Their language is bioluminescence—a flash to startle, a flash to signal, a flash that means nothing at all. Their lives are slow, cold, and distributed: spawn loosed into the current, young drifting as part of the snow, old shells settling at last into the silt. Nothing is wasted because nothing is hurried. They are not masters of the place. They are eddies with appetites, brief patterns in an immense and ancient turbulence, living inside the water as the water lives inside them.
