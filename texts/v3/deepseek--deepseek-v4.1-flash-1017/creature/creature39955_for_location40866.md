The ones who live there are the makers — and the plain itself is their work, their shell, their slow accumulated body.

They are not much to look at, if you are looking for an animal. A maker is a pale, low, knuckled mass, waist-high at most, dry and closed, with no leaves and no mouth you can find. It looks like something that has been left out in the sun until it hardened: a fist of mineral flesh, a knot of pale matter squatting on the bone-colored ground. You can walk an hour across the plain and pass four of them. They do not startle. They do not turn. They are set into the surface at the base like teeth in a gum, and that is the whole of their posture — rooted, patient, facing nothing in particular.

Each one sits at the center of its own disc. From the base, a thin mineral liquor sweats out and spreads a hand’s width, then another, and hardens. The disc grows around the maker the way a shell grows around the animal that made it, except here the shell is the ground and the ground is the world. Where one disc meets another, the material joins seamlessly, faintly banded where the two batches met, and in a season even the join is gone. So the makers are not exactly neighbors. They are contributors to the same continuous surface, each one adding its ring to a floor that thickens downward into itself, generation on generation of the same slow cement.

Their lives are measured in water and heat, not in events. All day the floor takes the sun and gives it back; the air shimmers in the first meter and then clears. A dry wind moves over the flat with nothing to break it, carrying the only loose thing there is — the pale flour of the material itself — and dropping it in the faint hollows and in the ring around each maker. By evening the wind lies down and the floor releases its heat into the dark. Then the condensation comes. The surface cools faster than the air, beads all over, and by first light the whole plain is wet and slick. The makers have swollen slightly in the night, drinking through their bases. By noon the water is gone and the floor goes white where it dried.

They do not move, but they are not inert. They cure. They accrete. They mend. Where the plain cracks as it hardens, fresh secretion closes the crack. Where the edge of the material is still ragged and wet and spreading, there are young makers — small knots not yet old enough to have made anything round, ringed by tacky membrane instead of finished disc. In the low places where rain stands warm and brassy for a few days, the makers cluster two or three together, as if something in the standing water suits them. The hollow dries and salts over and waits for the next rain, which may be a long time. The makers wait too. Waiting is most of what they do.

There is no other life to speak of. No birds, no insects in the air or the water, nothing moving in the dust. No animal has ever hunted here or hidden here or called across this ground. The makers do not call either. They have no voices and no eyes you can find, and no need to answer anything. Their only relation to one another is the joining of their discs, the slow sealing of one batch of mineral liquor against the next. They are not solitary in the way a hermit is solitary. They are sparse in the way a first draft is sparse: there are only so many of them yet, and the world they are making is still mostly ahead of them.

If you walk there, you are the first thing that has ever crossed that ground on legs. The surface near the growing edges is still tacky enough to take your print, and it keeps it. The makers do not notice you. They are busy with the only work they have: sweating out the floor beneath themselves, drinking the night’s condensation, thickening the plain by half a millimeter, and waiting for the water to leave. In a thousand years there will be depth to the place, and layers, and perhaps something will have learned to live on it. For now the makers are the life and the geology at once — a hot, flat, quiet country of pale knuckled things, entirely made, and still being made.
