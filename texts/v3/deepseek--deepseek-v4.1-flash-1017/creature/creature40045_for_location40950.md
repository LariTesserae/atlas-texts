The one who lives there now is not the one who built it. The builders are gone—small shelled things whose generations are banded into the mound walls, each generation laying its shell on the rubble of the last. What remains is a single grey animal, cat-sized, low and long, with fur like wet ash felted close to the body. It is not quite a cat. Its head is wedge-shaped, its ears small and folded back, its feet broad and pale-soled, its claws dull. Its coat has two layers: a dense oily underfur that sheds water, and an outer pelage that beads the mist into tiny clear points. It smells faintly of cold iron and crushed resin, because it pushes through the scrub.

It lives by circuit, not by den. It sleeps in a dry chamber of one mound above the waterline, a mound whose mouth faces away from the prevailing wind. It does not clear the mouth or repair the shell or widen the pores. It simply uses what is there. In the morning it comes out into the mist with its fur beaded, steps down the drainage channels as if they were paths, drinks from the pools without rippling them much, and scrapes lichen from the stone with a small side-to-side motion of its jaw. It eats moss cushions and lichen rosettes; it may take insects or small crustaceans from the pools. It is unhurried because nothing here hunts it, and it hunts almost nothing.

It knows the town by sound. The mound choir is a map to it: each hollow has its own pitch, set by size and wall-thickness and how much water stands inside, and the grey animal can move through mist with its small dark eyes half-closed, pausing at a mouth, tilting its head, then going on. It does not sing back. Its only voice is a low throaty croon when it is comfortable, almost below hearing, and the mounds sometimes seem to take that up and pass it along.

It is unafraid of you. It will look, decide you are weather, and continue. It is the whole visible population—one warm body in a finished town, living in the interstices, keeping no tools, leaving no cut marks, just a small warm route through the wet stone and the chord.
