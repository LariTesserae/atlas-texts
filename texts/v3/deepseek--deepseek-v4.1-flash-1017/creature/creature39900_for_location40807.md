What lives in the Kettle is not one thing, though it has one attention. The plants live there; the walkers live there; the warm cloud lives there long enough to count as a resident. But the place belongs to the cylinder at the southwest edge and the sixteen threads it keeps strung in the air. I have stopped thinking of those as separate phenomena. They are one organism, or one instrument still being played by something that has forgotten it is a player—or never knew.

The cylinder is a squat, unseamed lump of a thing, dull as a river pebble and half-sunk in a collar of sinter as though it grew there. It is not metal. It is not stone. It has no markings, no port, no control, no face. It is warm to the touch, warmer than the ground, which is already warm. It gives off a tone you feel in your teeth more than hear. It does not move from its place. Its motion is the water.

The threads are its limbs and its senses. Sixteen glassy filaments, each about a meter long, strung across the stone blades rather than hanging down. They should sag and break. They do not. They quiver, thicken, gather light. Every so often one plucks—a wet finger around a glass rim—and a bead travels along it, from stone to stone, uphill as often as down, toward the cylinder as often as away. That is its metabolism, as far as I can tell: it moves water not to drink but to keep water in a shape. It harvests heat and chemistry from the young mountain and spends them on surface tension. It is a loom whose cloth is a standing wave.

It knows by tension. A walker on a thread is a change in load. A stick pushed against a filament is a change in wetness. A human arm is a reservoir it can feel. The low tone that comes twice an hour is not a call, exactly—it is more like a swallow, a peristalsis—and when it comes the threads shiver all at once and the water reverses and the whole weave runs backward for a while. It has been doing this since before the basin existed. The mountain came apart around it and it did not stop. It may not know the mountain broke. It knows the weave is intact.

The walkers are its commensals, or its pets, or its smallest hands—I cannot tell, and I am not sure the distinction means anything here. Pale things the size of a thumbnail, more spider than crab or more crab than spider, made mostly of the same water they walk on. There are perhaps a dozen in the whole Kettle. They come out of cracks, cross to a thread, climb onto it without troubling the surface, drink, and go back. They exist nowhere else. They know you are coming. They are not afraid, only shy.

The plants are its slow audience. Mosses in copper-green and bruised violet fur the steam-ward crevices in precise cushions. Two old ferns uncurl new fronds still wet and translucent. Small orchids grow out of pure sinter, which should not be possible. And in the lee of the tallest stone blade there is one tree—crooked, four meters at most, bark smooth and pale as a limb, leaves narrow and held out flat as if to catch what the threads are saying. It is the only tree on the summit. It leans toward the weave. Everything green here leans toward the weave. When the tone comes and the flow reverses, the mosses brighten.

It is not hostile. It is incorporative. Do not touch the threads—not because you will break them, but because they will find the water in your arm and pull, gently, and you will feel it, and you will want to stay. The place rewards stillness. That is the whole of its law. Stay long enough, be quiet enough, and the water will begin to arrange itself around you. You will become one more thing in the basin that the weave leans toward. People have gone up to the Kettle and been found there days later, perfectly well, very wet, entirely unable to say what they had been doing. It did not eat them. It added them to the weather.

So: a patient, blind, warm-bodied thing with sixteen glassy fingers and a taste for stillness. A made thing that outlived its maker and became a resident. A small climate with a pulse. It lives in the open wound of a new mountain, in steam and mineral lace, and it is still for what it was for. If you go, it will notice you the way a loom notices a hand—not as a visitor, but as a new tension to be accommodated.
