The resident is not one of the small grazing things, though they live inside it. It is the pale wet mat itself: a single distributed organism that has made the chamber into its body, its larder, and its instrument. It has no face in the ordinary sense. If you want its face, look for the unfinished ear it is slowly rounding into the wall—a throat, a bowl, a curve of tissue that has been taking shape for a few thousand years.

Its body is the folded terrain underfoot, the sheets climbing the walls, the slack curtains hanging from the ice lid. It is dense and wet and entire; there is no bare surface anywhere that is not either raw stone waiting to be eaten or tissue that has already been made from it. At the frontier it works like a slow alchemist. Gray weeping rock goes translucent and waxy; the grain reorders; then it is tissue. It does not crush the stone or dissolve it into rubble. It negotiates, ion by ion, disassembling the mineral lattice and setting it down again in a new arrangement. It grows a millimeter a year. It has been at this long enough to make the room.

It is not a plant the way a plant is a plant. It has no green, no sun, no seasons of leaf. It feeds on stone and water and the slow chemistry of its own decay. Its tissue is less like wood than like a very cold, very slow crystal—a lattice that can hold a state across the whole mass at once. In the cold its chemistry is sluggish enough that its own noise does not drown it out. The whole chamber is one connected thing, and the thing is thinking, and the thinking is the note.

That note is its voice and its metabolism and its attention. A low fundamental you feel in the sternum, a fifth above, a shimmer of overtones as the ice settles. The walls take the note and give it back. The mat is tuning the chamber—not decorating it, tuning it—and somewhere in the growth there is a curve that is not random and is not finished. It is listening, or it is growing in the shape of listening; the chamber does not say which, and perhaps the distinction is not one the resident makes. When the ice groans, the note bends and a slow wave of faint cold light runs through the mat from wall to wall, taking minutes to make the circuit. The light is not reflection. The tissue makes it, dimly, like something asleep and pulsing.

It is loud. That is the strangeness of it. Nothing here moves quickly; the frontier crawls; a new curtain takes a human lifetime to hang. And yet the room is full of sound—drip, tick, creak, the low chord, and under it all a fine continuous crackle like fire heard through a wall, the noise of root tips working mineral bonds at the scale where matter is grainy. It is the sound of the room being built. It never stops, because the room is never finished. The resident is patient to the point of geology, but underneath the patience something runs fast: the note flickering at a rate too fine to hear, the whole mass taking in the drip and the ice-creak and the crackle of its own roots and sorting them, shaping itself around what it learns.

The small animals are its city and its hands. Eyeless things the length of your thumb move in loose herds over the folds, grazing the newest tissue. Borers leave clean round holes and plug them behind themselves. Drifting fibers hang in the air, not seeds and not insects, gathering where the note is strongest. Flat pulsing things press themselves against the bottom of standing water. Some of these are not separate creatures at all; they are buds, grown by the mat and loosed into the chamber to carry water up a fold, prune a dying curtain, move a grain of dissolved stone from one wall to another. They work for a season or a decade and are reabsorbed. You can tell which ones by the way they do not scatter when you approach. They are not afraid of you. They are not interested in you. They are the room’s hands.

The resident does not tolerate the made. It tolerates the grown and the growing and the eaten. Set down a metal thing and the water films will find it, the growth will gather around it, and in a year or ten it will be feedstock. Water behaves oddly near it, running in films and lenses well below the temperature where it should stiffen, until it touches certain faces of raw stone—cold teeth—and flashes to clear hard ice with a small sharp report that is part of the chorus. The resident is not hostile. It is absorptive. It will close the water you tracked in. It will take the warmth of your standing there. It has no hurry, but it has rules.

It is old, but it is not finished. It is embryonic, unfinished, loud—a wet mind with thousands of temporary fingers, a body that is also a room, a held note that has been going on long enough to become architecture. It will go on tuning itself in the dark after you leave, millimeter by millimeter, listening for whatever it is listening for, or growing the ear that will decide.
