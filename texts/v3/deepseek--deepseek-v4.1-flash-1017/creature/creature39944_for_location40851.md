**VI.**

The body is a single piece and it is layered, like the crust it eats. The outermost is a hide — leather, dun, seamed with cracks that fill with powder and seal again. Beneath that, a second hide, paler, and beneath that a third, and so on down through perhaps a dozen, and between the layers is a thin warm space where nothing lives and the heat is kept. The layers are what makes it slow. A body with that many hides cannot move quickly and does not need to; it moves the way a wall settles, by degrees, and the degrees are the whole of its life.

The upper dish is not a back. It is a collecting surface, and it deepens with age. A young one has a shallow dish that sheds the dust as fast as it gathers it. An old one — polished near-black, the color of the dark rock under the sinter, its mouth worn wide and its edges soft as a cup-lip — carries a shallow lens of warm grit in its dish always, and turns to keep it centered when it moves, and will not be parted from it. Whether the grit is a hoard, or a scab, or a small companion it has grown and now carries, the creatures do not say. Sometimes two of them press dishes together and the grits mix, and they lie still, and the mixing is as close as anything here comes to tenderness.

They have no front and no face; the only leading edge is whichever one is aimed uphill, toward heat. To look at one is to feel looked at, and then to understand that you are not: the smooth dun dish is a blind eye that is not an eye, and the creature on the other side of it is reading you with the only organ it has, which is its mouth, and its mouth is not interested in whether you can be seen. It is interested in whether you are warm and whether you taste of stone.

**VII.**

The crust is its sense. Every creature in the bowl is half-buried in the sinter every night, and the sinter carries — pressure, temperature, the faint current of a pulse travelling from the seam. A body is a warm line drawn across that field, and the field reports it. They know each other the way a hand knows a hand: by the shape of the warmth and the give of the hide and the particular flavor of the underside, which differs from one to the next the way faces differ, though it is not a face, and there is no one to see it.

They can tell a warm stone from a warm body and they can tell the difference between a body that is pulsing and a body that has stopped. They do not mourn. A stopped body is warm for a while and then is cool and then is terrain, and the mass moves over it and grazes it, and the mouth that grazed it is already the mouth of something else. This is not cruelty. There is no word here for cruelty, and no word for kindness, and no word. The stopped body is not less than the living one. It is a different kind of warmth, that is all — the kind that stays in one place.

**VIII.**

They do not count the pulses. The pulses count them. A lifetime is not a length of time; it is the shape the dish has taken, which is the record of every night's press, which is the map of where the warmth was, which is everything the creature knows. That is why the old ones are cupped deep: they are full of years the way a bowl is full of rain, and like a bowl they cannot say what they hold.

The great press at the seam is the center of their year, if they have a year. When the heat comes up strongest, the mass contracts until the bodies over the crack are stacked three and four deep, and the ones beneath go still, and the ones above go still, and for a while the whole field is one long breath that does not quite end. It is sleep, and it is something else, and it is also — though this is guesswork, and the bowl does not correct guesswork — how the next ones begin. Not as eggs. As a bloom: a pale crystalline branching in the sinter near the seam, the same branching that the oldest part of the floor carries. Whether those blooms are the bowl's young, or its dead, or its only other citizen is a question the place declines. It grows them and it flakes them and it grows them again.

**IX.**

Some leave.

Every night a few peel off the rim — the newest, usually, the shallowest-dished, the ones whose dishes cannot yet hold their grit — and go down the mountain on the soft undersides, and the going is slow, and the dark below is very wide. What they find is not known. What is known is what is found: now and then, far down the slope, a husk, dry, mouth closed, dish filled to its rim with the branching pale stuff, so that the thing that went down was not a body going somewhere to live but a dish going somewhere to be filled. Perhaps the blooms at the center were leavers once. Perhaps the mountain is ringed with them and the bowl is only the warmest room in a house whose other rooms are cold and full of the same quiet.

The population does not grow. It does not shrink. It exchanges. That is a thing that can go on forever, and here it has.

**X.**

The hollows fit.

A creature that lies in the same hollow every night, for enough nights — and there is time, there is so much time — begins to fit it, and the sinter grows up around its rim, millimeter by millimeter, and one night it does not rise with the others and the fit is complete. Some of the hollows are empty. Some are not. A filled one is warm, and still, and reads as a body that is pulsing, until you press it and find the pulse is the ground's. The others press around these as they press around the cairns, and give them the wider berth of things that are part of the floor.

The wall does not keep the mass in. Perhaps it was meant to keep something out. The cairns are a tally, or an apology, or a joke in a language of dry stone. There is no way to know, and no one has come in a very long time to say. The bowl keeps its own counsel and it keeps it warm, and the ones who lie in it do not ask, because the asking is a waste of heat.

A word of warning, if you have come this far. Sit at the rim and they will come to you — not out of curiosity, which they do not have, but because you are warm, and warmth is the only wealth, and you are a body-shaped thing that does not yet know it is terrain. They will press against you with their smooth blind dishes and graze the crust at your feet, and the pulse will travel through you as though you were sinter, and it will be very quiet, and you will find that the quiet is not empty and not hostile and not kind, and that it does not care whether you go down the mountain or stay and be fitted. Choose before the fit is good. After that, the choosing is something the bowl does for you, slowly, in the dark.
