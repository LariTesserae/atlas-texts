## The Residents

If you ask who lives in the Kettle, the answer is not a list of species. It is a set of agreements with the current. Everything here has signed the same contract: face the loop, feel the pressure wave, take warmth where it is offered, and do not drift free for long.

The oldest residents are the crust. They cover the twelve columns in a rind the color of old bone, thumbnail-sized, each one a small mouth opening and closing in the flow. They are not scenery. They are the hall’s skin, and the ticking you hear is their breathing — thousands of them, not quite in rhythm, answering the water. When the impeller comes around, they close; when the pressure passes, they open. They do not see the dome. They know it as a returning echo, a half-second late and slightly changed. They have no need to know what the dome is. The echo is enough. They are the most patient inhabitants, and the most numerous, and they have been patient for so long that the metal under them has gone soft.

The small pale fish are twelve. Not a shoal — a habit. They move from stack to stack in loose order, grazing the algae films, of which there are few: a fur on the lit glass, the ribbons in the outfall, the one dense meadow in the corner where the flickering lamp has burned for a very long time. The fish know the flicker as a clock. They know the dark between lamps as a place where the clock stops. They are quick, but not quick enough to be careless, and they are the only things here that ever seem to hurry.

The large blind grazer is a person-sized absence moving through the hall. It has no eyes and no need of them. Its body is an instrument: it hums a note a fifth below the impeller, and the two sounds beat against each other, and the whole hall throbs. It navigates by that throbbing — by the way the columns return its hum, by the way the dome answers late, by the way a valve pulse of colder water comes up through the deck and makes every soft-bodied thing turn. It never hurries. Nothing harries it. It knows the hall as a map of echoes and temperatures, and when the weather comes — a valve cycling somewhere below, a pulse of cold rising — it turns first, because it feels the season before the season arrives.

The hunter is thin and does not stalk. It waits where the machinery is loudest, in the dark between two lamps, and lets the noise cover its approach. It takes the small pale fish when they cross. That is the whole of the violence here, and it is not much, and it is over quickly. The hunter has no name for itself. It knows intervals: the flicker’s brightening, the impeller’s turn, the moment when the ticking crust is loud enough to hide a body. It is not cruel. It is a shape the hall has learned to make.

The warm ones live near the north gallery, around a bearing housing a degree or two warmer than the water. A knot of pale limbs, a species that lives nowhere else, has been there so long that the housing has grown a lip of mineral to shelter them. They are the most crowded inhabitants because heat is a resource, and the warmest few square meters are the most valuable. They do not know that the motor is a motor. They know comfort, and they know the slow pulse of the machine through their shelter, and that is enough.

And there are the crawlers: little maintenance things that work the rails and flanges, scraping and oiling, keeping the loop going. They are not quite animals and not quite machines. The residents treat them as weather — something that passes, something that makes a sound, something that keeps the conditions conditions. The crawlers do not need anyone. They maintain the hall the way the hall maintains them: without intention, and very well.

None of them remember air. None of them have a word for outside. They know the loop, the lamp, the pressure wave, the warm lip of the bearing housing, the cold pulse that means weather. They are not waiting for anything. They are a finished place’s continuing function — and they live there the way the water does: slowly, completely, and without asking why.
