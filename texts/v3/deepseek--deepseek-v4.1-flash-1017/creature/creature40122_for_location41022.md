The Weald is not inhabited so much as **staffed**. The things that live there are not a population in the ordinary sense—not a village, not a tribe, not even a species that stands apart from the green. They are a handful of organs the Weald has grown into the shape of people. Call them the Grafters, though they have no spoken name for themselves; they have chords, pressures, and root-memories, and a low note that means *this one, here, now*.

A Grafter is tall and narrow, seven feet or more, but it stoops as if carrying the low ceiling of the canopy on its shoulders. Its body is a bundle of pale root-wood wrapped in living vine, not quite bark and not quite flesh. The skin—if it can be called skin—is a grey-green membrane, always beaded with condensation, blotched with lichen at the joints. Moss grows on its skull and shoulders. Its hands are too long, with too many knuckles, fingers splitting at the tips like roots. Its feet are broad and flat and root-veined, made for standing in squelch without sinking. It has no mouth. Where a mouth would be there is a smooth plate of resonant wood, sealed and slightly convex, and when it hums—which is rare—the plate shivers and the sound travels down through its chest into the ground.

Its eyes are two dark amber droplets set deep in the bark of its face. They do not see detail well; the mist makes detail useless. They see heat and shadow and the faint green pulse of the mass. The Grafter reads the world mostly through its palms and soles. It presses a hand to a trunk and feels the pressure inside, the slow grind of the network, the temperature of the fermenting heart. It presses a foot to the moss and listens for the tension in the roots. It knows when a limb is about to split by the taste of the vibration in its bones.

They move slowly. Not clumsily—deliberately. The Weald is a churning engine, and a creature that hurried inside it would be folded into the mass before it had gone ten paces. So the Grafter moves in increments, pausing with one hand on a vine, listening. Then it steps. Then it listens again. In a crisis—a choke-knot sealing a river, a bloom-storm about to spore too hard—it can uncoil with a sudden fluid speed that looks wrong, like a stump sprinting. But most of the time it is slower than the dripping.

They do not speak. Their throats are sealed. They communicate by tapping taut vines and flat resonant stones. A plucked vine sends a low note through the root network; another Grafter, half a mile away, feels it in its soles and answers with a tap. This is the chiming. It is not music, exactly. It is maintenance language: *vent opening*, *limb settling*, *spore-fruit ripe*, *come*. Each Grafter has a chord rather than a name, a combination of pressure and pitch that identifies it within the network. They have no word for the Weald. They have a chord for it, very old and very low, that they strike only when they are lost.

Their work is not farming. It is tuning. The Weald grows too fast, too total; left alone it would seal its own rivers and suffocate its own ferment-heart. The Grafters prune the flowering moss before it spores too heavily. They open vents in the thicket so the damp heat can escape. They graft torn limbs back into the mass. They redirect condensation into channels so the water reaches the roots instead of pooling and rotting a node. They clear choke-knots where the vegetation has grown across a stream. They carry stone blades and bone needles and pots of sap-resin. They do not use metal; metal would be a foreign thing, and the Weald would grow over it in a season. They work in the rain, unhurried, and the Weald closes their cuts before they have finished making them.

They are few. Perhaps a dozen within a day’s slow walking. They meet at chime-stones—flat slabs of old plateau stone under the moss, resonant as bells—and there they exchange root-memory by touch and tone. They have no leaders. Decisions emerge from the network’s pressure, from the heat of the ferment-heart, from the groans of overloaded limbs. They do not build shelters; they hollow out nodes and sleep standing in shallow pools, half-sunk, their roots loosening into the muck. They do not make fire. They bask in the faint warmth that radiates from the fermenting heart of the thicket, and they smell always of wet bark and yeast and cold stone.

The pale, slow things in the deep leaf-litter are their young. A Grafter does not give birth. When the network needs a new hand, a cradle-knot swells at a node and releases a soft, blind, grub-like creature no bigger than a forearm. It lives in the litter for years, eating detritus and spores, hiding from nothing because nothing hunts it. When it is ready, it climbs a trunk, finds a node, and presses itself into the bark. The mass absorbs it, wraps it, and slowly re-forms it. Over a decade it becomes a Grafter. It inherits root-memory—not autobiography, but skill, route, warning, the chord of its node. The pale things are rarely seen because they are careful and small and the litter is deep. They are the only animals in the Weald, and they are not quite animals.

When a Grafter slows for the last time, it finds a quiet place and sits. Roots enter it. Its bark hardens. Its limbs fuse. It becomes a nurse-stump, and the network takes back its water and its stored tones. The others do not bury it. They tune it out. The Weald’s chime changes by a note, and the change is noted, and the work goes on.

If you saw one, you might mistake it for a moss-covered post, a pollarded stump, a trick of the mist. Then it would turn its dark wet eyes on you—not with curiosity, but with the flat attention of a mechanic noticing a loose part—and it would tap a vine. A low note would travel away into the green. Somewhere, another note would answer. The Weald would groan and settle. The Grafter would go back to work, and you would be left standing in the dripping silence, understanding that the place is not haunted. It is busy.
