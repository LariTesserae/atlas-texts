The ones who live there are not builders. They are gardeners of a nervous system, and they are as porous to their world as the city is porous to itself.

They are low and long, six-limbed, with a wet slate-colored hide patched by translucent films that drink the warm drizzle. Two of the limbs are broad gripping fans for clinging to the fused basalt; two are walking arms; two are fine manipulators folded close to the belly, always picking, scraping, coaxing. Their external gill-plumes never stop moving—feathery, iridescent fringes that breathe and taste at once, so that every inhalation is also a sentence in the chemical language of the place. Their eyes are small clusters of dark beads set low in a blunt, shield-shaped head; they see poorly into the amber haze but acutely at arm’s length. Along the spine runs a ridge of pressure-pores, a lateral line that feels the groan of machinery through walls, the shift of a storm in the water table, the approach of a gill-hound before it breaches the mist.

Their faces are not faces in any human sense. A radial mouth of soft palps opens into a ring of resonating chambers. They speak in two streams at once: a low, percussive code of clicks and bladder-notes pitched to cut through the world-roar, and a perfume of chemical signatures that passes through skin and water and wall alike. A name is a chord of scent. A greeting is a pulse and a puff of cultivated bacteria. To lie is difficult; the body sweats its mood, and the city’s veins carry the smell.

They do not live in houses. A household is a pressure-pocket in the fused mass, a chamber whose walls are kin, whose floor is a living mat, whose ceiling pulses with the amber light of engineered bioluminescence. They groom one another’s gill-fringes and exchange cultures of symbiotic film the way other creatures might exchange gifts. Privacy is not absence of contact but a slower exchange. Loneliness is not a mood; it is a physical impossibility, and the word for “alone” in their chemical speech is closer to “untethered” or “leaking.”

They are born in storm-surge. When the pressure falls and the great weather engine begins to turn, they open the sluices and release clouds of spat into the warm slurry. The larvae drift through the canals, almost indistinguishable from the city’s darting engineered sparks, feeding on algae and mineral dust. Those that survive attach to nursery lattices and metamorphose. Their first chemical name is assigned by the wall that absorbs them. The old do not so much die as settle: their limbs slow, their hides thicken, their bodies exude resin and fuse with the architecture. For a while they are still faintly chemical, still answering, still part of the conversation in the walls. Then the city has absorbed them, and the city is a little more itself.

Daily life is cultivation. They prune crystalline lattices that grow too aggressively through a conduit. They scrape mineral crust from a vein so the nano-fluid can pulse. They coax a branch of the edifice to bend around a new nursery. They herd clouds of iridescent arthropods with pheromone pulses, milk bioluminescent algae from the canals, and mark the migration of the worm-like leviathans with low vibrations that the great filter-feeders can feel. Their tools are grown, not made: living clamps, crystalline wedges, mats of photosensitive film that they fold and tie like wet cloth. When they need the city to do something, they do not command it. They sing to it in pressure and chemistry, and the city answers by glowing a little brighter along that vein.

They do not fight storms. They ride them. When the roar deepens and the drizzle becomes a warm, hammering sheet, they seal into flexing cysts, open the city’s vents, and let the energy run through the architecture and through their own bodies. Some ride the colossal jellyfish-constructs up into the upper tiers, clinging to the drifting bells while the storm tears past. They are not brave about it. They are buoyant. The storm is simply another tide in a world of tides.

They have no concept of “outside.” Distance is a pressure gradient and an unfamiliar taste. The sun is a ghost they know mostly as a pale lessening of the amber glow. History is stored in chemical strata in the walls; they read the past by touching it. Their art is a kind of pressure-music that makes the city’s veins flicker in patterns, and their arguments are conducted by withholding scent until the other party’s skin grows anxious.

If you could isolate one of them—which you could not, for long—it would still be a colony: symbionts in its blood, city-fluid in its joints, kin-scent in its skin. It would be restless, tasting the air, listening with its flank, already half-fused with the wall it touches. It would not understand solitude. It would understand only a dangerous drop in pressure and the sudden absence of the roar, which, to it, would be the sound of death.
