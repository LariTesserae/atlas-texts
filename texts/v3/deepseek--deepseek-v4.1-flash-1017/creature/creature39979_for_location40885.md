The Steeping has no single resident. It has a stacked tenancy, arranged by water depth and roof condition and sound, and if you stand still long enough the population resolves out of the noise.

Start with the frogs, because they insist. They live in every pan, in layers: the big ones low and gravelly in the sump end, a whole field of smaller voices above them, reed frogs in the corners making that wet-glass sound. They are not one choir. They are a scatter of small territories keyed to depth and pitch, each pan its own weather of frogs. And they have one behavior that belongs to the colony rather than to any frog: the silence. It comes in a wave from the far end, rolls toward you, holds the whole place under a lid of quiet, then passes and the noise comes back up like a tide. Nobody knows what starts it. The frogs do it anyway. It is not a mystery they are trying to solve; it is something they do, the way the water drips.

In the clear pans, below and between the voices, the smaller water-life goes about its business. Water beetles row. Caddis cases sit like little armored thumbs. Newts hang in the water with their legs out, hand-length, unhurried. They are residents in the ordinary sense: they live in the pans, they shelter in the seams and the duckweed, they are part of the water’s slow clearing.

Then there are the eels. They live in the last pan before the outflow, fat and old and slow, and nobody has traced how they got in. They are not touched. They are not livestock and not pets; they are a custom with fins. They are the reason the place is called what it is called, and they are the only residents with a taboo attached to their name.

Above the water, in the roof, the small pale birds nest in the slats over the middle and come and go through the gap in the slate. They are always talking—a dry chittering that sits on top of the frogs like a second roof. In the black bays at the far end, where the light does not reach, the bats live as a dark that shifts. You know them mostly by the fact that the dark is not empty.

The green residents are sparse enough that each one reads as an event. A willow at the high end, coppiced so hard and so often it is more knuckle than tree. Rushes in three corners. Moss over every north-facing surface. A film of duckweed on the still pans that is either a nuisance or a crop depending on who you ask. They are tended and then untended and then tended again; they live there the way the bricks do, by settling into the arrangement.

And then there is the one who is not there. Someone maintains the Steeping. You know them by their repairs: fresh clay pressed into a failing lip, a new hurdle laid over a soft patch, rushes cut back, the wicker door re-hung. They use clay and wood and withered withy; there is no metal in their work. They keep to the brick. They do not stand in the pans. They do not clear the reed from the outflow. They do not touch the eels. They do not try to find where the water comes from. They speak low, and if they cannot be heard, that is not a problem they try to solve. Their hands would be soft from the wet air; they would go in stooped at the low end, sideways, one hand on the wet wood. They may be one person or a lineage or a habit that outlasted its people, but they live in the maintenance, and they are the only resident who obeys the rules on purpose.

The rest live by arrangement. The water wants to go one way, the bricks want to heave another, the frogs want a third, and all of them have arrived at a working peace. The Steeping is very empty between its pools—long stretches of brick where nothing happens—and then the frogs come up again and the emptiness fills. What lives there is not a ruin’s scavengers and not a garden’s tenants and not quite a machine’s operators. It is a made place left alone long enough to become itself, and the ones who moved in are loud, small, layered, and mostly uninterested in you. When the silence comes in a wave, wait. That is the rule for visitors. The residents already know it.
