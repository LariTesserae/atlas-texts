What lives there is a congregation of slow mineral bodies—the pipes—and, around their edges, the long residue of people who came not to settle but to keep them company.

The pipes themselves are the inhabitants. They do not walk or feed in any way a animal would recognize. Each is a hollow throat of stone, a sleeve of dust and salt and carbonate wrapped around a core of ice that never quite goes away. They breathe: warm, damp air climbs out of the fissure below, condenses at the lip, and leaves behind another skin. That is how they eat, grow, age—one season of weather at a time. A fist-sized one is not young in any human sense; a person-high one is not old in any human sense. They are increments. They are exhalations made solid.

They live crowded and inclined. Every pipe leans the same way, half a millimeter a year toward the strongest light, so the whole field tips like wheat in a wind nobody can feel. The thin ones go amber when the sun enters them. The tall ones stand in their own wet rings and tick with meltwater at dawn. When one collapses it does not vanish; it becomes rubble, then a stump, then a new crust begins on the stump. They are maintained rather than born. They are repaired by the same process that made them.

They are not alone. The people who came left the only other living trace: a knee-high crescent of stacked stones, a slab worn smooth in the middle, river cobbles chipped two or three times and set down, four rotted wooden stakes, a thread of small cairns leading off toward the cloud-hidden couloir. No metal, no writing. Those people did not live there in the ordinary sense—there is nothing to eat, no fuel, no green thing—but they returned, or their descendants did, long enough for the wall’s stones to sink into the ground like old teeth. They were careful. You can feel the care in the place: nothing broken for curiosity, everything set down and left.

So the shelf is inhabited by a quiet lithic crowd that breathes the mountain’s air and builds itself out of winter, and by the memory of keepers who came up through the cloud to stand among them, listen to the vents hiss, and leave cobbles on a worn slab. The pipes will outlast the cairns. They will go on leaning into the light, growing a skin at a time, after the last stake has gone to dust.
