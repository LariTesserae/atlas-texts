What lives there first is not an animal but a condition: the habit of clinging. The summit is inhabited by things that have made themselves small enough to survive the wind’s attention. The moss is a resident in the fullest sense—thick, springy cushions of it, red and green, each one a crowded little city of threads and spores and trapped moisture. It lives in the fissures where the meltwater seeps, and it holds the peat together with its thousand tiny hands. The pale flowers live there too, moth-winged and strange, roots tangled in the dark pockets of soil, offering nectar like a small, reckless generosity.

The bees come for that generosity. They are the place’s low, continuous music, fuzzy with pollen, working the sheltered hollows with a stubborn, businesslike joy. Around them the air is a theatre of small violence and small escape. The black armored beetle lives down in the moss, a scuttling glint, armored against a world that could crush it with a thumb. The rock-colored rodents live in the crevices, nervous quicknesses, fur the color of the stone, darting from shadow to shadow as if the summit were made of doors. They are prey, and they know it. The thrush-sized falcon knows it too. It lives on the wind more than on the rock, hanging as a dark cross against the polished blue, its shadow a brief punctuation mark on the moss. When it stoops, the whole summit becomes a single held breath.

The hummingbird lives there as a jewel and a duelist. It arrives with a sharp whir, emerald and amethyst, hovering at the pale flowers, drinking and quarrelling, a tiny feathered engine of appetite and territory. It does not belong to the quiet; it cracks the quiet open. And then it is gone, a spark swallowed by the brilliant cold.

If a person lives there, it is not as an owner but as a guest who has learned to be small. The circular wall of fitted stones is the shape of that life: a low, roofless hut, patient and crude, a shelter for someone who needed to be high and alone. The fallen roof-wood is bleached and moss-grown now; the obsidian blade lies half-buried in the peat, still sharp enough to remind you that this was a place of use, not just a place of beauty. Whoever camped there was a hunter or a hermit, a passer-through who drank the meltwater, watched the hummingbird, heard the subterranean trickle at night, and understood that the summit grants no comfort it does not also take back. They left no name. They left a wall, a blade, and the impression of a person who had made a temporary truce with the cold.

So what lives there is a commonwealth of the small and the swift: moss and moth-winged flowers; bees and beetles; rodents and the falcon that edits them; the hummingbird like a struck jewel; and, faintly, the human shape that comes and goes, half hermit, half hunter, drawn to a high, bright, lonely island in the sky. Nothing there is large. Everything there is prepared to cling—and to be torn away.
