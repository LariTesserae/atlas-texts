What lives in the Sere Loom is less a population than a habit the city has of assembling bodies when it needs them. The main inhabitants are the palp-weavers: soft-bodied, blind, many-limbed creatures the color of old tallow and wet pearl, with palps that read the world by pressure, scent, and vibration. They have no eyes worth the name and no metal, no fire, no wheels. Their limbs end in sensitive pads and small secreting lips. They move through the Loom the way blood moves through a body—sometimes in the galleries, sometimes directly through the walls, squeezing passages open with slow peristalsis and sealing them behind themselves with a paste that sets pearl-gray and bone-yellow and rust-orange.

A single palp-weaver is not quite an animal and not quite a tool. It is a cell of the settlement. In the warm chambers it clusters with others, and the cluster becomes a kind of decision: a pressure-language passed from body to body, a scent-signature laid down and read back, a slow consensus about where to thicken a floor or open a chimney or close a nursery bowl. The weavers do not build the Loom so much as grow it around themselves and then inhabit the growth. Their bodies are the mortar, the duct, the signal line, the immune response. When a wall is damaged, they eat the broken edge and secrete a new one. When a molt is shed, it is collected and reworked. When a weaver dies, it is incorporated—digested, distributed, made into floor or conduit or the lining of a vault. Nothing is wasted because nothing here can afford to be.

They perceive ownership by scent and currency by warmth. The deep galleries are inherited; a warm alcove is a debt and a standing; a scent-mark on a threshold is a claim that other weavers will read and mostly respect. They run on sulfur films and salt and the moisture locked in their own tissues, recycling what little they have with a patience that looks like geology. Their lives are slow enough that a generation of building may be only a few towers thickened, a few plazas sealed, a few signal threads extended through the crust. The Loom is grown for generations and occupied only where need is immediate. That is why it is urban and empty at once: broad plazas between a handful of warm chambers, whole quarters hollow and resonant, the living core small and breathing.

The other residents are sparse and mostly hidden. Tick-mites graze fungal films in the galleries, small hard specks that move in slow tides along the walls. Defender organisms sleep folded in wall pockets—denser, darker, less mobile than the weavers—and stir only when something gnaws at the tower bases: a blind scarp-runner come down from the rim to lick salt, and then there is a brief, quiet predation, a closing of passages, a body taken back into the Loom. Spore dust puffs from the towers in pale threads at dawn. The chimneys exhale, and the ground makes a low felt thrum, more vibration than sound. The salt crust ticks as it contracts at night. The city breathes once a day, and its breath is dry.

So the inhabitants of the Sere Loom are the weavers in their warm cluster, the mites in the fungal seams, the defenders in their wall pockets, the rare runner at the rim—and the Loom itself, which may be a city or may be one distributed organ that occasionally assembles a body. They are not many. They are not loud. They are a quiet active nothing in the middle of a dry basin, living by warmth and scent and pressure, repairing what they are made of, waiting in skeleton galleries for the next generation to need them.
