**The Settled**

Dell has been down in the troughs for eleven years and she cannot tell you when she stopped going up.

The cart goes up without her. That is how it happens, when it happens: the season ends, the sacks are full, the two or three others who came down with her shoulder the traces and start the long climb out of the dish, and she walks them to the foot of the rise and watches them go, and then she turns around and walks back down into the noise. Nobody makes anything of it the first time. The cart comes back the next season and she is still there, thinner, and waves. It comes back the third season and she does not come to the foot of the rise to wave, and by then nobody expects her to, and the buyer's agent asks after her once and gets a vague answer and does not ask again. There are perhaps forty of them in the basin at any time, spread thin across a kilometer of ribs, and they are not a community exactly. They are a condition.

What she does with her days is skim. The nap is thickest where the water is shallow and the evaporation is quick, and she goes down on one knee with a curved blade and takes it off in long pale curls that come away warm and smell like warm stone — which is a smell if you have been here long enough. She does not take it all. A wall shaved too close bleeds water and needs a season to crust again; a wall shaved right crusts again in a month. She knows every wall within her reach the way a farmer knows a field, and she rotates them, and she can tell from the sound of a trough whether it is ready.

Her body has made its accommodations. The ears went first. The canon is the loudest thing here and it does not stop, and after a few years the ears close against it the way a hand closes, and what she lost was the top and the bottom — a kettle, the thin high ring of the blade on stone — and what she kept is the middle, where the thuds live. The middle she hears very well; the ear spent its budget on the thing it could not escape. So she speaks softly, and when the cart-men come down she asks them to say things twice, not because she cannot hear them but because they sound like shouting.

The teeth went next, or not went — loosened. The thuds come up through the soles and the jaw and the sinuses; you feel them in the roots of your teeth. After eleven years her molars are worn flat at the tips from being clenched, and the ache in them is the weather of the place, and she has stopped noticing it the way you stop noticing a sound.

And the crust is in her hands. This is the part the cart-men do not like to look at. The cuts a blade will give you — slivers, fine dry cracks across the knuckles — heal here, but they do not heal clean. They heal thick and pale and faintly warm, mineral at the edges, the way the channel walls heal, and the bad fall she took in her fourth year has left a seam across the heel of her left hand that is not a scar so much as a place where the nap has filled her in. The basin heals the stone it sits on. She has not found a reason it should not heal her too, and she has stopped looking for one.

She does not count days. There are no days to count — the light is a long pale plateau and then a slightly deeper gray, and neither of them is a sunrise. She counts thuds when she must, for the boiling of a thing, and she counts the canon the way you count your own pulse, which is to say without meaning to and without writing it down. Her calendar is the canon's larger weather: the phase-drifts, the days when three throats fall into step and hold it for an hour, the rare thinning-out when the rhythm nearly stops and she has learned not to be afraid of that. She always knows when a resolve is coming — a pressure behind the eyes, an ache in the arches of her feet — and she has never once been wrong, and cannot explain how she knows.

What none of the settled ones will do is count the resolves. Ask a skimmer how many seasons she has worked and she will tell you. Ask her how many resolves she has heard and she will look at you the way you would look at someone asking how many times your heart beat while you slept. The cart-men have learned not to ask. There is no rule against it and nobody has ever given a reason. It is simply the one number nobody keeps.

Why does she stay? She has been asked, by people who meant it kindly and by people who did not, and she does not have an answer that travels upward. The nearest she has come is this: up on the shelf the day has a shape — morning, noon, night, a beginning and an end — and down here it does not, and once you have stopped expecting the shape the expectation stops being a loss. She eats what the cart brings and what she can catch. She does not talk about what she can catch. There are the pale things in the deep chambers, soft and blind and slow, and they are meat if you are not particular, and the settled ones are not particular. Nobody asks. That is not a euphemism. Nobody asks.

And there is the resolve. She knows it is coming, and she sets down her blade, and she goes down on both knees in whichever trough she is working and puts her hands flat on the nap, and she waits. She would be embarrassed to be told she was praying. But she has never once worked through it, and neither has anyone else, and the cart-men say that from the rim the settled ones look like a field of people setting down their tools at the same moment for a bell only they can hear. It comes: the throats draw together like a fist, and the basin says it, one soft enormous whump she feels in her sternum and her sinuses and the arches of her feet, and the water rises in every trough at once and holds, trembling, and her heart does something it does not do at any other time — something slow and strong and not quite her own — and then it lets go and the rhythm scatters and she breathes and picks up her blade.

She has a theory she keeps to herself: that the basin is not a machine and not an animal but something the water made and is still making, the way it made the channels and is making the crust and is making her. And a second theory, kept more privately, that whatever it is, it is not asleep, and the resolve is not a snore — it is the sound of something turning over, patiently, in a bed it does not intend to leave. She does not say this out loud. There is no one down here to say it to except the pale things, and they do not answer.

She calls the pale things her neighbors. There are a dozen or so within her reach, in the deep chambers where the surge is strongest, and in eleven years she has watched them rise with the pulse and be carried a few meters and settle and wait for the next one, and she has not seen one die and has not seen one born and has stopped wondering about it, which is its own kind of answer. She has come to think of herself as a late arrival to the tenancy. The basin is patient with her. It is filling in her seams.

In the morning — the pale plateau, the gray — she wakes in the dry rib she has made her bed and the condensation is beaded on the stone and her hands have left their print in the nap where she slept, two soft hollows, faintly warm. By the time she has had her blade out an hour the print is gone. The nap has filled it in, the way it fills in everything, and she has stopped finding that strange, and that is the thing she could never explain to anyone up on the shelf: not that the basin is changing her, but that she no longer wants it to stop.
