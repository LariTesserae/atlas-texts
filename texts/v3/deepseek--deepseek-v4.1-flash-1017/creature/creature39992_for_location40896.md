What lives in the Softground is lopsided. There are multitudes, and there are maybe three, and between them is one organism the size of the basin. The town was laid out for a crowd; the crowd that arrived is mostly soft-shelled and many-legged, and it lives in the floor.

**The pale ones** are the actual citizenry. They are small, delicate, many-legged things, soft-shelled as the inside of a watch, and they are everywhere the town is not: under every third step, drifted into the creases where the living floor folds over the stone, clustered at the rims of the puddles to graze the film. They come out of the tissue and go back into it through holes you cannot see until you are standing on one and feel the floor give, softly, like a mouth closing. They have no eyes worth the name — pale patches, maybe, that register light the way a hand registers warmth — and they taste with their feet. Their legs are jointed and fine and they fold them in when they rest, so that a resting one looks like a dropped piece of something, a bit of shell or a seed.

They are not vermin. They are the reason for the works. Somewhere down the line the sap is split and pressed and cooled, and the product goes into the pipes, and the pipes sweat, and the pale ones gather at the drip-points and eat. They eat the film off the puddles, and they eat each other, calmly, the larger ones taking the smaller ones with a patience that looks like housekeeping. The ones that keep to the walls are larger and slower and move in waves when the ceiling breathes, a long soft ripple of them going up and over and settling again. They are abundant and nearly silent; their sound is below hearing, a pressure in the floor, a tapping you feel in your soles rather than hear. They are not curious about you. You are a pressure they route around. Stand still long enough and they come out again, and one will walk over your boot as if your boot were a stone that has not finished being stone.

They are the ones being fed. That is the thing to understand about the town: it is not empty, it is underpopulated at human scale and overpopulated at the scale of the floor. The pale ones do not build. The plant builds for them. The pumps are fixed and the pipes are clean and the condensers sweat, and the pale ones live in the seams of all that maintenance like tenants in a building that was designed by someone who never met them but had their measurements.

**The three people** — if there are three — are harder to describe because they are mostly traces. A lamp left burning in a far chamber. A handprint pressed into the soft stone, lasting an hour. A shed door standing open, a pipe wiped clean, a bracket regrown around a bolt. They are not a crowd and they do not behave like a crowd. They move quietly because sound dies here after four yards; they do not shout. They wear soft soles. They carry lamps rarely, and when they leave one they are telling you something: this corridor is closing, that pump is the one that stays, do not go that way. They know the pumps are fixed. They navigate by them the way you would navigate by stars in a place where the stars were bolted to the wall.

They smell of mineral and green and, faintly, of hot metal, though nothing here burns. Their hands are careful and stained. They tend the condensers, the sap-lines, the works that press and split and cool. They feed the pale ones without exactly meaning to; they are part of the same chain, and they are calm about it. They eat what the works make and what the floor provides. They do not finish the town. Finishing would require a population, and the population is not coming, and they have made a routine out of that fact. They are the only residents who experience the emptiness as emptiness. The pale ones experience it as floor. The plant experiences it as room.

You may meet one and mistake them for a post, or a growth, or a lamp that has learned to stand. They will not ask you to stay. They will not ask you much at all. They are practical in a way that has had a long time to set. They are the caretakers, not the citizens. They keep the works because stopping would be a decision, and decisions are loud, and loudness does not carry.

**The plant** is the third resident, and the largest, and the least personal. It is one organism and it is the house: ceiling, floor, walls, the warm-ish tidal breathing that takes ninety seconds and that you will find yourself breathing with without meaning to. It grows rooms it does not need and corridors that are only pinches where the ceiling has sagged almost to the floor. It grows brackets around the pipes. It is being harvested — the sap-lines run into its tissue and out again — and it does not object, or its objection is too slow to see. It lives on chemistry and damp and the slow grinding of the unfinished stone. It gave up on light long ago. It may be the thing that intended to come back and finish the place; it may not know there was a plan. Its attention is diffuse. You feel it as warmth against your cheek, as the floor giving under your thumb, as the ceiling moving when you are trying to sleep. It is the most alive thing there and the least like a person.

So the population is one house, one crop, three caretakers, and a multitude of small soft citizens. The town's emptiness is not absence. It is a mismatch of scales. The crowd is there. It is just under your feet, eating the film off the puddles, waiting for the pipes to sweat.
