The Sump Terraces are inhabited by a small, damp-handed maintenance culture and by everything the water can persuade to grow. First the people: perhaps a dozen adults, their children, and two old men who sit where the warm air rises and claim to remember the first bolts. The adults dress against the wet in oilskin coats, rubber aprons, felt boots, gloves mended with copper wire. Their hands are stained green-brown at the knuckles; their faces are pale in the fog. They carry gaskets and tallow, short bars and long-handled nets, and they speak in valve numbers and pipe diameters. They know the terraces by sound—which drip is merely patient, which hiss means a fitting has begun to fail. Their work is the patient work: trace a root, route it away from a manifold, brace a weeping pipe, harvest the green soup from a reservoir, patch what can be patched, and leave the rest to negotiate.

They live mostly on what the place yields. Greens from the trellised gardens taste strongly of iron. Small pale insects are pressed into cakes. The pool harvest comes up in nets—soft, translucent things and green matter—and is dried, salted, divided. A few fowl are kept for eggs and occasional meat, and they eat the silverflies the children catch. The people sleep in small chambers tucked under the upper basins, where the pumps' hum comes through the walls and the blankets smell faintly of mildew. They are not waiting for a signal from below the fog. They are not besieged. They are simply holding a constructed mountain together while the green finds every bolt.

The children are the only ones with surplus motion. They are born into the sound of water and learn the drainage lattice as a map before they learn the names of the valves. They climb the iron trellis, stalk midges in the channels, dare each other to stand at the very edge of the lowest terrace where the spillway leaps into white. Their first rule is the place's one prohibition: walk on iron, never on the raw slope below. The unfinished earth is not ground to them but a rumor, a thing under the fog that must not be touched. They are taught to route vines, not rip them out, and they learn early that the overgrowth is both a tenant and a crop.

The two old men are a category of inhabitant almost to themselves. They oil hinges that do not need oil, correct the children's valve names, and tell stories about the earliest construction that no one can verify because the ledgers have gone soft with damp. They may be lying. They may be the only honest record left. They sit near the warm updraft with their coats open, as if the moisture were a kind of company.

Then there is the green, the largest population. Moss films every ledge; ferns uncurl from drainage slots; vines sleeve the railings and bury the valve manifolds; the two upper basins hold trees whose roots were laid in gravel by workers and now grip the structure like slow hands. The green lives on the mineral film the water carries down from the condensers, and it pays for its room by filtering the water and eating the iron. The keepers speak of it with resentment and tenderness. It is not wild—there is no wild soil here—but it is rampant, and it is winning by inches.

The animals are mostly small and wet. Silverflies hang in columns over the warm channels. Water-midges work the standing pools. A few drab birds nest in the trellis and stalk the drainage slots with the air of inspectors. In the reservoirs, the green soup teems with soft-bodied things that the crews net at intervals; they are livestock without pens, a crop that swims. And beneath all of it, the structure itself keeps its own company: the pumps' low organ hum, the tuned dripping into basins of different depths, the slap and fizz of overflow. To live in the Sump Terraces is to be amphibious without ever swimming—moisture on every surface, water running downhill through everything, and the whole small world talking to itself in water.
