The plateau is inhabited by a community that never quite separates into individuals. Its largest citizens are the stone-eaters: hill-sized domes that are not boulders but slow, warm architectures of flesh and mineral. A stone-eater is a hill that has learned to breathe. Its crust is lichened and cracked, its seams open and close with the patience of settling bedrock, and from those seams leaks a faint internal heat, enough to bead the mist and keep the frost at bay. It does not see; it feels pressure, damp, the long weight of weather. It does not walk so much as redistribute itself, swelling and settling until a decade has passed and it has shifted a few yards, leaving a shallow furrow where minerals have been stirred and frost gathers.

Around each stone-eater lives its retinue. The moss-backs are soft-bodied, fibrous-coated grazers, their backs like damp felt, their speed indistinguishable from the spread of lichen. They cluster in the warm lee of the great domes, cropping the green-black crust in slow, rasping passes. Their coats soak up the perpetual mist and hold it; they are little reservoirs with legs, and their breath is part of the low, rhythmic exhalation of the plateau. When one dies, its coat keeps growing for a while, a green shroud, until the pale tuberous fungi push through it and break it down. The moss-backs are the plateau’s wool and compost, its pasture and its slow decay.

The crawlers are the nervous edge of this life. They are hand-sized, many-legged, their shells the color of wet slate. They live in the seams of stone-eaters, under the rims of the cold pools, in the cracked lichen mat. They move in short, scuttling advances and then freeze, becoming wet stone again. They are the only things here that startle. They feed on fungus and detritus, on the soft leavings of moss-backs, and they keep the stone-eaters’ crevices clean by crawling through them, picking at the accumulated damp. Their eggs are laid in warm folds of stone-eater flesh; their shells click faintly against rock, the smallest sound in a place built from silence.

The vegetation is not scenery but slow organ. Lichen mats are the pasture; wiry shrubs grow in the wind-shadow of the stone-eaters, stunted and tough, their roots holding the thin crust together; pale fungi push their heads through the moss and do the work of decay. Nothing is taller than a knee, and nothing grows quickly. A shrub may take a century to thicken by a finger’s width. A patch of lichen may be older than the memory of any moving thing.

Together they make a moving village around each stone-eater. The great creature’s warmth and disturbance create a microclimate; the moss-backs follow its mineral trail; the crawlers follow the moss-backs; the fungi follow the crawlers’ waste and the moss-backs’ dead. When a stone-eater finally dies—over decades, its warmth fading, its crust collapsing—it becomes a hollow, then a shallow pool, then a nursery. Its body is reabsorbed into the plateau’s slow economy. Nothing here lives alone, and nothing here is in a hurry. The true inhabitant is the relationship itself: a damp, patient metabolism, breathing in stone, lichen, and soft bodies, and asking for no name.
