**The residents are few, and they are all extremists or refugees.**

That's the first thing. A place this loud does not invite. It selects. Whatever lives in the Kettle lives there because it has found the one arrangement of its body or its habit that the noise cannot delete, and it holds that arrangement with everything it has. There is no room here for the merely tolerant.

**The bloom comes first.**

Before anything with a face, there is the light, and the light is alive. Single cells by the billion, suspended in the milk, and they are not photosynthesisers — there is no sun here worth the name, only that flat grey dusk — they are eating the mountain. The water comes up pregnant with what the rock has been dissolving in the dark, and the bloom takes it, and what it cannot use it turns into more of itself, and the surplus is what you see hanging in the churn like dust in a sunbeam that has lost its sun.

They came up from below with the water. That's the part worth sitting with. The fracture opened and the water came and the bloom was already in it, from a darker place, a place with no light at all, and it did not so much arrive as *surface*. It has been here exactly as long as the Kettle has, and it will be here after the last animal and the last bird and the last person have gone, and on that day the water will still be glowing, patient, unbothered, eating the mountain a molecule at a time.

The glow is not steady. It brightens when the upwelling surges — you can watch it pulse, a slow heartbeat in the boil — and I think, though I cannot prove it, that the pulse is the bloom's own doing, a chemical conversation, a crowding and a releasing. Everything else here navigates by it. It is the only landmark in a place where maps are a joke. The bloom is the map.

**The animals are the ones who ride.**

The length of your finger, blind, the colour of the inside of a wrist. You will not see one clearly until it stops, and it stops only when the boil does.

They live in the column. They let it take them up and throw them, and they turn in the air, and they go down again under their own power, and they do this all day, and nobody has ever seen one eat. What they eat is the bloom, most likely — the lattice of light along their flanks is the bloom, grown into grooves in their skin, farmed. When an animal is riding, the lattice blazes; it is the brightest thing in the water, and it is the same colour as the water, and so the animal has no edge. It is a brightening, a thickening, a length of the glow that happens to be going somewhere.

When the boil pauses — and it pauses without warning, for a second, sometimes two — every one of them stops at once. They hang. They go dark. The lattice dims to almost nothing, and they become pale shapes in pale water, and they hold absolutely still, and they wait. I have watched this a dozen times and I am still not sure what the stillness is for. It might be that motion in still water is the one thing that can be detected. It might be that they are listening. It might be that the pause is the only time they can be seen, and going dark is how they hide from whatever is looking, and they have learned this the way anything learns a thing that keeps it alive.

Then the column takes them, and the light comes back, and they are gone into the noise.

You can count them, on a good day, if you are patient and the steam lies low. The number does not change. No young, no old, no small ones at the edges. All the same length, all the same pallor, all riding. I do not know how that is possible. I have stopped trying to make it possible.

**The bird is the one who is safe.**

Small, brown, unremarkable — you would not look at it twice anywhere else. It nests inside the loudest fissure on the north rim, in the full scream of the steam, and this is not eccentricity. It is architecture. Nothing that hunts by ear can hear anything in there. The bird has built its life in the one pocket of the mountain where silence would be a liability, and it goes in and out all day, and you never hear it once.

I think it does sing. I think it must. But its song is pitched to the fissure — folded into the resonance, married to the shriek — so that if you stood at the north rim and listened for a year you would hear the fissure, and the fissure would have a bird in it, and you would not be able to separate the two. Its voice is a private thing, sung into a stone throat that swallows it and gives it back as weather.

There is a possibility I keep turning over. The stakes along the inside of the rim are driven into the fissures with the best pitch. The bird nests in the loudest fissure. Did the bird choose the fissure because the stakes had already made it louder — because someone, years ago, tuned a room for it without meaning to? Or did the person drive the stakes into the fissures the bird had chosen, following the bird, listening for where it went? The stakes hum. The bird's fissure screams. It is not possible, now, to say which came first, and I have come to think that is the honest answer and also the whole of the answer.

**The mat is the one who loses.**

At the waterline, where the spray falls, there is a scalded mat of something plant-like — olive at its living edge, rust where it has been cooked. It smells like a hot iron. It never gets more than a hand's width from the water. When the level rises it is boiled back to nothing; when the level drops it creeps out again, a centimetre at a time, and is boiled back again.

Everything else here is being made or unmade at speed, and none of it seems to mind. The mat minds. The mat is the only resident engaged in a genuine argument with the mountain, and it is losing, and it has been losing for as long as anyone has come up here to watch. There is something in that worth respecting. It is not stupid. It is not hopeful, either. It has simply found the exact line where the water might not kill it, and it holds that line, and it is killed, and it comes back.

**The people came, and worked, and left, and do not count.**

But they left marks, and the marks are worth reading, because they are the record of someone trying to solve the place rather than live in it.

The ring of stones on the south lip, twenty paces across, hand-stacked, half-eaten by new sinter. Inside it, the rock is worn smooth in a circle. People stood there. Many people, for a long time, in the same place, facing in. I do not think it was a ceremony. I think it was a *waiting*. The silence pockets drift over the basin unpredictably, riding the steam like bubbles in glass, a metre or two across — and a ring of stone, I think, is a way of making one more likely to pass through a particular place. You stand in the ring and you wait to be given a volume of true nothing, large enough to hear yourself think in. And you stand there for years. And the rock wears smooth under your feet, and the sinter grows up over the bottom course, and one season you do not come back, and the next, and the ring is still there, eating itself, waiting.

The platform on the east lip is a different temperament. Split wood, lashed with cord, level, still standing, carried up from a timberline that does not grow this high. On it, a shallow depression worn into the boards, the shape of a person's weight over a great many seasons. One person, or a succession of them, kneeling or sitting in the same spot, in the same posture, facing the water. The platform is higher than the ring and further out. From it you can see the whole basin. I think the person on the platform was the one who set the stakes, and I think they sat there to hear the chord — a dozen fissures humming at a dozen pitches, a chord that is not a chord — and the depression is the shape of listening. There is no metal anywhere on this mountain. The only tool marks on the stakes are the ones a blade makes. Whoever it was did the work by hand, alone, over years, and left, and the mountain is unmaking it at a millimetre a year.

The stakes are a route. I am nearly sure of this. Distance here is measured in noise, not space — the far rim is clear and you cannot walk to it in any predictable time, and the route moves. A line of stakes, each in a fissure of a different pitch, is a path you follow by ear. You go from stake to stake by listening. The pitch tells you the next one. It is a map that a person can carry in their head and that requires the mountain to keep singing the right notes, and the mountain does, mostly, and the person kept re-setting the stakes as the route drifted. That is why they are no longer quite aligned. That is why the hum is no longer quite a chord. The path is half a step out of true.

**And then there is the reflection, which is a resident too.**

The surface lies. Look down and you see a calm sky with a sun in it, at the wrong angle, at every hour the same angle. Look down and the rim is crowded. Look up and you are alone.

But the crowd in the reflection is standing in the ring. All of them, facing in, toward the water, in the worn-smooth circle, and the ring on the rock is empty, and has been empty since before you came. I do not think they are ghosts. I think the surface is showing you the only place that remembers them, and the only posture it can remember them in, which is waiting. They are still waiting for the silence. They are still facing in. They do not turn when you look.

And the homesickness — that is theirs, I think, as much as yours. Stand in the basin long enough and the outside gets very slightly less real; you can see it from the rim and it has begun to feel like a rumour. The people in the reflection are the ones who stayed too long. They are still here, and they are made of the thing they were homesick for, and the surface is the only place it shows.

**So: the bloom, which eats the mountain. The animals, which ride it. The bird, which has made a house of the noise. The mat, which loses. The people, who came to solve it and were solved. And under all of them, the low throb that comes up through the sternum, water and rock arguing in the dark, which is the mountain itself, and which is the resident the others are only visiting.**

To live in the Kettle you must either be too small for the noise to find you, or you must make the noise your house. There is no third way. The mat is trying to find one and it will not. The bird found one and it took a fissure. The animals found one and it took the boil, and they go dark in the pauses, and they wait, and they are taken again.
