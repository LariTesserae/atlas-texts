The people of Hadal Spire are not cave-dwellers in the usual sense. They do not huddle in the dark; they keep house inside a living ceiling. They are small, close-grained people, their bodies shaped by wet cold and permanent twilight—skin that holds a faint mineral sheen, pupils wide enough to drink the wormlight, fingertips thickened from gripping slick stone. Their voices tend low. Not because they are solemn, but because the cave is already loud, and they have learned to speak underneath its noise.

They dress in dark, close-fitting layers, studded with bioluminescent pins so that they become a constellation moving through the mist. You can see a family coming from a long way off: five or six slow stars turning along a spiral corridor, a child’s marker blinking green, an elder’s steady amber. Their clothes are always a little damp. Their homes are warm pockets carved into the cold—small rooms clustered around geothermal vents, lit by worm-jars, their walls sweating and their floors dry only because someone has learned exactly where the water will run.

They have jobs the way anyone has jobs. Worm-tenders walk the luminescent curtains at fixed hours, reading the pulse for sickness or hunger. Filter-farmers tend the grey mats threaded through the city’s veins, feeding them waste and dead things and harvesting the clean, bland material that comes out the other side. Strut-lacers follow the sand-machines, checking the new ceramic growth, tapping the walls to hear whether the stone is still settling. Chamber-mappers go out with coils of marked cord and come back days later with sketches of passages that did not exist last season. Vent-keepers keep the heat from wandering. Listeners sit near the lower shafts and write down what the great singer does.

They are not survivors. They will correct you if you say so. Survivors are people waiting for rescue. The people of Hadal Spire are residents. They argue about whether the worms should be harvested at dusk or dawn, though there is no dusk and no dawn—only the city’s artificial rhythms, the slow dimming and brightening of the worm-farms. They have festivals when the great singer sings a passage that the listeners agree is beautiful. They have funerals where the body is carried down to the filter-feeders and given back to the cycle, and the mourners tell stories until the worms above them pulse in a slow, sympathetic wave. They have children who play a game called *find the new ledge*, and the children usually win, because the stone is still growing and the city is still annexing and there is always a new ledge.

The grey-eyed woman I spoke to was a pulse-reader. She could tell from the worms’ light whether the water above was shifting, whether a vent had opened, whether the great singer was dreaming or restless. She lived in a warm pocket with a hanging garden of filter-mats and a child who had never seen the surface. When I asked her what she thought of the darkness, she did not laugh. She looked around at the pulsing ceiling, the dripping struts, the spiral of lights going up and down into the stone, and she said, “What darkness?” She was not being clever. She was telling me what she saw.

The creatures are not separate from the people. They are the light, the food, the building material, the warning system, the thing that keeps the city awake. The luminescent worms are the most visible: long, glassy threads that cling to the ceiling and pulse in coordinated waves, so that the whole cave sometimes seems to breathe. The people read those waves the way you might read a clock or a face. When the worms pulse fast, something is moving. When they dim, something is wrong. When they go dark, everyone stops.

The filter-feeders are slower and stranger. They live in the walls, in the warm wet seams where the city’s waste runs down. They are not loved exactly, but they are respected. They take what is finished and make it into what can be used again. The people say the filter-feeders are the city’s stomach, and they do not say it cruelly. They give their dead to them. They give their garbage to them. In return they get food that tastes of iron and salt, and material that can be pressed into cloth or brick or the soft grey lampshades that make a room feel less like a hole in the rock.

And below everything, in the black water, the great singer. No one has seen it whole. It is enormous, maybe singular, maybe a colony pretending to be singular. Its song is a long grinding vibration that moves through the stone and into the bones. The people propitiate it with offerings of metal and silence—silence meaning that during certain hours the city dims its lights and stills its machines and does not speak above a whisper. They do this not because they think it is a god, but because it is a keystone. When it falls quiet, the smaller things stop singing. When the smaller things stop singing, the city goes dark. No one knows what happens after that. No one wants to.

The sand-machines are not alive, but they are inhabitants in their own way. They work in tides, laying down struts and corridors and new rooms the way coral lays down reef. The people do not worship them. They maintain them. They follow them. They have learned to live inside a building that is still building itself.

So what lives in Hadal Spire? A chorus. Humans in dark clothes and bright pins, moving sure-footed through mist. Worm-tenders and vent-keepers and children who have never missed the sun. Filter-feeders turning death back into food. A great singer in the black water, singing because it sings, and a city that has learned to keep time with it. Stone still growing. Water still dripping. Machines still lacing the walls. A whole ecology that has learned to keep house.

It is cold, and wet, and dark, and loud, and alive. And somehow—this is the part I still cannot quite explain—it is hopeful. Not because it is waiting to be finished. Because it is not.
