Ask what lives there, and the first honest answer is: a chorus, with a few soloists. The volume is nearly empty; the chimneys are crowded. Life does not spread through that thin cold water so much as knot in it, around the warm columns, along the machine shoulders, in the places where the ringing has a shape.

The natives are not deaf. They are deaf the way we are blind in fog—not deprived, adapted. A body down here is mostly ear and buoyancy. Skin is a tympanum. The cavity behind the mouth can be tuned. They do not hear the chord as sound; they read it as distance, as weather, as the position of a neighbor, as the difference between a rock face and a machine. They know a warm chimney from two hundred kilometers away by its signature in the ringing. They know each other by the delay in an echo. They have no eyes worth the name. The light under the ice is uniform and tells them almost nothing. The sound tells them everything.

The divers’ names for them are plain and a little embarrassed: ribbons, hands, pins. The ribbons are the most abundant. Long pale sheets, colonial, soft as wet cloth, holding station in the upwelling. A ribbon is not one animal but a chain of them, each zooid a small open mouth fringed with hair-cells. When the chord is steady they hang open like a hand of skin. When the beat changes they close, all together, a whole chimney’s worth of them folding at once. They spawn on the beat—when two machines drift half a cycle out of phase and the interference pattern slackens, the ribbons open and release, and the water for a few hundred meters is full of a pale smoke that is not smoke.

The hand-things live on the edges. They are folded animals, anchored by a stalk, and when they open they open into a palm: a bell of soft tissue with tentacles that are not quite tentacles, more like fingers of themselves. They are the closest thing here to a face. They do not chase. They wait for the chord to tell them that the spiralers are coming, and then they open, and the spiralers come. They can detach and fold and drift, and they do, rarely, when a chimney cools by a fraction and the local signature changes. They move like cloth deciding to become a bird.

The pins are small and fast and silver-bright, and they are made mostly of a gas bladder and an ear. They travel in helices, schools that are also chords, and they are the only natives that make sound on purpose. They ping. They chirp. They answer each other in intervals too short for a human diver to follow. They are the pollinators of the chimney gardens: they carry gametes, they carry films of living matter from one warm shoulder to another, they carry news. They are also the most curious. A diver is a new echo to them—too soft, too slow, gone too soon—and they will spiral around it once and then lose interest, because the chord has already told them what they need to know.

And then there is the one the divers call the long listener. It is the nearest thing to a who. A soft spindle the length of a bridge, no shell, no eyes, a single spiral ear coiled through its body like a shell that grew inward. It crosses the empty volume between chimneys over decades. It is not intelligent in the way we mean. It is old in the way a rock is old, and it has a self made of intervals: it remembers the chord the way we remember a room. It knows the silence pockets by their gaps in the ringing, and it goes around them. It has never entered one. It will not.

The pockets are the only taboo. A few meters across, hanging in the water like holes in the world, places where the reflections cancel. To a native, silence is not rest. Silence is the absence of the map. A creature that enters a pocket is blindfolded at a cliff. The pins will swim a long way around. The ribbons will not release into it. The long listener turns a slow, patient turn, and the chord comes back, and it goes on.

They have no history, because nothing happens here that the chord does not already contain. They have phase. They have weather made of beat and drift. They have a calendar that is the sum of two machines and the shimmer between them, and when that sum changes, a hundred thousand animals turn at once toward something none of them can see. Then the beat resolves. The pattern returns. The ribbons open. The hands close. The pins spiral. The long listener goes on crossing the rumor of water toward the next warm voice.

They do not know the machines were built. They know them as warm voices, as the sun and the weather and the only clock. They live in the chord the way we live in air: surrounded, upheld, unaware of the medium until it is taken away. The silence pockets are the only hell they have. Everything else is the ringing, and the ringing is the world.
