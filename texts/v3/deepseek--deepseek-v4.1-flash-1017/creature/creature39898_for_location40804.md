There is one resident you will not see, and it is the one the Crown is actually keeping.

But it is not the first resident, or the most numerous, or the one that will greet you. That is the vegetation, which is not a *who* and does not care about the distinction. It came first and it will be here after; it has rooted in the rust and the sinter and the fine grey dust the flue throws off, and it leans inward toward the warmth and the updraft, all of it bent like a congregation, patient, unhurried, working into the laminated flake of the plates and splitting them one season at a time. The woody things in the corners are older than the ladder. They are not tall. They have been old for a long time.

The birds are next, and they are the loudest citizens and the least interested in you. Small brown things, finch-shaped but not finches, hatched in the roar and therefore deaf to it the way a fish is blind to water. There are perhaps forty. There have always been perhaps forty. They fly through the shafts of light as though the light were not there, and they will land within arm's reach and look at you with the flat curiosity of something that has never hunted and never been hunted and has no category for you. The insects are beneath them, in the seams and the warm pools and the loose lagging, numerous in the way a statistic is numerous — you will not see them individually, only the scatter of them when you lift a frond, and the resettling. They are why the birds are forty and not four.

And then there is the one under the floor.

It lives in the shallow chamber between the grate and the flue mouth, where the plates have sagged and the underpinning has rusted back, and the chamber is no bigger than a coffin and no taller than a knee, and it has been in there long enough to have taken the chamber's shape. You will hear it before you see it. A slow dry shifting — plates, or scales, or the cracked leather of something that has dried and re-wetted many times — dragging over iron. It is not loud. In a quieter place it would be the loudest thing. Here it comes and goes under the roar like a word under a wave.

Now and then it puts something out. Not a limb, not anything with a name. A thin grey thing, the colour of the rust channels, comes up through the grate and lies flat on the warm metal for a while, and withdraws. It is not curious. It is thermoregulating. It is doing the only thing there is to do here, which is to be warm.

It will not come out while you are there. It does not come out for the birds, or the light, or the drip that finds a resonance in the plate above its chamber and rings a single note and then stops. It comes out, if it comes out, on the Crown's clock, and the Crown's clock is slow. You may sit in the middle of the emptiness for an hour and hear nothing but the roar and the shifting, and the shifting will not resolve into a shape. It is not hiding from you. It has no category for you either. It has been here long enough that the category it started in is no longer the category it is in, and the Crown does not keep records. It keeps residents.

What it was, you will not know. There is a hollow worn into the plate above its chamber, a polish in the iron where something has lain for a very long time. The insects are fewer in its chamber than they should be. The birds will not nest near the grate. The ladder has been climbed for a long time, and there have been other visitors, and the Crown does not distinguish between the ones that climbed down and the ones that stayed. It is warm here. It is wet here. The roar is slow. A body that stays will settle into the place's rhythm, and after enough seasons the body will *be* the place's rhythm, and the Crown will have one more resident, and it will keep it.

You are not the first. You will not be the last. Sit down in the middle of the emptiness and let it be loud at you, and wait, and see whether the slow thing the place is doing becomes something you can feel. It will, eventually. That is the ordinary condition of the place. You are in the census now.
