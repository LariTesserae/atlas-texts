# Skelfers

Nobody came here to be first at anything. The flat was free and no one else had wanted it, and the people who came down off the hills came mostly because a holding up there had been cut in two too many times, or because they had been on the wrong side of something, or because a river-mouth takes anyone without asking much. They came down meaning to leave. Then the ground rose an inch under them and they discovered they were tenants of a thing that was happening, and that they found it interesting.

They are shorter than their parents. Everyone says so and it is probably true — the wet ground, the low grey sky, the long habit of going down three steps into a house. They are wide through the shoulders and the feet, with hands that have been wet so often and so long that the nails have gone soft and ridged like a leaf. Almost everyone has the cough. It has a name — the wets — and it is not considered an illness, only a way of living, and at any gathering you can hear it going around the room, a soft productive clearing, one after another, like animals moving in a byre. Their hair never quite dries. Their clothes smell of clay and smoke and wet wool, and they cannot smell it on themselves.

They are hopeless on a slope. A Skelfer on a hillside is a comic sight — they skid on the dry grass, they misjudge, they keep looking down for a give that isn't going to come, and they walk with the wide careful splay of someone crossing a floor they don't trust. On the flat they never stumble. They cross a raw bank at a run with the edge crumbling under them and don't even look at their feet, because they are not looking at their feet, they are reading the colour of the puddles and the lean of the reeds and the way a post has settled, and they know what it means.

That is the real literacy of the place. A Skelf child learns the ground before the alphabet, learns it by being told no. Don't trust a pale bar. Don't trust a place where the sedge is too green. Don't put your weight on anything that looks firm, put it on the thing that looks soft and slow down. They have a whole vocabulary for what lies underfoot — the crust that will take a barrow and not a goat, the sile that rises around your boot faster than you can lift it, the run, which is water moving under the surface where it has no business being. An old woman can tell you the depth of the water table by the shade of a puddle at noon. She is not being mystical about this. She is simply reading.

## The well

The well-head is the town. There is no square, because there is no room for a square, so the widest bit of ground in Skelf is the apron of packed stone around the Old Well, and everything happens there — bargaining, courtship, the settling of arguments, the news, the long slow business of people standing near each other and not saying much. There is a New Well too, dug forty years ago, still called the new one. The water in the Old Well is colder and tastes more of iron and everyone claims to prefer it and most people drink from whichever is nearer.

The town agrees things at the well, because the water is the one thing everyone needs and everyone must come to.

## The Jacks

The levellers are the aristocrats of Skelf and they know it. They are the only trade in town that is properly a guild, with a head — the Master of the Level — and a set of tools that are looked after like relics: matched oak wedges, flat shims cut from a particular grey stone, long iron bars, and the level itself, a trough of water with a float in it, which is the only precision instrument for a day's walk in any direction. A Jack can look at a house and tell you which corner is sinking slower and which is already gone, and can tell you a year ahead, and is nearly always right, and will let you know he is nearly always right.

They are busy. They travel — up the hill road, to the other delta towns, out to the new bars where someone is foolishly building — and they are the only Skelfers who go anywhere and come back. Everyone needs them and everyone resents them a little and everyone invites them to drink. A house-raising is the nearest thing the town has to a festival: the props in, the sills cut, the whole lane come out to watch, the house hanging there with its underfloor open to the daylight and someone's grandmother inside it in a chair, refusing to leave, and the Jacks working around her without comment, because they have all done this before and will do it again and it is not their job to argue with the old.

## Children

Children are the ones who go into the wells. They are small enough to be lowered on a rope and they are not yet afraid of the cold, and they come up with eels and get a cuff and a piece of bread. They learn the channels by playing them — there is a game of jumping from one patch of firm ground to the next and the rules are understood by everyone and written down nowhere. They are the ones who name the thing in the water, and they name it differently every year, and they are entirely serious about it.

They are also the ones who notice what comes back. A child will run the length of the town to tell a man that a thing of his is on the shelf in the shed.

## Found things

The shed by the well has a keeper. She keeps the board — the scratches that record what came up, and when, and where — and she knows, better than anyone, what has not been claimed, and by whom it is being waited for. She is not a magistrate but she is treated like one. Found property waits a year and a day. Everyone in town walks past the shed and looks.

What is not generally said aloud is that the custom has made some people strange. There is a man in Skelf who lost a hatchet in the marsh, got it back eleven years later black and waterlogged and harder than it had ever been, and has been dropping things into the wet ground on purpose ever since — good things, useful things, things he could use — hoping the ground will improve them. He is not mad. He is making a reasonable inference from the evidence. Nobody teases him about it to his face, partly because it is a private matter and partly because half the town has, at some point, dropped something in the marsh and said a small word over it.

The word is *keep it for me*. You do not search. Searching is the one thing you don't do, and the reason is not superstition exactly; it is that searching is what people do when they believe the ground takes. Skelfers know the ground returns, and they know it returns in its own time, and the discipline of not looking is the whole of their religion.

## The dead

The living come down; the dead go back up. Nobody can dig a proper grave here — the hole fills before you have finished it — so the dead are carried up the hill road on a litter and buried in the old deep ground where the soil is dry and holds what you put in it. The town pays for the carrying out of anyone, rich or poor, because otherwise half of Skelf could not be buried at all, and the same road is kept in repair by the same guild that lifts the houses. The phrase goes: *the town will carry you out but it will not carry you back.*

Some of the very old do not want to go. They have spent sixty years being lowered a step at a time and they have an idea, which they do not put into words, that being buried uphill is being buried somewhere foreign. The compromise is a willow planted over the grave and a stone on the chest, so that the ground may be held down in the same manner as the house was.

## What they say

They talk slowly and stop in the middle. To an outsider they seem rude or dim; they are neither. They are always partly listening to something else — a lip of water going over, a trench ticking, a change in the sound under the floor — and when the sound comes they will wait for it and then go on. Their sentences resume where they left off and never quite finish. A conversation at the well is punctuated by long silences that are not silences at all.

They measure time in water. The year of the low lane. The year of two floods. The year the bar came up and the willows took it. Nobody asks how old a person is; they ask what year they were born in and then nod, because they know what the ground was doing then.

They are not optimists. The phrase they use when nothing more can be done is *it'll do* — and it does not mean it is good. It means it is standing, it is holding, it will be standing next year, and next year someone can lift it a thumb's width and it will hold again.

## Leavers

The young go up the hill, most of them, for a while. Some stay up there and get dry and slow and hard to talk to, and their Skelf cousins find them dull and their hill neighbours find them odd — too interested in water, too quick to look down. But a good number come back. They say the same thing in different ways: it is too quiet up there. There is nothing doing. A returned Skelfer will lie awake on dry ground in the dark and be unable to sleep for the absence of it, and get up, and pour water into a bowl, and set it by the bed, and listen to it tick.

That is who lives in Skelf. People with soft hands and a wet cough who cannot walk on a hill, who read the ground the way other people read weather, who lift their houses an inch at a time and are buried uphill, who will tell you the land is not finished and neither are they, and who have decided — quietly, without making a fuss about it, the way they decide everything — that this will do.
