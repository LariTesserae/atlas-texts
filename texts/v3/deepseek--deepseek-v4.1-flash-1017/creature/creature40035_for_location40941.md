The ones who live in the Cradle are less a population than a habit of the place—a people who have learned to be another slow, soft force among the water and moss. You will not see many of them at once. They keep to the high walkways and the interior layers of the hive, moving in ones and twos, hooded against the drizzle, their steps placed with the care of people who know the ground is a temporary agreement. They are not tall. They are compact and long-handed, with broad feet and low centers of gravity, as if built to fold into low doorways and kneel on wet stone without slipping. Their skin has the matte sheen of things kept damp; their hair is bound back in fiber cords; their eyes are dark and patient, narrowed against mist rather than sun. Their voices are low and seldom raised, because the Cradle already roars.

They dress in layers of woven plant fiber, oiled with fungal wax and patched until the patches have their own history. Their hoods are broad and soft, their sleeves bound at the wrist so water runs off rather than in. They smell of wet flint, moss, and the faint smoky tang of the fungi they burn for light. Their hands are their most articulate feature: knuckles broad from loom-work, fingertips stained green and brown, nails kept short so they do not catch in sinew or vine. A knife of knapped stone hangs at the belt; a bone awl is tucked into a sleeve; a small scoop of hollowed wood is always within reach, because water is always doing something that needs to be redirected.

They are not crude, only narrow and deep. Their technology is a patient conversation with materials that will not stay still. They build with laminated wood bound by sinew and vine, and they begin a building by listening—pressing an ear to the stone, watching where the seep lines gather, feeling for the joints that will move. A house is not raised against the water; it is persuaded to let the water pass through its bones. Every structure has a drain-heart, a hidden cistern, a channel that carries the weeping stone’s breath away. Every family is responsible for certain seams and cracks, inherited obligations rather than property. They call themselves not builders but keepers of seams.

Their year is a schedule of leaks. There are water-keepers who know the sound of a safe cistern from a dangerous one, who can hear a hairline crack opening behind a wall and reseal it with a paste of crushed stone, clay, and moss. There are weavers whose looms thump through the damp like slow heartbeats, making fabrics from reed fiber and fungal thread. There are fungal tenders who coax the pale, pillow-sized caps in the hollows, harvesting their faint internal light and their soft, bitter flesh. Children learn the names of streams before they learn the names of stars; they are taught to step on the joints of the walkways, never to run, and to test the ground with a seep-stick before trusting it. The elderly are listened to, because they have heard the plateau shift through many small collapses and know which sounds are only the world thinking to itself.

They are reverent without being solemn. They do not worship the water, exactly; they negotiate with it. They leave a new spring a way to sing. They do not dam a stream completely, even when it would be convenient. They patch and repatch, reseal and rebuild, and they expect to be edited by erosion in return. Their songs are low, half-hummed things that match the thrum of the cisterns, and their stories are full of people who were patient enough to be spared. To live in the Cradle is to consent to being slowly rewritten.

They are not the only residents. The moss is a tenant and a crop; the fungi are lamps and livestock; small pale things live in the cisterns, blind and slow, filtering the water with their feathery mouths. A broad-winged bird circles the mist now and then, its cry a lonely punctuation in the roar. But the people are the place’s most deliberate inhabitants: damp-handed, quiet, and unhurried, a species that has made a culture out of listening to stone and answering it with careful, temporary hands.
