They call themselves something that translates poorly—closer to *the Held* than to any word for a people. Outsiders might call them the Cradleborn. They are few: a few hundred, perhaps, clustered in family knots and craft-lines along the rock river, each person known by the pitch and rhythm of their name.

They are not tall. The weight of the world above and the thickness of the air have made them short, broad, and dense, with low centers of gravity and a way of standing that looks less like waiting than like settling. Their torsos are deep and barrel-like, their limbs thick and deliberate. Their skin is warm and leathery, mottled in gray-rose and dull amber, always faintly slick with the cavern’s oily brine. Sparse, wire-like bristles grow along their forearms and scalps, more sensory than decorative. Their hands are broad-palmed and strong-fingered, with nails thick enough to scrape resin and pad-like soles that grip the spongy ground without sound. They wear little—belts of woven tendril-fiber, straps of resin-cured fungal leather, copper rings and obsidian studs that click softly when they move. The click is not accidental. It is a signature.

Their faces are wide and quiet, with large dark eyes that open wide in the dim phosphorescence and narrow to seams when a fungal pulse brightens. A second, translucent lid slides across those eyes when the air grows thick with spores. Their ears are small, but they do not rely on ears alone. They hear through their jaws, their chests, the soles of their feet. The cavern’s low hum lives in them as a bodily fact. When they speak, their voices are low and layered, carrying farther through the dense gas than a shout would above. Their language is a woven thing: tongue-clicks, lip-whistles, chest-hums, and long resonant tones that blend into the natural chorus until it is hard to tell where a person ends and the Cradle begins. A name is a chord. A greeting is a palm pressed to stone and a hum allowed to travel through it. A lie is a flat note, and everyone notices.

They are warm-blooded in a slow, banked way, their bodies burning the heavy air like embers. Their lungs are broad and deliberate; they can hold a breath for a long time and exhale it as a humid, aromatic sigh. They do not run. Running is rude and dangerous in a world of soft rock and living walls. They move in a gliding, weight-shifting walk, placing each foot as if listening through it.

They do not build so much as persuade. A dwelling begins as a fungal bulb, hollowed with obsidian blades and shaped by generations of pruning, humming, and resin-setting. The walls are living barriers of woven tendril and hardened sap; the floors are packed fibrous mat; the doors are curtains of leathery frond. A family’s history is written in the growth rings of its house and in the knots of resin tapped into the lintel. Their tools are few and cared for: cold-hammered copper awls, obsidian knives, bone-smooth wooden mallets, coils of fiber cord, pots of resin glue. They have no fire. Open flame is the one unforgivable thing, a memory of blight and collapse. They work copper cold, polish obsidian patiently, and cook fungal flesh in stone vessels warmed by buried chemical heat or by the slow decay of compost bulbs.

Their day is not a day. It is a sequence of pulses—the brightening and dimming of the cavern’s flora, the opening and closing of great fronds, the slow click of tendrils growing against rock. In those rhythms they tend luminous crops, harvest bulbous fungal flesh, weave tendril-fiber, repair resin walls, and trace the health of the great network that laces the ceiling. The work is constant but unhurried. A weaver may spend a pulse-cycle on a single cord. A shaper may hum to a young bulb for years before it becomes a room. A deep-listener may sit for hours with both palms flat against the rock, interpreting the cavern’s hum for signs of strain, blight, or slow collapse.

They are a communal people, but not a loud one. Decisions are made in listening circles, where overlapping hums gradually settle into a chord everyone can feel. Children learn to listen before they learn to speak; their first names are given as short repeated tones, and they are corrected by rhythm more often than by scolding. Elders are not rulers but resonators—people whose long familiarity with the Cradle’s pulse makes their judgment valuable. The dead are given to the fungal beds, not buried in stone, so that the community’s warmth returns to the growth that feeds it. They have poetry, law, and history, but they keep them in song-maps, resin beads, and the trained growth of living walls.

They are cautious, warm, and quietly proud. They know their world is a held breath, a warm stone hand closed around a small spark. They fear fire, blight, and the sudden cracking of the rock river. They avoid sharp noises. They do not shout. They touch stone when they are uncertain. And when the cavern hums at its lowest, they hum back—not to master it, but to answer, to say *we are here, we are listening, we are still part of the pulse.*
