The Bellows is not empty; it is *spaced*. The presences in it are scattered the way lamps are scattered in a long street at night—far apart, but enough to tell you the street is lived in. The whole basin might hold a few hundred people, if “people” is the word, and they are not gathered in villages. They live in small chords of five or twelve, each household claiming a cluster of chambers and a stretch of gallery, and between one household and the next there is often more silence than wall.

Call them the Answered, though they would not use a name that grand. They are tall and narrow, built on the proportions of the place: long arms, splayed toes, a habit of folding themselves into gaps. Their skin is the color of the inside of a stem, damp and cool, and their hair comes in fine patches like mold on bark. Their faces are mostly listening. The eyes are small and dark and patient; the ears are not flaps but long channels sunk into the skull, tuned to the low frequencies of the plant. They have a vocal sac under the jaw and a second resonance chamber in the chest, so when one of them speaks to you, you feel it in your sternum before you hear it. When they are content they hum without knowing it. When they are frightened they go still, because stillness is cheaper than a scream.

They do not build. They ask. This is the central fact of their lives. A room is widened by singing into it—not once, but over days, at the right pitch, until the walls answer by thickening outward. A room is closed by hushing it, by letting the silence press inward season after season until the chamber has become a stem again. A doorway can be opened with a chorus and narrowed with a whisper. A household’s home is therefore not a structure but a negotiated agreement between the family and the plant, and it is always being renegotiated. The children are loud on purpose; a nursery is kept shrill so it grows. The sick are kept in quiet rooms, which are slowly closing, and everyone knows it. The old learn to speak lower and lower, not because they are weak, but because a lifetime of speech has taught them exactly how much sound a wall needs.

Their economy is sound and food and silence. They eat what the Bellows grows and what lives on it: the soft scraped pulp where the grazer has passed, beetle larvae, the green water of the standing pools strained through their teeth, the pale moths when the moths are thick. They do not cook. They drink condensation by pressing their mouths to the seams of the stems and waiting for the bead to run. They sleep in the dry upper chambers, curled in membrane hammocks that are themselves grown, and they groom the seams with their hands—not to repair, because nothing is repaired here, only thickened, but to encourage. The plant polishes where it is touched. Their hands are part of the polish.

They have no writing. They have echo-memory. A message can be sung into a resonator and arrive three galleries away, an hour later, in a voice not quite the singer’s. This is useful and it is dangerous, and they have built a whole etiquette around it. You do not call a name in the deep galleries. You do not make a promise in a chamber you do not want to keep. You do not shout indoors unless you want a door. They know the Bellows by note the way a sailor knows a coast by color. They can tell the temperature by the pitch of the choral insects in the walls. They can tell whether a chamber is healthy by the way it returns a hum. They can tell that someone has been silent too long by the sound of a room getting smaller.

Some of them choose the silence. These are the Closed, and they are not dead. A person who is tired of being answered—or grieving, or devout, or simply finished with the noise of other people—will take a small chamber and stop speaking. The walls begin to thicken inward. At first the room narrows by a hand’s width a season; then the person eats less, and the plant feeds them through condensation and warmth; then the chamber is a stem, and they are inside it, breathing. You can hear them if you press your ear to the seam. The community taps to them. Sometimes they tap back. It is not quite a funeral. It is a retirement from sound. Children are told not to shout near the Closed, not because it would hurt them, but because it would open them again, and they have chosen to close.

The grazer is the other great citizen. It is low and wide and soft, with no fear in it at all, because nothing here hunts by sight and its own low hum is boring to the acoustics. It crops the walls. It is not owned and not tamed; it is a public utility with a heartbeat. Its passage keeps the thoroughfares from closing, so the Answered time their journeys to its grazing. Children ride it. Old people lean on it. When it lies down, the plant grows a shallow socket around it, and the socket becomes a bench, and the bench becomes a place where people leave messages by tapping. The grazer never hurries. It has nowhere to be that the Bellows is not already growing.

Around it and above it and inside the walls live the rest: the moth-things, pale and slow, ticking against the condensation; the gliders that run the upper galleries and never touch the floor, thin as peeled bark and shy; the beetle processions that clean the seams with a tiny clicking broom; the choral insects packed into the hollow stems by the thousand, whose drone is the Bellows’ pulse. None of these are pets. None are pests, exactly. They are neighbors in a house with very thin doors.

The only hunting is acoustic. There are things that listen—not animals exactly, or not only animals: a wrong note in a chamber, a shout that travels into a resonator and comes out somewhere else, a voice that is not yours. The Answered have a phrase for the moment when the walls begin to lean toward a sound. They say someone is *being answered*. If you are answered badly, the chamber narrows on you. You can die that way, pressed by soft green walls, not even bruised, just held until you stop. So they speak softly. They choose their words the way other people choose their tools. They know the difference between a sound that opens and a sound that closes. Some sounds are predators. Some sounds are doors.

They do not worship the Bellows. They live in it the way you live in a house that maintains itself, and they know the single law: the Bellows listens, and it answers, and it answers late, and it answers in the shape of the walls.
