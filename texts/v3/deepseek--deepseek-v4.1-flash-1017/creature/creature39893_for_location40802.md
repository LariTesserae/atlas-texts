What lives in the Basin of Held Water is, first, a sparse, damp, broad-handed people who do not think of themselves as owners of the basin so much as its keepers — and second, a fauna so settled into fixed rounds that the animals seem less to inhabit the place than to be built into it.

The people are quiet the way the place is quiet: not solemn, not hushed by rule, but speaking in voices that do not carry far against moss and turf and fog. They go about in wool and copper. Copper at the hinges, copper at the gutters, copper buttons bright on doors and at cuffs. No iron anywhere. Their hands are the hands of quarrymen and chiselers, wedge-and-mallet hands, though the stone they work does not weather under them; they cut a fresh edge and the edge stays fresh. They walk a minute between doorways. They keep sheep on the long commons and on the turf roofs. Their knees go moss-dark from kneeling at wall-joints, and their children are always wet to the ankle from the drains.

Their day begins with the fog. Doors open on copper hinges that have dulled to exactly the same patina they had a generation ago. The morning work is inspection — not repair, because nothing needs repairing, but replacement, because wood is the one thing here that ages. A household’s standing can be read off its lintel. A new lintel is a small civic event. A dark, soft, sagging one is a clock that has nearly run out. Beams, doors, cart-shafts: these are the only true clocks in the basin, and the town’s whole economy and sense of time turn on them. They quarry stone from a wall or a quarry face and leave a clean socket behind, as sharp as the day the stone was first lifted. They rebuild in the same footprint, generation after generation, so that the town is a hundred rebuildings stacked side by side rather than in depth.

They are not idle, and they are not stagnant. They terrace the slopes and bank the streams and walk the stair-streets. They tend the orchard terraces and cut the wet meadows. They fish by the frog choruses — setting their hours by which chorus is singing — and they wade the shallow clear rivers that run over clean stone with no sand in them. They fill the cisterns from the condensation channels and the walled rain column. They keep the covered aqueduct of dry-laid slabs, though keeping it mostly means walking its length and seeing that it is still exactly itself. The moss does not climb higher on the wall. The seedlings come up in their same slots. The wind leans the orchard trees all one way, and the people have learned to live inside that lean.

They do not experience the held-ness of the world as a curse. It is a floor under their feet. No famine-drift, no flood-scar, no soil washing down. When they need stone, they take it; what they leave behind is as clean as what they take. They know that the water runs but does not carve, that the rain column falls in its fixed ten paces, that the green holds its breath. They know almost nobody says it plainly. They are a people with very little ruin in their landscape and very little progress in their plan — and they are busy, and skilled, and exact.

Around them the animals keep their fixed rounds. Small dark deer move through the orchard terraces. Marmots sit on every wall like small installed ornaments. Cranes come in to the meadow in long lines; herons stand in the pools so long they become furniture. Swifts and martins nest in every gap in the masonry, and a chough with a copper bell on its leg is treated as a sort of civic official. The hovering flies hang in their little clouds, not drifting, not swarming, just held. Children catch them in jars and let them out again, and they return to the same air.

And the people themselves are the only things in the valley visibly spending themselves. A chimney puts up a thin line of smoke; the one wind takes it; it goes the way all smoke goes here, over the raw bright ridge and out of sight. Every household is a small clock burning down, and its lintel is the dial. They live inside a piece of music stopped on a held chord — listening, always, for the next piece of wood that needs replacing.
