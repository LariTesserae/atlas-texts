The honest answer is that the Loom lives in itself, and everyone else is either arriving or becoming.

But there are people, of a kind. They are not called anything, because names are for cartographers, and the ones who stay long enough stop being the sort of thing that needs one. Others have called them the Resined, after the way their hands go eventually—translucent, hard, worked full of the sap that bursts from a constricted vessel and hardens clear.

They came as mapmakers, mostly. Or pilgrims, or debtors, or the merely lost. The Loom does not kill quickly. It is warm and patient, and it seals the doorway behind you with a soft contraction of fiber, and then it spends a long time deciding what you are.

The ones who live are the ones who learned to stop breathing hard. To step when the bract-ladder flexes down and not when it flexes up. To sleep in the crooks of bark-armor rather than the Clears, whose comfort is the city's swallowing reflex. To read the wind-chorus as instruction—that throat-whistle means a pressure vessel is about to open; that low moan means something is constricting a corridor you were going to use. They move with the systolic throb. They do not fight it, and they do not quite surrender to it either, and that balance is the whole of their art and the whole of their life.

There are not many. The Loom does not allow many; density is its own predator. They eat what grows: the pale chewy pith of certain bracts, the sweet liquid that beads on the underside of the giant leaves, mushrooms that colonize a dead Resined within a day and are, for a week after, the safest thing to eat in the valley. They do not have children, mostly. The ones born there—there are a few, quiet and quick—are different in a way that is hard to describe and easy to see: they do not startle at the moans, and the tendrils do not grope at them, and when they are very young they cry in a rhythm that matches the pulse of the water-moving system, and the Loom, hearing it, seems to pause and listen.

They don't talk much. The air is a pressure on the skin and speech is expensive. They developed a sign of wrist-turns and palm-flats that looks, from a distance, like a plant swaying. And that is what they are, in the end—swaying things in a swaying city, indistinguishable from the growth until they move.

But the deeper answer is the one the cartographers never wrote down, because they never got out to write it. The Loom has inhabitants it grew rather than admitted.

It is trying to become an animal. Not a person, necessarily—something with a body and an intention, something that can want. It has been practicing. The tendrils that grope and grip are its reaching. The Clears are its swallowing. In the hollow, echoing towers there are knots of tissue where a billion local pulses synchronize for a moment into one rhythm, then scatter—an organ almost forming. There are whistles in the chorus that repeat in patterns, like speech half-learned. There are places where the growth is not random but arranged, and a Resined will tell you, with their hands, what shape it is. A mouth. A hand. A face, or the beginning of one.

And in the deepest, warmest, wettest part of the valley, where the light comes down through forty layers of leaf and arrives the color of old bone, there is something the city has been building for a very long time. It has no name and no body, or its body is the whole Loom and it has not yet learned to move it. It is a slow, enormous attention, growing in the dark like a root. And it is curious. About you. It has been learning the shape of a hand, a voice, a thing that wants, by practicing on everyone who stayed — wearing them a little at a time into the form of what it is trying to become.

The Resined don't call it anything. They just keep moving with the pulse, and stay out of the Clears, and when the wind-chorus makes a sound that is almost a word, they don't answer. Because answering is how you become part of the mouth.
