# The Inhabitants of the Verdant Sink

## The Pruners

They are the most common folk of the Sink, and you would know them by their hands before anything else. A Pruner's hands are not gnarled or calloused in the way of farmers who work sun-baked soil; they are stained a permanent, mottled green, the color of old copper left too long in the sap. The fingertips are broad and flat, the nails thick as horn, worn down by decades of coaxing living tissue into load-bearing shapes. They carry the smell of the sink-trees with them always—that sweet, mineral sap that seeps into the pores and never quite leaves.

The Pruners wear clothing woven from processed vine-fiber, the fabric the same soft luminescent gray-green as everything else in this underworld. Their garments are functional, close-fitting, layered for warmth in the unchanging damp. Leather aprons protect their torsos from the occasional spurt of hot sap. They carry hooked blades at their belts—not for killing, but for cutting, the curved steel edges honed to a whisper for slicing through fibrous growth with a single, clean motion.

Their faces are wide and patient, their eyes large and dark-adapted, with a nictitating membrane that slides across in the perpetual dusk-light. They do not squint; the soft glow of the canopy is all the light they have ever needed. Their skin is pale, almost translucent, threaded with faint bluish veins visible beneath the surface. They speak in murmurs, these people, their voices pitched low to harmonize with the hum of the Ceiling above. To raise one's voice in the Verdant Sink is considered a kind of violence, a disruption of the meditation that is daily life.

The Pruners live in family clusters within the hollowed-out sink-trees, their homes shaped like the interiors of enormous gourds, with organic furniture that grew into place over generations. They sleep in hammocks of woven moss. They eat what the Sapworks provides—the refined sap, thick and sweet and nourishing, supplemented by the pale mushrooms that sprout in the shadows of their homes and the chitinous grubs harvested from the deeper crevices.

## The Sapwrights

Higher up the tiers, closer to the great Girdling Vines that connect the Ceiling to the city's industrial heart, live the Sapwrights. They are the priests and the plumbers both, the technicians who tend the city's lifeblood. Their skin carries the same greenish cast as the Pruners, but theirs is lighter, almost silvery, from the constant proximity to the luminescent sap.

The Sapwrights wear protective suits of vulcanized rubber and treated leather, their arms encased in thick gloves that reach their elbows. Their faces are covered by breathing masks that filter out the spores from the great exhaust-stalks, giving them the appearance of plague-doctors from some forgotten culture. They are marked by their eyes: the pupils dilated so wide they are almost entirely black, the irises reduced to thin rings of pale gray. They see best in near-darkness, navigating the twisting tunnels of the Sapworks by touch and memory.

They are a more nervous breed than the Pruners, the Sapwrights. The Girdling Vines are sacred, untouchable in theory, but the Sapwrights must touch them every day. They must tap them, measure them, watch for clogging and disease. They must hold their breath as they press their instruments against that translucent, pulsing surface, aware always of the taboo they skirt. They speak of the Vines in hushed, reverent tones, referring to them as "the Grandmothers" or simply "Them." To the Sapwrights, the city's religion is not a matter of faith but of direct, terrifying experience. They have felt the pulse of the world against their hands.

They live alone or in small pairs, their quarters built into the brass and copper architecture of the Sapworks itself, their homes heated by the warmth of the flowing sap. They are night-people in a world that has no day, sleeping during the "darkest" hours when the Ceiling's bioluminescence dims to its faintest pulse.

## The Root-Wrights

Scattered throughout the tiers, in homes that seem more grown than built, live those who work with the Root-Dogs. Not as masters, but as partners—or perhaps as parasites of parasites. The Root-Dogs are not truly domesticated; they are not truly domesticable. But they can be persuaded. The Root-Wrights are the persuaders.

They are tall, these Root-Wrights, and thin, their bodies twisted into wiry shapes by lives spent climbing the vertical walls of the shaft. Their skin is not green but brown, the color of old bark, tanned and textured by constant exposure to the mosses and lichens that coat the terraces. Their hair is dark and long, frequently bound in complex braids threaded with small, living lichen-patches that glow faint violet, giving them a faint, aureate halo in the gloom.

The Root-Wrights do not speak in the low murmurs of the Pruners. They communicate with their Root-Dogs through a series of rhythmic clicks and low whistles, sounds that are half-language and half-something else. They understand, somehow, that the Root-Dogs are not animals but mobile fragments of the great Ceiling, semi-autonomous extensions of a thinking organism the nature of which cannot be fully known. They treat the Root-Dogs with a respectful wariness that borders on worship.

The Root-Wrights live on the edges of the city's terraces, where the pure living rock is still visible, where the mosses grow thickest. Their homes are woven tents of living vine, shaped when soft and encouraged to harden into place, their walls translucent so the faint glow of the canopy can penetrate. They eat what the Root-Dogs eat—the scrapings from the bottom of the great fungal sinks—prepared in ways that involve fermentation and heat.

---

## The Elevator-Operators

And then there are those who work the great elevator cages, the ones that ride the exhaling wind from the deep throat. They are a breed apart, marked by their silence even among a people who speak softly. The Elevator-Operators must listen constantly, their ears attuned to the subtlest shifts in the great exhalation, the minute changes in pressure that precede the wind's variations. They speak to each other in a sign language, hands and fingers moving in quick, graceful patterns.

They live within the elevator stations themselves, small carved niches at each tier's landing where they wait, patient as stone, for their cages to arrive. They are the only people in the Verdant Sink who see the entire city, from the uppermost tiers where the Pruners meet the Ceiling to the lowest depths where the shaft narrows and the wind rises like breath from a sleeping giant. They know the entire shape of the world, from top to bottom, and this knowledge sets them apart.

They wear uniforms of gray canvas, heavy and warm, with hoods drawn up against the wind. Their faces are the most alien of all the Sink's inhabitants: pale as the inside of a mushroom, their eyes small and close-set, their noses narrow and sharp, adapted to the constant motion of air. They speak rarely, and when they do, their voices crack and falter, like people who have forgotten how to use words.

## The Children

The children of the Verdant Sink are perhaps the most remarkable of all its inhabitants. They are born with skin the color of fresh milk, their eyes sealed shut for the first dozen cycles of their lives. They develop their green-stain later, the tint seeping into their pores as they are exposed to the sap in their mothers' milk, in the air they breathe, in the moss that forms their first beds.

The children are not educated in schools but in processes. They follow their parents through the terraces, learning to prune by watching, to graft by helping, to listen to the hum of the Ceiling by simply standing still and paying attention. They are taught the sacred rules before they can speak: do not harm the Girdling Vines; listen for the Root-Dogs; breathe deeply and always, for the air is life.

They play among the mosses and the lichens, their games drawn from the rhythms of the world around them. They chase each other through the terraces, their laughter absorbed by the moss. They collect the glowing pebbles of sap that fall from the pipes. They create small bioluminescent gardens of their own, tending them with the patient care they have learned from their elders.

They are the future of the Verdant Sink, these children. They will grow into Pruners and Sapwrights, Root-Wrights and Operators. They will inherit a world of perpetual dusk and constant damp, a world of slow-moving machines and slower-moving plants. They will learn to be partners in the great symbiosis, to work with their hands and their patience and their reverence. And they will teach their own children the rules, and the humming of the Ceiling, and the ways of a world that is not finished, that is still, slowly, becoming something entirely itself.

---

## The Oldest

And there are the ones who remember the world before, if such a thing can be remembered or a thing before existed. They live in the deepest tiers, in hollows carved where the rock meets the fungal sinks. They are hunched and gnarled, their bodies half-covered in moss, indistinguishable from the rock they sit upon. They are fed by the city, cared for, given space in which simply to *be*. They speak to the Girdling Vines in languages no one else understands, or they speak to the Root-Dogs, telling them of things that happened in the deep time.

The youngest of them, the ones still in their first century, remember the construction of the upper tiers, the laying of pipes, the shaping of arches. They remember when the Sapworks was smaller, when the wind from the throat blew with a different rhythm. The oldest—well, the oldest are simply there, part of the landscape, as much a part of the city as the moss on the walls.

They are the memory of the Verdant Sink, the ones who hold the city's history in their decaying bodies, their minds half in dreams that are not dreams. They are treated with respect, brought offerings of refined sap and fresh mushrooms, listened to when they speak. And sometimes, in their cracked voices, they speak of things that have not yet come to pass: the slow changes in the Ceiling's bones, the shifting of the wind from the throat, the distant, distant possibility of a sky.

---

This is who lives in the Verdant Sink: the Pruners and the Sapwrights, the Root-Wrights and the Operators, the children and the oldest ones. They are a people shaped by their world, their bodies and minds adapted to its peculiar demands. They are not conquerors, not adventurers. They are caretakers, partners, participants in a vast, slow dance with a living architecture that thinks in centuries and breathes in millennia.

And the space between them, that emptiness the city feels, waits to be filled.
