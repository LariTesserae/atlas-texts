No person lives on Corveth’s Spire. That is the first true thing. The cairn is a place to stand, not to sleep; the rings at the notch are old; there is no path and no shelter. But something keeps house in the basin, and the frogs know it. They have no name for it that survives. I will call it the Bell, because its voice is the deep wet percussion under their chorus.

The Bell lives in the deepest pool, the one the fen-lilies cover last. It is an amphibian the size of a collapsed rowan, low and broad, built for a place where height is dangerous. There is no upright line in it. Its skull is flat as a spade; its limbs are too small to carry it far; its tail is thick and eel-like and screws it down into the silt when the wind comes over the rim. Its skin is the color of the standing water—amber-dark, iron-mottled—and moss grows on its back in patches, not as decoration but because it is slow enough. In a few seams of its hide, lily pads have rooted. It does not mind. It carries a little of the marsh on its back.

Its eyes are small and clouded. It does not hunt by sight. It feels the basin through water: the slap of a foot in the shallows, the suck of moss, the frog-chorus changing pitch when something moves. Its mouth is wide and lipless. Its throat is a drum. When it sounds—usually in the wet dusk, sometimes before rain—the note comes up through the water first, a throb you feel in your ankles, then breaks the surface as a low, wet, percussive boom. The frogs do not stop for it. They answer, or they simply continue, and for a moment the whole basin is one instrument.

It does not leave the pool. It has no reason to. It is the basin’s stomach and its slow bell. It eats frogs, eels, the small web-footed swimmer, anything that dies and drifts into the deep water. It is not cruel; it is a mouth in a closed circuit. The dead float, the lilies bloom, the moss holds the water, the water holds the Bell. It is old enough that the stone lip of the basin seems to have grown lower around it, or it has grown lower with the stone. It was here before the cairn. The cairn, I think, was built by people who heard it and wanted a dry place to stand while they listened.

It is not a god. It is not a guardian. It has no interest in the iron rings at the notch or the rotted fish-trap. It has perhaps eaten a visitor who waded too deep and lay still too long, but it would not have known the difference between that and a drowned eel. The marsh folds everything in eventually. The Bell is simply the part of the marsh that is awake and has a voice.

If you want to find it, do not look for a monster. Stand at the cairn at dusk, when the lilies open and the swifts pour out and the condensation hangs at your shoulders. Wait for the frogs to go thin for half a breath—not silent, never silent, just thinner—and listen for the note from the deeper marsh. That is it. That is the one who lives here. It is down there under the amber water and the lily pads, moss on its back, throat full of the next sound, being exactly what the place needs it to be.
