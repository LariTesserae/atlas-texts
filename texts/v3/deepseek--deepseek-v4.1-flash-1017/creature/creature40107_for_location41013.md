What lives on the Plateau of the Weeping Rooted is not a people in the ordinary sense. There are no villages, no fields, no roads between the damp hollows. The true inhabitants are a composite: a scattered archipelago of pocket ecologies, each one crowded and intimate, linked by drifting spores, insect migrations, and the slow, warm exhalation of ancient things.

The namesake residents are the Rooted themselves—colossal, root-like bodies that arch over the hollows and drip condensation from their undersides. They are not quite trees, not quite machines, not quite fossils. They are alive in the way a limestone cave is alive: slowly, mineralogically, with a pulse you feel in your teeth more than hear. Their surfaces are warm and pearlescent where they break the soil, and their “weeping” is not grief but irrigation—a constant, gentle rain that keeps the moss and fronds below them from ever fully drying. If they think, they think at the speed of stalactites. If they move, it is by swelling a millimeter over a century. They are the plateau’s slow heart, and every pocket of life is arranged around their seep.

Within those pockets live the frantic, miniature citizens. Clouds of iridescent beetles move between the oases like living smoke, their hum a bass note under everything. They are pollinators, scavengers, and food in equal measure; their larvae squirm in the iridescent mud of the warm springs. Jewel-feathered fliers dart through the root arches on needle-sharp proboscises, territorial and brilliant, crying out at the edges of each grove. They are the top predators of their tiny world—wasps and hummingbirds collapsed into one bright, aggressive shape. Beneath them, in every damp crevice, amphibians croak and whistle, and translucent crawling things mimic leaves so well that only their slow, deliberate wrongness gives them away. The air is thick with insects; the ground is thick with hidden mouths.

The vegetation is inhabitant as much as habitat. Blue-green fern fans crowd the hollows. Phosphorescent fungi the size of dinner plates bloom in frantic pulses after each warm downpour, releasing spore clouds that drift to the next pocket on the faint breezes. Vines with oily, leathery leaves climb the Rooted and the old artifacts alike, drinking the condensation. None of it sprawls. It erupts in nodes, then stops, contained by the dry stony sea between.

And there are the old ones—the artifacts. A curving wall of fused, pearlescent bone. A field of standing stones covered in script that rearranges itself when you look away. They are not alive, exactly, but they are inhabitants in the way a hearth is: they hold the warmth, wick moisture from deep rock, and release it as mist. They hum below hearing. They dream in mineral. The creatures of the plateau have built their lives around these quiet landlords without ever knowing them as makers. The makers are gone; the machines remain, still watering the garden.

So the plateau is inhabited by no single “who.” It is inhabited by a crowded, cyclical miniature biosphere—beetles and fliers, frogs and leaf-mimics, ferns and spore-blooms—all threaded through the weeping of rooted, ancient things and the slow, osmotic breathing of machines that have become geology. To live there is to be small, damp, localized, and synchronized to the next pulse of mist. The resident of the Plateau of the Weeping Rooted is the pocket itself: a composite organism of life and leftover technology, keeping itself alive in the ruins of a future that has already passed.
