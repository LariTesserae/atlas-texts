The grove has a tenant, and you have already met its handwriting. It is the three-fingered print on the north wall, the ledge worn smooth on the eastern side, the arc of flat stones around the sump, the smooth back that surfaces once and does not show itself again. Call it the Sump-Keeper if you need a name. It does not.

It is amphibious and unhurried, built low and long, with a back like a wet stone and skin the color of drowned wood. When it folds itself into the moss hollow it seems no bigger than a curled child; when it stretches out in the water it is longer than your forearm, and the duckweed closes over it without a sound. Its hands are broad and soft-palmed, with three fingers and a thumb that it can press flat enough to cling to vertical rock. The ochre print was not paint. It was the stain of that palm, pressed to the wall when the water reached a certain line, or the light changed, or it simply decided to leave a mark. The moss has almost taken it. The hand is still under there.

It breathes slowly, through throat and skin, and it can stay down in the sump for a long time without needing to show itself. When it rises, it does so at the edge, where the liverwort is thickest, and it watches. Its eyes are wide-set and reflect the fungi — two small lights among the hundred small lights. It does not croak. It does not scatter the beetles. It moves in single, deliberate increments, like something that has learned that haste is a waste of heat.

It lives by the grove’s clock. After every rain it visits the arc of stones and nudges one a finger’s width, not to build anything, only to keep count of the water. It sleeps in the hollow in the moss, curled so its breath barely disturbs the fern curtains. It feeds on the pale shelf-fungus and the fallen, sweetly rotten fruit, and sometimes on beetles that wander too close to the sump, though it does not hunt them. It waits for them the way it waits for everything. On the ledge it sits in the hour when the waxy flowers open, and its throat pulses, and it watches the coin of light above shift from silver to gold to blue. It has been doing this for a long time. The ledge knows the shape of it.

It is not cruel, and it is not kind. It is resident. It notices you — the ripples without cause, the stillness that is not empty — and it waits for you to climb out. It has seen others come down the root-ladder. It has watched them stand too long and breathe too loudly. It does not need to drive them away. The heat and the moss and the patient water do that.

Around it live the beetles in their slow processions, the frogs with toes like starfish, the fungi that glow just enough to know they are there. They are citizens of the grove. The Sump-Keeper is the one the grove has been used around. It is the oldest habit of the place, warm and wet and patient, and it can wait longer.
