What lives there is a keeper, and it is almost entirely a way of paying attention.

It is not the frogs, though it speaks through them. It is not the moss, though it wears it. It is one old animal — one of the few hundred kilograms of animal life on the summit, though good luck finding the rest of it — and it is the only thing up there that has intention.

It is small, as such things go: the size of a crouched person, or a large dog, or a folded coat, depending on how it is holding itself. It is low and soft and pear-shaped, with a mantle of pale moss that it does not shed. Under the moss is a shell, or a scab, of the same cream travertine as the spring-basins, built up in slow rings. It is warm. It is always warm. If you put your hand on it, it is like touching a stone that has been in the sun, except there is no sun.

It has no eyes to meet. Where a face would be there is a puckered siphon and a fringe of wet tendrils, pale as the ferns, which it uses to taste the air. It hears through the floor. It knows you are there by the way the water changes its note in the basins and by the interruption your feet make in the moss. It is not alarmed. Alarm is fast, and it has not been fast for a very long time.

It lives in one of the hollow growths — the person-sized ones that gurgle — and it is not clear whether it grew the growth or moved into it. It breathes through it. The whole summit is its lung, and it filters the air for itself, which is why the air is breathable for you. It drinks the water that condenses on its back. It eats little: insects come to its warmth, and it takes them with the siphon. It does not sleep, or it sleeps all the time; there is no difference.

The fern-movement is it, or a limb of it. You will never see more than the ferns closing behind it. It moves at the pace of a spring building a rim — not walking, exactly, but thickening forward, pouring its weight from one place to the next, leaving no trail because the moss closes. It has been there since the mountain was taller. It has watched the rain wear the summit down to a dome. It is not wise in any way you would recognize. It is finished. It has found its resting state and is resting in it.

It keeps the ring of nine posts. It does not worship there. It sits in the middle of the clean ground and listens. The frogs go quiet because the resident is using them as ears; it is listening with the whole dome. If you stand in the ring, it listens to you too — your blood, your breathing, the water in your body. It is not a threat. It is a scale. When it is done, it lets the frogs start again.

It thinks by warming the ovals. It picks one up, holds it until it is warm, and then lets it cool. The warming is a question; the cooling is the answer. It has been asking the same questions for centuries. It has not decided against you yet. It maintains the lattice walls, though they keep the rain off nothing. It runs its tendrils over the hundred thousand fine threads and the threads ring, and the ringing is a kind of memory. It walks the line of three walls and checks them. It is not looking at anything. It is checking.

It is not friendly. It is not hostile. It is domestic in a way that has nothing to do with you. It is the landlord of a very old, very wet apartment, and the frogs are its tenants, and the water is its clock. It does not want anything from you. If you sit still long enough, it may come and warm an oval in your hand. That is the whole of its welcome. It has been considering for a long time, and it will go on considering after you are gone.
