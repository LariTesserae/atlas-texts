Call them the Tenders, though they have no name for themselves that a human throat could shape. They are what lives there now—not ghosts, not survivors in the ordinary sense, but a small colony of people who have been slowly edited by cold, pressure, and the long hum of the engine until they fit the drowned city the way a mollusk fits its shell.

There are perhaps eleven of them. They are tall and attenuated, but not frail—buoyancy has replaced gravity in the architecture of their bodies, so they move with a slow, deliberate unfolding, like things that have learned to be heavy in water without ever being fast. Their skin is the color of the pale algae: wet chalk, faintly luminous at the joints and along the spine. Their eyes are cloudy, mostly closed, useful now only for distinguishing the weakest blue-gray gradients; they navigate by pressure and echo instead, by the subtle temperature of currents, by the vibration of the turbine traveling through bone. Along their flanks are fine, torn-lace folds—not gills exactly, but something older and more patient, a vascular fringe that drinks oxygen from the water at a rate their slow hearts can afford. Their hands are long, many-knuckled, webbed to the second joint, and they pull themselves through the labyrinth by handrails and cables and the soft lips of broken windows, never hurried.

They live close to the engine. Their sleeping chamber is a warm outflow hall where the water tastes of rust and heat and the crustacean carpet glows colorless on the stone. They wear almost nothing—scraps of salvaged strap, a cracked faceplate one of them keeps as a kind of mask or heirloom, a coil of wire wound around a wrist. They eat the blind crustaceans scraped from the hot rock, and the pale anemone polyps when the bloom is thick, and the mineral scum itself in lean times. They do not cook. They do not need much. Their voices are clicks and low subsonic hums; a question is a rising vibration in the water, assent is two taps on stone, grief is a long silence held until the engine's rhythm fills it again.

They tend the machine without understanding it. They clear silt from the turbine's intake. They coax the algae away from the few surviving sensors. They patch cables with a paste of crushed shell and mineral bloom. They do not know what electricity is, but they know the pattern of the stuttering lamps, and they know when a corridor has gone wrong by the way the hum changes in their teeth. They call the engine the Heart, or the Sleeper, or sometimes just the Low Sound. Their calendar is its rhythm and the slow cycles of the algae. Their map of the city is a map of pressures—where the water is warm, where the Long Fall pours endlessly from the ruptured reservoir, where a collapsed roof makes a pocket of stillness that smells of old chalk.

They are not sad. They have no memory of dry air except as a fever dream told in vibrations. The ruined towers are not ruins to them; they are the bones of a great shell, and they are the small soft thing that lives in it. They are quiet, patient, strange—the city's slow metabolism, its caretakers, its residents. The blind eels slide past them without fear. The anemones open in their wake. And deep in the dome of cracked glass, the engine turns, and the Tenders turn with it, keeping the heart beating for a world that will never wake up.
