What lives in the Kettle is not a population so much as a crowding: a tenancy of specialists, opportunists, and patient leftovers, all organized by heat and noise.

There are no people there. No settlement, no path worn by feet, no one tending the strange hardware up-valley. The closest thing to a human presence is the machinery itself — and the absent makers it implies. The masts, cables, humming shells, and half-buried nodes are not visitors and not quite tools anymore. They live there the way a barnacle lives on a hull: self-maintaining, recalculating, slowly being swallowed. They are the most patient residents in the valley, and they are losing.

The warm side is the crowded side. Along the fault, where the water comes up hot, the moss is a deep mattress of green that does not need sunlight because the ground feeds it and the fog waters it. It grows in generations, old layers holding up new ones, saturated and dripping, sprouting pale non-flowers. Insects rise off the warm pools in columns and are shredded by spray. Frogs live in the hot water and make the only animal sound that can stand against the river — a pulsing chorus that stops abruptly, so that when it stops you notice the water has changed pitch, and you understand that a bank has gone somewhere upstream. Under the moss there are small mammals in numbers, unseen, moving through a felted dark. In the warm water there are things that should not be comfortable at thirty-five degrees and are.

The cold side is quieter and clearer. Two hundred metres out of the warm water’s reach, the pools have ice rims and the gravel is frozen into a crust. The water there is so clear you can watch a fish turn over. The cold-side residents are fewer, sharper, more exposed: fish in the pools, small things under stones, plants that have learned to grow low and sideways. You can walk from summer to winter and back in a minute, and the populations change with you.

The birds work the waterfall margins — dippers, wagtails, small dark quick things. They have given up on song. Song is useless when the water is deafening, so they signal with wing-flashes and with calls pitched low enough to be felt in the sternum. They are residents of the spray, not of the valley floor. They live in the noise-shadow of the falls, where the roar drops just enough to be survivable.

The plants are residents too, in their slow way. Nothing tall has had time, and nothing tall would stand. The banks undercut themselves; everything rooted is leaning, clutching, growing on other plants. Ferns go up an arm in one season. Liverworts sheet over the warm rock. Pale grasses and a crouched scrub of willow and something like birch grow permanently away from the water, never above head height. The valley floor is felted with moss three layers deep in places. Step off the stone and you sink into it.

If the Kettle has a landlord, it is the fault; if it has a pulse, it is the river. The valley does not hold a shape, and its inhabitants are adapted to that. They live by sound-shadow and touch. A shout is inaudible; a footstep is nothing; the only way to be heard is to touch. Everything you carry becomes soaked and then cold, and the warm water is the only thing standing between you and a quiet loss of heat. The residents know this. The frogs know it. The moss knows it. The machines, still humming under slumped gravel, still holding themselves upright, have recalculated and intend to keep recalculating.

So: what lives there is a loud, packed, heat-split community of small animals, low plants, invisible mammals, spray-margin birds, and self-repairing hardware from somewhere further along. They live in contact, in fog, in a place that is still being made. Nothing there is settled. Everything is early.
