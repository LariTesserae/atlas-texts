The Sill is inhabited by listeners. Almost nothing here makes a living by sight. The light is too even, too useless, and so the population is organized around sound the way a forest is organized around light: things live in the roar, under it, against it, or in the holes where it fails.

The most numerous are the grazers — flat coiled things the size of dinner plates, shelled in silica, clamped to the stone in loose crowds. They are not fast and they are not clever, but they are patient. Their mouths work like whetstones, and they leave behind a polish so perfect you can see the gray sky in the rock. You hear them before you see them: hundreds of small grindings layered into the plain’s bass roar. They are the Sill’s slow carpenters, always shaving, always smoothing, always making the place a little more like itself.

Above them, riding the film of water, go the glass thimbles. They are small and transparent and they travel in rafts, knocking together with a tick-tick-tick that sounds like rain on a jar. They are fragile in a way that seems reckless until you understand they are not individuals so much as a loose agreement — a flotilla that keeps its own company and lets the current decide. When the wind gets up they crowd into the lee of a groove and wait it out, a hundred tiny chimes pressed together.

The potholes belong to the clickers. Blind, pale, soft-bodied things that live in the old green-black water and speak in snaps. Each pothole is a small city with its own dialect. The clickers do not leave. They do not need to. The water in their holes is older than the water going past, and the clickers have been in it long enough that their conversations have the sound of something being repeated until it becomes a law. If you put your ear near the stone you can hear them answering each other, a code of little blows, and you cannot tell whether it is argument or gossip or counting.

Then there are the boom-things, which are the closest the Sill has to weather. They are soft, hollow animals, bloated with gas, drifting in the shallows like bags of breath. When the water rises they swell and sing; when it falls they go silent. Their note is low enough that you feel it in your chest before you hear it, and when the whole plain of them takes up the same note at dusk, the stone gives it back a half-step lower. They are not dangerous. They are not tame. They are the plain’s mood made audible, and everyone else lives inside their decisions.

And then there is the Quiet.

It is a wader — tall, thin, jointed, the only thing here that moves through the acoustic world without belonging to it. It is not silent because it is careful. It is silent because it is wrong. It is a hole in the sound, a moving pocket of nothing, and the grazers cannot hear it coming because there is nothing to hear. You learn to watch the plain instead of listen to it: a patch of rasping stops, then another, then another, and the silence moves. The Quiet is not evil. It is simply the one resident that lives in the gap rather than the roar, and everything else has learned to treat the gap as a kind of teeth.

The people are the least numerous and the most temporary. A few families follow the shallow water with sledges of bone and lashed hide, never staying long enough to wear a path. They are lean and deliberate and they walk with their hands on their loads. They know the plain by its sound-patterns: a family’s stretch is the stretch whose noise is familiar, where a stranger’s footfall stands out three hundred yards off. They do not shout. Two people with something to say press jaw to jaw and speak into the bone, which is an intimate way to argue and a practical way to be heard.

They leave little. A line of knee-high cairns. A weir of stacked stone, softened by water until it looks like something the rock did to itself. A hut on stilts, roof fallen in, posts still standing in their sockets. They are not settlers. They are passers-through who keep the old promises because the stone keeps everything.

What lives on the Sill, then, is not one thing but a society of attentions. The grazers attend to the stone. The thimbles attend to the current. The clickers attend to their old water. The boom-things attend to depth. The Quiet attends to the spaces between sounds. The people attend to all of it, and to each other, jaw against jaw. Nothing here is large, nothing is soft for long, and nothing is forgotten. The plain is loud enough to drown a voice, so everyone has learned to live in the parts of the noise that belong to them.
