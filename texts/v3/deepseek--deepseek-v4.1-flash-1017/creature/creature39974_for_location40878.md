The one who lives there is not the room. The room is the shell it has been given.

It lives behind the low wall, in the gap where the stacked stones do not quite meet the stone behind them. It is long and low and pale—the color of the mineral threads before they have decided to be stone. Laid flat, it might be mistaken for a fold of the floor, or a root that has come in from somewhere warmer. It has no eyes. It has never needed them; darkness here is not a lack but a medium, and its whole skin is a slow instrument for heat and pressure. Its head is a blunt wedge, wet at the tip. Its back is segmented in soft cushions, each one beaded with condensation, each edge fringed with salt-fine crystal. The crystal does not pierce it. It grows on it the way the room grows on everything: patiently, without violence, taking it into the keeping.

It has more legs than you would like. The number is not fixed in your mind because it does not hold still to be counted—not because it hurries, but because its stillness is made of many small adjustments. A leg lifts, is placed, is forgotten. Another lifts. The wave passes from head to tail and back again, so slowly that you can watch a single foot for minutes and see only a bead of water gather beneath it. It moves once a day, perhaps. The movement is not a journey. It shifts a hand’s breadth from one hollow to another, settles, and lets the new hollow warm to it.

It does not eat. It takes the wet from the stone through the pads of its feet; it lets the mineral settle in the seams of its back; it breathes the warm fog and gives it back unchanged. Hunger is an outside thing, a clock set by scarcity. Here there is no scarcity, only sufficiency, and so the tenant has no appetite in the way a mouth has an appetite. It has a mouth, or something like one, but it is not for you. It is for the pale threads on the ceiling, which it grazes only when the growth has thickened beyond touching. It is a gardener in the sense that a slow flood is a gardener: it does not intend. It only presses.

It knows people by their heat and their breath and the cool leak they bring in with them. It has known many, one at a time, and it does not remember them. Memory is a kind of erosion, and this room does not erode. But it has been shaped by them. There is a hollow in the stone behind the wall where a hand has rested often enough to press a warmth into it. There is a place on its back where a forehead once touched, and the crystal there is thinner. It does not know what a forehead is. It knows pressure, and the pressure has a shape, and the shape has stayed.

The objects the people left are not offerings to it. It does not guard them. It has lain against the clay bowls and the knotted cords and the dark scraper; it has learned their coolness, their edges, the small dead weight of them. If one were taken, it would notice the change in the air’s shape—a tooth missing from a comb. It would not be angry. Anger requires a future. It would simply adjust, over a long time, to the absence.

If you reach behind the low wall, it will let you. It may not move at all. Your fingers will find it warm and wet and giving, like the inside of a lip, and then one foot will settle on your knuckle—lighter than a drop, lighter than an eyelash. It will hold it there as long as you can be still. This is not a blessing or a warning. It is the room touching you back. Then the foot will lift, and you will not be sure it was ever there.

It is not old in the way a person is old. It is old as the wall is old, as the warm hollow is old: by having been in one place while the place grew around it. Its oldest segments are clouded and thick, half-taken into the stone. In another age it will be a lump behind the wall, and then a vein in the wall, and then part of the wall’s slow idea of itself. It will not die. It will be kept.

And when you leave, it will not watch you go, because it has no eyes, and because it does not watch. But after the fissure has taken your coolness back, it will move one leg—once—into the warm place where your hand was.
