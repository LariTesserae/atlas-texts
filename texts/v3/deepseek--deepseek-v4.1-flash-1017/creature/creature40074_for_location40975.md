The ones who live here call themselves the Thren, though “call” is too sharp a word. The name is a chord—a low, woody pressure struck through the floor by a broad footpad and answered in the throat. To human ears it would be less a name than the sound of a distant door closing in a deep place.

They are tall and attenuated, standing perhaps three meters at their full height but rarely standing fully upright. Their bodies have the look of something grown rather than born: a narrow central trunk of pale, fibrous pith, plated over with overlapping scales like slate, jointed in six places. Two long legs carry them in slow, deliberate strides; four finer arms unfold from the chest and shoulders, ending in splayed digits that can tease apart a membrane or brace a settling wall. Their heads are wedge-shaped and almost featureless, save for a fringe of sensory cilia and two dark, glassy patches that drink the dim bioluminescence. They have no mouths in the human sense—only a vertical seam that opens when they feed, revealing a rasping tongue for scraping mineral crusts and chewing the fibrous flesh of the city’s growths. Their skin is dry as old paper, faintly warm, and smells of ozone and powdered chalk. From the joints they exude a thin resin that seals cracks and leaves a faint, varnished shine.

They do not build the city. They negotiate with it. They are pruners, grafters, listeners, and slow surgeons of living architecture. A Thren may spend a century widening a passage by encouraging the pith to split along a particular seam, or decades coaxing a filtration membrane to thicken. They carry tools that are themselves grown: scrapers of hardened fungus, bladders of solvent, tuning forks of bone that they press against a wall and strike once, then wait—sometimes for days—to hear what the structure answers. They wear dust-cloaks woven from spore-silk, and they adorn themselves with resonance beads that hum faintly as they walk, each bead tuned to a different chamber of the city. They have no fire, no metal, no wheels. They have chemistry, patience, and an intimate knowledge of the slow.

Their language is mostly subsonic. A conversation between two Thren can last an afternoon and consist of perhaps a dozen exchanges, each separated by long, deliberate silences. They speak through a drum in the throat and feel replies through the floor. They do not interrupt, because interruption is a physical event—a change in pressure that can be felt in the bones. Their personal names are pressure signatures, and their nicknames are often the way dust settles on a particular ledge, or the pitch of a groaning beam. They greet one another by touching footpads and humming a phrase that means, roughly, “You are still here, and the hum is still true.”

They are sparse in the way that great age is sparse. A district the size of a human town might hold a few dozen adults. They live in hollowed towers shaped like seed pods, in chambers that pulse gently with the city’s breathing. They sleep in resonance hollows where the planetary hum is amplified, and they wake when the spore-clouds dim. They know when another Thren is in the next chamber by the change in the floor’s song. They are rarely surprised. They are rarely alone, even when they are the only living thing for a kilometer.

They feed simply: mineral blooms scraped from the walls, salt lichen, fibrous pith, the occasional fallen bell of a drifting jellyfish. They drink nothing. Their bodies wring what they need from chemical reactions and the slow metabolism of stone. They reproduce at long intervals, exchanging spores in a ritual that lasts a season. The city incubates the young in membrane cradles. For the first few decades the young are small, quick, low and wide, armored like the scuttlers that keep the dust—runners who explore the passages and taste everything. Then they enter a settling, wrap themselves in a chrysalis of pith, and emerge as adults: slow, tall, and patient. A Thren may live for several thousand years. They measure time by the settling of towers, the turning of the spore-clouds, and the long, almost imperceptible shifts in the under-song.

They gather rarely, at the Listening Wells—shafts where the city’s muffling walls fall away and the planetary hum comes through clean. There they do not worship. They answer. They add their own harmonics to the deep roar, and the city’s membranes tremble in reply. These are the closest things they have to festivals: a few dozen tall, grey figures standing in a circle in the dark, humming back at the world. The drifters—the great bioluminescent jellyfish—are their weather. The Thren read their light patterns and the rhythm of their pulsing bells. They never hunt them. When a drifter dies and falls, the Thren collect the membranous bell and use it as a lamp, a bowl, a window. The armored scuttlers are their neighbors and occasionally their pests; the Thren guide them away from nurseries with low clicks and patience.

The city itself is not merely their home. It is their host, their archive, and their instrument. Its conduits carry nutrients and information, and the Thren are its attention—the part of the reef that can feel a blocked channel, a torn membrane, a tower settling too fast. They store history in memory membranes: patches of wall stained with chemical signatures that a trained Thren can read by touch and taste. A historian will press her palm to the wall and stand motionless for an hour, tasting the slow decay of a thousand years. Their dead are not buried so much as given back. When a Thren feels the final settling begin, they walk to the Deep Dust, lie down in a chosen hollow, and let the city grow over them. Their names are added to the walls as a faint new stain. They do not fear death. They fear the silence that comes when a name is no longer read.

They are not human, and they would find human hurry incomprehensible—a kind of frantic noise that never resolves into music. They are a quiet people in a deafening place, a sparse people in a vast living cathedral. They move slowly because the world moves slowly. They listen because the world is always speaking. And when you see one standing in a passage, still as a pillar, one hand pressed to the membrane wall, you are seeing not idleness but work: the long, patient work of keeping a city alive by paying attention to its song.
