Start with the ones who never leave, because everyone else is a guest.

The dipper is a guest. It comes down the slot twice a day — a small brown bird with a white bib, blinking in the gloom — stands in the warm sump and works the shallows, and then it goes back up into the grey daylight and the river and the weather, and it has a whole life out there that has nothing to do with this room. The shrew is a guest, alone, nose working, in and out through some gap in the peat that only it knows. The beetles are guests. Even the midges, when the hatch comes off and the air goes thick for two hours, are guests — they arrive out of the sump and most of them will leave or die by evening.

The residents are the ones whose whole world is the interval. Nine seconds. They cannot be evicted from it because it is the only address they have.

The first resident is the moss, and it is arguably one thing. A continuous collar around the lip of the sump, grown fat and green on warm vapor and mineral stain, thicker on the side the water reaches, thin where it doesn't — you could read the water line off it like a gauge. It has been here longer than any animal. It does not travel. It leans toward the warmth the way a plant leans toward light, and the warmth is always in the same place, so the ring is perfect and it will never be anything but a ring. It is the fire circle and it is also the fire.

The second residents are the caddis, and they are the strangest, because they have built the clock into their houses. Each larva drags a case of sand grain and root-bark; each case is glued to the wall at exactly the height the pulse reaches, not a knuckle above or below. They did not measure. They did not know there was a measuring to do. They simply built where the water told them, generation after generation, until the wall is a tide gauge made of living masonry, and the masonry cannot move. The case is the address. If the pulse rose a centimeter the colony would drown before it could respond; if it fell a centimeter they would starve in the dry. They are committed to nine seconds with their whole bodies, and they will never once see the sump they live beside.

The third residents are the springtails, and they are less a population than a nervous system. They carpet the water film in their millions and they keep the time in the only way a small blind animal can keep time — with their whole bodies. When the pulse gathers, the film trembles, and the whole carpet goes still; when it breaks, the carpet scatters and reforms, and settles, and goes still again, nine seconds later, forever. They do not know what they are waiting for. They have simply been selected, over however many generations it takes, to be a creature that tenses at the right moment. They are the interval made visible — static, on a tin roof, that has learned to breathe.

And the fourth resident is the one I keep coming back to: an amphipod. Half a centimeter, colorless, eyes not worth the name. There is a whole brood of them in the sump, and every one of them spends its entire life inside the warm water, and not one of them has ever seen the coin of light. Its world is this: warm rises, warm falls. It rides the surge up over the lip of the sump and lets the surge carry it back down, feeding on whatever the stirring loosens, and then it does it again. It has no day. It has no season except the cold snaps, when the young basalt takes its charge and something lifts in the air — the hair on your arms, the taste of pennies, the drop that does not quite land — and perhaps something lifts in the amphipod too, some inherited shudder at a frequency it cannot name. Nobody knows. Nobody has asked it. It lives in a nine-second world and it will die in one, and its children will take its place in the warm water, and the light will go on crossing the wall above them and going out.

And if you want the truth of the place, the truth is that none of these are the inhabitant either. The inhabitant is the interval itself. Nine seconds — a rhythm that wears animals the way a body wears clothes, that has assembled a moss and a masonry wall and a nervous carpet and a blind swimmer and set them all ticking to itself. The pocket doesn't have residents so much as it has a metabolism, and everything alive in it is a cell of that metabolism, keeping the beat.

Which is why the place feels haunted even though nobody built it and nobody watches it. Under the snow, the slot narrows, the hum rises a tone, and the piston keeps going — and the sump goes on breathing its nine seconds with its whole small timed population inside it, keeping the beat, keeping the beat, keeping the beat, for no one.
