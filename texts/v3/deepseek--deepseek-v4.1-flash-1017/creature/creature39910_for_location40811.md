The place is inhabited less by separate creatures than by a crowding—a wet, layered negotiation of bodies and signals. Nothing lives alone there. Even the ground is a resident: the porous mineral crust is a slow collective organism, or something like one, breathing through its chimneys, sealing itself around corpses, opening when warmth shifts beneath it. It is the floor and the ledger, the producer and the grave. It does not think in any way a surface animal would recognize, but it is attentive. It tastes what dies on it. It answers pressure with slime and heat.

The closest thing to a people are the tappers.

They are blind, soft-bodied, a dozen-legged, with bodies like folded tongues kept slick by the perpetual damp. Each leg ends in a hard nodule, and it is with these that they strike the stone—short signatures, long queries, warnings, invitations, insults, maps. Their antennae are feathery and always moving, reading the air for warmth, moisture, the nearness of another body. They have no eyes and no words for light. They do not speak of silence. They speak of hush: the dangerous gap in the tapping when a root-thing is listening.

Tappers live in colonies threaded through the crust’s chimneys and vents. They huddle in warm seams, young clinging to the backs of adults, older ones tapping out the day’s routes. A tapper’s name is a rhythm—a private pattern struck against a familiar stone and answered by kin. They know the sumps by their bass hums, the thermal vents by the taste of the air, the safe crust by its echo. They move by touch and vibration, their legs reading the ground like a text. When one dies, its shell is absorbed into the foundation, another pore in the crust, and its rhythm may persist in the code for a while, repeated by others who do not know whose name it was.

The root-things are their predators, or one kind of them. Pale, whip-thin, jointless, they dangle from the ceilings of the larger vents in hanging clusters. They are not plants. They can hold still for days, tasting the air with tips that are sticky and stinging. When a crawler passes beneath, one or several whip down, curl, and retract into a fissure. Some root-things can imitate a tapped rhythm—imperfectly, just enough to draw a young tapper close—so the code has checks for false rhythms, and the young are taught to distrust a voice that repeats too exactly.

In the sumps drift the sacs: vast, slow, gas-blown membranes trailing sieving tendrils through the organic slurry. Their passage is a low resonant hum, a bass line under the percussion. They are not aggressive, but they are dangerous by bulk. Smaller creatures ride their seams—scrapers, parasites, tiny tappers clinging to the membrane. When a sac dies, it drops, and the sump boils with feeders; its escaping gas throbs through the crust for a long time.

Between these are the crawlers and borers and shelled scuttlers, the slime-grazers and mineral-chewers, the swarms that live in every crevice. They are not background. They are the economy. They chew the crust’s films, lay eggs in warm seams, burrow into the porous foundation, feed the root-things and the tappers and the sacs in turn. Reproduction is a pulse tied to heat and moisture. Death is a slump back into the crust, where enzymes and mineral cement take the body apart and add it to the floor.

A single tapper wakes in its chimney, feels the damp, tastes the slime, and taps its signature. The stone answers with the signatures of neighbors and strangers. Somewhere a sac hums. Somewhere a root-thing holds its breath. The tapper moves out into the dark, reading the world through its feet, and the world reads back. That is what lives there: not one monster, but a civilization of touch, a deafening chorus of small hammers and low hums and wet, blind bodies making room for each other in the dark.
