What lives in the Weft is mostly thin, mostly fast, and almost never heavy. There are no villages and no people; there is no hearth except the warm feet of the stacks, no road except the galleries in the felt, no roof except the glare. The inhabitants are not arranged in a food web so much as in a single long metabolism, and the largest of them is the ground.

The Weft itself is the first resident. It is one continuous growth, a felted body with a country’s worth of surface, and it lives by accretion — drawing dust, taking charge, laying down fibre, pressing its own dead into pale stone. It has no boundary you could walk to and say *here the organism ends*. Its seams are scars, its colour-patches are age, its springy give is a kind of slow attention: it lies down under the wind and rises again behind you, remembering its shape. It is not passive. Charge pools in its low places at night and makes them glow. Frost forms on it at dawn and sublimes off as a bitter white fur. Borers open it; kites drink from it; stacks grow out of it like organs. It is the floor and the oldest inhabitant at once, and everything else lives either in it, on it, or just above it.

The kites are the most visible lives. They are flat, wide, membrane-and-filament animals the size of a boat, and they do not flap so much as trim — warping a broad sheet of stretched fibre, ribbed with filaments, edged with frayed sensing hairs. One face is pale and reflective; the other is matte and dark. When a kite banks, it goes from white to nothing, a shape that erases itself against the glare and then writes itself back. Beneath it trails a hundred metres of fine root-like line, not an anchor but a proboscis: it ends in a frayed brush in the felt, where it draws charge and sifts mineral dust. The charge comes up the line as a slow pulse. The dust is sorted in the kite’s body and used to thicken its membrane, to repair its ribs, to keep its tissues dry and conductive. Kites are not many. Each one keeps a territory of silence around itself, and you can watch a single kite turn for an hour without another crossing your view. They are afraid of the ground and not of the sky, and they are right to be. The felt beneath them crackles. The line is the only part of a kite that touches it.

The borers are the mat’s gardeners. Small armoured things, some the length of a thumb, some as long as your hand, with shells of cemented grit and jaw plates of silica. They chew channels through the felt, galleries that run level and branching under the surface, and they keep the mat from sealing over into a shell. They eat dead fibre and the mineral bands that form in the deeper layers; their castings are a fine powder that the wind takes and the stacks inhale. They are blind in any way you would recognise and they know the world as vibration and current: a footfall above them, a discharge in the felt, the slow pressure of a kite’s line. At dawn they come to the mouths of their tunnels and scrape the frost before it sublimes. They are the only things here that make holes on purpose. Without them the Weft would close itself up and go quiet.

At the warm feet of the stacks the population thickens. This is the nearest thing to a bazaar: a ring of heat and sorted dust, and creatures that have come to live off the stack’s exhale. The sifters are sessile — fans and combs and sieves on stalks, facing the plume, their bodies banded rose and sulphur and iron-grey by what they take. Some are no bigger than a fist; some stand as tall as a person. They are not plants; they are animals that have given up moving and become good at grading grit. The scavengers are low and fast and armoured, and they do not rot what they find — nothing here rots — they disassemble it. A dead kite, a shed stack-fin, a sifter whose stalk has worn through: the scavengers reduce it to powder and the wind carries the powder back to a stack. And around them are the things that have no name in any language you would recognise: ribbon-things that live in the hot shadow; jointed things that mimic a piece of fluting; a creature that is mostly a mouth and a hooked foot; things known only by their tracks and by the way the chord changes when they move. They are not built. They are grown into their shapes, and their shapes are organs, and no one there has ever needed the distinction.

Inside the stacks, where the dust is hot and fractionated, there is another life. Flat, blind, warm things live in the bands, grazing the mineral strata and coloured by what they eat. They know the stack as a sequence of vibrations and pitches, a warm tube that sorts the world. Their castings add to the fluting. When a stack sheds a fin, some of them are cast out with it and become food at the feet. They are the stack’s gut flora, and the stack is their weather.

Above the stacks ride the pale shapes of whatever eats the kites. They are not kites; they trail no line. They are long and flat and almost invisible against the glare, and they climb on the stack thermals in slow spirals, higher than the kites go. They are the only large animals here that never touch the mat. They wait until a kite banks and its line goes slack, and then they fold and drop. They cut the line with a hooked limb, or shear the membrane, or strike the central knot of charged tissue; the kite’s stored charge discharges in a white flicker, and the pale shape takes the body and the charge together. Their shadow arrives before they do. They are rare. They are afraid of the ground and not of the sky. They mate in the air, in the thermals above a singing stack, and they never land.

So the Weft is not empty; it is thinly inhabited, and the thinness is part of the design. The lives are small and fast or large and rare; the ground is slow and continuous. The borers keep the mat open. The kites drink its charge and sift its dust. The stack-foot things eat the stack’s sorted breath. The pale things eat the kites. The mat takes everything back eventually, not by rot but by abrasion and powder and re-deposition, and lays it down in another band. Nothing here is tame and nothing is idle. You stand in the bright flat roaring, and you listen to which stack-voices are loudest and which are behind you, and you know where you are. Every living thing there is listening too.
