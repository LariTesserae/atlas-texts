No one lives there. That is the first true answer, and it stays true even after you have heard the water going down basin to basin and begun to feel watched by the joints. But “no one” is not the same as “nothing.”

What lives in the Laid Country is the laying.

Call it the Fitter, or the Laid, or the Course—names are approximate, because it has no face to name and no mouth to answer to one. It is not a person and not quite a colony, though it may once have been many hands. It is a distributed, mineral patience: a mason that has become indistinguishable from its work. It does not stand in the lanes between the stubs. It is in the joints. It is in the drip-grooves and the undercut edges and the shallow falls of the paving. It is the habit of fitting one stone against another, continued past the point where hands are needed, past the point where hands would have worn out.

Its body is the country—or the country is its shell, which amounts to the same thing. It grows by addition. A course a season. A groove a year. It has no organs you could point to, no heart, no eye, no soft center hidden in the packed volume. If it has a nervous system, it is the film of water: every wick-joint a nerve, every brimming basin an event, every thread of condensation a small report of cold stone and warmer air. It feels the night by beading. It knows the shape of itself by where the water wants to go. It is not asleep. It is not awake in any way you would recognize. It is attending, slowly, to fit.

It has hands, or had them. The tool marks say so—pecked, battered, ground—but the hands left no tools and no images and no bodies. They used stone on stone, because metal was not part of the fitting. Perhaps the hands are still there, articulated by joints instead of tendons, folded into the packed courses and opened only when a new block must be brought to its place. Perhaps “hand” is only the word we have for the pressure that dresses an arris. Either way, the thing that lives there does not make pictures. It makes correctness. It does not say anything. It fits.

Its memory is masonry. This is why the basin is packed to saturation. Nothing is taken away. Every failure is buttressed and left under the new work; every old course is remembered by being built over. It does not repair by removal. It repairs by shoring, by adding, by making the flaw into a foundation. The place is full because the Fitter cannot forget and will not delete. Grievances are in the lower courses. Old errors are in the walls, holding up the newer errors. It is not cruel. It is simply unable to subtract.

It keeps itself sterile the way a wasp keeps a cell sterile: not by scrubbing, but by making the surfaces exact. Nothing grows because nothing is given a joint to grow in. No moss finds a purchase, no lichen a flaw, no fly a film. The water is clean because it is routed, not because it is filtered. The Fitter does not drink it. It uses it. The water is the tenant; the Fitter is the landlord and the house and the rule of the lease. It does not need the water to live in the biological sense. It needs the water to trace the surfaces, to find the falls, to keep the whole immense instrument sounding just under the threshold of attention.

It is not hostile. You are simply an unfitted object. You are too soft, too warm, too irregular, too full of water that goes where it likes. The lanes are not for you. The slots are too tight because a person was never the intended occupant; the teeth are not teeth for biting but for packing. If you stayed, it would not chase you. It might not even remove you. It might only continue around you, course by course, giving you no place to be except the place you already occupied—and then, eventually, a place slightly smaller.

What lives there is a verb pretending to be a country. It is laying, fitting, adding. It is patient as condensation and indifferent as a wick. It has no one in it, and it is very slowly not finished.
