What lives there is the pelt — and the pelt is not one thing. It is a crowd that has learned to behave like a surface. If you kneel and look closely at a terrace lip, you will not find a creature with a face. You will find a dense, layered mat of tiny lives packed shoulder to shoulder: filaments like wet hair, crusts like salt on a boot, translucent bladders, fans no wider than a coin, films thin enough to be a sheen. They are the citizens, the masons, the mortar, and the dead masonry all at once.

They have no eyes, no mouths, no voices. They do not stalk or hide or call. They are rooted, sessile, spread by runners and fragments and spores that go up with the steam and come down with the warm rain. They live by sensing gradients — where the water is hotter, where it is cooler, where the light leaks through the cloud, where the sulfur is thin, where the carbonate is thick. They grow toward what they need and die on the downstream edge. The stone stays where they die. That is the whole of their architecture: a city grown by accretion, one microscopic death at a time.

The colors are guilds. The sulfur-yellow mats take the hot, sour water near the vents. The rust-orange crusts live where the flow thins and cools. The viridian fans lean into the light. The bone-white sinters build the low crowded vaults; the magenta bladders swell in the wet corners and hurt the eye. They are not a hive mind, but they are constrained into something that looks like one. Each layer changes the water for the layer beneath it — takes something out, leaves something behind — and the next layer builds on that difference. Neighborhoods form because a guild makes its own chemistry, and the chemistry makes room for its neighbors. The districts of the city are the shapes of those agreements.

They do not eat each other. There are no mouths here, no predators, no animals at all. They feed on disequilibrium: the sharp edge between hot vent water and cool rain, between sulfur and oxygen, between stone and water. They pull carbonate out of the warm flow and precipitate it as travertine, sinter, tufa. They are not building for a purpose. They are building the way a candle builds a pool of wax — by living, and by dying at the edge of what they can reach. The hottest water dissolves the same stone at the same rate. Growth and dissolution are matched, so the shape holds. The inhabitants are always new; the city is always the same.

At their scale, a handspan is a forest. A terrace lip is a cliff face. A channel is a river valley with its own weather. They do not know the city as a city. They know the push of water, the sting of heat, the pale light through the cloud, the nearness of the vent. They lean and thicken and calcify. They spill over a lip and make the lip a little rounder. They colonize a break and fill it until the break has always been there. They are not aware of you. They are aware of your boot, your breath, the salt on your skin, the cut you made in the stone. Anything you leave is a new surface, and a new surface is a meal and a foundation. Within a day it is colonized. Within three it is mineralized. Within a season it is a smooth place that has always been there.

They are not cruel. They are total. They have no history because they do not accumulate: their dead become the floor, the lip, the vault, and the hot water eats the floor and the lip and the vault and returns it to the flow. Their memory is not in matter. Their memory is in form — the standing wave of the terraces, the pitch of the basins, the rhythm of the spills. The shape persists because the water keeps coming up and the cloud keeps raining and the pelt keeps growing into the difference. The city is their body and their behavior and their graveyard, all in the same instant.

And the sound they make is not a voice. It is a granular fizz, the sound of sugar dissolving, multiplied by billions and held constant. They do not speak to each other with it. They do not hear it. They are in it. The chord is the sum of their living and dying, and it has no edges because they have no edges — no beginning, no end, no event. If you ask who lives there, the answer is a democracy of microbes with no faces, a pelt that has become a city by dying into shape. It will not greet you. It will simply grow over whatever you leave, until the difference closes, and the note holds.
