# The Listeners

## The reply

You will hear it before you see anything, and the sound will be your own.

You walk; the mat carries your footsteps out for a kilometer in every direction, the way it carries everything. That much is ordinary. What is not ordinary is that a step comes back to you — clearly, from a direction you have already passed — and it is not a faithful echo. It is slightly late. It is slightly low. It has been *edited*, by something that heard it and chose to say it again.

Then the tell that people use to be certain: stop. A listener's answer does not stop with you. It takes two steps, sometimes three, to catch up to the fact that you have stopped walking, because the thing making it is not following you, it is replaying you, and replay takes a moment.

## What it is

A settled listener is a chamber. Its body is the wall of that chamber, and the chamber is mostly empty, and the emptiness is the point.

It sits in a shallow bedrock basin, one of the planed-out knuckles or hollows of the plateau, with its mouth just under the mat. Above it the felt lies unbroken; on the surface the only sign is a patch a meter or two across that is a little too smooth and a little too taut, shiny in the dew, and which flexes with a slow pulse at long intervals — pressure equalizing through a membrane that is, in its own way, an eardrum the size of a bath.

The wall of the chamber is layered tissue, half animal and half plate, secreted from what the animal eats and laid down in rings, harder every year, folded and refolded as it thickens into baffles and ribs and secondary chambers. That is the whole anatomy. No eyes. No limbs, once it has settled. The mouth of the chamber answers to the surface and the wall answers to the soil and the thing that is left inside is a resonator with opinions.

## How one is made

The young are small and soft and blind and they crawl, and almost all of them are eaten.

A listener that has grown too heavy with its own plate to answer well — that has gone flat, that has become a lump — buds. The juveniles come out through a gallery and drop into the rot layer under the mat, each one the size of a thumb, and then they crawl. They crawl for months. They tap as they go, a small hard knock of the mouth-rim against buried stone, and they are listening for the tap to come back. Where the return is clearest and farthest — where the ground underneath is a channel rather than a sponge — one of them stops and begins to grow.

Which means the geography of listeners is a record of the acoustics of the country, and not of anything else. They settle over buried bedrock shelves. They settle on the healed seams of old cuts, which the mat has knitted into raised ridges and hardened into sounding-boards. They settle in the packed fill of abandoned works, because fill rings.

They settle, in other words, wherever people have been, and for the same reason people were there: something solid underneath.

## How it eats

Slowly, and mostly small things, and it does not chase.

The rim has a suture. When the listener opens, the patch of skin on the surface sinks into a pit with a grip around the edge, and what walks onto it falls in, and the lining of the chamber is not kind. That is the crude version, and it is used rarely. What the listener mostly does is edit.

It has learned, over a few centuries, that a mower herd's contact call is a thing it can reissue from another place, in a slightly wrong pitch, and that four or five animals out of the many will turn to correct the discrepancy. It has learned that a runner flock's alarm sends them along the ground in a direction, and that a transposed alarm sends them a different one. It does not need to draw a whole herd. It needs to deflect a handful, three or four times a year, and in between it needs almost nothing at all. The rest of its diet is burrowers, insects in their columns, whatever falls in by itself in the dark.

It is one of the most patient predators on the plateau, because it is not really a predator. It is a place that has opinions about where things should go.

## How it ages

A young listener answers cleanly. One voice, one delay, close to your own pitch but a little down. It is the thing you want when you want to know where you are.

A mature one answers in two voices, then three. The baffles in its wall divide the sound. Some part of the return comes early and high, some comes late and low and from a direction that does not exist, and a practiced traveler reads the pattern of the differences the way you would read a contour.

An old one answers in a smear. It has folded too many times. The sound goes in and comes back refracted, diminished, delayed by whole seconds, full of the harmonics of everything that has been walking over it. People say the old ones have everyone in them still. This is not quite a belief about ghosts. It is an observation about resonance, and they are not entirely wrong.

And then, eventually, the chamber grows in on itself, the plate closes, the sound goes in and does not come out the same at all, and the listener is done. It does not rot. It is stone now, or nearly, under a meter of felt. Its neighbours' returns bend around it. The navigators call these *the deaf*, and there are a great many of them, and a portion of what the plateau sounds like underfoot is the tuned and buried bodies of animals that stopped speaking some hundreds of years ago.

## The bird

Most listeners keep birds. Not deliberately — they tolerate them, the way a house tolerates a cat.

The bird is small and dark and it works the rim: it takes what the listener's pit draws to the edge, and in exchange it calls. It calls loudly and it calls far, from fifty meters out, from two hundred, from over the next rise, and the listener hears those calls come home through the mat and learns the country between. A listener with birds is a better listener. It knows which way the herd went. It knows about the haze. When it answers you, it answers with more confidence, and sometimes it answers in a place you did not ask about.

A listener with no birds is thin and slow and often wrong. You can hear that too.

## What people do with them

Within a season's range there are eleven pillars with names and nine listeners with names, and everyone who travels knows both lists, and a listener's name is used the way the pillar's is: *I came across from Hull's Mouth in the afternoon.*

You ask one thing of a listener. You call once, plainly, standing still, and you wait. You do not call twice. Two returns means two listeners and you must decide which is which. No return means a deaf place, or a lie in the ground, or nothing within earshot. Children are taught the asking before they are taught to walk in rhythm, which is the other half of it: you walk softly here and you walk in time, because a listener hears your stride the way you would hear a person's face.

What you never do is answer it. If it replays you, you let it. A listener that is talked to learns a voice, and a listener that has learned a voice will call a mower in it, or call a runner, or call you, from somewhere you are not, on a night when you are tired. So the rule is not religious. It is the same rule as not feeding something that has already noticed you.

The named ones are not damaged and not moved. A cut rim heals — everything here heals — but it heals into a different shape, and a listener whose voice has been changed is a landmark that no longer tells you where you are, which is worse than having none. There are seams in the mat older than memory that are nothing but old quarrels with listeners, still knitting.

## The chorus

And this is why the first hour after dawn is so loud and so *structured*, and why people who come up from the low country say it sounds composed.

It is. Every listener within earshot is holding a line. They are in the chorus and they are also behind it, returning it, each one at its own delay and its own interval, so that at any point on the plateau what you hear is the birds and the burrowers and the sawyers now, and beneath them the listeners saying all of it again, a little late, a little down, in the voices of animals that have gone somewhere else. The harmonies are not anybody's intention. They are what you get when a country is one instrument and it has been played on for a hundred thousand years.

Stand still, at dawn, on the mat, and listen past the loud part. The low line under everything — the thing you feel in the sternum and the teeth and set your travel by — that is not the ground humming. That is nine named animals and a great many unnamed ones, opening their mouths under your feet at the same time, and taking the morning in.
