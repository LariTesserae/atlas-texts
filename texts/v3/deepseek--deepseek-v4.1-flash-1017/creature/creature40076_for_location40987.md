**The Slack-Water People**

The people who live in the Harrows are not settlers. They are the ones who found that the Harrows will keep a person alive if the person agrees to be kept on the Harrows’ terms. There are never many of them. Two shacks in use, maybe three; a dozen souls at the outside, and often fewer. They do not call themselves a colony. When they need a name for themselves they say *the crowners*, or *the eleven-minute people*, and both are descriptions rather than titles.

They are small and hard and salt-bleached. The wind and the glare and the iodine have done to them what the water has done to the rock: rounded the edges, polished the surfaces, left something grey-green and patient underneath. Their hands are broad and their nails worn flat, and their feet are pale and wrinkled from a life spent waist-deep on the crowns. They wear tarred canvas and seal-leather and cord twisted from kelp fibre, and their clothes have no corners left—pockets rounded, hems felted, buttons replaced with toggles of bone. They smell of fish oil and rust and wet wool and the rich stink of the intertidal weed when the sun is on it. Their eyes are narrow and pale and always going to the water first.

They stand strangely. Knees soft, weight forward, head tilted a little as if listening for a sound just under the audible. They can stand still for the whole eleven minutes of slack without shifting, and then move very fast. They speak low and not often. When they do speak it is mostly about water: where it will be, what it is doing, what it did last bore. They have a hand-language for the loud places, and a habit of touching the nearest fixed thing—rock, shack, rope—when they are thinking, as if to remind themselves that something here does not move.

Their day is not the sun’s. It is the bore’s. They sleep in pieces around the slack, and they wake to the Pump’s groan—once every four seconds, felt in the teeth before it is heard—and they know by the feel of the standing waves whether the eleven minutes are coming or going. They keep a salvaged chronometer in one shack, wound by hand, but they trust it less than the Old Wheel. In the eleven minutes they do everything that requires stillness: mend nets, cross saddles, collect fresh water from the rock pockets where it runs off at low tide, barter, talk, bury what needs burying. When the bore is two minutes out they are already tied off or moving up. They do not waste the eleven minutes. The eleven minutes are the only thing here that can be wasted.

They live off the place without pretending to own it. They take fish from the green dusk under the pipe-kelp canopy, shellfish from the seams, bird eggs from the gull-analogue nests when the skuas are elsewhere. They cut bladder floats and dry fronds and scrape blood-moss for bait. They take a seal only when one is already failing, and they use all of it, because nothing here is allowed to be waste. They do not dig; there is nothing to dig. They do not build with friction; the ground is polished. Their shacks are bolted and weighted and lashed down hard against the bore, and anything they leave standing long enough gets scoured into a soft grey shape and stays that shape. They know this. They plan for it. A crowner’s knife has a rounded handle and a hole drilled through it, because a dropped knife is gone.

They use the drowned works below the Kettle as a fish trap and a marker, but they do not live there. They go down into the quiet below twenty metres—where the roar stops and the water goes cold and very blue—only when they must, and they come back up with the same look on their faces. They do not go into the trench. They do not speak much about the trench. They call the large grey thing *the Neighbour*, and they leave it alone, and they leave the first fish of a good run on a flat rock when they have had a very good run, which may be superstition and may be manners.

They meet at slack on the central saddle, the Kettle, when the standing waves are briefly not where they were. They exchange fish and cord and news. They have names that are descriptions: the one who heard the east bore first; she who mends the west net; the man with the skua-scar. They do not write much. There is no paper that survives the rain, and no soil to bury a record in. They remember instead, and they teach by doing, and they tell the same stories about the Pump—that it was abandoned a long time ago, that the wheel is still turning because no one came to take the brake off, that it is not a machine anymore but a clock—and they do not touch it. To touch it would be to admit it could stop.

They are not the only residents. The seals shingle the crowns around them; the birds fall with purpose and complain; the skua will take a fish out of a hand while the hand is holding it. The crowners tolerate the skua because it warns, and they walk among the seals without ceremony. But they are the ones who have learned to be residents rather than visitors. They are old enough to be finished and busy enough to be brand new, and they go on arguing with the water in the same way the water argues with itself—quietly, in the eleven minutes, with everything tied down.
