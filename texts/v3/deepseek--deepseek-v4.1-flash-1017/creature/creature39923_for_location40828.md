The ones who live in the Aethelgard are a quiet, amphibious people—sparse enough that a whole level of the spire may hold only a family, a few fungal bowls, and the sound of slow breathing. They are not builders in the ordinary sense. They are gardeners of a living architecture, tenders of the world-plant, and their bodies and habits have been shaped by the same cold, patient world they tend.

They are long and smooth-limbed, adapted to cold and buoyancy rather than speed. Their skin is grey-green and faintly mottled, like the porous wood of the Aethelgard; their hands are broad and webbed, strong enough to wedge a bone blade into a seam of new growth, delicate enough to stroke a young tendril without breaking it. Their feet are paddles. Their blood is dark and slow, and their flesh holds heat stubbornly; they conserve warmth by stillness, by curling together in the warmest inner cysts, by letting the cold press on them without ever quite entering. They can draw breath from water through their skin and from air in the chambers, though their voices carry best as low vibration. Their eyes are large and dark, patient with the silver light, and along their flanks runs a sensitive line that reads the shudder of the trunk and the click of a hunting fish before either is consciously heard.

They live in cysts and corridors grown by the plant and shaped by generations of careful pruning. A home is a smooth-walled hollow, sometimes open to the grey-green water, sometimes sealed against it, lit by shallow bowls of bioluminescent fungi or by the diffuse gift of light from above. They use bone and stone—scrapers, wedges, blades, bowls—never metal, never fire. Heat is made by bodies and kept by closeness. A chamber may sit empty for a long time, and that emptiness is not neglect; it is part of the balance. The plant is slow, and the people are few.

Their day is a negotiation with the spire. They listen before they cut. A groan in the trunk may mean a current is stressing a wall; a pop may mean a new chamber is ready to be opened; a change in the sap’s thrum may mean the fungi need feeding or a corridor should be cleared. They prune and graft and redirect growth, but they do not force it. They harvest small fish and swarming crustaceans, cultivate fungi in shallow bowls, drink sap from carefully tapped veins, and give their dead back to unused cysts so the plant may reabsorb them. Nothing is destroyed, only returned. Their tools are simple because their work is patient; a wedge of bone may guide a decade of growth.

Socially they are scattered and unhurried. Small kin groups keep to their own levels, meeting at crossing nodes to exchange news by touch and low song. Their language is full of borrowed sounds—the plant’s groan, the chitter of crustaceans, the distant boom of something immense and unseen—so that a sentence may be half imitation and half meaning. They have long memories and no writing; they remember in the tuning of chambers and in songs that are also maps. Names, where they exist, are phrases of rhythm and place: the one who hears the third groan, the keeper of the warm cyst, the child of the low corridor. They do not crowd. Loneliness is not a fear among them; silence is a material they use.

They know the Aethelgard as kin, law, and clock. The pulse of sap is the measure of time; generations of fungi are a calendar; the slow climb of the spire is history. They do not imagine themselves masters of the plant. They are commensals, listeners, small warm creatures inside a vast cold body that tolerates them because they tend it. Around them live the chittering crustaceans, the clicking fish, the unseen deep caller—neighbors in the same volume of water, none of them citizens, all of them part of the symphony. The people of the Aethelgard are the ones who read it, answer it, and keep the patient partnership alive.
