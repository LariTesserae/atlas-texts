No one lives in the Sump in the sense of a house with a door. What lives there is a population held in a bowl and a rhythm.

The first resident is the four-second tide. The spring comes up warm and mineral and faintly sulfurous, pools two meters deep, and breathes: rise a finger’s width, fall back, slap the concrete edge, tick from the turbine, rise again. Everything else in the bowl has signed on to that interval. The frogs in the wet rubble sit in rows like something counted them, and when the water moves they move with it, a small collective adjustment. The snails are pasted in sheets on every submerged surface, grazing the algae film down to the concrete. The small silver fish work the margins in quick nervous passes; the longer, slower shape under the overhang works the fish. The heron stands in the settling tank with the patience of a statue that has learned to eat. At dusk the swifts and kites come down in a loose spiral, the bats come out of the fissures, and for a while the air at the rim is thick with wings — but the sound stays low, almost nothing, because this is a predator’s room and quiet is how it is furnished.

The fig tree lives there too, though it was there first. It grew in a crack before the ground opened around it, and now it leans out over the bowl toward light it can suddenly see, roots gripping a fissure too young for it. The scrub lives at the lip, crowded shoulder to shoulder, leaning inward and downhill toward the smell of water. The smell is the announcement: warm stone, wet chalk, iron, something greenly bacterial underneath.

And the works live there. Not as ghosts — the pump is water-driven, a bucket-sized turbine hung in the spring’s pressure, turning a shaft, lifting a thin thread of water up the north wall and over the rim so the scrub can drink it. It ticks. It has ticked unattended for nobody knows how long. The pump is the mechanical heart of the place, and the shed above it holds the residue of the men who built it: a chair, a hurricane lamp with soot in it, a coil of wire, a grease gun, a logbook rained into a single wasp-nest-textured object. The men do not live there anymore. Their intention does. The chair lives there as an empty shape; the pump lives there as a working habit.

The dead live there too, in their way. The water preserves. Birds settle into the silt in the shallows, feathers intact, eyes intact, months old. Snails keep their gloss. It is not a graveyard so much as a collection — the spring keeps what it takes, and the algae grows over the kept things, and the fish swim through them, and the four-second tide moves them gently back and forth. They are part of the crowding.

So what lives in the Sump is this: a warm, wet, quiet little room in a dry country, packed with frogs and snails and fish and birds and bats and one patient heron, one leaning fig, one ticking pump, a standing population of the preserved, and a four-second breath that holds them all in the same interval. It is dense and it is quiet and it keeps everything it is given.
