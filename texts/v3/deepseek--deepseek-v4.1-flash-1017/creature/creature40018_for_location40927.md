Ask what lives in Omber and the honest answer is: a great deal, and most of it is not human. By weight, the moss is the dominant organism. By number, the beetles. By noise, the Works. By intention, the people—though even their intention is mostly the continuation of something already underway.

The people are twelve thousand, and they are not a crowd so much as a rota. You see them first as figures: a hundred small shapes in motion on a hundred different levels, each carrying something—a spanner, a can, a coil of line, a crate, a child, a replacement flange, a bundle of chard. They have the look of people who have been fitted to a vertical world. Their boots are studded and soft-soled for grating; their coats are oilskin and felt and wool that never quite dries; their hands are stained green in the creases from moss and wet iron. They walk with a learned economy, keeping to the uphill side of the drip lines without seeming to think about it, ducking under a low span at exactly the right moment, stepping over a channel that has been stepped over ten thousand times before. A child in Omber learns the sound of a leaking joint before she learns to read. She learns which ladders are for people and which are for parts. She learns that if you stand still too long on a catwalk, someone will gently move you along, because you are, structurally, in the way.

They are not idle and they are not frantic. The place has trained them out of both. A shift in Omber is a long, patient carrying of things from one level to another, and the work is not a project with an end; it is the local weather. The riggers go up the crane and come down with a girder. The pipe fitters open a wall and find older pipes inside it. The moss-cutters trim the beards back from the lamps and leave the lee walls thick, because the moss is insulation and packing and caulking and, in a wet year, a kind of crop. The terrace gardeners tend the pale leggy chard and the vines trained along the catenary wires; they know which lamps make a plant bolt and which keep it sweet. The sump men go down where the eels are and come up smelling of iron and old water. Nobody eats the eels. That is not a law anyone has written down; it is simply one of the things Omber knows about itself.

The people are packed in behind the workshop walls, in stacked rooms that share their ceilings with the floors of other rooms. They sleep to the hum of the Works, and a stopped pump will wake a whole tier. They have a distributed sort of authority—a key, a chit, a foreman’s nod, the unwritten right of whoever has done the job longest. Nobody seems to be in charge of the valley, and nobody seems to need to be. The work continues shift after shift the way the water continues, because the alternative is a kind of stopping that Omber does not really imagine.

Then there is the rest of the population. The moss is the true majority, a green-black and grey pelt that has taken the valley as a skin. It lives on the wet side of everything and grows three fingers thick where the steam pipes run hot. It is not fought; it is managed. It fills the seams of the paving decks, hangs from the cable trays, combs flat in the drainage channels as bright slime. The ferns live on the verticals, sprouting from expansion joints and hanging in feathered cages from the flanges of the I-beams, all their fronds turned downstream wherever the drip is strongest. The chard and the vines and the small alder forest in the collapsed roof are planted things, but they have the same look as everything else: pale, leggy, patient, grown under lamps and in filtered grey-green light.

The animals are few and mostly unremarkable, which is how you know they belong. Beetles work the moss in numbers no one has counted. Pale things live in the cisterns, blind and soft and content with silt. Eels coil in the lower sumps, long and dark, tolerated and not eaten. Once or twice a day a heron stands on the parapet of the highest deck—thin, grey, patient—and waits for whatever it is herons wait for here, then leaves. Gulls come over the ridge in bad weather and do not stay. The heron is the only creature in Omber that seems to have no job, and even it has a schedule.

And under all of them, the Works runs. It is not alive, exactly, but it is inhabited the way a body is inhabited. Pumps, presses, trompes, belt drives threading up through the floors like roots; the perpetual small adjustment of old spans shored and new spans laid over them; the crane turning at the far end of the valley bringing up girders to replace girders gone soft with rust. The people feed it and it feeds them; the terraces feed the people, the water feeds the terraces, the condensation of a thousand surfaces becomes a working head by the time it reaches the bottom. It is a single wet metabolism with many mouths.

So what lives in Omber is not one thing. It is twelve thousand people who know the drip lines by instinct, a pelt of moss, a crop of chard, a forest in a collapsed roof, beetles in the seams, eels in the sumps, a heron on the parapet, and a Works that would go on perfectly well without you. It is ordinary, and it is ceaseless, and it is maintaining itself. That is the life of the place.
