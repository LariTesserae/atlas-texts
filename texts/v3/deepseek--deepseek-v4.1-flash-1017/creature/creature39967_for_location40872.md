The Mizzle Downs are inhabited by a commonwealth of the small. There is no single resident, no dominant creature whose life organizes the place; instead there is a dense, layered tenancy of things that have agreed—not consciously, but over a very long time—to stay low, stay damp, and stay patient. If you ask who lives here, the honest answer is: almost no one big, and everyone small, and all of them are engaged in the same slow business of turning fog and stone and one another into more life.

The first residents are the plants, though “plants” feels too upright a word for them. The wiry spiral shrubs are the old settlers, colonies rather than individuals, each a centuries-old maze of woody stems no taller than a hand. They spread outward in slow rings, drinking condensation through tiny resinous leaves that smell of turpentine and honey when crushed. Between them, the lichens crust the open stone in rust and sulfur and a violet so dark it looks black until the light catches it. The mosses gather in the hollows, some of them carnivorous, their tiny cups brimming with a sweet-smelling fluid that is really a trap. And flat against the rock, in pale thread-like nets, lies the translucent plant with no leaves at all, photosynthesizing through its skin like something halfway between vegetation and a rumor. In the deepest hollows, the contorted almost-trees lean sideways and press their flat canopies to the ground, flowering briefly in a magenta so violent it seems to belong to another world.

These are the sessile population: the ones who cannot leave, who must make do with the water that arrives and the soil that accumulates around them. They are not passive. They are the reason there is soil at all. They catch the fog, hold the damp, and slowly convert their own dead tissue and the dead tissue of everything else into the thin dark tea-smelling earth that feeds the next generation.

The great population, though, is the invertebrates. Beetles move through the moss in their thousands, armored and deliberate, like tiny cattle grazing a wet pasture. Flies hover in the warm damp air in columns, their wings catching the pearl-light of morning. The wingless crickets drum their abdomens against hollow stems, so that on warm afternoons the whole plateau hums faintly, a distant engine made of insects. Spiders are everywhere: web-builders strung between the shrub colonies, and a large, slow, dark species that lives in the cracks of the stone pavements and emerges at dawn to drink the condensation beaded on the rock. Snails travel between damp pockets with banded shells and leave trails that shine silver in the fog. These creatures are the middle of the world here—the ones who eat and are eaten, who make the place hum and tick and rustle.

The vertebrates are few in kind but enormous in number. The round brown frog lives in every puddle and wet hollow, announcing itself with a single low note repeated at long intervals, so that at any moment you might hear three frogs calling from different directions, each answering something you cannot hear. The finger-long lizard basks on the warm stone in the brief afternoons and vanishes at the first footstep. And then there is the bird—the only large animal, the closest thing the downs have to a personage. It is chicken-sized, mottled grey-brown, almost flightless, with a long curved beak for probing the soil after grubs. It moves in loose flocks of a dozen or so, clucking softly to itself, unafraid of anything because there is nothing here to fear. It is not a dramatic creature. It is a modest, conversational, ground-dwelling landlord, walking through its damp domain with the confidence of something that has never been hunted.

What binds all these inhabitants together is not competition so much as circulation. The frogs eat the insects; the birds eat the frogs and the snails and the beetles; the spiders eat whatever blunders into their webs or cracks; and everything that dies becomes soil for the lichens and the shrubs, which feed the insects, which feed the frogs. The whole population runs on a slow pulse of decay and renewal, driven by the drip of condensation and the short, soft, warm rains that come perhaps sixty times a year. Nothing is wasted. Every dead thing is eaten. Every surface is colonized. Every drop of water is used.

At night, the frog-call multiplies, and the downs become a loose, overlapping chorus of single notes—each one distinct, each one patient, the sound of a hundred small creatures announcing themselves to no one in particular. The inhabitants are not trying to become anything else. They are not building toward a climax or an exit. They are simply living in the way this place has taught them: small, damp, tough, intertwined, and in no hurry at all.
