The one who lives there is not in the lit water. It keeps to the lower reaches, where the amber haze gives out and the pipes go on being pipes in the dark.

It is tall and narrow, longer than a person should be, with a back that has learned to fold under valve housings and shoulders that can pass between pipe bundles without scraping. Its skin is not quite skin: a wet, greenish hide, furred in places with a moss that is not accidental, and in other places smooth and pale as the inside of a ceramic shard. Copper has gotten into it. You can see the faint blush of it in the creases of its joints, along the ridges of its forearms, in the thin webs between fingers that are too long and too jointed and end in broad, blunt pads. Its hands are the hands of something that has spent centuries seating gaskets and turning iron. The nails are dark and thick and shaped like small keys.

It has no need of eyes in the deep, and the ones it has are not much use. They sit shallow and clouded, like pearls in warm water, and they do not track you so much as register the pressure of you. It knows where you are by the displacement of your body and the warmth you bring and the small, stupid noises you make. It has a mouth, a horizontal slit, but it does not speak with it. When it wants to answer the machinery, it clicks. The click comes from somewhere in its throat, low and dry, and the pumps click back, and that is most of the conversation.

It breathes the water. Not fast. Its ribs are ribbed like pipe segments, and along their sides are frills of red tissue, the same red as the growth that climbs the hot copper. It draws the warm water in and lets it out, and the motion is so slow you could mistake it for the sway of a fern. Its pulse is slower still. It beats with the engines, or the engines beat with it — one deep, patient thud, then a long nothing, then another.

It does not eat the moss. It does not eat the ferns, though it prunes them where they would choke a valve, and it clears the drifting leaves from the weir grates with a careful, sweeping motion of one hand. It does not graze. It does not need to. It sips the water, and the water is warm and tastes of iron and salt and copper, and that is enough for a thing that has learned to live on heat and mineral and time. It is why the place is so quiet. It is why nothing darts in the green. There is no prey here, and no predator. There is only the work, and the one who does it.

You will not see it if you are looking for a person. It moves along the upper surfaces of the pipes, pulling itself by the rungs and the valve wheels, and it leaves almost no wake. It can hang for hours in the shadow of a weir with its frills folded flat and its mossy back turned to the light. It is not hiding. It is waiting for you to finish being surprised. If you touch a valve it does not want touched, it will not chase you. It will close a different valve, somewhere below, and the water will go where it should go, and you will find yourself drifting in a direction you did not choose.

It is the reason the place feels as though someone has just left. It has just left. It is always one chamber down, or behind the bundle of copper lines, or under the sheet of water that spills over the weir without a sound. It is the warmth in the pipe you put your hand on. It is the fiddlehead that has been eased out of a valve mouth rather than broken. It is the screw you find lying on a ledge, moved from where it fell, because it was in the way of a grate. It leaves order instead of footprints.

It has been there longer than anyone remembers, and it does not remember its own name. It remembers the mountain when it was colder and emptier, and it remembers the first pipes going in, and it remembers the hands that laid them. It does not remember whether those hands were its own. It may have been human once. It may have been several humans, or one human and something else, or a thing the mountain grew around the work. It does not think about this. It thinks about the weir, and the engines, and the slow closing of green over every seam. It thinks about the next valve, and the one after that.

If you go far enough down, past the last of the light, you will find it in the warm dark, hanging among the pipes with its cloudy eyes open and its red frills breathing. It will not greet you. It will not ask you to leave. It will simply continue to turn the water around the mountain, patient as a heart, and you will understand that you are the only thing in the basin that is in a hurry.
