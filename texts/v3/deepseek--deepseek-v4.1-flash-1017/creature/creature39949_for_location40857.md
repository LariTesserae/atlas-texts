What lives here is a single, large, slow animal, and there are perhaps four hundred of them in the whole highland sea. They are called **the grazers** by no one, since there is no one to name them, but that is what they are.

They are the shape of a fat comma, or a seed pod, or a folded leaf — two and a half meters at their longest, and they are made almost entirely of a clear, gelatinous tissue that holds its shape the way a water-filled balloon does. You can see through one to the fronds behind it, though the light bends slightly at the edges, so a grazer at a distance reads as a wobble in the green, a place where the forest is briefly not quite itself. They have no shell, no skeleton, no limbs. The outer skin is faintly opalescent when the light catches it, and it is stippled with thousands of tiny ciliated hairs that beat in slow waves, rowing the animal forward at about the speed of a leaf falling through water.

Along the underside of the comma — the belly of it — runs a broad, soft foot, like a snail's, and this is the part that does the work. The grazer settles onto a stand of middle-story fronds and the foot spreads over them, and then it does not so much eat as *absorb*. It presses down and the fronds soften and go pale beneath it, and over the course of a day the leaf-stuff becomes grazer-stuff, slowly, without hurry. The leftover mineral grit, the fine pumice dust the plants take up, collects in a pale gut-sac at the narrow end of the comma and is released in a thin, continuous plume, a soft rain of clean rock-dust drifting back down toward the ridges it came from. This is why the grazers are always slightly upstream of where the water is clearest: they are not cleaning it on purpose, but they clean it anyway.

They do not see, exactly. There are no eyes. But the skin of the grazer is light-sensitive everywhere, and it is also *pressure*-sensitive — the cilia feel the slow breathing of the water body, the lift and settle of the canopy, the pressure change that means a current has shifted somewhere out on a ridge. A grazer knows when the forest above it has risen a meter, and it knows it hours before a human swimmer would. It responds to this by doing very little. It may drift upward a little. It may let go of its frond and tumble, passive, until it touches another. The grazers do not seek. They arrive.

What is strange about them, and what makes them the true inhabitants of this place and not just its livestock, is their reproduction, which is tied to the flowering of the drowned woodland. When the canopy flowers — which it does in loose, unsynchronized waves, so that some part of the highland sea is always flowering somewhere — the pollen drifts in its slow clouds, and the grazers take it in through the same skin that takes in their food. For most of the year this does nothing. But twice in a decade, when the warmth from below and the warmth from above line up just so — when the trapped heat in the deep basins rises and meets the humid lid and the water holds a particular even temperature for many weeks — the pollen in the grazers does something. The animals, which have been solitary and scattered across the whole drowned upland, begin to drift together. Not toward each other exactly — the currents do most of the work — but they stop resisting the currents, and the currents are slow and patient and wide, and over a season they gather the grazers into loose knots in the deep saddles between ridges.

And then the knots come apart into something else. A grazer in the gathering will begin to divide — not split in two, but to *grow outward*, its clear tissue ballooning and thinning until it is a sheet, until it is a film no thicker than a leaf, and then the film tears along the grain of its own cilia and becomes many small copies of a grazer, each one the size of a thumbnail, each one pale and incomplete. These are released into the warm water in immense numbers and most of them die. The water in the saddles turns faintly milky with them for a month. The ones that survive settle onto the low creeping weed and grow, very slowly, over years, into the clear fat commas. A grazer lives a long time — the length of a human lifetime, perhaps longer — and it spends almost all of that time doing what it does: pressed to a frond, absorbing light and leaf, releasing dust, drifting when the water tells it to drift.

They are quiet animals. They make almost no sound except a soft, low-frequency thrumming when they divide, a sound that carries for kilometers through the warm water and is felt rather than heard, like a hand pressed against a drum from the other side. The rest of the time they are silent. A swimmer passing through the forest might pass within a meter of one and not notice, because a grazer at rest inside the layered green is nearly invisible — a lens, a wobble, a place where the fronds bend slightly and then bend back.

And in the cold seams down between the ridges, in the deep fractures where the young rock still lets out its heat in thin lines and the water is not warm but merely not-cold, there are things that are not grazers. They are slower. They are harder to see. They may be the grazers' young, or their dead, or something else entirely that has learned to live in the seams and never rises. Nothing in this place has decided yet. The rock is young. The forest is new to the water. The animals are still figuring out what they are.
