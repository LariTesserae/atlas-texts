No one lives on the Dauber Flats in the sense of keeping an address. The place is inhabited anyway—sparsely, exactly, by things that have passed the test of being able to stay.

The first inhabitant is the wind. It is not a ghost or a symbol; it is the only full-time resident, and it lives there the way a river lives in its bed. It has routes and habits. It comes off the high ground and pours across the flats, spins dust devils in hundreds, braids its own tracks into the rock, carries grain like a slow tool. It is the thing that erases footprints, tunes the standing rods, finds the hollows in old metal and makes the whole plateau ring. If you arrive, the wind is what notices you—not as a person, but as a new warmth, a new obstruction, a small change in pressure. It is the landlord and the tenant both.

Then there are the placed lives. Not a population; a placement. The gray-green mats of woody lichen creep along old fractures in slow fractal spreads, furred against the grit, holding their little seam of shelter for centuries. The stiff pale grass tufts stand bristled and alone, each rooted by a single deep taproot into a crack, as if the plateau had permitted one root and no more. The dwarf pines—if they are pines—are the closest thing here to persons: each one a person’s height, twisted permanently in the direction of the prevailing wind, standing alone on its own small pedestal of rock with a century of wind-halo around it. They are not a forest. They are a scatter of individual gestures, each one a long answer to the same question.

The animals are rare and exact. A knuckle-sized beetle, lacquered black, crosses a varnished slab on business that has nothing to do with you. A kestrel hangs motionless in the gale, using the roar of the whole plateau as its hunting instrument, then is gone. Their tracks are the only handwriting the flats permit, and the wind erases the record within the hour. Nothing here is abundant. Everything here is economical, armored, patient.

The old works are inhabitants too, though not biological ones. The inlaid metal lines that follow fractures for a hundred meters, flush and tarnished but unweathered, humming faintly at a certain pitch. The low pier cantilevered out over nothing, neither rusting nor chalking, simply waiting. The field of thin standing rods arranged on a spiral, no taller than a person, through which the wind passes and becomes not noise but a minute correction of pressure. These are not ruins; ruins imply collapse. They are reflexes left running—a maintenance intelligence so old it no longer needs to speak. They respond to wind and to presence, but they do not answer. They live the way a thermostat lives: as a tendency toward a condition.

Their makers are not ghosts. They are extinct in the way a solved equation is extinct—not absent so much as finished. They left no dramatic wreckage, only a standard of care. They may have scoured the plateau flat, or finished it flat; the difference stopped mattering long ago. If you want to meet them, watch the rods turn a gust into a quietness, and understand that someone once thought that was worth doing well.

And you, if you are there, are an inhabitant too: brief, warm, anomalous. A second warm body in that landscape is a significant object. The plateau will feel you as a change in the wind. Your tracks will last an hour. You are the only thing there that is in a hurry.

So what lives on the Dauber Flats? A gale with habits. A lichen that thinks in fractures. Grass that has decided on one root. Pines that are permanent gestures. A beetle with private business. A kestrel that hunts with the whole sky. A maintenance intelligence older than ruin. And the temporary weather of anyone who comes. It is not empty. It is sparsely furnished with the exact things that can stay.
