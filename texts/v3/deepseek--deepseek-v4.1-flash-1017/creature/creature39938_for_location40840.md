They are a people who have been pressed into the shape of the deep. They would not call themselves a nation, or even a tribe; they name the room they sleep in, the current that carries a smell, the bowl that holds a strain of light. They are small and compact, shorter than we would be, with broad torsos and limbs that move in slow, economical arcs. Their skin is the color of pale fungus—gray-green, blue-white, sometimes flushed with a sickly amber where the bioluminescence touches them—and it is always slightly damp, mottled with darker patches like the caps they cultivate. Their hands are wide, the fingers long and thick-jointed, webbed to the second knuckle; their feet are broader still, splayed and soft, made for pushing against water rather than walking on stone. Their eyes are large and dark, the pupils slow to open and close, and they have a way of looking past a lamp rather than into it. Their voices are low and used sparingly. A conversation sounds like water moving over a lip of stone.

They are not fish, but they are not quite air-creatures either. They can spend a long day in the cold water, breathing in slow, held measures, and they return to dry chambers caught in the old architecture—pockets of air sealed with fungal paste, renewed by the patient chemistry of the stone. They move between water and air as we move between rooms. Their lives are governed by the slow turn of currents and the growth of crops, not by any sky. They have no word for day. They have words for *the long dimming*, *the brightening*, *the current that comes from the west and smells of old shell*, *the time when the kelp is ready*.

A household is a niche in the porous city: a sleeping shelf, a rack of drying kelp, a low table of smoothed stone, a cluster of bowls. The bowls are the center of the home. Each holds a captive culture of luminous fungus, fed on mineral grit and shaded with carved lids. By moving a lid, a person can make a room brighter or dimmer, can send a slow pulse of light along a passage, can mark the turn of a conversation without speaking. This is their craft and their grammar. A light-keeper may spend an hour coaxing a strain until it burns a clean, cold blue; then carry the bowl to a child’s sleeping niche and set it where the glow will not fall directly on the eyes.

They harvest the kelp in long, tough ribbons, cutting with bone hooks that are heirlooms. True bone is rare now; the hooks are cut from hard white spars found in the oldest strata, and no one living has seen the creature they came from. The hooks are reworked and handed down until they are more handle than blade. The people eat the kelp and the fleshy fungi; they press the caps into cakes, ferment them in stone jars, strip the kelp into thread and chew it slowly. They do not cook. Heat is a thing they know from bodies and the friction of tools, not from fire. Their meals are cold and quiet, taken in the dark or by the faint light of a bowl.

They are many, but they live in a dense, quiet saturation rather than a crowd. They cluster in the old swollen structures, in the spaces between domes and ribbed towers, in passages that intertwine and grow through one another. There is no king among them, no army, no market in the way we would know it. There are light-keepers and current-readers, growers and menders, elders who remember the long cycles of the water. The rules are not laws so much as procedures: conserve the light, tend the crops, respect the currents, and remember that the world above is a story and the world below is forever. Children learn by imitation, their hands guided over the lip of a bowl or the curve of a kelp blade. History is held in the shape of tools and the route of a hand through a passage. They mark bowls with notches, but they do not write; they remember.

Their language is low speech and touch. Because sound travels strangely in the deep, they lean close and speak into a shoulder; they press fingertips to a forearm to say *yes*, *wait*, *not this passage*, *the current is turning*. They use light as punctuation. A slow dimming can mean *listen*; a quick double pulse can mean *come*. They are not silent people, but their sounds are few: the scrape of a bone hook, the soft thrum of water against a sealed wall, the murmur of a conversation held in flickering blue.

They tell of the world above as a story—a ceiling of shifting silver light, a place of air and dry stone. It is not heaven to them. It is simply where the story begins. They do not long for it, because longing requires the sense of another way, and they have no other way. The drowned world is old, so old that it has settled into its final form, and they have settled with it. They tend the city as a garden. They clear the soft growth from a passage, harvest a crop, feed a bowl, mend a seal. A life is a long maintenance.

The dead are carried to a far chamber and left to the fungi. Their names are spoken while anyone remembers them, and then they are not spoken again. If a bone is sound, it may become a tool, and the tool may outlast the name. The people do not find this sad. They are the last inhabitants of a drowned continent, and they are not waiting to be rescued. They wake, they tend, they harvest, they sleep. The city has grown around their small warm bodies the way the fungus grows around a bowl. They are the slow pulse in the liquid dark.
