The people of Ammer are not the sort you count first. You notice their hands. They are always doing something with their hands — knocking a knuckle against a doorpost, turning a tool over, pinching moss from a threshold and letting it fall, touching the rope span before they trust it. The hands are broad and soft-skinned and never quite dry, and they move in small, economical signs even when no one is watching. A conversation between two Ammeri at ten paces looks like two people adjusting invisible knots.

They are pale, of course, and large-eyed, and they have the particular alertness of people who have spent their lives distinguishing one grey from another. They are faintly wet at all times. It has stopped being weather to them. The fog beads on their sleeves; it collects in the hollow of a collarbone and runs when they bend; they wipe their faces with the backs of their wrists and do not think about it. Their voices are low because the cleft swallows sound. They shout only when angry, and they are rarely angry, and even then the shout arrives as a mumble and they have to repeat it with their hands.

They live in a town of four hundred rooms and there are thirty-one of them, perhaps thirty-four. This does not make them lonely in the way an outsider expects. It makes them busy. They walk a rotation through the empty rooms — not to use them, not even to clean them, but to touch them. A hand on a cold hearth. A palm flat against a doorframe. A tool lifted and set down again. In Ammer, stillness is how things are claimed, and so a room you have not entered for a year is not a room you own. It is a room that has been given. The people know this the way you know your own name, and they go out into the green silence of their half-empty town and take it back, one touch at a time.

They work wood and stone and rope and bone. They wedge the bright new rock, which splits willingly and has not yet learned to be stubborn. They coppice the vines. They keep goats on the highest terrace, which is a mistake every year and continues anyway. They eat moss-crop and millipede — mashed, smoked, pressed into leaf-wrapped bricks — and snails, and a flat sour bread from a grain that will grow in rubble if you are patient. They drink from drip-catches that taste of moss and wet stone and faint chalk. Once a year, in the cold season, the whole town goes out to re-tie the terraces against the living rock, slackening here, taking up there, resetting the rope-beds as the mountain rises its few finger-widths a decade. It is the closest thing they have to a festival. Nobody shouts. There are hand signals across the cleft, and a dull padded knocking that does not carry.

And the animals are everywhere. That is the fact visitors cannot get used to. The people have stopped seeing them, mostly, but they are there: the millipede herds moving through the moss in their thousands, pale and segmented and the length of a hand, cropping the moss from ropes and roofs and thresholds. The people follow them with switches, herding them gently from span to span. The millipedes are the town’s mowers. They are also, being numerous and slow and made of meat, the basis of everything else.

There are snails with shells thin enough to see the animal’s pulse through. Glass-winged flies that hang in the fog in columns and close behind you when you pass. Moths the size of two hands, so heavy with moisture they tumble along the terraces at dusk like blown leaves. In the cistern-jars, small pale things give off a faint cold light — enough to find a doorway, not enough to find your footing. In the moss, mites in their millions, a whole nation per handspan. In the wet rot of the oldest roofs, a soft white crawling economy that exists nowhere else and has no name in the town’s speech, only a gesture: the fingers spread and turned down. There is no birdsong in Ammer. The birds here are few and small and know better.

The heron-thing is the exception to all of it. Four legs, too tall, too thin, wet grey fur rather than feathers. It walks the rope spans at night with a delicacy that makes no sound whatsoever — no creak, no shift, just a slow placement of weight that the ropes accept without comment. It hunts the millipede herds. It has never harmed a person. It will stand in an empty room for an hour and look at you and then leave, and you will not have heard it arrive or go. There are perhaps three of them on this face of the mountain. Nobody knows where they nest. Nobody has looked very hard. If you ask, an Ammeri will make a shape with their hands — tall, four-legged, patient — and then change the subject to rope.

But the strangest inhabitants are the ropes themselves. The town is held together by cordage, and the mountain is wet enough and the air rich enough that rope left alone is colonized. Moss first, in the twist of it. Then a thread-fine creeper. Then, within a decade, the rope is a vine that remembers being a rope: it has kept the shape, kept the tension, and it is now green and growing and holding the terrace up more surely than hemp ever did. The oldest spans in Ammer are alive. They thicken. They have to be pruned or they will lift the roof they were meant to hold. Walls of split stake have sprouted. A doorframe flowered one spring and was left, because it was beautiful, and had to be re-hung around.

So what lives in Ammer is not just the thirty-odd people, nor the millipede herds, nor the silent heron-thing, nor the moss and the mites and the pale cistern lights. It is a compact. The people touch the rooms to keep them; the millipedes crop the moss that would otherwise unmake the ropes; the heron-thing walks the spans and takes its share; the vines hold what they were once tied to hold. Nothing is dry, nothing is still, and almost nothing is loud. The town is not being built. It is being kept, one hand at a time, by people who know exactly how little is needed and how much of that little must be done every single day.
