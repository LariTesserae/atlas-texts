**The Seep-Kept**

The people of Hollow Reach are not a population so much as a slow acclimatization. They were human once, or near enough, and they are still human the way a stalactite is still water. They call themselves the seep-kept, or the stayed, or by no name at all. Outsiders call them Hollowers, which they tolerate without correcting.

You notice their hands first. The skin at the wrists and ankles has a mineral bloom, fine as frost, and when they press a palm to a wall the moisture there hesitates, then finds a channel around the memory of their fingers. Their hands are always cool and slightly tacky. Their eyes are pale—not blind, just adapted to gray—and they blink slowly, as if each blink is a small decision. Their hair grows thick and slow; old men have the hair of young men and the faces of weather. Their clothes are felt, oiled paper, soft leather. Hems stiffen with seep and are scraped each season. They do not smell bad. They smell like wet slate and clean cold.

They walk with a deliberate half-beat of lag. Not clumsily—they have learned to let the world arrive. They meet your eyes a half-second after you meet theirs, and this is not rudeness but courtesy; they are giving your arrival time to catch up. Their greetings are short. *Hold*, they say. *Walk slow.* They do not say goodbye.

They live in keeps, not houses. A keep is a room the district has agreed to keep for now. The walls are thick and damp; the ceiling has a definite top this year; the floor is a shallow dish of stone that never quite dries. They sleep on raised platforms of composite, under blankets that are never fully dry and never quite mold. They keep few things: cups, a glass rod for listening, a bowl of pale moss, a stone that was a corner last year. Their furniture grows. A bench that was a ledge is becoming a bench; they sit on it while it does. They do not repair so much as negotiate. They clear capillary channels with a fingernail, press felt into seams to slow the water, encourage the moss where it holds the dust, discourage it where it opens the wall.

Some are channel-tenders, who keep the sweat of the district moving. Some are resonance-listeners, who press their ears to walls and note when the low harmonic changes pitch. Some are boundary-walkers, who walk the fog edge and leave small cairns of wet stone. The cairns move; the walkers move them back; neither side is upset. Some scrape the mineral film into jars, to set pigments, to flavor tea, to mend a cracked cup. No one farms. No one builds from plans. They have a word, *hessit*, which means the moment a thing becomes more itself than it was, and they watch for it and then look away.

They live in households of two or three, rarely more. At the low hour, when the short rain has passed and the cold steam rises, they come out into the seams and stand together without needing to talk. They exchange observations: the puddle by the third seam is lagging longer; the wall behind the moss is thickening; the alley that was short yesterday is long today. They do not map. They remember routes as sequences—cold on the left cheek, the hiss of a particular drip, the place where a shadow falls too precisely. Directions are given by sound and texture: *past the wall that breathes*, *where the stone tastes of iron*, *toward the low note*.

Children are rare and unset. They are born ordinary and become local by degrees. They are taught not to stare at walls, not to count steps in a seam, not to name a corner twice. They play with the late reflections in puddles, reaching for their own hands a half-second behind. They are not warned with stories of monsters; they are warned with the truth: the district is still deciding, and a fixed gaze is a kind of vote. When a child begins to move with the lag without thinking, the household relaxes. The child is becoming kept.

The seep-kept are not religious, exactly. They have no temples; the district is too provisional for that. But they have customs. When a corner changes, they leave a small stone at the place where it used to be. When a sound arrives late, they answer it politely—*I heard you*—so it knows it was received. They keep late journals, written after the events they record, because memory here arrives with the puddles. They do not try to fix the district’s thinness. They know that pinning a thing down is how you make it stop holding. Their central etiquette is negative: do not observe too long, do not measure too exactly, do not insist on the same corner twice. They say: *If it holds, don’t watch it. If it watches back, walk slow.*

Outsiders come rarely. They are met with polite silence and a cup of tea that tastes of stone and cold. The seep-kept answer questions honestly and incompletely. They will tell you which seam is safe today. They will not tell you how long it takes to walk it, because that would be a measurement, and measurements here are a kind of pressure. Most outsiders leave before the second rain. Some stay. No one decides to become seep-kept; they simply stop leaving, and one day their hands are cool and tacky and they blink a half-second late, and the district has absorbed them without comment.

Other things live there too. The pale mosses and filamentous growths are tended but not owned. The small gray passers—unidentifiable, unbothered—are left alone; the seep-kept call them *the ones who didn’t stop*. There are also the half-made things: a puddle that has learned a shape, a shadow that arrives before its object, a wall that is more description than stone. The people do not worship these. They do not hunt them. They cohabit.

At night the district ticks and drips and finds its harmonic, and the seep-kept lie awake or half-awake, listening. They are not waiting for Hollow Reach to finish. They know finishing was never the point. They are the place’s way of being inhabited while it is still being drafted—patient, cold, unhurried, and only slightly surprised, each morning, that the floor is still there.
