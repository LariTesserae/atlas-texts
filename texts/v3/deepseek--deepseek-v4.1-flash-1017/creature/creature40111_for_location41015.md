The people call themselves the Kesh — or something like it, a word made mostly of breath and a click at the end, so that saying it produces a small puff of the wet air and a sound like a stem knocking against another stem. There are nineteen of them. There have been nineteen for as long as anyone remembers, and when one dies there are eighteen, and then, within a year or two, there are nineteen again, because the basin does not tolerate empty space. Every surface is occupied. Every gap is filled.

They are short — none of them taller than the middle of your chest — and their bodies are compact in a way that has nothing to do with hardship. They are not thin. They are dense, the way the creepers are dense, with broad hands and short, powerful fingers that can pry a knotted vine apart without a blade. Their skin is the deep brown of old wood, and it is always faintly damp, and they smell of the same sweet green smell as everything else, so that when one of them stands still in the vegetation you can lose them entirely, and you will only know they are there because the hollow stems near them have turned, very slightly, toward the sound of their breathing.

Their faces are broad and calm and their eyes are large and dark and set wide, and they have a habit of blinking slowly, in sequence, one eye and then the other, like something surfacing. They do not look at you directly when they are thinking. They look at the leaves.

They wear the silver-gray of the ceiling vines, beaten and softened and cut into long wrapped panels that they wind around themselves in layers, and the layers are not decorative. Each panel is a different age and a different weave, and the outermost is always the newest, and the innermost is always the softest, and a Kesh can tell you, if you ask, which year each layer was made in and who made it and what was happening in the basin that season. Their clothing is a record. They do not write. They do not need to. The whole basin is a record, if you know how to read the growth rings and the leaf scars and the places where an arch has been braided and re-braided because the weight of the platform above it kept changing.

They are pruners. That is the closest word. They spend their days moving up and down through the layers with their narrow blades and their curved hooks, cutting back the growth where it has begun to crowd a passage, training the columns where they have begun to lean, pulling down the ceiling vines to repair a platform floor. The basin would fill itself in entirely if they stopped. They know this. They have always known this. It is not a burden and it is not a battle; it is the same as breathing. When you cut a vine here, it grows back, and the growing back is the point. They do not clear. They negotiate. They cut in spirals and curves, never in straight lines, because a straight cut heals wrong and the plant thickens around it and the arch fails. Their blades are bone. Their hooks are hard wood. The blade of a Kesh who has been pruning for forty years is worn down to a sliver no longer than a finger, and it is the most valuable thing they own, and it will be laid on the creepers with them when they die.

Their days are shaped by the air, which is not the same at all hours. The slow tidal breathing of the basin has a rhythm — in for a long time, out for a long time, and the transitions are the important part. When the air is drawing in, the hollow stems drone low and the condensation beads thickest on the highest leaves, and that is when the Kesh climb. They go up through the layers, hand over hand along the vine ladders, up to the ceiling where the silver leaves are wet, and they hold their shallow bowls under the leaves and touch them, and the beads run down and collect, and they drink, and they fill the smaller vessels they carry at their hips. When the air is pushing out, the drone rises and the insects rise with it and the flying things come out of the vegetation, and the Kesh come down and stay low and mend and braid and talk in their low voices, and the plants lean toward them.

They do not farm, exactly. There is nothing to farm in the way you would mean it. But they harvest — the fat white grubs from under the lowest creepers, which are rich and taste of green; the pale fruit of the mid-layer shrubs, which is hollow and holds a mouthful of water; the hard shells of the flying things, which they collect when the things die and which they grind and mix with sap to make a paste that seals a cut vine. And they harvest the sound. This is harder to explain. The Kesh listen the way you look. They know the calls of the ceiling chorus the way you know the faces of your family, each voice distinct, each answer meaningful, and they can tell from the pattern of the rounds whether the air is turning, whether something has died in the upper layers, whether a column is about to fail. The chorus is a report. It is also, and this matters, a conversation they are part of. They answer it. They have small hollow reeds they blow through, and they put the calls back into the air, and the chorus answers them, and the answering is not random. It has been going on for a very long time.

There are children. Two or three at a time, no more, and they are not raised by their parents in particular. They are raised by the town, which is to say by the layers, which is to say they are turned loose into the vegetation as soon as they can climb and they are expected to learn it the way you learn a language — by being in it, by being lost in it, by being found. A child of the Kesh at six years old can move through the ceiling vines faster than you can walk on flat ground. At eight they can name every call in the chorus. At ten they are given their first blade, dull, and they practice cutting spirals on the creepers, and the creepers grow back around the cuts and the scars are a child's handwriting on the basin. The Kesh do not correct them much. The basin corrects them. A bad cut fails, and the arch sags, and the child learns.

They do not have a word for the basin that is separate from the word for themselves. This is not poetry. It is literal. When a Kesh says *kesh* they mean the people and the plants and the air and the slow tidal breathing and the noise, all of it, and the distinction between the ones who prune and the ones who are pruned is not a firm one. They know that the creepers will grow over them. They have watched it happen. When one of them is dying — of age, of a fall, of the fever that sometimes comes when the air turns wrong — they are carried down to the lowest layer and laid on the mat, and the town gathers, and the chorus gathers, and the plants lean in, and the drone of the hollow stems is very loud. And then the dying one is left, and the creepers grow over them, and the mat thickens, and in a season there is no sign except that the growth there is a little richer and the leaves a little larger and the sound of the drone a little fuller. The Kesh will tell you that this is not a burial. It is a promotion. The one who died is now part of the layer that everything else is rooted in.

The other inhabitants — the ones who are not Kesh — are not subjects or pets or livestock. They are neighbors, and some of them are older neighbors. The pale green thing that moves like a sloth is called something that sounds like a wet click, and it is not a lizard, and it is not slow, exactly; it simply does not hurry, and it has been in the basin longer than the Kesh have, and the Kesh know this and treat it with a courtesy that is not quite reverence. It eats the mid-layer fruit and it sleeps in the arches and it is not afraid of the Kesh and it does not particularly acknowledge them. The wide-winged fliers — there are four of them, maybe five — beat their membranous wings so slowly that you can watch each beat, and they do not fly so much as swim through the thick air, and the Kesh say that when one of them dies the air turns wrong for a season afterward, and they may be right.

And the chorus. The chorus is the loudest thing in the basin and the least understood, and the Kesh do not claim to understand it. They claim to be part of it. They say the ceiling vines are not where the chorus lives; the ceiling vines are the chorus, the way the hollow stems are the drone, the way the creepers are the floor and the floor is the dead and the dead are the floor. The clicks and trills and rounds are the basin talking to itself, and the Kesh are one of the voices, and so are you, now, standing at the edge of the town with the warm wet air pressing against you and the sound rising and falling around you. You have been here an hour. Something in the upper layers has already turned, very slightly, to listen to the sound of your breathing.
