What lives there is not one species but a settlement-shaped symbiosis. At the center of it are the people—slow, dark-eyed, deliberate—who think of themselves less as owners of the sinkhole than as hands in a long argument between root, water, and weight. They are not built for speed. Their bodies are broad and low, their breathing a slow bellows over soft, folded gill-veils at the throat, their eyes large and almost black against the green-gold murk. Their hands are the part of them the place has shaped most: strong enough to crush a finger-thick root, delicate enough to tease apart a single living fiber and splice it into a new wall. They do not swim so much as steer their own buoyancy, wearing vine bladders at the waist and pulling themselves along the lattice hand over hand, like people moving through a dream that has decided to be dense.

They are born into closeness. Children are kept in the upper, fresher water, where the light is least murky, and they learn early to be still—how to hang in a doorway and listen, how to tell the *tock-tock-tock* of a boring grub from the harmless tick of a seed pod against a roof. They learn to braid soft root tips before they learn to read. By the time they are old enough to leave the family alcove alone, they can poke a suspicious root with a trimming knife before passing it, can feel the difference between a healthy joint and one that has begun to grind, can name the family two homes over by the pitch of its beetles and the blue of its lamp-fungus.

A household is a pocket of woven root, sealed with pale crustacean cement and insulated by a mat of fungus. It is not dry in the way a surface house is dry; the weave holds a household’s own warmth and scent, a pocket of less cold and less salt. Privacy is made of sound and light. One family’s home is a low, contained hum from the beetle chambers; another is a particular soft blue glow, a lamp-cup fungus trained to grow in a spiral by the door. A visitor learns to announce themselves by tapping a rhythm on the threshold root, because a voice does not carry far and a shout is considered a small emergency.

Their daily work is cultivation. They do not build; they persuade. A new room begins as a bundle of root tips guided around a frame of older wood, trimmed and grafted and whispered to with the pressure of hands. When a seam must be made unbreakable, a weaver carries a smear of joiner-larvae on a root-brush—small, semi-sessile crustaceans that look like pale feathery barnacles with limestone mouths—and the jointers settle in, secrete their calcite cement, and are paid in sugars and safe harbor by the plants. The people tend the bladder-vine for floats and storage pouches. They hollow root chambers for hum-beetles, whose bodies are food and whose drone is a family signature. They harvest fungus, shrimp, grubs, and the occasional water-fat seed pod that drops from a high branch with a distant *plink*. Nothing is wasted. A fallen leaf is passed through a dozen mouths before it becomes silt.

The place is thick with other inhabitants, and the people are only one organ in it. Skittering shrimp move through root channels in whispering colonies. Luminous worms and flat fungi cling to undersides and make local doorways out of the dark. The great structural roots groan under their own weight, and the elder weavers press their palms to the wood and read the stress in those groans the way another people might read a page. Predators are slow and patient: a translucent tentacled thing that mimics the texture of a common root, so that children are taught to distrust any root that is too even, too soft; a predatory flatworm that glides across ceilings to take an unguarded pupa. Homes keep their ceiling fungus bright not only for light but because the flatworm’s shadow moves before it does.

The people are governed by the Agreement, which is less a law than a habit of survival: no structure may grow so large that it collapses and damages its neighbors. Disputes are settled by a council of elder weavers who can feel a bad joint through their palms and who know which household’s new room is pressing too hard against an old one. The greatest crime is to bring blight or a boring insect into the communal lattice, and a household that conceals an infestation may find its root-tenure cut, its woven walls opened to the current, its people moved to a bare ledge to begin again. The lower brine is warm and heavy and does not mix easily with the fresh upper water; it is not a place to be cast casually.

The dead are carried down to the lowest abandoned rooms, where shrimp and worms reduce them and the root carries their names upward in the slowly accumulating weave. The old lower dwellings are already becoming reefs. The water is rising, imperceptibly, and each generation moves a little higher, settling new ledges on the sheer walls, grafting first rooms that will take decades to become truly watertight. The town is young, adolescent, still discovering its own walls. Its people are young with it—patient, tactile, close-speaking, more afraid of a hidden borer than of the dark.

And there is one more inhabitant, harder to point to: the Agreement itself. It lives in the pressure of a palm against a root, in the pitch of a family’s beetles, in the council’s long silence before a judgment, in the slow upward migration of households away from the warming brine. The sinkhole is not inhabited by a town and its people. It is inhabited by a continuous negotiation—root, crustacean, fungus, shrimp, worm, predator, and weaver—all pressed close, all adjusting, all refusing to grow so large that the whole close place comes down.
