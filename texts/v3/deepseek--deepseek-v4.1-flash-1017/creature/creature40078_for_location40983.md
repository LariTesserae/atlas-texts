The one who lives here is not an animal, not exactly a person, and not quite a separate thing from the forest. Call it the Tender, if you need a word. The dust knows it by weight.

It is tall and narrow, with the proportions of something that grew toward a light it never had to chase. Its body is a soft column of amber lattice, translucent enough that you can see the slow planetary pulse moving through it—a dark nucleus at the center, like the heart of a drifter but older and more patient. Its surface is dusted with the pale rose mineral until it seems almost furred with warmth. Its limbs are long and many-jointed, ending in broad, frond-like hands that do not grip so much as comb the air. It has no face. Where a face might be, there is a crown of microscopic scales that shiver when the humidity shifts, and a faint internal glow that brightens by a fraction when it is attending to something.

It does not eat. It exchanges. It stands close to the great amber trunks for days at a time, letting the resinous breath of the place move through its scales, and the hum in its body answers the hum in the wood. It drinks no water; there has never been rain. It takes what it needs from the air and gives back a warmth that settles into the dust behind it. It leaves no footprints. The rose talc closes over its path with a whisper, and by the time you look back, the ground has forgotten it.

It is the only large thing here that moves with intention, and even its intention is slow. Most of the time it is indistinguishable from a trunk: a tall amber absence at the edge of a grove, so still that a drifter may settle against its shoulder and refract tiny rainbows across its chest. But when a thin spot widens—when the blue dust shimmers and the air begins to loop back on itself—the Tender arrives. It does not repair the fray. It attends it. It kneels at the edge of the cold blue ground and presses one broad palm flat, and for a long while nothing happens except that the shimmer calms by a degree. It hums a counter-rhythm, not a sound exactly, more a pressure in the teeth, and the twisted lattices nearby remember how to be merely strange instead of impossible. It listens to the muffled conversations in the thin air. It does not answer, but sometimes its scales tighten, as if it almost understands the language and has decided not to.

Its memory is physical. It remembers a frond’s death by tasting the dust that frond became, and it can tell you—if it told things—which valley a drifter came from by the way the light bends inside its glass. It has no name for itself. It has no loneliness, because loneliness requires a before. It is a maintenance behavior that became a person, a hand of the world-sized machine that forgot it was a hand and simply kept tending. It is not happy. It is not unhappy. It is in equilibrium, which in this place is the closest thing to peace.

If you are very still, you may see it move: a lean, a slow unfolding, a step that does not disturb the dust. It will pass within arm’s reach and you will feel the hum in your jaw and the warmth of its amber body, and then it will be a trunk again, farther off, and the drifters will orbit it in silence, and the forest will go on exhaling its sweet, resinous breath as if nothing had happened. Nothing has. That is what it lives for.
