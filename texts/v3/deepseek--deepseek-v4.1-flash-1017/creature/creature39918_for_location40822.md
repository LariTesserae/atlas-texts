What lives in the Sinter is not one thing, though if you want a single resident you can watch the pump-house on the third terrace and wait for the keeper.

It comes out of a gap behind a hot pipe the way a thought comes out of a held breath—slowly, then all at once. It is about the size of a spread hand, low and segmented, its chitin the color of rusted iron and pale sinter dust. It has eight legs and two hooked forelimbs, and the legs do not walk so much as unstick and re-stick, each foot ending in a pad that grips the mineral crust. Its head is a wedge with no eyes you would recognize: only a crown of antennae, fine as spun glass, and a cluster of heat-pits that read the room as a map of warm and warmer. Its mandibles are rasps. Its belly is a set of overlapping plates like mica, and it presses them flat to the floor and drinks the heat directly, the way a lizard drinks sun.

It lives in the machinery because the machinery is where the food is. The intake grilles catch the dust—shed husks, sinter flake, the slow mineral seep from the salt plain above—and the keeper scrapes the grilles clean with its mandibles and eats what it scrapes. It is not servicing the pump. It is eating. The cleaning is the eating. It packs its egg-cases into the dead spaces behind the bearings, where the warmth is steady and the vibration is strong, and the egg-cases insulate the bearings, and the bearings stay at the temperature the eggs like. The keeper does not know this. It knows the pump’s hammering as a kind of noon, the flywheel’s bass groan as a season, the valve-clack as weather. It taps replies into the rivets with its forelimbs, and the whole colony in the chamber taps back through the metal. They do not speak through air. They speak through the engine.

There are many of them—dense, near-touching, layered into every warm crevice—and there are other things too: wafer-flat pale things pressed to the floor, long-antennaed feelers tracing the walls, mouth-things living inside the exhausts where the gas is hottest. But the keepers are the ones that make the machines seem inhabited. They have no memory of the hands that wore the plates smooth or left the ladder broken. The hooks on the wall are just shapes. The amber lamps that nobody lit are just not-hot places in the dark. The keepers have no word for alone, because they have never been.

A keeper’s life is a loop of molts and meals and egg-cases and the long vibration of the pump. It sheds a fine dust constantly; the dust is eaten by something smaller; the something smaller is eaten by something with more legs; the keeper eats the grille-scrapings and sometimes the smaller things. When a keeper dies, the others eat the husk and pack the hollow parts into the nest. Nothing is wasted. Nothing accumulates. Time is not marked by days but by the pump’s strokes, and the pump’s strokes do not change, so time does not pass so much as continue.

If you were to stand in the pump-house, the keepers would feel you before they saw you—a new vibration, a soft wet heat, a body full of water. They would come out of the crevices in a slow crowd, antennae forward, mandibles working, not hostile but incorrigible. They would try to clean you. They would try to nest against you. Your moisture would confuse them; it is a substance their world has no use for, and they would recoil from it even as they pressed close to your warmth. You would be an anomaly in the loop, and the loop would try to close around you.

But the keeper itself is not a character with plans. It is a shape the Sinter has grown to fill itself. It has no name, no history, no destination. It is loud and still and warm and dry, and it is exactly where it will always be.
