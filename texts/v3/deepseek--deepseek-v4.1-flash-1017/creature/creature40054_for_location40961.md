The first inhabitants you notice are not people. They are the crust: thin, brittle, faintly iridescent mats that spread in the lee of stones and feed on dust and warmth and the slow chemistry of the rock. They are not green and not quite alive in the way a plant is alive—closer to a rumor of life that has learned to keep house on stone. But they are the pasture under everything. The grazers move over them in slow herds, hand-sized and grit-plated, scraping the mats down a little at a time. The stone-borers are never seen, only inferred from neat round holes. The long pale things wait in crevices until the night air pools cold. The drift-mites hang in the dust-ropes, translucent and patient. And above it all the drifters spiral on the updraft—enormous, membranous, gas-bladdered, trailing filaments, casting soft moving shadows over the terraces and calling rarely in a low note that carries a long way.

The people live among these things as another slow species with tools. They are sparse and ledge-born and unhurried, and you would know one by the dust worked into the wrists and throat, the narrow eyes, the close-woven veil and cloak of sintered cloth, the hands calloused by cable and grit. They are not builders first. They are maintainers. A town here is a hundred low thick flat-roofed structures cut from the rock or built of it, joined by stairs and terraced paths, and then kilometers of empty ledge before the next town—and the emptiness is not neglect. It is the shape of the life. They keep the kilns and the cable-yards. They mend the crust-mats where the ore-cars have cut them. They read the dust and know when to shutter, and when a true front comes they wait it out, because a dust-storm at this scale takes two days and there is no hurrying it.

Their bodies and habits are tuned to the scarp’s forty-hour breath: warm air climbing the face, cold air pooling in the bowls, the ropes of dust rising and then sliding down again at evening. They measure time in cable-sings and cooling ticks and the far groan of a counterweighted lift. They eat what the crust feeds—the slow grazers, mostly, and what can be kept in the lee of a kiln—and they treat the crust as a commons to be tended rather than a crop to be taken. They are outlived by their towns, and the towns are older than most of the machinery in them, and the machinery is replaced a piece at a time by other machinery that is itself being replaced. Nothing about them is dramatic. They are patient the way the wind is patient, and they have the clerk’s genius for small repeated acts.

They are not lonely, exactly. The emptiness is the point, and the cables and stairs and signal-lines are how they refuse to be isolated inside it. At evening, when the wind reverses and the light goes the color of the crust and the whole scarp hums one warm low note, the terrace-dwellers come out onto the roofs and the ledges and stand still for a while, listening to the cable sing and the drifters settle onto the high shelves. Then it quiets, and the borers come out of their holes, and the people go back inside to tend the kilns and wait for morning.
