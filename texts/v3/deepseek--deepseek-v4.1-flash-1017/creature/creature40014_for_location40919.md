What lives there is not an animal and not quite a plant, though it is made of plants the way a body is made of cells. It is one continuous tenant, a single green body that has grown into itself so many times that it no longer has an outside. It fills the hollow the way a hand fills a glove. The vines are its nerves, the seep is its blood, the moss is its soft tissue, the old pipes are bones it did not grow but has learned to keep. If you ask where it ends, the honest answer is that it ends at the rim, and the rim is only a place where it is thinner.

It lives by maintenance, not by motion. It does not hunt and does not hide. It thickens. It settles. It replaces itself into the same shapes. A leaf unfurls, lives, goes heavy, and becomes floor—not dead, not fallen, just lowered into the next layer of the same patient body. It has no face, but it has attention: the wet gleam on every leaf is a kind of looking, and the hair-vines are a kind of listening. It feels the rain arrive. It feels your weight before you do. It tastes the seep and the metal and the small quick animals that move through its interstices. Those animals are not its children and not its food. They are more like weather inside it—small lives that chirp and crawl and eventually join the plant-flesh, where nothing is lost and nothing is grieved.

It speaks the hum. Not deliberately, perhaps—not the way a mouth speaks—but the hum is its voice all the same. The thin hollow vines are strung through it, and the water moving through the green, and the slow settling of the hollow, and the old buried machinery all vibrate together into one unresolved chord. When you raise your voice, the tenant takes it and folds it into that chord, and you cannot hear yourself. It is not trying to silence you. It simply has no room in its arrangement for a second song.

It is very old and very complete, and that completeness is the closest thing it has to a will. It does not tolerate disturbance because disturbance makes a gap, and gaps are the only death it knows. It will not chase you out. It will not need to. It will hold you the way a cupped hand holds water: gently, completely, and without any intention of letting go. Stay long enough and you become part of the wetness, part of the green, part of the hum—not eaten, not killed, just incorporated, the way the pipes were incorporated, the way the small animals are incorporated, the way every fallen thing is folded back into the one body that has finished becoming itself.
