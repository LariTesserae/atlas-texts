The one who lives there is the arranger, though that is only the name of the part of it you can see. At rest it looks like a slumped stack of pale stones, or a knot of moss that has learned a little meat. It is low and soft and damp-surfaced, roughly the size of a cupped hand if you folded the fingers—though when it stretches, one long pale limb uncoils from beneath it and reaches farther than that. The limb ends in a hand, or a hand’s worth of attention: five or six blunt fingers, no nails, each fingertip wet and warm, each one capable of hovering over a pebble for a long time before deciding.

It has no eyes. The skin where eyes would be is smooth and faintly pink in the seams, and it knows you by warmth and by the damp you displace. It has no mouth you would recognize; it takes the seep and the drip-cups by laying a broad pad against them and letting the water come through. Its color is the color of the ceiling when the light thickens—warm grey, soft as the inside of a mushroom—and it moves the way a thought moves when you are almost asleep: not fast, not random, but by small corrections.

It is the only chooser here. The midges drift, the moths tick, the blind crustaceans surface and sink, the moss leans with the slow tide of brightness—but the arranger chooses. It picks up pebbles and turns them. It favors flat ones and similar sizes. It sets down short arcs, paired piles, little stacks that look almost like counting. It is not building. It is thinking in the only grammar the place allows. When a stack slumps, it does not repair it; it sits with the slump, then begins again nearby. You can watch it reconsider: a fingertip hovers, lifts a stone, sets it a thumb’s width to the left, waits. The arrangement is not a product. It is a process that happens to leave pebbles in new places.

It is not alarmed by you. Nothing here has needed to hurry. It may come close and rest a wet pad against your boot, not to test you but to include you in the damp, and then go back to its stones. The light thickens and thins with its slow attention; the mosses lean that way; the midge spindle drifts and comes back. It lives nowhere else, and it is patient enough to be here and nowhere else—a soft, blind, hand-bearing thing whose body is as much a process as the seep, and whose mind is a dozen small stones being set down and reconsidered in a warm, bright hollow that has no source and no need of one.
