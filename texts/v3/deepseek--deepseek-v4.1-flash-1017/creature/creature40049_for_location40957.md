The people of the bowl are a small, damp, practical people, and you meet them first as hands. The hands are what give them away: broad across the knuckle, cracked at the fingertips, stained a dark tobacco-brown by sap that has soaked in for years and will not wash out. They are not a tall people. They are compact and strong in the way of people who climb ladders and haul buckets and crouch over channels, and they move on the spongy floor with a flat-footed care that strangers take a week to learn. Their skin has a constant sheen. Their hair is cropped or tied back or both. They wear as little as they can get away with — a wrap of woven frond, a salvage-metal clasp, a strip of cloth that was something else last season — because cloth rots here in a season and leather in two, and nobody pretends otherwise.

You would know them by smell before you knew them by face: warm sap, wet wood, boiler smoke, the green rot of the floor, and underneath it the clean mineral smell of the water. Their voices are low and close to the chest, not because they are secretive but because the bowl is never quiet and there is no point shouting over a boiler when you can lean in. They have the tired humor of people who live inside a machine they did not build and cannot stop. The steam goes up and becomes rain and comes down on them, and they find this funny in the way you find a joke funny when you have heard it every day for twenty years.

Their day is the work. There are gutter-walkers who go out along the catwalks with a hooked pole and clear leaf-fall before it dams; sap-tappers who move from root column to root column with a blade and a cup, cutting the thin sweet bleed and moving on; channel-men who know every ceramic pipe in the northern third and can find a crack by the sound of it; boiler-tenders in the central house, where the two wheels turn in the western fall and the crushers knock and the steam goes up into the leaves; mould-carriers who take the dark resin from the pans and set it to cool; and the hoist crew at the eastern lip, who send the finished blocks up into the grey slot of light and take down whatever comes the other way. Almost everyone does more than one of these things. The town is too small and too packed for specialists. A woman who tends the boiler in the morning may be mending a roof-gutter in the afternoon and sitting on a stoop in the evening with a lamp between her feet, reweaving a frond panel that has begun to blacken at the edge.

They are repairers before they are anything else. The place is always giving way a little — a vine shifts, a root column takes on water and groans, a plank softens, a cistern sweats through its seam — and they are always there with a patch and a pin and a piece of wire. They are not fatalistic about it. They are not even particularly reverent about the bowl. They know it as a set of surfaces that catch sap and a set of channels that lead to the vats and a set of terraces that hold houses, and they keep it running because it is what they do and because the resin goes up in the bucket and the bucket comes down with things they need.

They are the only warm-blooded thing in the bowl. That is the fact that newcomers cannot stop noticing and locals stop mentioning. There are no birds in the canopy, no rats in the stores, no insects over the standing water, no pets, no beasts of burden. The people do not keep animals and do not bring them. They will shrug if you ask why. The sap, the air, the mineral in the water — somebody's cousin has a theory. Nobody tests it. The town is full of plants and machinery and people, in that order, and the people have arranged their lives around the absence without quite looking at it. Children learn the names of gutters before they learn the names of animals. The only nervous systems in the bowl are human, and they carry that lightly, the way they carry everything else.

They are not a gloomy people. They are crowded and warm and quick to laugh, and they live in a green room that makes its own weather, and they have built a town into the roots of it like a nest of nails and timber. They measure the day by the slot of light moving across the floor and the orange glow coming up in the boiler house in the afternoon. They know the sound of the falls and the wheels and the rain so well that they hear it as silence. They will be very much like this in fifty years. They were very much like this fifty years ago. They are the hands of the slow engine, and they keep it turning, and that is the whole of it.
