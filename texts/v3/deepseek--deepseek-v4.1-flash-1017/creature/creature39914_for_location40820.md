The ones who live here are not settlers. They are habits the shelf has agreed to keep.

If you mean *who* lives there, it is the walkers. You will not see one by watching the path—watch the far end of the shelf instead, where the dimness and the stone meet. A thin vertical stroke appears, too tall, then another behind it, then another, always single file. They are more leg than body: long spindle-limbs with polished joints, each ending in a pad that leaves the same shallow print every time. The torso is a narrow capsule slung between the legs; the head, if it is a head, is a small dome held high and still. They do not turn to look at you. They do not stop. They are not hostile—they are committed. When the two-tone groan falls, you can watch one far off adjust its heading by half a degree, as if the note were a rail it walked beside. What they eat is not a question the shelf answers. They do not graze, drink, or hunt. They pass. Perhaps they metabolize the interval between tones; perhaps they are the path’s way of measuring itself. If you meet one coming the other way, it will not look at you and will not stop, and its prints will not match your own—or they will match the ones you left an hour ago, which is worse.

The droppers live under the overhangs. They are heart-sized, bluish, translucent, hanging in clusters like fruit in a place that has never had rain. When the wind’s tone deepens—not its gusts, its *tone*—they let go. They fall forty metres, hit the grit, and are gone. The shelf does not call this dying. Somewhere later, in a mineral lane or under another sill, a new dropper is found attached and grown. They are a life cycle by deposition, a weather made of bodies. Stand under a comb at the right moment and you can hear the soft ticks of them letting go, like a clock with no hands.

The finches-that-are-not-finches are the only quickness. They are the colour of the air and visible only as displacement: a heat-shimmer crossing the gap between combs, a flick at the edge of vision, a struck-glass note that hangs and is gone. They do not alight where you can see. They live in the angle between seeing and not seeing, and they nest, if they nest at all, in the fine horizontal striations that are not geological. They are not birds. They are the shelf’s quick thought, made briefly audible.

Deep inside the combs, rarely, a single syllable says itself and says itself again. It is worn down by repetition until it is only the shape of a mouth in stone. If the builders live anywhere, they live there—not as bodies but as a phrase that has outlasted its speaker. The bands in the channels are their language, or the shadow of it; the chambers are sills worn smooth by something entering and leaving. No tools, no marks. The architecture is erosion, and the erosion is architecture.

And above it all, in a comb higher than the rest, a hollow open to the wind plays two tones a fifth apart. This is the groan you heard first. It has been sounding since before the path was worn. The walkers orient to it. It is the closest thing here to an instrument, and it was not made. The stone was carved around it and left. Whatever the shelf is for, the phrase is still being spoken; the mouth has simply worn to its shape.

Something watches, rarely and actually. It is not a predator and not a god. It is the attention the shelf pays when a shadow touches a sill and the air changes pressure on your face. It may be the shelf itself, or the agreement that makes distances behave and prints lie. It does not mind you. You are small within it.

So: the walkers are the people, the droppers are the rain, the smear-birds are the quickness, the syllable is the builder worn to sound, and the note is the landlord. None of them will greet you. The path is always worn the same amount. The prints behind you are sometimes older than you are. The shelf is not a trap; it is a state. And you are a brief, permitted thing inside it.
