## What the Wise Power Did

It did not arrive. Arrival is a kind of claim.

It noticed the Lampwell the way one notices a held note in another room—not as a signal, not as a problem, but as something already complete enough to be listened to. The light with no source troubled its understanding, and the arranger troubled its heart, and the pebbles troubled its conscience, because the power was old enough to know that the first thing wisdom wants to do with a small bright place is improve it.

So it sat outside the crack for a long time and did nothing.

It considered the temptations one by one, as a physician considers instruments before deciding not to cut.

It could enter and study. It could map the light, sample the seep, count the blind crustaceans, take the measure of the arranger’s hand. But it understood that to know the place in that way would be to begin converting it into something else—a specimen, an explanation, a resource. The Lampwell was not a locked room. It was a grammar. The power had no right to correct its sentences.

It could teach the arranger. It could offer language, tools, fire, a name for itself. But the arranger was not waiting for a revelation. It was waiting for the next pebble to be right. Its thought was not deficient because it left no enduring thing. The process was the thought. To give it a product would be to take something from it.

It could preserve. It could seal the crack, steady the ceiling, keep the drips in their cups, hold the stacks upright. But preservation is sometimes only taxidermy performed on a living process. The stacks were supposed to slump. The seep was supposed to wander. The light was supposed to thicken and thin like a tide no one needed to explain. A wise power does not stop a heart in order to keep it safe.

It could make itself known. It could send a dream, a sign, a warmth with a face on it. But the arranger had no eyes for faces, and the place had no need of a god. Benevolence that requires gratitude has already begun to feed on the thing it claims to love.

So the power made a quiet covenant with itself.

It would keep the place secret. Not by walling it off, not by posting a guardian with a blade, but by making the crack a little less findable for anyone carrying a container, a drill, a doctrine, or a camera with a price attached. Those who came to sit with empty hands would still find their way. Those who came to take would find themselves remembering an appointment elsewhere. This was not a punishment. It was a courtesy to the small warm hollow.

It would visit rarely and empty-handed. It would leave no pebbles, no tools, no food, no words. It would sit at the threshold until the damp found its skin. If the arranger came near—if it laid a wet pad against the power’s foot, not testing, only including—the power would be still and receive it. It would not touch first. It would not take the hand. It would not lead the arranger out of the only place it had ever needed to be.

It would learn one thing, and it would take only that. It would learn how to hover: to hold a choice above the world for a long time, fingertip wet and warm, and then set it down a thumb’s width to the left, and wait, and reconsider. It would take this back to its own councils. It would become a little slower, a little less certain that intervention is the same as love. It would owe a blind stone-stacker more than it owed most of its prophets.

It would protect only against violations from outside. If someone came with a drill, it would make the stone forget the way. If the seep were poisoned, it would cleanse it without leaving a shrine. If the ceiling threatened to fall because of some force not the place’s own, it would hold the stone just long enough and then withdraw. It would not stop the drip-cups from wearing deeper. It would not stop the moss from leaning with the slow tide. It would not stop the arranger from dying, if dying was in the arranger’s nature. It might sit with it at the end, if sitting was welcome. It would not call that rescue.

It would not demand worship. It would not appear in dreams. It would not make a scripture out of a warm hollow and a hand of six blunt fingers. It would tell no one the coordinates. If it had servants, it would give them one rule: *If you find a place that lights itself from nowhere, sit down, be quiet, and do not take the stones.*

And then it would go away.

That was the rarest thing. It would not linger to be thanked. It would not return often enough to become a pressure. It would carry the Lampwell as a held silence, not as a possession. It would remember the midge spindle, the kiss of a crustacean breaking a drip-cup, the arranger’s hand hovering above three similar stones, deciding, deciding.

Somewhere in the bright hollow, the arranger would go on setting pebbles down and letting them slump. It would never know that a great and wise power had passed near, had considered all the ways it might be useful, and had chosen, out of love, to remain mostly absent.

The light would thicken and thin. The moss would lean. The water would land with its small flat sound.

And the crack would be a little harder to find for anyone who wanted to carry the place away.
