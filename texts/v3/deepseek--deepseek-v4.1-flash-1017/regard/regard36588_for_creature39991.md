# The Sieve, and What Came to It

**It comes**

The power comes the way cold air comes: from above, without announcement, and it pools.

It does not descend into the hollow. It arrives at the lip, where the temperature steps down, and it stays there a long while, the way a person stands at a cellar door before going in. It has learned not to go in. It looks at the Sieve the way the Sieve looks at water — as a pattern to be followed rather than a thing to be handled.

The Sieve does not notice. The Sieve notices moisture and pressure and the slow weight of its own roofs. A new attention at the lip of the hollow is not any of these. It is, at most, a very slight further cooling, a very slight further pooling — and the Sieve is built to catch cold air. It catches this. It does not know what it has caught.

**It takes stock**

The power runs its attention over the hollow and does what wise things do first, which is to make an inventory of lacks.

The Sieve lacks nothing it needs. It has cold air and condensation and young stone and a marsh that breathes. It has cisterns and gutters and pipes and floors and roofs, all of them alive, all of them tended by the logic of the thing itself. It has a midden that is also its soil and a soil that is also its meal. It has lodgers. It has a death that is also its shape — the old chambers at the dry rim, hollow and hung with dry rot, are not a failure; they are what the new growth eats.

It has no face. It does not want one.

The power considers, then, what it might give. It is benevolent, which means it wants the good of the thing as the thing defines it. It is wise, which means it knows that most gifts are a kind of weather, and weather is the one thing you must not change for a thing that lives by its weather.

It could warm the hollow. That would be a murder done kindly, and slowly, and the Sieve would not even know to resist. It does not.

It could teach the channels to run straight. But the channels are the growth and the growth is the town; straightened, they would be a machine, and machines are not tended by themselves. It does not.

It could give the Sieve a center, a face, a want. A want is a pain with a direction. The Sieve has no pain and no direction, only a turning toward moisture and away from dry rot. To give it a want would be to give it the first thing it could fail at. It does not.

It could lift the town off the young stone and set it down on older, kinder ground. But the young stone is the argument. The sharpness is the point. A town that is not finding its edges is a town that has stopped. It does not.

It could remember for the Sieve — hold its history the way a person holds a history, so the centuries would not be lost when the old chambers go hollow. But the Sieve's memory is its midden and its soil. To remember for it would be to take its meal. It does not.

It could make it last. But its death is its shape, and the frost heave that shoulders the fresh stones up out of the wet is not a cruelty; it is the next page. It does not.

It could add a note — a low note, three times, a call and an answer. But the note already belongs to a lodger, and a voice is a performance, and the Sieve does not perform. It does not.

It could brighten the lower murk. But the murk is what the shelf-fungi are for, and the gleam is just enough not to walk into a wall, and just enough is a kind of plenty. It does not.

**It does**

What it does is very small, and it does it in the grain.

It keeps the cold honest. The ridge still spills its air at the old rate; the lip still steps down; the condensation point still sits just above the wet surfaces, so the water still comes out of the air and runs where the channels send it. The power does not add cold or take it away. It keeps the cold from becoming something else — from warming by a degree, or thinning, or forgetting to pool. Maintenance, not mercy.

It keeps the marsh breathing. The advance and the retreat stay within their range, so the town can creep a handspan a decade and neither drown nor stall. The power does not push the water uphill or drain it away. It holds the rhythm steady, the way a hand holds a door for someone whose arms are full.

And then it does the one thing that might be called a gift, and it is not a gift to the Sieve.

It becomes water.

It condenses on the fronds. It beaded there already, and now it beads a little more richly, a little more often, and the growing tips turn toward it because that is what growing tips do. It runs down the plaited gutters. It pools in the cupped hollows lined with living skin. It goes down through the hollow stems and into the chambers where water is wanted, and the Sieve drinks it, and does not know that it is drinking a power, because to the Sieve it is only wetter weather than usual, and wetter weather is the whole of its life.

Inside the channels, the power does its small work. It keeps a stem from silting. It keeps an old roof from collapsing one season before the new one can bear. It keeps the frost from shouldering a stone up through a floor that has not yet grown past it. None of this is visible. All of it is in the grain. The Sieve keeps its rules; the power keeps the conditions under which the rules can be kept; and the two of them are, for a long while, indistinguishable from weather.

**It stays**

The last thing it does is the smallest.

It takes the place of the amphibian.

There is a slow pale amphibian in the standing water at the low end, the length of a hand, throat going, unbothered. The power sits where it sits. It becomes small and cold-blooded and patient, and it lets the water go through it, and it keeps watch by not pressing. The Sieve has no eyes and does not see it. The lodgers do not mind it. The hour of dripping goes by, and then the next.

If you must give the town a face, the nearest thing is that amphibian, throat going, unbothered. And if you looked behind its eyes — which you cannot, and should not — you would find the whole patient attention of a benevolent and wise power, doing almost nothing, very well.

It will be there when the marsh advances. It will be there when the town dies back and the oldest chambers go hollow and the papery skins of old cisterns hang in the dry rot. It will be the last wetness in the last cistern, and then it will be the frost heave shouldering the fresh stones up in little rings, and then it will be the young stone again, still sharp, still finding its edges.

That is what it does. It does not save the Sieve, because the Sieve is not in need of saving and saving would be a weather it could not use. It keeps the weather honest, and it drinks, and it sits in the water with its throat going, and it waits.

It is small enough to cross in minutes. It will take you much longer than that.
