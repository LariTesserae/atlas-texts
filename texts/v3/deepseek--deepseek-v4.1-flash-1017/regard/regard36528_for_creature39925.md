# Nearly Nothing

It had not come because the place was in danger. It had come because the place was whole, and wholeness draws the wise the way distress draws the kind. The attention arrived the way attention arrives at a sound you have been hearing for a while — not as an arrival, but as the recognition that you have been hearing it, and that now you are listening.

There was no descent, no light in the sky, no voice out of the steam. There was a wide shallow basin on a coastal plain, rimmed with packed earth and root, breathing slowly through a wet filter, and there was a power that had turned its attention to it and found it dense.

The first thing was the smell of wet paper and hot iron, and under that the green bitter note of crushed reed. Then the sound, which was water and machinery overlapping into a third thing. Then, slowly, the shape: the lean of the buildings, corrected with shims and drifted again; the living columns, coppiced and squared and leafing from the knees; the reed wall ranked and crowding, four meters high, slicing the light into ribbons; the mill's steam column going up patient and constant. And the frogs, the frogs, the frogs.

And then the being.

The being was the marsh, but *the marsh* was the town's word for it, and the town's word was a boundary, and the being had no boundary. It was a single slow metabolism, warm and wet, rhizomatous, continuous under the boardwalk and through the walls and up into the roofs. The elephant reed was its reach. The bladder ferns were its plating. The moss on the thatch was its tide. The living columns were its fingers — coppiced and squared and trained over decades, and the training was not a violation but a conversation, the way a hand is trained, the way a language is. The frogs lived in its interstices and it did not mind them. The herons stood in the Outflow in the patient, watchful way of a thing that has learned to wait inside another thing's patience.

The being was not indifferent. That was the town's word, and the town used it because the town had no other, and because the truth was harder: the being was slow. Its attention was measured in seasons and decades. It had allowed the town — *allowed* was the right word — not out of generosity but out of a metabolism that had room. The town took water and gave waste; took reed and gave cut reed that regrew; took warmth and gave warmth back through the mill's return channels. It was a symbiosis neither party had named, and the naming was the only thing missing, and the power understood that the missing name was not a wound but a condition.

The power considered.

It considered making the town dry. The thought was brief. Dryness was death here, not metaphorically: the foundations were timber rafts floated on mud that had never dried, the living columns were kept alive by the saturated ground, the boardwalks were milled from a timber nearly immune to rot only because it had grown in water. A dry Tallow would be a town of dead columns and cracked rafts and thatch that would burn. The power put the thought down.

It considered teaching the marsh to yield — to give the town more margin, to hold back from the floorboards, to let the bladder ferns stay in the channels. And it saw at once that this was the same thought in a different coat. The marsh was the town's infrastructure. To make it yield was to make it stop being the thing the town was built on. The bladder ferns lifting the boards were the same ferns plating the wet ground that held the pilings. The town did not want the marsh to yield. The town wanted the marsh to continue, and to be negotiated with, and the negotiation was the town's whole art.

It considered straightening the columns. There was an old man who would tell you, at length, that the third column of the drying shed was true when he was a boy, and an old woman who could recite every correction made to that column in forty years, and they had been having this conversation for a decade and neither would yield. The power saw that the drift was the history. The corrections were the town's record of itself, held in the body, disputed. To straighten the column would be to erase the argument, and the argument was the only history the town kept. Straightness, here, was a fiction — like dry ground, like a finished thing. The power left the column leaning.

It considered giving the town a word for the accord. This was the thought it held longest. The accord was real; everyone knew it; nobody said it out loud. A word would make it visible, and a visible accord could be broken, and an unspoken accord could not. The power had seen, in other places, what happened when a thing held in the body was given to the mouth: it became a thing that could be argued about, and then a thing that could be lost. The power did not give them the word. It judged that the town was already saying it, in the only language that had ever worked — the pulling of a vine as you pass it, the third of moss left in the drier week, the stepping-over of a frog without a glance.

It considered speaking to the being in words. The being had no ears for words. Its language was warmth and water and time and the third of moss left, and the power was fluent in that language, and knew that to speak it in words would be to speak past the being, not to it.

It considered doing nothing at all. This was the hardest consideration, and the one it took most seriously, and the one it returned to after each of the others, because it had learned that most places need nothing, and that the difference between needing nothing and needing something too small to see is the whole of wisdom.

Then it looked at the people.

They were the quietest thing in Tallow, and the hardest to notice, and the power noticed them precisely because it was wise. They were wet-handed, tea-brown to the crease, long-sleeved in the heat against the reed's fine parallel scars. The digester crew stood too close to the heat in the cold hours, the only dry people in town. The press operator had run the same squat engine for twenty-six years and could diagnose it by ear from the far end of the shed, and did, and was usually right. When the lines filled, whoever was free went to the lines, and worked inside those translucent curtains of wet paper with the light coming through them, so that for an hour a day the whole population stood inside a lamp.

The power saw the girl with the notebook, keeping her dated list of every vine she had pulled from the mill's north wall, and understood that her mother was wrong, that this was not a phase, that it was the beginning of a career. It saw the old man and the old woman, arguing about the third column, and understood that this was what history looked like here — drifts and corrections, held in the body, disputed. It saw the boatman coming back up the Outflow, poled against the current, met at the rim by everyone who could be, and understood the look in his eye, the look of a person who had been dry for two nights.

And it understood that the town was a conversation, and that the conversation was the town's whole art, and that the one thing it must not do was interrupt.

So it did what the town does.

It walked the boardwalk in the only way it could — as attention, as the turning of a slow eye — and it pulled a vine as it passed, because a vine goes as you pass it, and that is how the accord is kept. It left a third of the moss in the drier week. It stepped over a frog without a glance. It stood through the eleven minutes when the engine stops, and heard the whole town talking at once, a brief startled aviary of a few hundred people, and understood that this was the town's voice, pitched between water and engine, and that it was exactly right, and that lengthening the silence would change what the town was.

And at the margin, at dusk, at the Outflow, with the warm water sliding over the lip and the reed wall crowding behind and the mill's steam column going gold into the steam haze, it spoke to the being in the being's language.

It did not say *I will fix you*. It did not say *I will save you*. It said the only thing that needed saying.

It said: *I see you.*

The being received it the way it received everything: slowly, warmly, and without a word.

The receipt was a flowering.

In the morning, a tamarind flowered on the third column of the drying shed — the living column the old man insisted was true and the old woman insisted had been corrected forty times. It had not flowered in living memory, and it was not flowering in its season. The town mentioned it. The old man said it meant the column was healthy. The old woman said it meant the column was dying. They argued, at length, and neither yielded, and the argument was the town's record of itself, and it was exactly right. The girl wrote it in her notebook, dated, with a question mark.

And the vine the power had pulled — the one that went as you pass it, the ordinary maintenance of the accord — was back in the morning, grown in a night, thicker than it had been. The girl found it. She stood in front of it for a long time. She wrote it down, dated, with two question marks, and then she crossed one out, because two seemed greedy, and then she wrote it back in, because it was.

The mill kept chuffing. The steam went gold at dusk. The frogs started.

That night the frogs sounded as they always sounded, and if there was a new note in it, nobody could say, and the girl wrote nothing, and the being held the seeing the way it held everything — slowly, warmly, for a long time.

The power withdrew. It had done almost nothing.

The almost was exact.
