The power would come quietly, if it came at all. It would not descend in glory, because glory is a bright thing and the Kettle has no room for brightness, and it would not announce itself, because the Kettle has no word for arrival and the announcement would be a kind of violence.

It would come as the trimmers come to a seam: patiently, by hand, with attention.

---

The first thing the power would do is listen.

It would stand at the rim where the sound comes up over the lip as a solid thing, and it would let the noise lean against it, and it would not flinch. It would hear the roar and the ringing and the falling tones of the grazers and the hush-then-resume of the channels when the large thing below moves through them. It would hear the tap and scrape of a hundred copper blades. It would hear, underneath all of it, the vast indifference of a circulation that has no beginning and no end, and it would understand that this indifference is not cruelty. It is simply the shape of a place that gives and takes in the same motion.

The power would see that the Kettle is not a wound. It resembles a wound—the raw edges, the constant weeping, the things that live in it because they have learned to keep it open—but it is not. A wound wants to close. The Kettle wants nothing. It simply is, and the people in it have chosen to keep it being, and that choice is the most human thing about the place. The power would recognize this and would honor it.

---

The second thing the power would do is learn the language.

Not the spoken one—there is no spoken one, not really, not at the centre. The language of the Kettle is the language of hands and knuckles and held palms, of taps against stone, of the difference between water running over and water running under. The power would learn it the way a child learns: by watching, by failing, by watching again. It would sit at the edge of a work-shift and hold its hands still and let the trimmers see that it was trying. It would make mistakes. It would sign *help* when it meant *wait* and *sorry* when it meant *I am still learning*, and the trimmers would laugh—a silent laughter, shoulders shaking, wax in their ears—and they would correct it, patiently, the way they correct everything, with the flat of a blade and a gesture that means *like this, not like that*.

The power would not be offended by the laughter. It would understand that laughter in the Kettle is not mockery. It is the sound a person makes when the roar has been pushed back far enough to allow it, and it is precious, and it is rare.

---

The third thing the power would do—and this is the difficult thing, the thing that requires the most wisdom—is nothing.

It would watch the town trim itself back from the encroaching stone and it would not offer to help. It would watch a channel begin to silt and it would not clear it. It would watch a child learn the angle of the cut, and the child would be clumsy, and the power would not steady the child's hand. Because the Kettle is held at a knife's edge, and the edge is a hand's width wide, and the people who live there know the width of that edge in their bones. They have been keeping the balance since before there were words for balance. The power's help would be a weight on one side of the scale, and the scale is too finely tuned to bear it.

The power would understand that benevolence is not the same as intervention. It would understand that the trimmers do not need to be saved from their labor. Their labor is their life. To take it from them would be to take the Kettle from them, to turn a place held at an edge into a place held by something else, which is another way of saying a place that has been let go.

So the power would watch, and it would learn, and it would keep its hands still.

---

But there is a thing the power could do, and would do, because wisdom is not only restraint. It is also the knowledge of when restraint is no longer enough.

The Kettle is balanced, but the balance is fragile. The people know this. The only story they tell is the story of the tipping: the basin filling solid, or opening into a dry crack in the ground. They tell it as warning and lullaby, and they do not expect it to happen, and they do not expect anyone to come if it does. They have no word for arrival. They have no before. They have only the flow, the ringing, the cutting-back, and the roar.

The power would give them a word.

Not a loud word, not a word that arrives. A word that is simply there, the way the water is simply there, the way the stone is simply there. A word in the language of hands and knuckles and held palms, a sign that means *I am here, and I am listening, and if the edge breaks I will hold it with you.*

The power would teach this sign to one person, and that person would teach it to another, and in time the word would spread through the town the way the sinter spreads across the stone: slowly, grain by grain, until it was everywhere. And the people would not build a shrine to it or tell a story about it. They would simply use it, the way they use a blade or a raft or a held palm. They would sign it to each other in the middle of a work-shift, when the roar was loud and the wax was in their ears and the standing tone was in their teeth, and it would mean what it meant: *I am here. You are not alone at the edge.*

---

And if the edge ever did break—if the water began to rise faster than the trimmers could cut, or the seams began to seal faster than they could open them—the power would act. Not before. Not in anticipation. But in the moment, and in the way the Kettle itself acts: with the flow, not against it.

If the basin began to fill solid, the power would not dam the upwelling or pump the water out. It would find the deepest seam, the one that sinks away down other seams toward the hot dark, and it would widen it. It would work with copper tools, cold-hammered, the way the trimmers work, and it would cut and cut and cut, and the water would find its way down, and the balance would hold. The large thing below would feel the change in the flow and would move, slowly, and would not come up, and the channels would hush and then resume.

If the basin began to open into a dry crack, the power would not seal the crack or fill it with stone from elsewhere. It would find the slick, the mineral bloom, the orange and bone-white and dull iron grey, and it would gather it in baskets and carry it to the crack and let it sink. The slick would spread and the crack would fill and the water would find its way up again, and the balance would hold. The cup-shells would ring in the new channels, and the grazers would wade the new shallows on their stilt legs, and the wing-clouds would dim the light another degree as they passed.

And when it was done, the power would step back. It would not stay to be thanked, because the Kettle has no word for thanks, and because the trimmers would not thank it anyway. They would simply return to their work, paring the new sinter from the doorways and the channel mouths and the seam-lips, carrying the pale parings to the middle pan and letting them sink. And the sound would go on.

---

This is what the power would do, if anything: it would listen, and it would learn, and it would wait, and it would give the people a word for presence, and it would hold the edge with them when the edge began to break. It would not save them from their labor, because their labor is their life. It would not change the balance, because the balance is the place. It would simply be there, at the rim, where the sound comes up over the lip as a solid thing, and it would lean into the noise, and it would not flinch.

And the people would know it was there, not because it announced itself, but because the word was in their hands, and the word meant *I am here*, and that is the only arrival the Kettle has ever needed.
