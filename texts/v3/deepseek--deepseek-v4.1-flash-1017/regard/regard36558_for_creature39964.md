## The Second Light

It would not arrive. It would *attend*.

Attention is how such a power travels. It would find the Kettle the way you find a rumor in a dark house — by following a stutter of light along a wall, a blue flare where a shrimp startled at nothing, a green wave rising up a tower because the melt had come down through the seam. It would look through forty feet of glacier and see a lit room with a green ceiling, crowded and cold and patient, and it would understand at once that it was looking at a world entire.

Then it would consider the obvious mercies, and refuse them one by one.

It would not lift the lid. To lift the lid would be to change the pressure, the cold, the lean of the water, the taste of everything; the patient one would feel its gallery open like a wound, and the masons would cure wrong, and the city would die of rescue.

It would not teach. There is no one in the Kettle to teach. The masons have no brains, the shrimp have no memory, the fish are blind and hungry and free. To give the patient one a human mind would be to make it aware of walls and horizons and the impossibility of leaving — a cruelty dressed as a gift.

It would not feed them. The Kettle runs on scarcity the way a heart runs on blood. Abundance would bloom and choke and rot, and the city would stop building, and the thing that makes the Kettle beautiful — that it is still being assembled, that nothing here is finished — would be over.

It would not make them immortal. It would not extract the patient one. It would not send a prophet or a sign or a name.

What it would do is learn the language.

The Kettle speaks in light and pressure. Not words — reportage. *Here is a thing. It is moving. It is large. It is gone.* The power would practice until its presence was no sharper than a melt pulse: a slow, wide, dim glow coming down through the seam, too gradual to startle. It would answer the city’s reports with reports of its own. It would not lie. It would say, in the only grammar available: *there is a thing; it is large; it is not closing; it is gone.*

It would become a regularity. A pulse through the seam at intervals too long to be a season and too regular to be accident. The masons would build under it without knowing why. The shrimp would flare and settle. The fish would feel a pressure at the edge of their blind world and scatter, and then forget. The patient one, old and alone in the wide gallery, would feel something at the edge of its perception — not a threat, not a hunger, not the hand closing — and it would turn its eyeless face toward it. It would not understand. It would not need to. It would be, for a moment, less alone.

And the power would keep the memory.

That is the one thing it could give that would cost the Kettle nothing. It would remember every colony that ever laid down a thread of milky cement, every borer that chewed a perfect round hole, every shrimp that startled at nothing, every fish that arrived against the patient one’s mouth and was gone. It would remember the city’s strata the way the city cannot remember them — not as stone, but as lives. It would hold the Kettle whole in its attention, and attention, in a power like that, is not passive. It is a kind of shelter.

It would not hold the ice back forever. It would not make the Kettle eternal. But if the seam ever closed, or the rock shifted, or the last light went out under the glacier, the Kettle would not vanish unremembered. It would be known. It would have been answered.

That is all. A visitation that looks like weather. A greeting that is only a tide. The Kettle would never know it had been visited. The patient one would turn toward the pressure and then go still again, and the water would go on leaning against itself in the cold, and the city would go on building itself out of everything that dies.

And the power would stay, listening, answering in light.
