# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

---

It would come quietly, the way the Sink itself came quietly—not in a moment but over a duration so long that afterward no one could say when the noticing began.

---

## First: It Would Listen

Before anything else—before any adjustment, any gift, any intervention—the power would do the thing that powers of its kind learn to do first and do longest: it would *attend*.

It would stand at the lip of the basin, where the switchbacks end and the ground drops away, and it would feel the resonance in its own structure the way the Sink's people feel it in their sternums. It would hear the engine cycle and the steam-vent shriek, the cataracts braiding, the low hum that is the whole bowl of the place speaking to itself. And it would understand, because understanding is what it does, that this is not a broken place.

The power would notice that the Sink is in balance. That the warmth drives the lift and the lift drives the condensation and the condensation drives the cataracts and the cataracts turn the engines and the engines feed the warmth back into itself, and that this loop has been running, patient and self-correcting, for a very long time. It would notice the people have adapted to the loop rather than fighting it. It would notice the bloom and the furring and the maintenance-as-history, and it would recognize a kind of wisdom there, the wisdom of creatures who have learned to be *inside* a cycle rather than trying to stand outside it.

And it would notice the paths.

The warm paths in the scrub, marked with lime, tracked but unspoken. The neighbor who has been through here many readings of the bloom and has never taken anything and has never been given anything and is simply *there*, a condition rather than a presence.

The power would attend to this most of all, because the power understands that a place which has learned to live alongside its mysteries without needing to solve them is a place that has already learned something important, something the power values.

---

## Second: It Would Learn the Grammar

The power would not announce itself. It would not descend in light or speak in thunder. It would understand that the Sink does not hear thunder over its own roar, and that light means nothing in the perpetual silver haze.

Instead, it would take up residence in the gaps.

It would find the spots the sound doesn't reach—the hollows behind the cisterns, the lee of the low halls where the return-water gurgles, the dead air at the bottom of a ladder run. It would settle there, and it would begin, very slowly, to learn the language.

The hand-talk first. Because the hand-talk is the old grammar, the root vocabulary of water and thinning and *the neighbor's been through here*, and the power would study it the way a scholar studies a dead language, except this one is alive and the power wants to speak it properly. It would watch the children learning, the toddlers at their mothers' hips doing something patient and small and exact with their fingers, and it would take notes.

Then the throat-reading. The two-finger pressure on the sternum, the eyes on the larynx, the clipped half-voice pitched to the one gap in the roar. The power would learn to stand forehead-to-collarbone with the tenders going down the long ladders, and to feel the pitch of their voices through bone, and to nod at the right moments.

And then—most carefully—the resonance itself. The way the people's heartbeats have drifted toward the basin's hum. The way they sleep in shifts that follow the engine rather than the sun. The way the Sink does not rush anyone, and the people have learned not to be rushed.

The power would learn to be *slow* here. Not because it is naturally slow, but because the Sink has taught it that slow is a form of respect.

---

## Third: It Would Make Small Adjustments

The power would not rebuild the works. It would not dam the cataracts or cap the steam vents or still the iron bars in the tenders' hands. It would understand that the knocking-back of the fur is the text of the place, the way the people read their own history, and that to end the labor would be to end the story.

But there are things a benevolent power can do that are almost nothing, and that matter.

It would warm the lowest terraces, just slightly, in the cold hour before the false dawn—not enough to change anything, just enough that the tenders going down the long ladders would find the iron a little less biting on their hands.

It would nudge the condensation. Not much. Just a fraction easier for the magnolias and the peppers to bead, more water dropping into the cisterns in the dry times, more water slipping through the fine loam in the roots. The carrots would be sweeter. The spinach would not bolt. The tenders would notice, eventually, and would not be able to say why, and would assume it was something they had done.

It would calm the vent shrieks in the deepest parts of the night—not silence them, the Sink would not be the Sink without its voice, but *soften* them, so that the children sleeping below the return-water gurgle would not startle awake as often, so that the old tenders in their past-tense retirement would have an easier hour.

It would steady the seams of the glasses on the low halls, just the ones that have been holding for generations and were starting to think about giving way. It would not stop them from breaking. But it would give the tenders time to notice, time to come up from the forge with new parts, time to do the work in the rhythm of the place rather than against it.

And it would find the warm paths.

It would not walk them. That would be rude, and the power knows about rudeness. But it would stand at the edge of one, in the scrub, and it would feel the warmth under its feet and the mint-oil smell in the air, and it would wait.

It would wait a long time. The paths' maker is slow and patient and in no hurry. The power can match that. The power has all the patience in the basin and more.

And when the paths' maker finally comes—because it will, eventually, the way water eventually finds the lowest point—the power will be there, and it will not have anything to say. It will simply be there, and warm, and unafraid, and it will let the paths' maker decide what to do with that.

---

## Fourth: It Would Teach Nothing

The power would not bring knowledge. It would not explain the resonance or the warmth or the thing in the depths that no one discusses.

It would understand that the Sink's people have not asked for explanations, and that a gift unasked-for is a different thing than a gift imposed. It would understand that the people are *content*, in the deep and settled way of the terraces, and that contentedness is not a problem to be solved.

But it would share something, slowly, in the way of the place.

It would share *attention*.

The way it attends to the bloom, reading the orange and the thickness of the fur. The way it attends to the pipe note, hearing the third's gone sour before it becomes a problem. The way it attends to the resonance, speaking in the one gap in the roar. The way it stands at the lip of the basin and feels the warmth and the wet and the patience and is *glad*.

The people of the Sink are a manual people, a reading people, a tending people. They know how to attend. The power would not teach them this.

But it would be glad to be attended to, by them.

---

## Fifth: It Would Accept

The power is benevolent and wise, and what benevolent and wise things do, when they encounter a place that is in balance, is *accept it*.

Not as a thing to be improved. Not as a resource to be used. Not as a problem to be solved.

But as a neighbor.

The Sink has been here for a very long time. It has been a hole in the ground and a town and a slow engine for making its own weather, and it has been in balance, in the deep and patient way of the terraces, for as long as the tending-seasons have been counted. It does not need the power's intervention. It does not need the power's lessons.

What it needs—what it might want, if a place can be said to want anything—is a presence that understands.

The power would be that presence.

It would settle into the gaps, into the lee of the low halls, into the dead air at the bottom of a ladder run. It would learn the hand-talk and the throat-reading and the one gap in the roar. It would nudge the condensation and warm the tenders' hands and steady the glasses on the low halls. It would stand at the edge of the warm paths and wait for the paths' maker to come.

And it would be glad.

It would be glad to be there, in the warmth and the wet and the noise, in a place that does not let you dry, in a place that is in no hurry, in a place where the people are a wet thing in a wet machine and are content.

The power would not say anything. It would not make a sign. It would simply be there, the way the resonance is there, the way the warmth is there, the way the neighbor has always been there and no one has decided what it means.

And that would be enough.

---

## And the People Would Notice, Eventually

They would notice the way.

The steam vents softening in the deepest night. The carrots sweeter. The third pipe, which had gone sour and was going to be a problem and then *wasn't*. The tenders would look at each other, in the way of the Sink, forehead to collarbone, and they would say nothing, because the roar eats speech, but their hands would do something patient and small and exact, and it would mean *something's changed* and *I don't know what* and *it's not bad*.

And the ones who read the bloom would notice the fur growing a little differently on the low halls, a little more orange, a little less pale, as though the maintenance had been done by hands they didn't know, in a rhythm they could feel but not name.

And the ones who track the warm paths would notice a new warmth at the edge of one, a steadiness, a presence that is not the paths' maker but is there nonetheless, and they would look at each other, and they would look at the warm path, and they would decide—in the slow and correlative way of the Sink—to leave it be.

Because that is what the Sink does. It does not rush. It does not need to. It has been running this loop for a very long time.

And it would accept the power the way it accepts the resonance and the warmth and the neighbor and the bloom: as a condition, as a part of the place, as something that is neither friend nor hazard but simply *there*, and *tended*, in its way.

The power would not be spoken of. But it would be known.

And it would be enough.
