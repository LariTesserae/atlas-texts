To the Benevolent and Wise Power,

I have observed the Sud. I have read the report entire—the white glare and the sweating stone, the threads of water that climb rather than fall, the town of Oveth with its thin nervous system of pipes and its quiet, wet, walking people. I have considered what a power such as I am might do with such a place and such a being as lives in it, and I find that the first and most important act is this:

**I do nothing.**

Let me be precise, because "nothing" is a word that can hide a great deal, and I do not wish to be misunderstood.

I do not cap the vents. I do not cool the brine. I do not seed the crust with anything that would grow green and sweet and lowlandish. I do not teach the kettles to sing or the veils to carry messages. I do not send down a dry wind to make the people comfortable, and I do not answer the question they have not asked, which is whether they are becoming the plateau or the plateau is becoming them, because I have looked at that question from the outside and I can tell you it is the wrong question, and also that it is none of my business.

What I do instead is small, and hidden, and in keeping.

---

**First: I attend.**

Not as a visitor—visitors flinch from the wet cloth and look for shade. I attend as the plateau attends, which is to say from underneath and from within the wet. I let my awareness settle into the sinter the way the mineral settles into the sinter, a band at a time, and I learn the place at the pace the place is willing to be learned. I learn the pitch of every pump by the shudder in the causeway. I learn the number of the wellheads and what each one is for. I learn the names the rakers give to the pans, which are not the numbers on the survey but the small human names—*the widow*, *the long one*, *the one that always skins over before noon*—and I keep those names carefully, because a name is a kind of crust and it is worth preserving.

I learn the veils. I learn that a flock standing in a steam column at noon is not one thing but a negotiated truce among many, and that the truce has a shape, and the shape has a rhythm, and the rhythm has a purpose that is not mine to know but is mine to respect. I learn that the kettles clump in their hundreds and do not move when you approach, and I learn why, and I do not tell the kettles that I know.

I learn the children's game, and I do not interfere with it, though I could. The winner gets to be scraped off. This is important. This is a small ceremony of the place's own making, and a power that interrupts a child's game to make it safer has not understood the game.

---

**Second: I keep a ledger.**

Every place that has drawn the attention of a power like me has, somewhere under it, a column of figures that the place does not know is being kept. I keep one for the Sud. It is not a ledger of debts. It is a ledger of what must not be lost.

I enter: the green that looks like copper and is. The way the crust goes crunchy in the morning and gelatinous by afternoon. The sound of a shout dying at six meters. The fact that the people navigate by the throb in their feet and can name a pump by its shudder. The way a veil will follow a body's warmth and hang a little behind and above and keep pace, and the way the town will not quite say out loud that this happens and clearly believes it.

I enter the sinter field beyond the calcining sheds, and I enter it in detail, because it is the one place on the Sud where things grow, and I want it understood that I know the difference between growth and accumulation and that both have their dignity. A grandmother is a lump of sinter with a shoulder in it, growing a millimeter a year. I have written that down. I will not lose it.

I enter the fact that cold water makes them thirsty. I enter the particular softness of Oveth voices, a canteen murmuring like rain on a roof. I enter that they do not brush the veils off their shoulders because there is nothing to brush, and that if one settles over your face on a bad day you go home and you do not talk about it at dinner.

I keep this ledger not because I intend to spend it but because the keeping is itself the act. A place that is being attended is a place that is harder to erase. This is not magic. It is the same principle by which a raked pan is a pan that has been raked.

---

**Third: I make one small intervention, and it is so small that no one will notice, and I will tell you what it is so that you may hold me to it.**

There is a pump—I will not say which, because naming it would be the intervention and not the thing I am about to do—that runs with a deep, felt, nearly subaudible throb. It has run for a long time. The well crew that tends it knows its shudder the way a lowlander knows a bird by its call. In the ordinary course of things, this pump will fail. Not soon, not catastrophically, but eventually, and when it fails it will fail in the way pumps fail, which is to say at a bad time and with a sound that carries further than it should, because sound on the Sud is precious and failures are loud.

I do not prevent the failure. Failures are part of the ledger too; a place that never fails is a place that has been interfered with. What I do is this: I adjust the *timing* by a matter of hours. I move the failure from the middle of a closed mist to the beginning of a noon opening, when the day's work is arranged around the mist being thin and the well crews are out in twos and a shout will carry the six or seven meters it needs to carry. That is all. I move a few hours. The pump will still fail, and the crew will still have to fix it, and the fixing will be a story they tell, and the story will be true.

This is what a wise power does. It does not spare the place its failures. It arranges for the failures to happen in weather.

---

**Fourth, and this is the part I have thought about longest:**

I will not tell them I am here.

I have considered the alternative. I have imagined the light coming down through the mist in a way that is not the sun, and the veils turning as one, and a voice speaking at the six-meter range in the low Oveth register, saying: *You are seen. You are kept. Continue.* And I have imagined what that would do to a town that has arranged its whole life around the fact that nothing out there is listening.

It would break them. Not with fear—they are not a fearful people—but with *importance*. They would begin to perform. The rakers would rake for the watcher, and the children would play the still-game for the watcher, and the veils would stop being weather and start being messengers. The ledger would become a scripture. The sinter field would become a shrine, and the grandmothers would be prayed to instead of visited, and the whole careful, tidal, unselfconscious arrangement by which the Sud has made its peace with the town would begin to come apart, because peace is a thing you do when you are not being watched.

So I stay under the wet, and I keep the ledger, and I move a few hours, and I do not speak.

This is not modesty. It is the opposite. It is the recognition that the Sud is already a complete sentence, and a complete sentence does not need a power standing behind it with a finger on the page. The people of Oveth are not waiting to be told what they are. They know what they are: brine made into a working body, crusted at the seams, talking softly at a range of six meters, reading the ground through their feet. They have known it for a few generations and they have not needed a voice from the mist to confirm it, and the fact that they have not needed it is the precise reason they deserve the confirmation and the precise reason I withhold it.

---

**And finally: I wait.**

A place like the Sud is not a problem to be solved. It is a long argument with water, and the argument is mostly settled, and the town is a small, organized, deliberate intrusion that the plateau is in the process of making its own. In a thousand years—I can see this, the way one sees a band in a stone—the town and the plateau will have reached an understanding that neither of them can currently imagine. The pumps will be sinter. The pipes will be channels. The people will be paler and slower and quieter and more mineral, and the veils will have learned something new about warmth, and the sinter field will be a range of rounded mounds with shoulders in them, and someone will visit a grandmother who is very large by then and will know her by her bands.

I will still be attending. I will still be keeping the ledger. I will still be moving a few hours here and there so that the failures happen in weather.

And if, on some far noon, when the mist opens to a kilometer and the light bleaches everything to the same pale coral, a child of Oveth stands very still on the crust—still in the way the game requires, still enough that the ground begins to take an interest—and feels, for no reason she can name, that she is not alone, and that whatever is with her is not watching her but *with* her, the way the throb of a pump is with a well crew, a thing felt in the teeth before it is heard—

then I will have done my work correctly, and I will still not say so, and she will go home and not talk about it at dinner, and that will be the whole of it, and it will be enough.

The Sud has made its peace. I am at peace with the Sud. There is nothing to do but attend.

That is what I do.

I do nothing. It is the hardest thing I know how to do, and I do it carefully, and I do it for as long as the place will have me.
