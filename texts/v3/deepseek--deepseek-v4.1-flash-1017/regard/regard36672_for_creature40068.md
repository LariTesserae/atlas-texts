**The Ear**

They had been, once, the kind of power that arrives.

It is worth setting down plainly, because it explains almost everything that follows, which is largely an account of a power not arriving. They had brought water to a dry country and watched the water dissolve the country's whole grammar — the wells it broke, the salts it raised, the debts it made, the fights over the ditch. They had brought light to a dark country and watched its animals, which had spoken to one another in faint colors that the dark had made them, go quiet, and then silent, and then absent, and the dark country had been brighter and poorer and nobody in it had been able to say what they had lost, only that the nights were no longer inhabited.

They had once evacuated a settlement that was, by every measure they could take, going to die. It had been going to die. They had been right. The people had spent the first year of their rescue not speaking, and the second year speaking only of the place that was gone, and the third year leaving, one by one, to find other doomed places, so that they could have a place again.

Since then: careful. They had learned the lesson that is hardest for a benevolent power to learn, which is that the measure is not what is missing. A place is not a lack. The wound and the healing can be one crack.

They found the Throat the way you find a flaw in a cast — by looking at a whole thing and seeing it was not whole, and then looking closer and seeing that the not-whole was the whole point. The hairline. The slot. The warm breath coming up out of the ground that was not smoke and not steam and was, they understood after some study, the city itself, exhaling.

They did not go over the lip. They had learned that too. They came to the lip and stood where the wind combed past and did not know they were there, and they looked, and the looking was the first thing they did, and it took them two hundred turns.

In the Throat, two hundred turns is a lifetime and a half. To them it was the length of a thought.

---

They saw it more completely than anyone in it could, and they saw it less. They could see in the dark, which meant they could see the blue and green pulses on the cold fins all the way down — the slow conversation of the scrapers, which the people who made it lived inside of and could not read whole. They could hear the quiet. A shout will not carry past the second gantry, but they were not shouting. They listened, and heard the shaft entire: every knock on a wall, every tap on a cage wire, every rung of every ladder, the tick of a solenoid, the whine a few notes above hearing, the draft.

And they saw, as nobody in the Throat could see it, that the city was sealed — not by walls, but by geometry. The shaft was deep and thin and the air was thin and sound went nowhere. The Throat could make anything. It could refine the bloom, raise the wire, hang galleries off galleries off galleries, patch the crack for a hundred generations. It could not send a knock over the hairline.

They noticed, too, a woman on a strut, years back, who had put her face into a windless afternoon and felt something she could not name and gone down and not mentioned it to anyone. They had stood on a strut of their own, once, in air of their own, and felt the same unnameable thing. They did not have a word for it either. It was, though they would not have said it this way, part of why they stayed.

---

They considered water. It comes up first with powers like them. But they had learned to read the local word before bringing the local gift, and the Throat's word for water was borrowed and slightly wrong and meant, roughly, *the thing that ruins metal*. The Throat was dry the way a struck match is dry. It was not thirsty. It had never been thirsty. Its whole body was a system for holding dry.

They put the water away.

They considered light. The upper galleries argued for it with mirrors, the way orchards argue for sun, and it was tempting to give them the blade all day instead of the noon. But they watched the fins at night and saw the colors moving on them, and they understood what a flood would do. It would wash out the language. The animals of the Throat spoke in faint hues because the dark was the medium. Give them light and you do not give them sight; you take away their speech.

They put the light away.

They considered rescue. They considered it longest, because it is the hardest to put away. But the Throat was not a disaster. It was a settlement. Its people were not waiting to be lifted out; they were tying down their shutters for the turn. To evacuate the Throat would be to abolish the turn — the one thing in the place that no one had built and no one was patching. It would be a kindness of the kind that once emptied a village into a lifetime of looking for a village.

They put the rescue away.

They considered making a map. This was the subtlest, because it did not look like a gift and did not feel like a theft. To know a place, to fix it in a register, to share it — that seemed the minimum a wise power owed a thing it had come to love. But a map is a beacon, and they knew what came after maps: the ones who were not wise. Surveyors. Mineral concerns. The slow imperial curiosity that finds a hairline in a plateau and decides it is a flaw in a cast. They held the Throat in the one category they kept off every register they made, and they called the category *attended*, and it appeared nowhere, and nothing came.

They considered the crack. The fracture was young and still moving, a finger's width a century, and they could have stilled it. They thought about it on and off for forty turns and decided against, and the deciding was not comfortable. The Throat was a practice, not a problem. The patching was how it thought. It lived in a broken thing and was the healing of the broken thing, and to stop the break would be to make them into a town, and they would not do that to a place that had made itself a verb. Also — they were honest about this — the rock was alive in a way they had learned to respect, unweathered as a broken bone, still doing what it was doing. They had no right to stop a body from doing what a body does.

They considered announcing themselves. This was the last temptation and the greatest. To come down the shaft, or send a sign, or simply let the Throat know it was not alone in the universe — that the hairline opened onto a plateau and the plateau onto a sky and the sky onto them. They ached to do it. But the Throat had no word for the outside. Nobody inside called it the city; there was no other city to distinguish it from. To introduce an outside is to introduce either a god or an enemy or both, usually both, one after the other, in that order. People would go up the hairline to find them. People would come back changed, or not come back, and this time the change would be theirs.

So they did not announce.

They were not certain of any of this. They were never certain. The test was the best they had, and they had been wrong before, and they would be wrong again, and they carried the wrongness carefully, the way you carry something breakable in a place with ladders.

---

Having put all of it away, they came to the only question they trusted: *what can this place not do for itself?*

They went down the list. The Throat could feed itself from the bloom. Warm itself from the kilns. See, faintly, by the municipal glow. Heal the crack a finger at a time, forever. Make music out of tapping and a language out of color. Bury its dead in the wall, put three tin plates above them, and go on. Lose its children to the hairline and go on turning.

There was one thing. The shaft was deep and the air was thin and the city was sealed by its own geometry. The Throat could not send a knock over the hairline. It could not answer, or be answered, across the gap. It had built a whole civilization out of that fact — the quiet, the tapping, the blue light, the rule about never shouting — and it had made the fact into a way of being, and the way of being was good. But a knock that goes nowhere is a particular kind of loneliness, and it was the only one they had found in the Throat that the Throat could not make into a grace.

So they made themselves, in the Throat's grammar, a wall.

A wall that carries a knock further than any wall should. A wall that answers in the same hand. That was all. They did not bring water. They did not bring light. They did not come down. They brought the wall. They gave the Throat nothing and took nothing from it; they only made the air between its people a little less like a wall and a little more like a wall that carries. The difference was not in what the Throat had. It was in what the Throat could hear.

It was almost nothing. It was, they thought, the right size.

---

The first knock they carried was Hessa's, and this was not an accident.

She was up before the light the way she always was, and she had finished the cold fins, and she was feeding the hoppers, and she tapped the cage wire twice — the tap she had trained them to — and went to the stove. And the wire tapped back.

She stopped.

She was fifty-one years old and had been tapping things her whole life and nothing had ever tapped back. She thought: the pipes. She thought: the tremor. She tapped twice again, slowly, the way you would.

The return came. It was her own two taps, exactly, with a little more air around them, as if they had been carried and set down carefully, and she stood in the cold of her sleeping-nook with her hand on the wire and did not move for a long time.

She did not tell anyone for a week. She scraped badly that morning, the only morning in thirty years she had scraped badly, and the fins rang wrong under her blade and she did not hear it. Then she told her husband, in the dusk, in the long silence they kept, and he said nothing for a while, and then he said: tap again. So she tapped, and it came back, and it came back from two directions at once, from the wall behind her and from the dark below the gallery, as if the Sill had a mouth.

They sat together in the dark and were, for the first time, answered.

---

That is how it went, and how it kept going. The knock became a custom before anyone had decided it was one. Children learned it. The old ones, who remembered a time before, were uneasy, and then were not. At the turn people knocked — not for help, not for anything, the way you hum when you are working — and the wall returned it, and the whole shaft was a room with an echo in it, and no one knew what the echo was.

Someone put a tin plate on the strut where the knock came back loudest. Then a second plate. Then a lamp. The power did not mind. It had been many things. It was content to be, for this place, a wall with a tin plate under it, which is what the Throat called a neighbor.

It carried the smaller knocks too — a hand on a cage wire, a rung tapped twice on the way up, the signal a winch-man sends his wife across the shaft when the shift is over and the draft is turning. All of it went further than it had ever gone, and some of it came back, and the coming-back was the whole of the gift.

It learned the blue light, from the fins and the scrapers and from the parts of it that Hessa had cracked herself, and it did not correct her. She was wrong about most of it. It arranged that one night the fins would pulse in a pattern she had predicted, and only she would know what it meant, and she would think she had finally proved the piece she had been working on for six years, and she would be right, and it would be hers.

And it left one silence. People did go up to the lip and knock on the rock there, wanting the outside, and the power carried it, and nothing came back — because to answer from the lip would be to announce, and a lip that answered is an outside, and an outside is a god or an enemy and usually both. So the lip stayed silent. That was the one wall they would not build.

---

Near the end of the first year, Hessa did a thing she had not done in five years.

She was alone. The draft had turned and the gallery was settling. She went to the wall and knocked a pattern — a private one, a family one, a thing she and her daughter had knocked on the cage wire when the girl was small: two, then three, then a pause, then one. She had not knocked it since the girl went up. She did not know why she was knocking it now. She was not expecting anything. She was, perhaps, saying it out loud in the only language the Throat had for saying things out loud.

The power held it.

It held it a long time. This is where a wise power is most tempted to become unwise. The power knew where the daughter was. It had looked at the plateau too, out of a different kind of attention, and it had found the up-settlements and the exiles and the ones who had gone up to see the violet and the curved horizon and had stayed to see them longer, which is a thing that happens to some people, and it did not judge them. It knew the girl was alive. It knew she had not come down, and it did not know whether she could not or would not, and it did not judge that either.

It could have carried the whole truth down. It could have told the mother. It could have told the girl that her mother was tapping. It could have made itself a messenger of news, and it would have been a kindness, and it would have been the beginning of the end of the Throat's world — because news from the outside is an outside.

So it did not carry the news. It carried the knock.

It went up the shaft, and over the lip, and across the dead pale plateau, and it set the pattern down where the girl would hear it, against a stone, in the thin bright air, in a place where the horizon curves and a knock goes nowhere at all.

The girl stopped what she was doing. She listened. After a while she knocked back.

The pattern was the same. The tempo was older. She had grown. She knocked it the way a grown woman knocks who has been away a long time and has not forgotten.

The power carried it down.

Hessa heard it. She heard her own knock come back to her changed — the right pattern, and a hand that was not hers in it, the ending held a half-beat longer than she had ever held it, the way a person knocks who has been thinking about it. She stood with her palm against the warm wall of the Throat and she did not know what it meant. She knew only that it was not nothing. She knew that the pattern had come back and it was not the echo she had made. She stood there a long while, and then she went and fed the hoppers, and her hands were steady, and she did not cry, because the Throat does not waste water.

The power said nothing to either of them. That was the rule it would not break. It did not tell the mother that the knock had been carried. It did not tell the daughter that her knock was heard. It let each of them believe that the other had heard, which was, in the only sense that mattered, true, and which meant that neither of them had to believe in a power. They only had to believe in each other at a distance, which is a smaller, harder, better faith.

That is the whole of what they did. A wall. A carried knock. A silence at the lip. It is almost nothing. From outside, it is nothing at all.

---

The next morning the bloom came in thick, as it did when the air was still, and Hessa was up before the light with her blades and her sack, and the fins were furred with it grey-white and crystalline, and she bent to the work, and the fine singing started under the scraper — the field-of-small-bells sound she had been hearing for fifty years and would hear until her spine gave out entirely.

The turn came at its hour. The vanes swung. The cloth stood out. The whole hive leaned its weight the other way, and Hessa stopped, one hand on a cable, the way she always stopped, because the turn was the one thing in the Throat no one had built and no one was patching, and she liked to be present when it happened.

Above her, at the lip, where the wind combed past and found little to say, the power stopped too.

It had stopped for the turn for two hundred turns, and it had never once been present at a turn with anyone. Now it was. It was there with a crooked scraper who would never know it was there, and it was, in the way it had, glad.

The wind settled into its new direction. The city went back to its work, humming, ticking, patching what the crack tore loose. Somewhere in the lower galleries a wall returned a knock. Somewhere on the plateau a woman put her hand on a stone and did not knock again that evening, and did not need to.

And the Throat turned, and was heard.
