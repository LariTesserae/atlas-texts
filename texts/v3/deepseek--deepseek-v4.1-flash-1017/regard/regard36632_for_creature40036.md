The power would not come down.

It would arrive first as attention—weightless, patient, and unannounced—and it would stay at the rim for a long time, learning the grammar of the place before it presumed to speak it. It would watch the dew come up through the stone. It would watch the flat ones file along the runnels and the round ones turn by degrees in their scallops. It would watch the birds defend basins they did not need and the pale things below keep the water in the rock. It would watch the walker come out of the housing at the far fold and begin, without hurry, at the near fold, going up.

And it would look for need.

It would find no emergency. It would find no sickness in the pulse of the housing, no tyranny in the circuit, no misery in the small grey lives. It would find a bench that was old and working, a being whose unnecessary work was the only prayer left on the shelf, a silence that was not loneliness but a native tongue. It would understand that age is not abandonment, that idleness and long use can look the same from the outside, and that a thing can be in balance without being dead.

So it would consider intervention and refuse most of it.

It would not speak. Its voice would either be swallowed by the stone or, worse, carry—and a place that has conducted its conversation in water and silence for so long should not be interrupted by a power that has just arrived.

It would not repair the housing. The slow pulse was not a fault. It was a heartbeat, or something that had learned to do the work of one.

It would not increase the water. That would rewrite ownership, and the ownership here is older than law. The birds know it. The slow things know it. The bench keeps its accounts.

It would not take away the walker’s rounds. The work is unnecessary, yes, but that is precisely what makes it the being’s own. To relieve it without asking would be to steal the shape of its care and call the theft a kindness.

It would not reveal itself. Revelation is a kind of claim. It would change the conversation from water to power, and the conversation was not about power.

What it would do is smaller and harder.

It would become a good guest. It would learn the drinking hour. It would learn the windward lip. It would drink and leave the rest. It would clear no runnel that was not its to clear. It would stand in the enormous bright quiet and listen to almost nothing, and it would not mistake that nothing for emptiness.

And because it was a power, its guesthood would have weight. It would set a quiet guard around the bench—not a wall, not a miracle, but a forgetfulness. To the fast instruments of the world, the shelf would read as dry rock, old, unremarkable, not worth the fuel. It would not stop rain or wind or the slow wearing of stone. It would not make the place safe from time. It would make it safe from hurry.

Then it would consider a gift, and it would choose the smallest one it could. On the far fold, where the circuit passed but did not linger, it would widen a scallop by the width of a hand and wear the lip to the old curve. It would be the kind of basin that holds a lens of water two days longer in the dry. It would not connect it to a tinaja. It would not make itself necessary. It would be an offer. The walker might clear its throat. It might not. Either would be an answer. The birds might claim it. The slow things might sit in it. The bench might take it into its grammar and forget it was ever new.

And then the power would do the hardest thing: it would not wait to be thanked. It would not make the being need it. It would not return with a lesson. It would leave one thing behind that was not an object—the fact of having been attended to. The bench would not know. The walker would not know. But the conversation would have a listener now, and that is not nothing.

If you go over the rim years from now, you will see the same bench sweating in the early light, the same grey walker going up from the near fold, the same birds arguing in their dull small voices. You might find one scallop that holds water a little longer than it should. You might not. That is how you would know the power had been there: it left the place more itself, and it left no thanks.
