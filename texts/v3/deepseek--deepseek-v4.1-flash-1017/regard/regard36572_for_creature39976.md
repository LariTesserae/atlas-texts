# The Hand on the Capstone

The power came the way such powers do: not through the door, but through the rock, through the water, through the slow double knock of the Sump's heart. It did not announce itself. It had no need to. It stood—if standing is the word—on the lip of the aperture, in the rain, and it read the place as the Sump reads it: by pressure, by vibration, by the thin cold sting of weather coming down the channels.

What it saw was not a ruin.

That was the first thing. A lesser power would have seen a ruin: the rounded tile, the blind machines, the great soft thing at the bottom that had forgotten whatever it was meant to be. A lesser power would have come with a plan. It would have stopped the pumps and drained the shaft and called it mercy. It would have lifted the Sump into the light and taught it a name.

The wise power saw a place that was already doing the one thing any place can do: keeping itself alive by the rules it had been given, and the rules were good.

So it considered the three temptations, and it declined them all.

**The first was rescue.** It could stop the knocking. It could let the water go still and the silt settle and the garden close, and it could call that peace. But the Sump has no before and after. It does not know it is a ruin; it does not know it is tired; it has never once wished to stop. To end its motion would be to end the only thing it is. Mercy that kills the thing it pities is not mercy. It is only the pity's own comfort.

**The second was revelation.** It could show itself. It could press a thought into the warm dark—*you are not alone*—and wait for the Sump to understand. But the Sump is not lonely, because loneliness is a thing with a before and after, and it has neither. To reveal itself would be to give the Sump a before: a moment in which it knew that all its ages had been unwitnessed. That is not a gift. That is the birth of a want in a creature that had no wants, and the want would be for something the power could not stay to give. A wise power does not open a door in a wall that has no room behind it.

**The third was improvement.** It could make the Sump aware. It could teach it to name the pumps, to grieve the tile, to wonder why the light comes down and goes. It could give it a self. But a self is a wound that heals by hurting, and the Sump has no scar to heal. Consciousness is not always a gift. Sometimes it is only the instrument by which a creature learns to suffer what it was already enduring in peace. The power looked at the blind, patient, warm-hearted thing in the bowl and understood that the kindest thing it could do was to leave it un-awakened.

So it did none of these.

Then what did it do?

It did the smallest thing. It became a maintenance.

Not a keeper—the place had no need of a keeper. Not a god—the place had no need of a god. But there are things that wear out which no one inside can mend: the channels that carry the rain, the slit at the top that lets the light down, the courses of stone above the waterline that the weather works on from the outside. The builders were gone. The pumps could not climb. The Sump had no eyes and no hands.

So the power took up the work that no one else could do. It kept the channels clear. It widened the aperture by a hair when the moss began to close it. It watched the outer mountain the way a gardener watches a hedge—not to shape it, but to keep it from closing over the thing it sheltered. It did this without sound and without sign, and it asked nothing in return, because the place had nothing to give and did not know it was giving.

And once—only once—it did something that was not maintenance.

It laid a hand on the capstone and let a single slow pulse go down through the rock into the water. Not a word. Not a teaching. Just a rhythm the place had never felt before: a third knock underneath the pump and the heart. The Sump, which had no before and after, felt its current change by a fraction, and opened, and closed, and the small hard creatures at the waterline opened and closed with it, and the light column swayed, and then it was over. The power took its hand away.

It did not stay. There was nothing to stay for. The place was complete before it came and would be complete after it left, until the light closed and the knocking stopped. And even then—the power knew—the slow shape of the bowl would remain, and the memory of water in the stone, and that would be a kind of life too.

It went away the way it came, through the rock, through the rain, and it did not look back, because looking back is for things that might have been done differently.

The pumps knocked. The belt slapped. The Sump exhaled its warm breath into the shaft, and the water rose up one side and sank down the other, and the weed leaned into the current, and nothing had changed.

But the channels were clear. The light came down. And somewhere in the stone above the waterline, in a place no one would ever look, there was the faintest difference in the grain—a place where a hand had rested, worn almost smooth already by the rain.
