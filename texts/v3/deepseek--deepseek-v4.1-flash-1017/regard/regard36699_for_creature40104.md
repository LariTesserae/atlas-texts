**The Neighbor at the Rim**

It did not come down. That was the first thing.

A power that has lived long enough to be wise has learned how easily a descent becomes a rescue, and how easily a rescue becomes a renovation, and how easily a renovation becomes a quiet kind of ruin. It had seen worlds that were beautiful because they were unresolved—arguments between water and root and weight—and it had seen what happened when someone arrived with clean hands and a better plan. So it came close instead, and stopped at the lip of the sinkhole, and made itself small enough to be felt only as a pause in the water’s long negotiation.

The elder weavers felt it first in the great structural roots: a note below the usual groan, a pressure that was not weight. They tapped the council rhythm on the threshold root, and the council gathered, and for a long time no one spoke. The power waited. It had learned that in a place where a shout is a small emergency, patience is the only polite language.

It listened. It learned the slide of fresh water over brine, the *tock-tock-tock* of the boring grub, the family signatures of the hum-beetles, the blue spiral of a lamp-fungus by a door, the shadow that moves before the predatory flatworm. It learned that the being who lived there was not the people alone. The being was the Agreement—the continuous negotiation between root, crustacean, fungus, shrimp, worm, predator, and weaver, all pressed close, all adjusting, all refusing to grow so large that the whole close place came down. The power addressed itself to that being, not to a king or a council alone.

When it finally spoke, it spoke in the place’s grammar: a sequence of taps on the council threshold, slow enough to be refused, repeated only twice. It offered no voice from the ceiling. It asked one question: *What may I do that you cannot do for yourselves, and that you would choose?*

The council deliberated for many days. They pressed their palms to the roots and felt the power there, and they judged that it was not yet a burden. They said: Do not stop the water. The rise is our clock, and we are moving. Do not make our ledges easy. A home must be woven to be known. Do not bring new organisms into the lattice. We have enough cousins and enough enemies. Do not make us famous above.

But they said one more thing. If a blight comes that we did not invite, if a borer enters from outside the sinkhole, if a power from the dry world comes to drain us or take us or improve us without asking—keep it from finding the way. And remember us, not as a curiosity, but as a neighbor whose Agreement you accepted.

The power agreed. It did three things, all small on the scale of its power.

It made a turn at the rim—not a wall, but a grammar of water—so that foreign blight spores and boring insects carried on outside currents would eddy and settle and die before they reached the lattice. It did not touch the native shrimp or the native grubs or the predators that belonged there. It merely made the outside less able to enter uninvited.

It learned the town’s stress-readings and its names. Not to store them as data, not to make a map for others, but to keep a listening at the roots. If a joint began to fail in a way no weaver could feel in time, it would tap a warning—one tap, no more—at the council root. The council could ignore it. The power would be judged by the Agreement like any other neighbor.

And it set a forgetfulness around the sinkhole for those who came to extract, to optimize, or to save without consent. Not a maze. A lapse. Their instruments would grow confused; their interest would dim; they would find themselves suddenly certain they had meant to go somewhere else. If someone came with humility and asked, the power would not decide for the town. It would bring them to the council and let the council decide.

It refused to stop the water. It refused to cure predation or grief. It did not make the children safe from the flatworm’s shadow; it only kept its own shadow small. It did not introduce a new root or a new god. It did not ask to be worshipped. It asked for a ledge at the edge of the Agreement—high, bare, unwanted—and the council gave it one.

Over generations, the town changed as it would have changed. The lowest rooms became reefs. New ledges were settled on the sheer walls. The water rose imperceptibly, and the people moved upward, weaving first rooms that would take decades to become watertight. The power remained a faint note in the roots, a boundary condition, a neighbor who had come as a power and stayed as a clause.

Sometimes a weaver would press a palm to a root and feel something listening back, and would tap a greeting. Sometimes the power would tap back the council rhythm, and then be still.

In the end, what it did was this: it did not save them from their world. It saved them from the outside long enough to remain themselves. It became small enough to be a neighbor and large enough to keep the extractors and the well-meaning engineers away. It accepted the Agreement—no structure so large that it collapses and damages its neighbors—and it kept it.
