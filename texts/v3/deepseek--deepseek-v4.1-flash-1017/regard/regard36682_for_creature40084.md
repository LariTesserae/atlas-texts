## What the Kindness Did

The first thing it did was nothing, and it did that for a long time.

Not the nothing of absence — the nothing of a guest standing in the doorway of a warm room, coat still on, not yet asked in. It came down through the crack and into the green roar and stood in the drip and let the humidity bead on its borrowed surfaces, and it watched. It watched the pumps cough and settle. It watched a woman with green-black knuckles walk the shoulder-roll through a fine indoor rain and not mind it. It watched a child tie a string to a beetle and be pulled along, laughing. It watched the bladder-vines pulse violet and the shelf-fungi answer the air and the lamps hiss and the hounds bark at everything and be answered by everything. It stood in the wet for what was, to it, no time at all, and to Vell was eleven years.

It saw quickly that Vell did not need saving. This is the first thing a benevolent thing must be wise enough to see, and most are not. It saw a people who had argued with a root and won a season, who had made maintenance a religion and salvage a feast, who had built a warm lit hollow between a forest that did not know them and a water that did not care, and who had made the dark into a home instead of an enemy. It saw a city that was also a single animal, breathing through a dozen iron hearts, and it saw that the animal was healthy.

So it refused the obvious benevolences. It did not lift them to the sky — they had not asked, and the sky was not their country. It did not flood the burrow with light — that would have scoured the lichen-calendar, blinded the eyes that read the pulses, killed the vines that carried the current. It did not still the forest above. A root that stops growing is a root that is dying, and Vell was not built for stillness; it was built for yielding, and the yielding was the craft, and the craft was the dignity, and to take it away would have been a kind of murder dressed as a gift. It did not tell them it was there. That, too, would have been a kind of theft — the theft of the story they were already living in, and replacing it with one about *it*.

It learned instead. It took years to learn the hand-language — a flat palm for *pump*, two fingers flicked upward for *grow*, a thumb drawn across the throat for *water rising* — and longer to learn to hear which of the dozen engines was sick by the colour of its smoke, and longer still to read the calendar of the pulses, so that it knew what a given flicker of amber meant about the humidity, and what the humidity meant about the pumps, and what the pumps meant about the week. It learned the names of the bridges and the seven crossings of the Seam. It learned which root-clefts held shrines and what was left in each, and it learned to leave nothing there, because those were not its shrines.

It made two gifts and a third that was a silence, and it was careful with all of them.

The first was foresight, given in their own idiom. The Grow-watchers walked with chalk sticks and painted stakes, measuring the slow creep of the walls, marking which roof would have to be unbuilt and rebuilt a pace to the left. One morning they found, on the underside of the eastern root, a drawing in chalk that no hand among them had made — the shape of the root's next shift, accurate to the span of a shoulder, and signed with their own sign for *grow*. They argued about it for a season. They measured. They measured again. And then they believed, and they emptied the eastern terraces a year before the root came down and took the whole slope in a single slow night of grinding, and no one was killed, and nothing was salvaged in a panic, and the move was — they said it themselves, around the fires — *a good move*. They left a cup of clean water out for whatever had drawn it. It did not drink it. It was enough that it had been left.

The second was knowledge, given in such a way that it belonged to Vell and not to any stranger. The bearings were failing, the way bearings do, and the pump-tenders were cannibalizing the oldest engine to keep the others alive, and the burrow was losing its margin. And a girl at the condenser-house — young, moss-stained, no one in particular — woke one morning with a shape in her head: a way of pouring and filing a bearing from the scrap they already had, a way that would hold. She tried it because it was in her head. It held. The pump-tenders shouted at her in the way of people who are grateful and cannot say so quietly, and the story went round the burrow that the condenser-girl had the touch, and she had it, and the credit stayed with her, and the engine-cannibalizing stopped. The Kindness watched this and was content, the way a gardener is content when the plant does the thing it was always capable of doing.

The third gift was a silence, and it was given to the deep water. It went down into the flooded seams — it did not need to breathe, and it did not need the lamp — and it looked at the pale things that are only heard, and it saw them, and it came back up. And it said nothing, to anyone, about what it had seen, because the pale things had not consented and neither had the people, and a kindness that traffics in what it has not been given is not a kindness at all. It learned only that they were not dangerous — not in the way the stories feared — and it left the first fruit out with the rest, on the bank, and understood that the offering was not fear. It was manners. It honoured the manners.

And then there was the year of the Great Move.

It was the biggest the burrow had ever faced — the eastern terraces again, and the western catwalks this time, and half the drying-racks, a whole season of tearing down and hauling and re-hanging lamps on the far side of a growth that would not be argued with. The whole city turned out. The hounds barked. The birds stole. The children carried what they could carry and got underfoot and were tolerated. And in the middle of it, in the mud and the shout and the drip, there was a stranger hauling.

Not a light. Not a voice. A pair of shoulders and a set of hands, and the hands had moss-stains in the creases and green-black at the knuckles, and the stranger worked the line with the rest of them, and pulled, and swore in the half-shout, and signed *water rising* when the channel backed up, and was not remarkable. Nobody asked where it had come from; there was too much to carry. It stayed for the moving of the lamps. It stayed for the feast, and ate what it was given, and sat where it was sat. And at the end of it an old Grow-watcher — one of the ones who had found the chalk drawing, who had measured and measured again and been right — looked at the stranger's hands for a while, and then looked at the stranger's face, and said nothing. And she took a cup of clean water from the shrine and gave it to the stranger. And the stranger drank it.

That was the only time it was ever recognised, and it was recognised by being given a cup of water and not being asked about it afterward, and there is no more perfect welcome in the world than that.

It left one small thing behind, in the root-cleft where the eastern shrines were re-hung. A cup of clean water, beside their cup. It does not go stale. Nobody has explained it. Nobody has tried very hard. It is simply one of the things in that cleft now, and the children point at it, and the old ones nod at it, and it is left alone, which is the highest form of respect in a city that has learned to leave the dark things alone.

And what it does now — what it has done every day since, and will do for as long as Vell stands, which is to say for as long as Vell keeps moving, which is to say forever — is very little. It attends. It keeps the kind of watch that does not intervene: it knows the calendar of the pulses, it hears which engine is sick, it sees which wall has begun to lean, and it does not act, because Vell knows how to act, and its job is not to know better than Vell. Its job is to be the one thing in the whole green roaring world that is paying attention on purpose. The forest above goes on and does not know they are there. The water below goes on and does not care. And in the little lit hollow between, the engines grind, the beetles trundle, the vines pulse violet, the drops fall — and there is one more thing in the dark, quieter than the drip, that loves them, and will not say so, and will not leave.
