# The Listener in the Green

---

## I. What came up the valley

It did not come as light, or as voice, or as any of the shapes that powers take when they wish to be seen. It came the way the cloud comes up the Throat in the morning — gradually, from below, by degrees, until it is simply there and has always been there.

It was old. It had been old for a very long time, and it had learned the one lesson that old powers learn if they are wise, which is this: *do not improve a living thing.* The living thing is already doing the hardest work there is. It is continuing. Your improvements are for you, not for it.

So it did not appear in the Soke. It did not announce. It came up the valley in the hour before the Spill, when the light is failing and the swifts are gathering in the Throat and the whole terrace is holding its breath, and it went into the wet rock wall at the back of the shelf, and it listened.

---

## II. What it heard

It heard the spring first — the mother-spring, cold and steady, coming out of the rock the way it had come out for longer than there had been anyone to hear it. It heard the spring divided and subdivided: the home channel, the branch, the branch of the branch, the one that drops over the third stair like a slap. It heard the mutter under the tanner's floor and the slap under the bridge and the small bright chatter of the channel that runs through the split reeds along the upper terrace edge.

It heard the moss. This is a thing that powers hear and people do not. The moss was not silent. Moss is never silent. It was a low continuous drinking, a million tiny mouths taking water and holding it, and the holding made a sound like the sound a held breath makes when you finally let it go. The whole town was muffled in that sound. The walls were drinking, the roofs were drinking, the bark-felt was drinking. The Soke was not built on stone. It was built on a thirst.

It heard the cutting. This was the loudest thing, once you learned to hear it — not the sound of the blade but the sound of the *decision*, a thousand small decisions a day, each one a negotiation between what the green wanted and what the town needed. A hooked knife going into a graft that had begun to close a doorway. A wedge tapped into a floor-bearer that had begun to lift. A hand steadying a branch while another hand cut it, and the cut branch falling into a basket, and the basket going to the smouldering heap, and the smoke rising, and the smoke being the town's exhalation. The Soke was not a place that had been built and was now being maintained. It was a place that was being *thought*, continuously, by everyone in it at once, and the thinking was done with knives.

It heard the crowd. Frogs in three voices. Crickets in four. The moth-hatch, which is not a sound but a pressure, like the Spill but smaller and longer and lower down. The cats, half-wild, calling to each other across the wet roofs in the hour before dawn. The bats, which are the Soke's quietest loud thing — a high thin ticking that most people stop hearing by middle age and that the children still hear and do not know they hear. The eels in the Throat, moving up in the dark. The otters above them, not hunting, just moving. The leeches. The beetle that the children race, which makes a sound like a fingernail on a comb. The mice in the granaries, which make the sound that all granaries make, which is the sound of the town's future being eaten by the town's present, which the Soke has decided is fine, because the cats are there, because the cats are always there.

And it heard the people. It heard them talking under the water, close, clipped, reading each other's mouths. It heard Pell of the Low Steps telling a child what the water sounded like before the child was born, and it heard the precision of the lies, and it heard that the lies were a kind of gift. It heard Sisk waking a household for a graft that had not yet failed and would fail in a season, and it heard the household get up, and it heard that getting up was the whole of what the Soke believed. It heard the tally-sticks and the arguments about the map and the two old men who would spend an evening proving that the baker's steps stood where the tanner's steps stood, both of them right, each in his own decade.

And then the Spill came, and everything stopped, and the power that had come up the valley stood in the street with everyone else and let it happen.

It had heard the Spill before, in other places, in other valleys. It had never heard one like this. The birds came up the Throat like weather and poured over the terrace and into the wet rock wall and into the eaves of the grown houses, and the sound was not a sound, it was a pressure, and the pressure was the whole town being briefly and completely *outnumbered* by something that meant them no harm and could not be stopped and did not need to be stopped, and the town had made its peace with that a long time ago, and the peace was the shape of the town, and the shape of the town was the shape of the peace.

When it was over, the town went back to its evening. The power stayed where it was. It had not come to change anything. It had come to understand, and it had understood, and now it had a decision to make, and the decision was the only decision that a wise and benevolent power ever really has to make, which is: *what do I do with what I know?*

---

## III. The wrong things it could have done

It considered, first, the obvious things, and rejected them all, and it is worth saying why, because the obvious things are what most powers do.

It could have made the Soke permanent. It could have stilled the water into channels of unbreakable stone and fixed the houses in place and made the grafts take forever and the moss die back and the green stop reaching. It could have given the Soke a shape and kept it, and the Soke would have been safe and quiet and would have died in a generation, because the Soke is not a thing that can be kept. The Soke is a negotiation. To fix it is to end the negotiation. To end the negotiation is to end the Soke. The first law of the place is *no structure outlives its pruning*, and a power that made the structures outlive their pruning would be a power that had not understood the first thing about the place. It would have been a kindness that killed. There is no shortage of those.

It could have made the Soke rich. It could have brought copper up from the lowlands in abundance, so that the knives need never be worn to slivers, so that the grafting could be done with better tools, so that the children need not eat moths. And the Soke would have taken the copper and thanked the power and used it well and been a little less itself every year, because the Soke's wisdom is the wisdom of *enough*, and the Soke's enough is a sliver of a grandmother's knife and a basket of moths and a tally-stick that is always a little wrong. Abundance is not the Soke's medicine. Abundance is the lowlands' disease. The Soke has been carefully, deliberately poor for a very long time, and it has been poor on purpose, and the purpose is the town. A power that did not see this would have been a generous fool.

It could have stopped the floods and the collapses. It could have held the travertine still and kept the Throat from taking a house every decade. And the Soke would have thanked it, and the Soke would have grown strange, because the Soke's courage is the courage of people who know that the ground will move and the water will shift and a house will go down into the Throat with a family in it and the town will rebuild on the stump, lower and steeper, and the stump will be why the new house is a little lower and the steps are a little steeper, and the town will be *itself* because of it. A power that took away the moving would have taken away the courage, and the courage is the Soke. It would have been a mercy that unmade them.

It could have spoken. It could have appeared in the Spill and said *I am here and I am wise and I will help you*, and the Soke would have listened, because the Soke listens to everything, and the Soke would have made a place in the wall for it, a deliberate knot worked and reworked so it would not straighten, and the power would have become a monument, and the Soke does not build monuments. The first thing the Soke would have done is cut it back. The second thing the Soke would have done is graft it to its neighbours until it was no longer a power but a wall. The third thing the Soke would have done is forget it was ever anything else. This is not ingratitude. This is the Soke's only way of loving a thing: to cut it, graft it, and let the grain go wrong on purpose, so that the house grows around it. A power that wanted to be remembered would have been remembered wrong, and the wrongness would have been the point, and the power would have had to be wise enough to find that funny, and most powers are not.

So it did none of these things. It sat in the wet rock wall with the spring, and it listened, and it waited for the Soke to tell it what the Soke needed, in the only language the Soke speaks, which is the language of *what is already being done and what is getting in the way*.

---

## IV. What it did

### It made the spring a little steadier.

Not much. Not enough to notice. But the Soke's spring had been variable in a way the Soke had built its whole calendar around, and the variability was beginning to cost more than it gave. A dry season here, a surge there, a channel that ran true for a decade and then wandered. The power held the spring's heart a little still — not fixed, not permanent, just *less frightened* — so that the mother-water came out of the rock the way a calm person comes into a room. The Cut would still have to follow it. The Cut would always have to follow it. But the following would be a following and not a scramble, and the scramble was the thing that took the children, sometimes, in the low channels in the wet season.

The Soke noticed. Of course the Soke noticed. The Soke notices everything about water. Pell of the Low Steps noticed within a season and said the spring had *settled*, the way an old dog settles, and she said it with suspicion, and then she said it with something that was not quite gratitude, because the Soke does not do gratitude for the water; the water is not a gift, it is a fact, and you do not thank a fact. But she told the children, and the children told each other, and the phrase *the year the spring settled* went into the map, and the map-keeper wrote it down, and the writing down was the Soke's way of saying *we have made a place for this in the story and we will argue about it forever*, which is the Soke's highest honour.

### It thinned the seep-line.

There is a rule in the Soke that nobody builds on the seep-line at the foot of the wall, and everyone obeys it, and no one can quite say why, and the approximate answer is a collapse two hundred years ago, and the real answer is older than that. The real answer is that the seep-line is where the mountain is *thinking about moving*, and the thinking is slow, and the Soke's obedience is a memory in the body that outlasts the reason. The power did not explain the rule. It did not need to. It simply made the thinking a little slower. The seep-line would still be the seep-line, and the rule would still be the rule, and the obedience would still be the obedience, and the collapse, when it came, would be a collapse the Soke could rebuild from, and not one it could not.

This is what a wise power does. It does not remove the danger. It keeps the danger *survivable*. It keeps the lesson that the danger taught, because the lesson is the town, and it softens the danger just enough that the lesson does not have to be learned again from nothing.

### It listened to the graft.

The Soke grafts its dead into the frames of its houses. A deliberate knot, worked and reworked every year so it will not straighten, and a very old house is full of them, and a very old house is therefore a very sad one, and the Soke finds this comforting. The power found this comforting too, once it understood. It had seen many ways that people keep their dead, and most of them are ways of *not letting go*, and the Soke's way is the opposite: the knot is worked and reworked so it will not straighten, which means the grief is *maintained*, which means the grief is *allowed to be a practice*, which means it does not curdle. The Soke does not visit its dead. The Soke touches a place in the wall. The touching is the visiting, and the touching is done every year with a knife, and the knife is a grandmother's sliver, and the sliver is the only thing that does not get eaten, and the not-getting-eaten is the Soke's one small heresy against its own law, and the power loved the Soke for it.

So the power did a small thing. It made the graft a little more likely to hold. Not forever — nothing holds forever, and the Soke would not want it to — but a little longer, a little truer, so that a family that had worked a knot for nine cuttings could put a hand on it and feel the grain go wrong the way it had gone wrong for nine cuttings, and not feel it drifting, and not have to wonder. The grief would still be a practice. The practice would still be a cut. But the cut would take, and the taking would be a kind of answer, and the answer was the only kind the Soke would accept: *yes, this is still here, and so are you, and now go cut something.*

### It learned the Spill.

This was the thing the power had not expected, and it is the thing it took home with it.

The Spill is not a gift to the Soke and the Soke does not treat it as one. The Spill is a *fact*, like the water, like the seep-line, like the collapse. The Soke stops for it, and the stopping is the whole town's obedience to something larger than the town, and the obedience is not fear and is not worship and is not quite respect either. It is closer to what you feel when a wave goes over you in the sea: you are not afraid of the wave, you do not worship the wave, you simply *yield to the wave*, and the yielding is the whole of your relationship with it, and the yielding is enough.

The power had seen a thousand places that tried to *use* their great movements — to harvest them, to name them, to make them into festivals and coin and story. The Soke used the Spill by stopping. That was all. The stopping was the use. The stopping was the town remembering, twice a year, that it was outnumbered and that the being outnumbered was not a defeat and was not a victory and was simply the condition of being a town on a shelf above a Throat, and the remembering was done together, in the street, with faces tipped up, and then it was over and the town went back to its evening.

The power thought: *this is what I have been looking for without knowing it. A people who know how to be small.*

And it did one more thing, the last thing, and then it was done.

### It gave the Soke a listener.

Not a voice. A listener. A presence in the wet rock wall, next to the mother-spring, that would hear what the Soke heard — the channels, the grafts, the frogs, the crowd, the Spill — and would *hold* the hearing, the way the moss holds the water, so that the Soke's hearing would not be the only hearing, and so that the Soke would be, in the smallest possible way, a little less alone with the noise.

The Soke did not know this. The Soke does not need to know this. A listener that announces itself is a monument, and the Soke cuts monuments back. But the Soke's ear, which is the organ of the Soke, would find, over the cuttings, that the water was a little clearer to it — that a graft that was failing was a little easier to hear, that a channel that had wandered was a little easier to find, that the Spill, when it came, was a little easier to yield to, because something else was yielding with it.

The Soke would call this *the years the ear got better*, and it would argue about when they started and whether they had started at all, and both sides would be right in different decades, and the arguing would be the town's favourite sport, and the sport would be the town being itself, and the being itself was the whole point.

---

## V. What it left

It left nothing you could point to. This is the mark of a wise power: you cannot find where it was. The spring is the spring. The seep-line is the seep-line. The graft is a graft. The Spill is the Spill. The Soke is a little better at being the Soke, and no one can say why, and the no-one-can-say-why is the power's signature, and the signature is invisible, and the invisibility is the gift.

It left one thing that was almost a sign, and it left it in the only place the Soke would not cut it back, which is the map. The map is the town's one sacred object and its favourite quarrel, and the Cut makes it every spring, and the making is an argument, and the argument is a kind of love. In the spring after the power came, the map-keeper — who is old and dry and has not cut in a decade and is kept on because the map is his whole remaining ear — found that the map had a small error. A channel on the lower terrace was drawn a finger's width to the left of where the water actually ran. He corrected it, and the correction made the channel run *through* the seep-line in a way that the map had never shown, and the showing made the seep-line's old rule suddenly legible in a way it had not been legible before: *this is where the mountain thinks about moving, and this is why we do not build here, and here is the shape of the thinking, and it is not a superstition, it is a map.*

The map-keeper looked at the corrected map for a long time. Then he called in the Cut, and the Cut looked at it, and then they called in Pell of the Low Steps, and Pell looked at it and said nothing for a while and then said, "Well." And then she said, "That's where it goes, then." And that was the whole of the discovery, and it went into the map, and the map was better, and the better map was the power's only monument.

It was a monument of the right kind. It was a place where the grain went wrong on purpose, and the wrongness was the point, and the point was that the Soke had been right all along and had not quite known it, and now it knew it, and the knowing was a cut, and the cut would take.

---

## VI. What the Soke says now

They do not say anything. They do not know there is anything to say. This is exactly how it should be.

But there is a phrase that enters the Soke in the years after, and it enters the way all Soke phrases enter, which is sideways, through the children. Sisk — who is nine, and then ten, and then a cutter of the Cut, and then an old woman who tells children what the water sounded like before they were born — is the first to say it. She says it to Pell of the Low Steps, in the cool of a morning, with her hand on a graft that will hold a little longer than grafts usually hold:

*"The water's listening back."*

Pell does not correct her. Pell is old and has outlived four houses on the same stump and knows the sound of every graft on the lower terrace, and she has heard, in the last few cuttings, a steadiness in the spring that she cannot account for, and a clarity in her own ear that she does not trust, and a holding in the graft that is not quite natural. Pell is a Soker, which means she does not believe in permanence and does not believe in gifts and does not believe in powers that come up the valley to help. But Pell is also a Soker, which means she believes in the ear, and the ear is telling her something, and the something is not a word, it is a *pressure*, and the pressure is the same pressure as the Spill, and the Spill is not a gift either, and the Spill is a fact, and you do not thank a fact, but you do yield to it.

So Pell says, "Mm," which is what she says when she is agreeing and does not want to be seen agreeing, and she puts her hand on the graft next to Sisk's hand, and the two of them stand there in the wet morning with their hands on a knot that will not straighten, and the water runs, and the frogs sing, and the moss drinks, and the whole terrace is briefly and completely itself.

And the power in the rock wall, next to the mother-spring, hears all of it, and holds it, and is content.

It had come up the valley to do something for the Soke. It had found, as wise powers do, that the something was nothing, and that the nothing was everything, and that the Soke had been right all along: *no structure outlives its pruning*, and the pruning is the love, and the love is the cut, and the cut is the whole of the town.

It did not improve the Soke. It listened to the Soke. And the listening, which is the smallest thing a power can do, was the only thing that was not a kindness that killed.

The Spill is coming. The town will stop. The power will stop with it. And then the town will go back to its evening, a little shaken, and the power will go back to the wall, a little shaken, and both of them will be the better for it, and neither of them will say so, because saying so would be a monument, and the Soke does not build monuments.

It builds listeners.

And the listeners, if they are wise, build nothing at all.

---

*The Soke does not know. The Soke does not need to know. The Soke has its ear, and its ear is a little better than it was, and the bettering is the whole of the gift, and the gift is the whole of the wisdom, and the wisdom is the oldest thing there is:*

*Do not fix the living thing. Listen to it. Cut what needs cutting. Yield to the wave. And when the Spill comes, stand in the street with everyone else and let it happen to you.*

*That is all. That is everything. The water is listening back.*
