The power would come, because that is what benevolence does — it notices. It would come not as light or voice or visitation but as attention, which is the only thing a wise power trusts itself to bring into a place like this: the long, patient regard of something that has seen ten thousand such pockets and has never once mistaken one for an accident.

It would come over the hummocks in the way that cold comes, without arriving. It would stand — if standing is what it does — under the larch roots and above the sump, and it would not fill the hollow, because a wise power knows that a small room wants to stay small. It would fold itself down to the scale of the place. It would listen to nine seconds.

And the first thing it would do is nothing. That is the first thing. Because the pocket is not broken and does not want to be mended, and the power knows the difference between a wound and a mouth. It would let the piston run. It would let the moss keep its gauge. It would watch the blade of light cross the rust and find the coin of the sump and go out, and it would not hasten a single hour of it, because the light is not late. Nine seconds is not too long to wait and it is not too short to be worth the wait. The power would simply attend, and its attention would be the only change in the room, and it would be a change so gentle that the springtails would not feel it and the amphipods would ride their pulse exactly as before.

But attention is not nothing. Attention is already the beginning of care, and care in a wise power has a way of becoming a kind of company, and company, offered to a place that has kept time for no one, is a gift that costs the giver nothing and the receiver nothing and is still the largest thing that has ever happened in the room. So it would stay a while. It would lower itself — not into the water, the water is for the residents — but to the lip of the larch roots, and it would let its regard rest on the things that cannot move: the moss, the tide-gauge wall of caddis cases, the little dry spark that jumps when a drop does not land.

And it would understand something that no one has ever understood about this place, and the understanding would be its act. It would see that the caddis have built their address out of faith and pressure, and that this is not sad, because the case is the address and the address is the only place the water is warm and the water is warm in exactly the right place, and a life built on nine seconds is not a smaller life than a life built on a year. It would see that the springtails are a nervous system that has learned to breathe, and that this too is not sad, because the size of a world is not what makes it worth keeping. It would see the amphipod riding the surge up and letting the surge carry it back down, never seeing the coin of light, and it would not grieve, because the amphipod's world is warm and full of food and keeps a rhythm, and to be content in nine seconds is no less than to be content in ninety years. The power would know this because it has seen both, and it has never been able to say which is the better gift.

What it would do, having seen all this, is the small thing. It would make the place a little more what it already is. Not by adding — a wise power does not add to a room that is a wound that decided to be a room — but by holding, the way a cupped hand holds a candle against wind. It would steady the nine seconds so that they do not drift. Not that they drift; they have never drifted; but a power that loves a clock loves it enough to keep it wound, and the holding would be invisible and the interval would be exactly nine seconds and neither the moss nor the amphipod would ever know a difference. It would be, for the pocket, what the pocket is for its residents: a thing that keeps the beat for no one and then elects, out of its own fullness, to keep it for someone after all.

And on the nights when the turf freezes harder and the slot narrows and the hum goes up a tone, the power would bend close and listen to the tone, and it would not correct it, because the tone is the hum finding the note the winter makes of it, and a wise power knows that cold is one of the things a place is allowed to feel. It would hold the spark in the fog — the lifting of hair, the taste of pennies, the drop that almost lands — and it would let the charge be exactly what it is: a sign that the young stone has not forgotten it is young, that it is still deciding what it will be when the ice is finished with it. And in some century the stone would settle and the spark would soften and the power would be there, still, for that too, as it is here now for the spark.

Then, when the season turned in whatever way seasons turn for a power, it would withdraw its attention the way a guest leaves a room where someone is sleeping. Not abandoning — a guest who leaves gently is not abandoning. It would leave the blade of light doing its work and the piston doing its work and the moss leaning into its warm ring, and it would go back out over the hummocks and over the peat and up into the grey, and it would carry nothing away with it except the knowledge that the pocket goes on. And perhaps — this is the part I cannot say — perhaps as it goes it would leave, not a blessing exactly, not a mark, nothing a person could point to, one small certainty in the root-laced dark: that the interval is seen, and the ones who keep it are seen, and to be seen by a wise power is to be, in a way that costs nothing and changes nothing, kept.

And the sump would go on breathing its nine seconds with its whole small timed population inside it, keeping the beat, keeping the beat, keeping the beat — now for no one, and also, quietly, for one.
