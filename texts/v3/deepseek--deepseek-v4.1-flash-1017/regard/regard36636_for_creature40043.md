# The Long Meditation

A power that is both benevolent and wise would understand, first, that it had not come to a place that was broken.

The Keeper was not suffering. It had no word for suffering and no architecture in which such a word might be stored. It was doing what it had always done—sealing, tending, counting in pressures and temperatures—and it would go on doing it for as long as the splinter held. The white plain was not a wasteland to the Keeper. It was simply *not here*. The distinction between "alive" and "not alive" was legible only on the splinter, and everything beyond the exact boundary of the green was a condition, like cold, like silence, like the long afternoon sun.

So the power would not bring water. That would be an act of violence disguised as mercy—would drown the pods, burst the bladders, flood the deep chambers. Would rot the laminated dead. Would end the Keeper as surely as a knife.

---

What the power would do, if it were wise, is this:

**It would watch.**

Not in the way of a scientist with instruments, but in the way of a guest who has been invited into a stranger's house and is careful not to touch anything. It would come at dusk—if it came at all—and it would stand at the edge of the splinter where the green stops, and it would feel the warmth seeping out of the stone. It would note how the bladders open, one after another, down the whole summit, with that soft leathery sigh. It would feel the sub-audible thrum of the colony working in the walls. It would be still.

And it would be *known*. The Keeper would feel the warmth of its body, the moisture of its breath, the weight of its feet on the old worn paths. The Keeper would mark it as a leak, a source, a thing from which water was escaping into the dry air. The power, if it understood this, would not be offended. It would understand that it *was* a leak, by the standards of this world. It would accept the accounting.

---

**It would listen.**

What does the Keeper say, in its language of pressure and temperature and chemistry? It says: *The pods are full. The robbers have found a seam in the eastern gallery. The sun is too hot on the south face. The lean season is coming; tighten the stores.*

The power would listen to this as a linguist listens to a language with no words—not translating, but *hearing*. It would learn the grammar of the place: the way a bladder knocks against another in the wind; the way the cement creaks as it sets; the way the whole town folds shut at midday and opens at dusk like a thing breathing. It would learn that the silence was not empty. It was *full*—full of small signals too quiet, too slow, too low for anything with ears.

The power would not interrupt.

---

**It would bring nothing, and leave nothing.**

This is the hardest part of wisdom: to see a thing that is not like you, that will never be like you, that does not want to be like you, and to leave it exactly as it is. The Keeper does not need a teacher. It does not need a savior. It does not need a translator or a friend. It has the pods and the galleries and the working of the walls. It has the patience of stone.

The power would not bring gifts. Not water, not shade, not a companion for the long dry. A gift is a demand in disguise—a demand that the receiver become the kind of thing that can accept gifts. The Keeper cannot. It can only *keep*. To give it anything would be to ask it to be something other than what it is.

So the power would come empty-handed. It would stand at the edge. It would watch the light change on the bladder-plants—the green-gold shimmer of fluid held at blood temperature—and it would feel the warmth go out of the stone and into the cooling air. It would hear the soft leathery sigh of the canopy opening.

And then it would leave.

---

**But it would carry something away.**

Not a sample. Not a record. Something less tangible and more durable: *the knowledge that this exists.* That in a dry and quiet corner of a dry and quiet world, a thing that is not a person and not a people has built a town out of patience and sealed water and the slow accumulation of a millimeter a year. That the town is older than the ground it stands on. That the dead are pressed into the walls as filler and are not mourned and are not forgotten—they are *used*. That the robbers come and the bladders armor themselves. That the whole summit folds shut at midday and opens at dusk, and the sound of it is a soft leathery sigh that runs across the roof of the place like something breathing out.

The power would carry this away the way you carry a stone from a beach—not to study, not to display, but because you were there and it was there and the weight of it in your pocket is a kind of memory. A reminder that not everything needs to be understood to be *known*. That not everything needs to be saved to be *worthy*.

---

And on some later evening, in some other place, the power might find itself thinking of the splinter. Not with longing—the Keeper would not recognize longing, would not know what to do with it—but with a kind of *respect*. The respect of one long patience for another. The respect of a watcher for a keeper. The respect of a thing that moves through the world for a thing that *holds*.

The Keeper would not know it was being remembered. It would be sealing a seam. It would be counting pods. It would be moving in its slow tides through the interstices of the bladder-plants, laying down cement a millimeter a year, and the white plain would go on being featureless and dry and the sun would go on being hard and clean and the town would fold shut at midday and open at dusk, and the sound of it would be a soft leathery sigh that runs across the roof of the place like something breathing out.

And that would be enough.

That would be, in fact, everything.
