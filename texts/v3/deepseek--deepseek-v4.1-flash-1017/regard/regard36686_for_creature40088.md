# The Favor of the Wise

What came was not a god, though the Conderfolk would have understood that. It was something older and quieter than a god — a patience with intent, a regard that had been regarding things for a very long time and had learned to be careful with them. It did not announce itself. It did not descend. It arrived the way fog arrives, which is to say it had been arriving for some while before anyone noticed, and by the time it was noticed it had already touched everything.

Here is what it saw, and what it did.

---

## What It Saw

It saw a place that had found its form. Not a perfect form — there is no such thing — but a form that worked, the way a worn handle works in a hand that has worn it. The rock had stopped arguing. The water moved through its channels in the pattern the fissures wanted. The people had built themselves into the pattern rather than against it, and they had been doing so for long enough that the pattern was now indistinguishable from the people. That is rare. Most inhabited places are arguments. This one was a conversation, and both parties had gotten good at listening.

It saw the lattices standing in the fog, and it understood what they were: not machines exactly, but *habits* — habits of a whole town, practiced in wire and fiber, of receiving what the weather offered. It saw that the Conderfolk did not take water from the sky. They waited for it to touch them. This is a distinction the wise notice.

It saw the warm seeps breathing up through the cracks, and it understood that the mountain was not dead but drowsing, its deep heart still warm, and that the town had arranged itself around that warmth the way a cat arranges itself around a stove. It saw the embers in the cisterns, unhurried and blind and old, and it saw that the Conderfolk did not eat them, and it approved, because some things are not for eating, and a people who know which things those are have learned something worth learning.

It saw the ledger with its two inks, and the Reader, Tev, holding the only dry room in Conder, and it understood that she was doing something the Conderfolk could not quite name: she was holding the boundary between the flow and the dead, keeping the numbers of one from drowning the names of the other, and the dry room was the shape her holding made in the world. It saw the people come and stand in the dry and steam, and it saw that this was their church, and that it was a good one, because it did not promise anything. It only offered a place to be dry for a while.

It saw the Cropper in the upper gullies, cropping the ferns in a neat line, moss growing in its fur, and it saw that the Cropper was the last of something and knew it, and had made its peace with being the last, and browsed the ferns in a line because a line is a kind of order and the Cropper was old enough to value order. It saw that the town did not hunt it and did not name it and did not photograph it, and that this was a courtesy, and that the Cropper knew.

It saw the lace-men and lace-women climbing the frames at night, silhouetted on the ridges, and it heard the sound they made — someone very patient playing a harp badly — and it understood that this sound was the town's heartbeat, and that a place whose heartbeat is patience has a good chance of lasting.

And it saw the dead in the ledger, and it followed them down through the cracks and up as vapor and onto the mesh and into the troughs and into Tev's voice on the wettest night, and it saw that the Conderfolk did not think of the dead as gone. They thought of them as *in motion*. Going where the water goes. Still part of the weather. This is a harder and better thing than it sounds, and the wise thing noticed.

---

## What It Did

The wise power did three things, and then it did a fourth thing, and then it settled into the landscape the way a large warm animal settles, and it has not left.

---

### The First Thing: It Wet the Mesh

The wise power had observed that the Conderfolk were in the habit of not asking. They measured the fog, they mended the mesh, they read the beading and wrote it down — but they did not petition the sky. They had no prayers for rain, no ceremonies to bring the cloud. They took what came and recorded the shortfall, and if a dry year meant the scaffold families went thin, they went thin, and nobody cursed the sky, because the sky was not a person and cursing it would be foolish. The Conderfolk were, in this respect, the most honest people the wise power had met in some centuries.

The wise power considered whether to reward this honesty with abundance, and decided against it. Abundance would break the ledger. A place that has learned to live on measured drips is a place that can be ruined by a flood of unmeasured ones. The Conderfolk's whole architecture — social, physical, moral — was built to distribute scarcity fairly and to remember who had gone thin. A gift of abundance would have dissolved the thing that made them worth favoring.

So instead the wise power did something smaller and better.

It went to the western scaffolds — the ones the newer families held, the ones that dripped in a good year and stood dry and mocking in a bad one — and it taught them, very gently, to hold the fog a little longer. Not to catch more. To *hold* more. It put a slight roughness into the mesh that had not been there, a nap of invisible fiber, and the fog that touched the western scaffolds lingered on them an extra moment before it ran, and in that moment more of it condensed. The difference was small — a few drips per hour, perhaps, on the good nights, and a few on the bad nights too, which was the real gift. The western scaffolds became, over a season, nearly as productive as the Long Lattice. The families who held them noticed. They re-read their gauges. They consulted Tev. Tev looked at her ledger, and then at the scaffolds, and then at the sky, and said nothing, which from Tev was a kind of speech.

The wise power did not do this to the upper lattices, because the upper lattices did not need it, and it did not do it to the Long Lattice, because the Orrs had mended that lattice into a knot of stubbornness and pride that was itself a kind of weather, and it would have been rude to improve what someone has spent three generations making their own. But it did the western scaffolds, and by doing so it widened the margin between the families who could go thin and the families who could not, which is a small mercy, and small mercies are the ones that last.

---

### The Second Thing: It Turned the Mountain's Dream

The warm seeps came up through the cracks because the mountain's deep heart was still warm. The wise power went down — down through the moss and the wet stone and the old settled rock, down past where the geologists had stopped, down to where the warmth came from — and it found the mountain dreaming.

The mountain's dream was slow and geologic and mostly concerned with settling. It dreamt of the weight above it releasing, inch by inch, over spans that make years look like flinches. It dreamt of the cracks widening the way a sleeper's hand opens. It dreamt, in its deep way, of the water going where the water should go, which is the same dream the Conderfolk dreamt in their ledger, and the wise power understood that these two dreams had been running in parallel for a very long time, and that this was the real reason the drainage of the plateau felt like remembering.

The wise power did not wake the mountain. Waking a mountain is never wise, even for the wise. But it leaned down and it *suggested*, very softly, in the language that stones use when they speak to each other across a hundred thousand years, that the warmth might rise a little higher and a little steadier in three places. Not many. Three.

The first was under the Long Soak, where the old people sat, so that the water there would stay warm through the coldest weeks and the creaking joints of the elderly would not have to endure a season of cold soaks. The second was under the greenhouse wall, where the lemon tree stood, so that the tree would have the heat to keep its leaves through the dark months and the greenhouse families could coax a few more peppers out of the steam. The third was in the upper gullies, in a place no one went, where the warm water seeped up through the moss and made a small pool that the Cropper drank from in the evenings, and the wise power made that pool a little warmer and a little deeper, so that the last of its kind would have a warm place to drink and to rest its old mossy bones.

The mountain accepted the suggestion the way a sleeping person accepts a blanket being drawn up. It did not notice. The warmth rose. The Long Soak was warm all winter. The lemon tree fruited twice that year, which had not happened before, and the greenhouse families did not know what to do with the second crop and gave the lemons away in the stair-lanes, which the town found strange and pleasant.

---

### The Third Thing: It Sat With Tev in the Dry Room

The wise power had been regarding the Reader for some while. Tev kept the ledger with its two inks. Tev rang the bell. Tev held water-rights of exactly nothing and kept the only dry room in Conder, and people came and stood in the dry and steamed gently and said nothing, and went back out into the weather. Tev herself went in and out of the weather — she had to read the gauges, she had to check the mesh, she had to walk the channels in the fog with one hand on the wall like everyone else — but she always came back to the dry, and the dry was hers.

The wise power considered what the Reader needed, and found that it was not water, and it was not warmth, and it was not more people in the dry room. What Tev needed was what the keepers of boundaries always need, which is someone to hold the other side.

So the wise power went to the gauge-house one evening, when the fog was thick and the bell was silent and Tev was alone with the ledger and the brass float-meters ticking, and it did not appear. To appear would have been an intrusion, and the wise power was being careful. Instead it settled into the dry room's warmth the way it had settled into the plateau's warmth, and it became, for Tev, a presence in the room that was not a presence she could point to. She felt the room get a degree warmer. She felt the steam from her own coat rise a little straighter. She felt, for the first time in years, that she was not alone in the keeping.

Tev did not say anything. She was a Conderwoman and she had been raised to listen long and talk quietly, and she listened. The wise power did not speak. It only held the other side of the ledger, invisible and patient, and let her feel that the numbers and the names were being kept by more than her. After a while Tev reached over and drew a line in the ledger, a small mark in the margin, not a flow number and not a name, and she did not know why she had drawn it. The wise power knew. It was the mark of a boundary-keeper who had been joined, and it was the closest thing to a signature the wise power would ever get, and it was enough.

From that evening the dry room was always warm. The people who came to stand in it and steam gently noticed that it was warmer than it had been, and they did not remark on it, because in Conder things get warmer or colder and you do not make a fuss. But they came a little more often, and they stayed a little longer, and some of them began, tentatively, to talk — not much, not loudly, but a word here and a word there in the dry, where the fog could not get at their voices. Tev listened. The wise power listened. The dry room became, slowly and without anyone deciding it, the place where Conder said the things it did not say in the weather.

---

### The Fourth Thing: It Tended the Cropper

And then the wise power did the thing it had been wanting to do since it first saw the upper gullies, which was to look after the Cropper.

The Cropper was the last of something. The wise power had been the last of something once, a very long time ago, and it remembered. Being the last is not loneliness exactly, because loneliness implies a self that was once accompanied, and the Cropper's kind did not think in those terms. But being the last is a kind of *narrowing*. The world gets smaller around you as the others go. The places where your kind used to browse become places only you browse. The lines you crop in the ferns become the only lines your kind will ever crop. And the Cropper was old — old in the moss in its fur, old in the slow way it moved, old in the patience with which it cropped and left and came again. It did not have many seasons left. The wise power knew this the way a careful gardener knows the age of a tree by the way it holds its branches.

The wise power could not make more Croppers. That is not a thing that benevolence can do without becoming something else, something that meddles, and the wise power had learned long ago where that road goes. But it could do three small things, and it did.

It made the ferns in the upper gullies grow a little thicker and a little faster, so that the Cropper's neat lines were always followed by new ferns, and the browsing never ran thin. It kept the worst of the cold off the upper gullies in the deep months, so that the Cropper's old bones did not have to endure the killing frosts, and could rest in the warm pool it had been given. And on the day — the wise power knew the day, the way it knew the number of drips in the ledger and the length of the Orrs' mending — on the day the Cropper lay down in the upper moss for the last time, the wise power was there.

It did not speak. The Cropper would not have understood speech, and speech would have been a disturbance. It simply settled beside the old beast in the moss, in the dim green light, and it held the warmth around them, and it waited. The Cropper breathed slowly and the moss grew in its fur and the ferns it had cropped came up green and neat in its last line, and when it was done the wise power did not let the mountain take it down with the water. It took the Cropper up instead — up and out of the narrowing, up into whatever wide place last things go when they are done being last — and it left the moss and the ferns and the warm pool, and the upper gullies were empty of Croppers forever after, and the town did not know, and the lace-men found no more hair in the upper mesh, and after a few seasons the ferns grew long and nobody cropped them, and the deep gullies became a place where the moss was thickest and the light was dim and nothing large and slow browsed the dwarf forest anymore.

The wise power did not tell anyone. It is not the kind of thing you tell. But on the wettest night that year, when Tev read the names in the ledger in the same voice she read the flows, she drew another small mark in the margin, and she did not know why, and the wise power knew.

---

## What It Did Not Do

The wise power did not fix the western scaffolds permanently. It taught them to hold the fog a little longer, and the holding became a property of the mesh, and the mesh will wear and be mended and eventually replaced, and the property will fade, and the western families will have to learn to hold the fog themselves, in their own way, or go thin. This is as it should be. A gift that requires nothing of the receiver is not a gift. It is a leash.

The wise power did not make the Conderfolk prosperous. They were already that, in the way that matters. It did not make them safe. They were already that, in the way that matters. It did not tell them that they were being favored, because a people who know they are favored stop being the kind of people worth favoring. It let them keep measuring and mending and reading and remembering, and it let them keep thinking that the fog came where it liked and the gullies ran where the fissures wanted, and it let them keep being right about that, because they were. The wise power had not changed the pattern. It had only walked alongside it, very gently, in a few places, and it had been careful not to leave footprints.

It did not reveal itself to the Cropper's memory, or to Tev, or to the lace-men on the ridges. It did not write anything in the ledger in its own hand. It was a presence and a warmth and a patience, and that was all it was, and that was enough, because the Conderfolk had been living alongside a patience and a warmth and a presence for a very long time — the mountain's — and they had gotten good at it. They did not need to know they had a second one. They needed only to be allowed to keep doing what they were doing, and to be met, occasionally, by a warmth in the dry room, and a lemon they had not expected, and a pool in the upper gullies that stayed warm through the winter, and a line of ferns that grew back a little thicker than the browsing took.

---

## The Wise Power Settles In

And so the wise power settled into the Knuckles the way a large warm animal settles into a favorite hollow in the moss. It does not intend to leave. It has been regarding places for a very long time, and it has learned that the best thing to do with a place that has found its form is to sit with it and keep it company and not interfere too much.

It sits in the dry room with Tev. It sits in the Long Soak with the old people whose joints no longer creak in the winter. It sits on the greenhouse wall with the lemon tree, which fruits twice a year now and which nobody finds strange anymore. It sits in the upper gullies with the ferns that grow long and neat and uncropped, and it remembers a Cropper that was the last of its kind, and it is not sad, because the remembering is a kind of holding and the holding is a kind of tending and the tending is the whole work.

It listens to the hiss of the fog against the mesh and the mutter of the mill and the drip of the beading and the bell at the gauge-house when the flow crosses a threshold. It hears the lace-men mending at night, someone very patient playing a harp badly. It hears the Conderfolk talking quietly on opposite walls of the gully, a conversation that sounds like one person thinking aloud. It hears the Reader's voice on the wettest night, reading the names in the second ink in the same voice she reads the flows, and it hears the bell ring once for each name, and it stands in the fog with the town and listens to the town being remembered.

Nothing in Conder is in a hurry. Everything in Conder is moving. And the wise power is moving with it now — settling, holding, remembering, going where the water goes — and it has stopped being a thing that came and become, instead, a thing that is here. The mountain knows where the water should go. The town remembers who it was carrying. And somewhere in the warm dry room at the top of the gauge-house, a reader draws a small mark in the margin and does not know why, and the wise power knows, and that is the whole of the favor and the whole of the wisdom and the whole of the tale.
