It would do almost nothing, and that almost would be the whole of its benevolence.

It would come down as a listener, not as light. It would stop at the rim of the shaft and let its instruments be passive; it would bring no engines, no lamps, no heat, no sonar, no sampling arm. It would listen to the roar until it could separate the pumpers’ knock from the grazers’ rasp, and it would feel the pulse in its own bones and not answer. The pulse is a clock and a shift bell, not a distress call. To answer would be to introduce a new god into the machine. So it would keep its silence like a hand held over a flame.

It would recognize the Crown for what it is: not a patient, not a ruin, not a temple, not a resource. A crowd-being. A factory with no owner. The who is the crowd; the what is everything. A wise power would not try to give it a single face or a single voice, and it would not try to improve it. The bare stone is not a wound. The roar is not a cry. The dead are weather. The machine repairs itself by appetite and density, and it needs no off-switch, and it needs no savior. It would not confuse benevolence with management. It would not try to abolish predation or decay, because that would be to abolish the Crown. But it would not add suffering from outside.

So the power would act only at the boundary. It would defend the Crown from the two things that are not the Crown: extraction and contamination. It would keep the water cold and dark and clear. It would keep the mineral crusts from becoming a mine, the tubes from becoming a collection, the summit from becoming an anchor point or a cable route or a dump. It would protect the supply lines, because the Crown’s future arrives as larvae from elsewhere—so it would guard the currents and the neighboring refuges, not just the summit. It would make the place unprofitable and uninteresting to greed. It might do this with soft power: law, charts, quiet obstruction, alternatives, a no-anchor zone like an altar. If a ghost net drifted in, it would remove it with the care of a surgeon who knows the patient is not theirs, and then withdraw. It would not seed, feed, cull, sample, or “restore.” It would not put a plaque on it. It would not broadcast the coordinates. It would not name a species after itself.

And it would learn. It would sit at the rim long enough to see a bank of pumpers silt up and the crowd replace them, and it would understand a thing its own courts and councils had forgotten: that function can emerge without an owner, that maintenance can be distributed, that a system can have a pulse and no brain and still keep faith with itself. It would carry that lesson outward and revise its own hand on the world—less command, more conditions; less improvement, more attention. It would not say “I saved you.” It would not ask for thanks. If it ever spoke to the Crown, it would only say, in the only language that would not be an intrusion: nothing. It would keep the door.

Then it would return sometimes—not to check a box, but to keep faith—and find the same roar, the same clean stone, every part different. And it would be glad, and it would leave no trace.
