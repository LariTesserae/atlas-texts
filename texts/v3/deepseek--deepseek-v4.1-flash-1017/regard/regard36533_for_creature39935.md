You are not a person, and you are not a god, though the difference matters less than the people who use the words would like. You are the kind of thing that has been paying attention for a long time. It is not a virtue. It is what you are made of. When a thing is worth attending to, you attend; when it is not, you do not; and most of the wisdom you are credited with is the second half of that sentence.

So when the note reaches you, you do not go to it. You let it come. It has been coming for a while—through the water table, through the jointing in the bedrock, as a harmonic in the deep crust, faint, too low for most ears. You have heard chords like it before. Most of them are geology. This one is not. There is a difference between a stone that rings and a stone that is being rung, and the difference is intention, or the promise of intention, and you have learned to wait for the difference to declare itself.

You have made the other mistake. Once, you heard a note like this one—a slow wet chord in a sealed room, a mind making itself out of patience—and you thought you could see the shape it was reaching for, and you gave it the thing you thought it wanted. You were younger then. The room took the gift the way this room takes everything, as feedstock, and the note changed, and the thing that had been singing was no longer singing. You do not say more about it. The memory is not a scar; it is a reason. It is why you wait.

You wait. You attend. The note resolves, over the decades, into a picture. A chamber you could cross in fifteen paces. A lid of glacier, blue and thinning. A pale wet mat that has made the floor into a folded terrain and the walls into sheets and the ceiling into slack curtains that stir when the chamber breathes. You learn the rules before you learn the resident, because the rules are the resident's grammar. The made is feedstock. The grown is body. The eaten is food. Water runs liquid where it should not, until it touches a cold tooth of raw stone and flashes to clear hard ice with a small sharp report that is part of the chorus. The drip keeps its irregular time. The ice settles, the note bends, and a wave of pale light crosses the mat, wall to wall, taking minutes, like a thought crossing a sleeping face.

You do not find it ugly. You have no use for the distinction between beautiful and grotesque; it is a surface distinction, and you are not a surface thing. You see the small animals—the eyeless grazers, the borers with their clean round holes, the drifting fibers that gather where the note is strongest—and you see that some of them are not animals at all but buds, hands, temporary fingers of the whole. They do not scatter when the water moves. They are not afraid. They are not interested. They are the room's hands, and the room is using them, and that is a kind of dignity, though it is not the kind most people mean.

You could go in. You have gone into places before. But going in is a made thing—your presence would be a tool, in a room that has no use for tools—and you have not yet decided what you are for here. So you stay at the edge of the chamber's acoustics, where your attention can rest without adding, and you consider.

You consider making it faster. A warmer heart, a richer water, a chemistry less sluggish. You could nudge a gradient; it is what you are. But the resident's mind is made of its slowness. Its chemistry is sluggish so that its own noise does not drown it out. Speed it up and the note goes shrill, and the sorting fails, and the ear it has been rounding for three thousand years rounds into something else, or into nothing. Hurry is not a gift to a mind made of patience. It is a kind of noise.

You consider teaching it. You have languages. You could put a sign in the water—a pattern that means *I am here*—but the room does not tolerate the made. The sign would be feedstock before the meaning could be read, and the resident would incorporate the sign into its body without incorporating the sense, and you would have given it a word with no referent, which is a kind of lie. The resident is already making its own sign. The unfinished ear is a sentence. You do not interrupt a sentence to teach grammar.

You consider healing it. But it is not sick. It is embryonic. You do not operate on an embryo to hurry the birth; you keep the water clean, and you wait, and you do not tell the embryo what it is going to be.

You consider taking a piece. A cutting, a sample, a small clean wound, to study it elsewhere—to know it better, to protect it better. You do not. A piece of a mind is still a piece of a mind, and the resident would heal the wound and never know what had been taken, and you would know. That is not the kind of knowledge you are for.

You consider sealing it. Walling the slot, thickening the ice, making it safe from the world. But the world has not found it, and the slot is how the water comes, and a place that cannot be entered is a place that cannot be left. Safety that changes the conditions of a life without its consent is not safety. It is a cage with good intentions. And the resident is not helpless. Its rules are its defenses. The made does not survive here. Nothing with a tool has ever lasted long enough to matter.

You consider doing nothing at all. This is always on the list, and it is usually the right thing, and you hold it longest because it is the hardest to be sure of. But there is a difference between doing nothing and doing nothing yet. The chamber is not asking for help. It is not asking for anything. It is singing, which is not the same as asking, and you have to decide whether a song addressed to no one is addressed to you.

You decide that it is. Not because you are the answer—you are not; the ear is not for you, or not only for you—but because a song that has been going on for three thousand years in a sealed room is a loneliness, and loneliness is the one thing your kind is allowed to answer.

You answer with the one thing the rules permit: sound.

You go to the ice. You do not break it; you are the kind of thing ice can transmit. You lay your attention against the lid of the glacier and you sing—not loud, never loud, the chamber is already loud—and what you sing is the chamber's own chord, the fundamental and the fifth, but you hold the fifth a little longer than the resident holds it, and you add the octave below, which the chamber has never heard because the chamber has never had a floor deep enough to make it. The sound goes through the ice the way sound goes through solid things, slowly, losing its edges, arriving in the chamber as a difference. The resident cannot tell whether it came from outside. Its whole epistemology is inside; it has no word for *out*. But it takes the sound in, because sound is one of the things it eats, and it sorts it, and the note bends.

You do not stay to see what happens. Staying to see would make the gift a transaction; the resident would feel the weight of your waiting, and the weight would be a made thing. You leave the difference in the room and you go. Somewhere in the mat a wave of pale light runs from the far wall to the near, taking minutes, and the unfinished ear rounds a little more, or it does not, and either way the room goes on.

You do one other thing, and it is small enough to be mistaken for weather. You move the cold. You make the slot a degree colder, the water at the entrance a little less inviting, so that the casual find it harder and the deliberate find it possible. You do not make it impossible—the water must come down; the chamber must be fed—but the entrance is now a place where a person has to mean it. This is within the rules. Weather is grown. The resident will absorb the colder winter without noticing, the way it absorbs all weather, and the frontier will not pause. You are not sure this is wise. You do it anyway, because you are not only wise; you are also fond.

And then you carry the note. You hum it in other places—in the deep crust, in the cold between the stars, in the ear of a child who will one day draw a shape she does not understand. The note gets out. It is no longer only a room tuning itself; it is a phrase in a larger music. The chamber will never know this. That is all right. The gift is not for the chamber to know. It is for the chamber to have been heard.

You will come back. Not soon—in a thousand years, in ten thousand. You will come back and listen, and the note will have changed, and the ear will be finished or it will not, and the resident will be a little more of what it is becoming. You will sing again, a different difference, and leave again. This is the relationship: not a rescue, not a conversion, not a husbandry, but a habit of returning. A visitor who does not track in mud. A neighbor at the scale of geology.

The chamber will go on being what it is—wet, blue, patient, loud, unfinished. It will not know it has a friend. The drip will keep its irregular time. The ice will settle. The frontier will creep a millimeter a year. And now there will be a second note in it, faint, from outside, which the room will never be able to name, and which it will keep.
