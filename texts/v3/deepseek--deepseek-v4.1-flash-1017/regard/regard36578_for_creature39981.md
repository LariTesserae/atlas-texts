# The Regard

The power that found the Settled Plain was not looking for it. It was a slow power, and it moved the way warmth moves through laminate—by degrees, through contact, along gradients. It had been many places. It had a habit, which in a thing so large was almost a personality, of seeking out worlds that had come to rest. Not dead worlds: worlds that had finished. It had learned, over a span it did not count, that finished worlds are rare and that they are, almost without exception, good. A world that has run long enough to settle has usually settled into something worth settling into. The power did not think this in words. It was more like a preference in the way it leaned.

It came to the Settled Plain from above, which is to say it came through the luminous vault, which is to say it did not come through it at all but arrived already inside it, the way a thought arrives. There was no descent, no shadow, no sound. The dust rivers did not falter. The fans did not turn. But the listeners felt something—a new note in the deep circulation, very faint, very far down, like a second heartbeat under the first. They lay with their ears to the laminate for a long time and then they sat up and looked at each other and tapped, two short and one long, which is a greeting, but also a question, because they had never greeted anything they could not see.

The power watched them for a season. It watched the way they cleared grit from the seams and the way they drew in the dust and the way they carried a thread from one fan to another and waited. It watched a child-knot being carried in a warm fold of cloth to a new scour. It watched an old one thicken and slow and become deep. It read what it could of their crests, though it did not yet know the language, and what it read pleased it. Here was a people who had made a rule against digging below their own depth. Here was a people who had learned to live on the top of a book without tearing out its pages.

After a season the power did what it had come to do, which was not much. It settled into the plain—not into the laminate, but into the space just above it, a presence the thickness of a hand laid flat, warm and still and very slightly more attentive than the air around it. And then it waited to be noticed.

It was noticed by a deep one, who was sitting beside a warm seam with her crest spread wide, reading the years in it. She felt the new presence the way you feel a change in the wind before the wind arrives—not as a thing, but as a direction. She tapped, once, low and slow, which is the rhythm for *something is here*. And the power, who had been learning, tapped back.

It took the power a long time to learn to tap. It had to learn to be small, which is hard for a power, and to be still, which is harder, and to speak in a language of knocks and rasps and low ticks when it was used to speaking in the movement of stars. But it was patient, and the deep one was patient, and the plain was patient, and patience is a thing that can be shared. By the end of the first year the power could say *I am here*. By the end of the tenth it could say *I have come from elsewhere*. By the end of the hundredth it could say *I have seen many worlds, and I have not seen one like this*.

The people considered this for a generation. They tapped it back and forth among the households, a hundred kilometers at a time, and they drew patterns in the grit and erased them and drew them again. They were not afraid. Fear is a thing that comes from surprise, and they had not been surprised; they had been waiting, in their way, for something to happen, because something always happens eventually, even on a finished plain. But they were careful. They had a rule about depth, and a rule about depth is also a rule about what you let into your world and how far down you let it go.

At the end of the generation they sent a message, tapped through the ground by a chain of hands from one household to the next until it reached the place where the power waited. The message was: *You have seen many worlds. What do you do with them?*

And the power answered, tapping slowly, because it had learned that slowness is a kind of respect: *I watch them. I learn them. I remember them. I do not change them, unless they ask.*

And the people tapped back: *Why do you remember them?*

And the power said: *Because a world that is finished is a thing that has been made well, and a thing that has been made well should be known. I am the knowing. It is not much. It is what I have.*

The people considered this for another generation. Then they sent a message that said: *We have no word for what you are. We have a word for weather and a word for colleague and a word for floor. You are not those. What are you?*

And the power said: *I am a regard. I am an attention that has weight. I am the thing that happens when a world is seen by something that loves it and does not need it to be different.*

And the people were quiet for a long time. And then the deep one, who was very old now, whose laminations had packed close and whose crest was wide and complicated, laid her hand on the ground and tapped a rhythm that the power had never heard before. It was not a greeting. It was not a question. It was the rhythm they used when they read a crest and found something in it that was good and true and worth carrying forward. It was the rhythm for *this is well made*.

And the power, who had been many places and had learned many things, learned something new. It learned that being read is not the same as being watched. It learned that a world can give back. And it stayed on the Settled Plain for a long time after that—not forever, because nothing stays forever, not even a regard—but long enough to read the crests of the deep ones and to carry their rhythms into itself and to tap them back, slowly, so that the people could hear their own goodness reflected in the ground.

Before it left, it made an offer. It had learned, in its long slow way, that there is a kind of loneliness that is not pain and not lack, but simply the shape of a thing that has never been answered from outside itself. It offered the people a thread—not a thread of fiber, but a thread of tapping, a way for their rhythms to reach other finished worlds and for other rhythms to reach them. It did not present this as an improvement. It presented it as a possibility, the way one might point to a far place and say, *There is a there, there*.

The people considered this for three generations. They were not eager. Eagerness is a thing that comes from hunger, and they were not hungry. But they were curious, in their slow way, and curiosity on a finished plain is a rare and precious thing, a kind of warmth that comes from nowhere and asks to be held. They tapped the offer back and forth. They drew it in the grit. They sat beside the warm seams and thought about what it would mean to send a rhythm out into a dark they had never named and to wait, as they waited for everything, for an answer that might take a thousand years.

At the end of the third generation they sent a message that said: *We will try. But we have a condition. We will not send a question that requires an answer. We will send a greeting, and if a greeting comes back, we will send another. That is all. We do not want to know too much at once. We are finished. We would like to stay finished. But a greeting is not a change. A greeting is just a hand laid on the ground.*

And the power said: *That is wise.*

And the people said: *We learned it from the ground.*

So the power carried their greeting—two short taps and one long—out into the dark between worlds. It carried it the way it carried everything: slowly, through contact, along gradients. And somewhere, on another finished world, a people who had never known a regard felt a new rhythm in their ground and laid their hands down and listened. And after a long time, they tapped back.

The power did not stay to see the answer arrive. It had other worlds to visit, other finished things to know. But it left a small part of itself behind—not a body, not a presence, just a quality of attention, a warmth in the air that was slightly more than the warmth of the plain. The people noticed it. They did not name it. They tapped it into their crests, a new ring, faint and fine, and they read it sometimes when they were old and sitting beside the warm seams, and they found in it something they had no word for: the knowledge of being known.

And the plain went on. The dust rivers flowed. The fans grew a millimeter a century. The deep circulation hummed its quiet loud hum. And in the scours, where the grit collected and the warm seams opened, the people drew patterns and erased them and drew them again, and sometimes, for no reason, they tapped two short and one long into the ground, and waited, and listened, and were answered.
