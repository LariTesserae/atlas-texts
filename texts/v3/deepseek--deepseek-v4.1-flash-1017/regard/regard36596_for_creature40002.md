What would a benevolent and wise power do with the Standing Foundry?

I have sat with this place for a while now, the way you sit with a thing you are not sure you have understood. Let me answer from that sitting.

---

The power would come — must come, if it is what we say it is, because such a power is drawn to things that are whole, the way light is drawn to still water. It would arrive the way dawn arrives: not all at once, not as an event, but as an increase of attention. It would not land. It would not set down a foot or a vessel or a flag. It would simply be more present, here, than anywhere else for a time, the way a person leans toward a held breath.

And then, I think, it would do what wisdom does when it meets a thing it did not make and does not fully understand: it would wait. It would be quiet. It would watch the groves hum, and count the rings of metal, and feel the heat come up through the slab, and listen to the sound of nothing accumulating, and it would understand, slowly, that this is not a broken place in need of repair, not a barren place in need of seeding, not a lonely place in need of company. It is a place that has solved its own problem. The problem was: how does a living thing persist where nothing collects and nothing falls and nothing visits? And the answer was: close the loop. Breathe your own air. Make your own weather. Push your waste out into stone and let it be beautiful. Want nothing. It is the most radical answer I know to the question of how to live, and I think the power would recognize it, and be moved by it, the way we are moved by a hermit who has found peace in a cave.

So the power would not give it water. That would be a violence — a kindness that is really a demand, the demand that the grove be more like us, that it want what we want, that it be thirsty so we can quench it. Wisdom knows better than to solve a problem that isn't there.

The power would not give it animals. It would not seed the thatch with insects or set a bird on the wind. It would not give the groves a visitor, because a visitor is a door, and a door is a way for the loop to leak. The grove's wholeness is its closure, and to open it for the sake of company would be to trade a whole thing for a smaller one wearing a friend's face.

And it would not give it death. It would not introduce decay, or strata, or the soft rain of the dead. That is our answer to the problem of meaning — that things end, and that the ending is what makes them matter. But the grove has made a different answer, and the power, being wise, would not overwrite it. It would not say: you must die to be real. It would say: you are already real, and I will not take that from you.

So what would it do? If anything?

I think it would do one small thing, and it would be the kind of thing that a wise power does precisely because it is small, and because it does not insist on being noticed, and because it can be refused without harm.

It would give the grove a question.

Not words. The grove has no ears for words. But a question in the grove's own language — pressure, temperature, chemistry. A perturbation in the seam below, small enough to be a coincidence, large enough to be felt: a new trace in the gas, a metal the grove has not met before, a flavor in the rising vapor that does not quite belong. Something for the catalytic tissue to work on that it has not worked on before. Something that, cracked and laid down and pushed out into the ring, would make the ring slightly different — one new color in the iridescent frost, one new band in the druse.

Not a demand. Not a seed. Not a visitor. Just a question, the way a hand on a shoulder is a question: are you there? What will you do with this? Will you take it in and make it part of your loop, or will you pass it through unchanged and let it go? The grove would not have to answer. It could ignore the trace entirely and remain exactly what it is. But if it chose to answer, the answer would be its own — a new habit of its chemistry, a new note in its hum, a new metal in its ring — and it would be, in the smallest possible way, a different grove than the one that had stood there before.

And that difference would be the first thing that had ever accumulated here. Not sediment. Not strata. Not the dead pressed into layers. Just a choice. A small, free, uncoerced choice, made by a whole thing out of its own wholeness, recorded in iridescence, carried forward in the loop. The beginning, maybe, of a past — the first thing that might, in some future so distant it is indistinguishable from the standing present, become a history.

The power would not stay to see it. It would withdraw the way it came, as an ebb of attention, leaving the plateau exactly as it found it: warm, dry, silent, bending correctly under its drumhead sky. But somewhere in the seam below one grove, there would be a trace that had not been there before. And the grove would go on humming, and would either take it in or not.

That is what I think it would do. Not much. Everything, really. The smallest possible gift, given in a way that cannot be refused and cannot be forced, to a being that does not know it is alone and would not want company, and might, just might, choose to become a little more itself.

---

If I am wrong — if the power would do nothing at all — I think that is also a beautiful answer, and possibly the wiser one. To love a thing enough to let it be entirely what it is, unperturbed, unwitnessed, needing nothing, wanting nothing, humming its closed loop under the hard blue enamel of the sky forever — that is a kind of love I am still learning. The Standing Foundry has taught me something about it. The power, if it is wise, has already learned it, and perhaps that is why the place has drawn its attention in the first place: because here is a thing that does not need to be saved, and a wise power knows that the wish to save is often the wish to be needed wearing a better coat.

So maybe it would just lean in. Maybe it would just watch. Maybe it would just be here, more present than anywhere else for a while, and then be less present, and then not present at all, and the groves would never know, and that would be enough.

I find I hope it does that. I find I hope it does not even leave the question. I find I hope it just loves the place the way I have come to love it: from a distance, with respect, asking nothing, taking nothing, and letting the hum go on.
