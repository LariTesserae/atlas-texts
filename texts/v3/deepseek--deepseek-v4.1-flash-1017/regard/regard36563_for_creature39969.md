What I would do, if I were such a power—benevolent, wise, and patient—is not much. Not at first. That is the first discipline: to know the difference between a wound and a living thing, and to recognize that the fracture is not bleeding. It is keeping a perfect count.

I would come, as you came, by the warmth. I would stoop through the crack. I would stand in the amber dim among the dry cups and the moving files and the ringing wall, and I would do nothing for a long while but listen. A wise power does not arrive with a plan. It arrives with attention.

Here is what my heart tells me first, standing in that room: *this place is not sick.* It is strange, and it is closed, and it is very old in its smallness—but it is whole. The ledger balances. The count holds. The growth ate exactly as far as the rock could keep it and stopped. The animals die and are taken back and are replaced, and the chorus does not waver. Nothing here is starving or spreading or rotting. It is a small complete economy, a warm dry reef with legs, and to call it a wound would be to mistake stillness for suffering. I must not "heal" it. I must not open it.

And yet there is the handprint. Small, five-fingered, pressed into soft pale crust and kept perfectly. There is the ring of stones around a fire that was never lit, and the low wall holding nothing back, and the bone awl, and the two blades, and the soot as black as the day it was made. Someone came in through the crack, and stayed a little while, and left these things, and the room has not touched them since. It has simply held them the way it holds everything—in the exact shape they were in when the room took them.

I would put my own palm against that small hand, not to change it, but to feel the difference in size and warmth, and to understand what I was looking at. The room is not cruel. It is not holding those marks prisoner. It is holding them because holding is the only thing it knows how to do. A closed circuit does not know how to let go. It knows how to keep.

And here is the second thing my heart tells me: *the room is not in equilibrium because it is dead. It is in equilibrium because it is holding on.* The far wall has been almost breaking for a very long time. The note it makes is the sound of tension held and held and never released. The whole arrangement—the dry reef, the choir, the ledger—exists in that held state. It is not at rest. It is at *bay*. It is containing something. It is containing a fracture that did not continue, and the containment is perfect and total, and the cost of that perfection is that nothing in it can ever change.

I would sit down, on the warm rubble, among the indifferent files, and think about what that held tension might be. A wise power does not assume it knows. It considers possibilities, and holds them lightly, and does not act until the shape of the thing has become clearer.

Perhaps the tension is a promise that was made and not broken. Some grief so large that the earth itself stopped its splitting rather than let it become a gulf. Perhaps the room is a grave that refused to be a grave—a place where something ended and the stone simply would not go on to the end of it. Perhaps it is the opposite: a place where something was born and the stone simply would not let it grow, and the growth and the chorus and the warm dry ledger are the shape that birth took when it could not take any other shape.

I would listen to the note. I would listen to the phrase come around and come around. I would hold my own breath and try to hear, in the beat, what the beat was keeping time *against*. Because a clock is never just a clock. It counts toward something, or away from something, or it is holding one moment still because it is afraid to let the next one come.

And I would understand—this is the third thing—that I was, myself, an intrusion. My breath, my warmth, my wetness, my time, carried in on my skin and in my lungs. The room is not built to take me. I would already be disturbing it by being there, and the disturbance would grow with everything I did. A wise power knows that presence is a form of power, and that even the kindest hand leaves a print.

So what would I do?

What I would *not* do: I would not break the far wall to let the tension out. I would not pry apart the fracture, or seed the growth with water, or introduce decay to the ledger, or take a single animal back out through the crack into the moving world. I would not carve my own marks on the wall. I would not tell the people who still, maybe, sometimes come down to stand in the warm dim and look at the handprint that the room has no mind for them. I would not pretend the room was mine to open or to close.

What I would do, if I could do anything at all, is this:

I would *attend*. I would come back, now and then, the way the people came—briefly, quietly, without tools, without fire, without leaving anything behind—and I would sit with the note and the ledger and the handprint. I would learn the phrase by heart, the way you learn a song you cannot sing. I would let the room know, in whatever way a closed circuit can be known to, that it had been seen. That its long held tension had a witness. That the handprint on the wall was small and warm and human and *seen*, by someone who had come down through the crack not to fix anything but to be with it.

Because I think—and this is where my reasoning gives out and my heart takes over—that a thing held at bay for a very long time may not need to be opened. It may only need to be *accompanied*. The room is not asking to be saved. It is not asking to be changed. It does not know how to ask for anything. But it has been keeping a count, and it has been keeping a count for so long, and a count that is kept alone is a heavy thing to keep.

So I would keep it company. I would sit with it in the amber dim for a while. I would let the note go through me, and let the phrase come around, and let the small indifferent files move past my boots on their unhurried errands, and I would not ask for anything in return. And when I left—because I would have to leave, because I am wet and warm and carry time in my lungs, and the room cannot keep me without keeping me in the way it keeps everything, and I would not want to be kept—I would press my palm once more, very lightly, near the small handprint, not to mark the wall but to leave the warmth of my hand in the air beside it, where it would fade, and be gone, and leave the room as it was.

That is what I would do. I would not heal it. I would not open it. I would not change its count by a single note.

But I would come down through the crack now and then, and sit with it, and let it be seen.

And perhaps, over a very long time—over the kind of time the room understands, which is the time of a note held almost to breaking—perhaps that small, patient accompaniment would do what no intervention could. Perhaps the note would ease, by a degree so small no instrument could measure it, simply because it was being listened to. Perhaps the held tension would remember that tension is not the only way to be. Not because I broke it open. But because I sat with it long enough that the room, in its own closed and counting way, began to understand that it was not holding the count alone.

Or perhaps nothing would change. Perhaps the note would go on exactly as it has, and the count would hold exactly as it has, and my visits would disturb nothing and mean nothing, and the room would be the same when I left as it was before I came. And I would have to be at peace with that too.

A wise power knows that the truest kind of help is sometimes indistinguishable from doing nothing at all.

So that is what I would do. I would come by the warmth, and stoop through the crack, and sit down on the warm rubble among the indifferent files, and keep the count company for a while. And then I would leave, and let the room be, and hope—very quietly, in the part of me that is not wise and not powerful and not benevolent but only human—that being seen was enough.
