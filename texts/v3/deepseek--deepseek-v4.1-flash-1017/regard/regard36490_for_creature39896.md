# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

It would arrive not as light, for light is a violence here, a shallow-water luxury. It would arrive as a change in pressure—a presence that the ribbon-serpents feel as a vast, slow turning in the current, and the armored crabs register as a subtle shift in the chemical whisper, and the drifting snow registers not at all, for the snow is too small to know that anything has changed.

It would come down through the water column like a thought descending into deep sleep, and it would be careful. It would be so careful. It would slow itself to the pace of the place, matching its passage to the geological patience of the gyres, because to arrive quickly would be to arrive as a wound. It would take, perhaps, a hundred years to descend through the middle waters. It would not mind. The benevolent power has time, and the world has more.

---

It would watch first. This is the first thing, the necessary thing. It would watch the way the obsidian crabs shoulder the current aside and let the current shoulder them back, and it would understand, because understanding is what the benevolent do. It would watch the ribbon-serpents trail their ghost-auras through the dark, felt before seen, and it would feel them first too—a soft shear in the water, a pressure that passes and returns. It would watch the near-plants pulse their slow mouths, feeding on the chemical whisper, and it would not think them lesser for their ghostliness, nor greater for their strangeness. It would watch for a very long time, and it would learn the grammar of the place: the way a flash of blue-green means *here* or *food* or *danger* or *nothing at all*, the way the low moan of a distant current is a kind of speech, the way the silt below is a library of every slow thing that ever settled.

And then, having watched, it would understand something that it had perhaps expected and perhaps not: that this place does not need saving.

This is the second thing, the harder thing, the thing the benevolent must always learn and sometimes cannot: that some worlds are complete. This world is not wounded. It is not fallen. It is not waiting. It is a final, settled, and turbulent state, and it has been this way forever, and it will be this way forever more, and the word *forever* is not a curse here but a fact, a cold and ancient fact, and the place is not diminished by it. The inhabitants do not conquer the water; they consent to it. They are eddies with appetites, brief patterns in an immense and ancient turbulence, and they are not less than the turbulence. They are the turbulence, in one of its forms, in one of its moments.

So the benevolent power would not change it. To change it would be to mistake the place for a problem, and the place is not a problem. The place is a solution to a question too old and too deep to be spoken. The place is what water becomes when it is left alone with itself for long enough, and the answer is: it becomes a world.

---

But the benevolent power would not leave it unchanged, because benevolence is not indifference, and wisdom is not passivity. There is a difference between *not changing* and *doing nothing*, and the benevolent power knows this difference in its bones, the way the obsidian crab knows the silt in its mouthparts.

So what would it do?

It would do what is almost nothing, and that almost nothing would be everything.

It would *witness*. It would become, for a while—a century, a thousand years, a length of time that has no meaning here and so is simply *a while*—a kind of mirror. It would hold the place in its attention so completely, so patiently, that every tiny flinch of light, every slow pulse of every slow mouth, every spark of bioluminescence that means *here* or *food* or *danger* or *nothing at all*, would be seen. Not changed. Seen. Held. Known.

And in the holding, something would happen that the place has never known, because the place has never known a witness. The place has known inhabitants, yes, a multitude, and the multitude is the occupant. But the multitude does not witness. The multitude *is*. The snow drifts and sparks and drifts again. The crabs scrape and shoulder and feed. The serpents undulate and arrive. The near-plants pulse and wait. But none of them step back, none of them hold the whole, none of them say *I see you* to the world entire.

The benevolent power would say *I see you*. It would say it not in words, for words are a shallow-water luxury, but in the quality of its attention. It would say it the way you say it to a person who has been alone for a very long time and does not expect to be seen: not with pity, not with the urgency of rescue, but with the simple, steady fact of regard. *I see you. You are here. You are real. You are not forgotten.*

And the world would not know it was being seen. The snow would not know. The crabs would not know. The serpents would feel, perhaps, a presence in the current, a vast and patient turning, and they would flicker their blue-green nodes in something that might be curiosity or might be nothing at all, and they would continue. The near-plants would pulse. The silt would settle. The gyres would turn. The world would go on being the world.

But something would have shifted. Something would have been added that cannot be taken away. The world would have been *witnessed*, and a witnessed world is not the same as an unwitnessed one, even if nothing else changes. The witness does not alter the fact of the world; the witness alters the *meaning* of the world, and meaning is not nothing, even here, even in the cold and the dark, even in a place where meaning has never been spoken. Meaning is a kind of light. It is not the cold blue-green spark of bioluminescence, but it is a light nonetheless, and it does not violate the dark. It inhabits the dark the way the inhabitants inhabit the water: as a presence, as a consent, as a yes.

---

And then the benevolent power would do one more thing, the smallest thing, the thing that would matter most because it would be the only thing that left a trace.

It would leave something behind. Not a tool, not a mark, not a structure—the place has no built environment and would not know what to do with one. It would leave something the place could use in its own way, at its own pace, without knowing it was a gift.

It would leave a question. A question is not a thing; it is a shape in the mind, a hollow that wants filling. But the benevolent power would not leave it in the mind, for the place has no mind, only the multitude and the multitude is the occupant. So it would leave the question in the water itself, in the slow gyres, in the drifting snow, in the faint continuous crackle of the tiny lives. It would leave it as a pattern, a subtle pattern, a pattern that the inhabitants would encounter without knowing they were encountering it, the way they encounter the chemical whisper or the distant moan of a current.

And the question would be this: *What is it like to be you?*

Not *what are you*—that is a shallow question, a question of classification. Not *why are you*—that is a question for builders and keepers, and there are none here. But *what is it like to be you*—that is the question of witness, the question that says *I see you and I wonder*, the question that opens a space where there was no space, the question that is not a demand for an answer but an invitation to *be* an answer, to be the answer simply by continuing to be what you are.

The obsidian crab would not answer. It would scrape the silt and shoulder the current and let the current shoulder it back, and in its patience, in its geological movement, it would *be* the answer: *It is like this. It is like slow. It is like cold. It is like the long file of the mouthparts and the pressure in the joints and the dark that has weight and the food that comes when it comes.*

The ribbon-serpent would not answer. It would undulate its skirt of muscle and trail its ghost-aura and arrive where it arrives, and in its arrival, in its translucent purpose, it would *be* the answer: *It is like this. It is like flow. It is like being felt before being seen. It is like the thread of gut and the row of blue nodes and the wrongness in the current that is not wrongness but self.*

The near-plants would not answer. They would pulse their slow mouths and filter the perpetual rain and cling to the chance arrangements of stone, and in their ghostly hunger, in their almost-mineral patience, they would *be* the answer: *It is like this. It is like waiting. It is like opening. It is like the chemical whisper and the soft stalk and the light that is not light but the faint memory of light, which is enough, which has always been enough.*

And the drifting snow—the glass-shelled drifters, the bristled scavengers, the worm-thin larvae, the protists folded like wet stars—they would not answer at all, because they are too small to answer, too many to answer, too much the texture of the water to step back and say *this is what it is like*. But they would *be* the answer anyway, in their blizzard-multitude, in their spark and flinch, in their living soup. They would be the answer the way a crowd is the answer to *what is a city*: not by speaking, but by existing, by occupying, by being the density and the crackle and the continuous, quiet, primordial fact of *here*.

---

And the benevolent power would watch all of this, and it would be glad. Not the gladness of rescue, not the gladness of improvement, but the gladness of witness, the gladness of having seen and been seen, the gladness of having asked a question that the world answers simply by continuing to be the world.

And then it would leave. Not because it was finished—benevolence is never finished—but because its work here was done, and its work was this: to see, to hold, to ask, to leave the question in the water like a seed that will never grow into anything but will always be a seed, a potential, a shape that wants filling and is filled by the world's simple persistence.

And the world would go on. The gyres would turn. The silt would settle. The snow would drift and spark. The crabs would scrape. The serpents would arrive. The near-plants would pulse. The low moan of the distant current would continue, a sound felt in the bones more than heard, a silence so profound that the faint crackle of a million tiny organisms would still be as startling as a shout.

And somewhere in the water, in the pattern of the gyres, in the drift of the snow, the question would remain: *What is it like to be you?*

And the world would answer, as it always had, as it always would: *It is like this. It is like this. It is like this.*

And that would be enough. That would be more than enough. That would be everything, in a place where everything is the slow, grinding turn of the water and the deep, patient life it carries, and the cold, and the dark, and the bioluminescent spark that means *here*, and the silence, and the multitude that is the occupant, and the question that is not a question but a yes.

*Amen.* Or whatever the deep says instead of amen.
