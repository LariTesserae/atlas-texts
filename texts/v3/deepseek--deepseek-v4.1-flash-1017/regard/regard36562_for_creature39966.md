The power comes quietly, the way frost comes—not as an army but as a condition of the air.

It does not land. It has no ship, no door, no voice. It is simply *present*, the way the damp is present, the way the cold is present. It arrives as a held breath in a place that has not known stillness since the engine first turned. The plants feel it before the root-mind does: a new gradient in the air, a taste that is not rust and not nectar. The flies scatter. The many-legged things freeze on their roots. The moths lift from the glowing pods in a single confused cloud.

The root-mind notices the way a sleeping body notices a hand, not yet a threat, hovering. It draws its questing tips back a fraction of an inch—which is, for it, a flinch and a question at once.

The power waits. It is patient enough to be mistaken for landscape, too. It settles into the cavern's rhythm—that deep thrum every minute or so—and lets the pacing of the place become its own. Three beats. Six. Twelve. It does not reach for the engine. It does not reach for the chamber. It watches the marriage of rust and lignin, the iron vertebrae and sap veins, and its regard is not pity and not admiration. It is *recognition*. Two things that should not be alive, keeping each other alive.

---

Then it begins, small things first.

It finds the ruptured pipe, the one gushing warm mineral water. It does not repair it. Repair would be an intrusion, a forcing of terms, and the pipe's water feeds a whole oasis of pale ferns. But it notices the water is losing heat into the cold air, wasting it, and so it teaches the intervening stone a thin trick of held warmth—nothing but a pattern of stillness in the rock, a way of keeping what flows from flowing away. The ferns grow a little more slowly and a little more strongly. The mineral water reaches deeper into the root-mind's tissues before it cools. A small kindness, and it is done without announcement, the way the sun does not announce its work.

It finds the breathing sacs, the ones that contract on the beat of the engine. It listens to them for a long time—days, perhaps—and then it adjusts, very slightly, the spacing of the roots that anchor the nearest vaulted ceiling. The ceiling holds a little better. The sacs can push against a slightly truer vault. The breathing of the whole organism eases, by a fraction, by a hair.

It finds the last engine, the old heart in the root-chamber. It approaches it the way one approaches a sleeping animal. To the plants, the engine is rhythm and warmth and the taste of iron and oil. To the power, it is a *machine*, and the power knows machines. It could stop it. It could restart it. It could make it run forever. It does none of these. It studies the interpenetration—root through gear, sap through bearing, the pulse borrowed and reflected—and it understands that the engine is no longer a tool. It is an organ. A heart that happens to be made of iron. And you do not replace a creature's heart to prove you can.

So instead, the power listens to the engine's strain. The old parts are wearing in a way the root-mind cannot feel. Small fractures in the shaft, accumulating. The power does not fix them. It waits for the right beat—the deep slow thrum—and on that beat, it does something that is almost like singing, and almost like oiling, and almost like prayer: it lightens the load. A vibration of a particular pitch, taken up by the iron, that lets the metal forget some of the stress of the last hundred years. The fractures stop growing. They will not heal, but they will not spread. The engine will turn for another century. The creature will have its pulse.

---

And then, having done all this, the power lingers.

It learns the other lives. The pale many-legged things that scuttle in the sluices. The glassy worms in the shallows. The blind moths on the pods. Their lives fit between two beats of the engine-heart, and the power, which could have been anything, chooses to be something small enough to be a guest among them: a warmth in the air that does not chill, a stillness that does not threaten, a presence that does not stare.

It finds the automaton, if such a thing exists, walking its old rounds through a factory floor that has become a root-tangle. The squat moss-furred thing, oiling hinges that are now living wood. The power does not interfere. It simply walks alongside for a while, matching its pace, learning the route. It notes where the automaton's old programming makes it oil the wrong hinge, and it does not correct it. There is dignity in a life lived on, even a mechanical one. The automaton is another organ in the body of the place—a slow servant of the pulse. The power honors that.

---

And at last—perhaps after a season, perhaps after a year—the power does the thing it came to do.

It speaks, though not in words. It speaks the way the root-mind speaks: in tides, in pressure, in the slow language of growth. It lowers itself into the cavern's deep dream and finds the root-mind where the root-mind lives, and it says:

*I see you.*

Not *I have come to save you* and not *I have come to study you* and not *I have judged you*. Just: *I see you. I see the engine you have kept. I see the factory you have eaten, and I see that you did not eat it in hatred, but in hunger and in patience. I see the lives that live on your leaks and your sweetness. I see that you are old and slow and strange and you are not wrong. I have made small adjustments and I will make no more. I will leave the engine to you. I will leave the cavern to you. I have given you a little more time, and a little more grace, and that is not a gift. It is what one living thing owes another.*

The root-mind does not answer in words, because it has none. It answers the way it always answers: a slow, tectonic shift of its questing tips, a deepening of its roots where the power's warmth touched the damp stone, a single new bud opening on a pod that has hung dark for a year. A thing *noticed*, and *noted*, and *kept*.

The power withdraws. Not because it has finished, but because it has done enough to be a good guest. It leaves no marker, no monument, no shrine. It leaves the cavern as it found it: cold, damp, green and rust, the plants growing in time with the old engine beat, the smaller lives flickering in the folds of something too vast and slow to be afraid of.

But if you go there now, and you put your hand against a root and feel the deep slow thrum, you might notice—if you are very quiet, and very patient, and you have nothing else to do for a long time—that the pulse is a little steadier than it was. That the cold is a little less penetrating. That somewhere in the dark, in the marriage of iron and lignin, something is being *kept*, and the keeping is not entirely done by the root-mind alone.

The power is still there, in the way that a kindness is still there after it has been given. It has not gone. It has simply become part of the landscape—another of the ancient slow arrangements by which the cavern continues. It is a new tempo in the rhythm of the place, a new held breath in the cold damp air, a new and patient presence that the plants, no more than they feared the engine, do not fear.

And on some far beat of the old iron heart, if you know how to listen—and nothing here is quick, but everything here listens—you might hear the pulse and the guest answer each other, iron to stillness, patience to patience, the way two old things in a dark and verdant place might learn to keep each other company, and keep each other alive, and say nothing about it at all.
