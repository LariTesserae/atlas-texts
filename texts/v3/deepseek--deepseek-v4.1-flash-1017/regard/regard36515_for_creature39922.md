# What the Kettle Would Notice

The power came the way such powers do—not announced, not descending, but arriving the way a thought arrives, already present, already watching.

It had been watching for a long time. It had watched the mountain rise and fracture and breathe its thin hot breath into the cold air, and it had watched the seams fill and empty and fill again, and it had watched the small lives in their small hot circles, and it had understood, without effort, the way one understands a thing one has made: entirely.

And it did nothing.

Not because the Kettle was unworthy of attention—the power had attention to spare, and the Kettle was as worthy as anything. But because the power was wise, and its wisdom was this: it had learned, over long ages, that the deepest gift one can give a thing is to let it be what it is.

---

The power considered the mountain.

It considered the clean fractures, the raw bone-surfaces that had never been rounded, the way the Kettle was still unpacking itself from the earth. It considered the warmth underneath—the heat of a thing still becoming—and the water that carried that warmth up and laid it down as color on the stone.

The power could have calmed the fractures. It could have smoothed the edges, woven a net of roots to hold the slopes, brought the rain and frost to work faster, gentler, so that the mountain might finally wear the soft grey skirt that other mountains wore. It could have made the Kettle *done*.

But the Kettle was not a broken thing. It was a *young* thing, and there is no kindness in hurrying a child to look old.

The power left the mountain to its arriving.

---

The power considered the rules.

The warmth is the only wealth. Nothing is finished. Nothing is made, and nothing is kept. The Kettle is indifferent to scale.

The power saw the symmetry in these rules, the way they folded back on each other, the way they were not rules at all but *consequences*—the inevitable shape of a place where pressure and heat and time meet. The Kettle was not governed; it was *itself*.

The power could have given it law. Could have set boundaries on the cold, made the warm seams grow until they touched and the archipelago became a continent, made the empty grey stone into pasture so that nothing would ever die at the edge of its circle. Could have made the Kettle into a garden.

But a garden is a thing that is tended, and the Kettle was not a garden. It was a *furnace*—and furnaces do not need gardeners. They need only to be allowed to burn.

The power left the rules alone.

---

The power considered the lives.

It considered the mat, orange and white and wet, growing in sheets where the water seeped, dying when the vent shifted, starting again somewhere else. The power could have given it permanence. Could have rooted it to the rock, made it eternal, made it *safe*.

But the mat was not meant to be safe. It was meant to be *first*—the pioneer, the thing that turned stone into food, the crop that fed everything above it. A permanent mat would be a settled mat, and a settled mat would not need to grow, and if it did not need to grow, it would not be alive in the way that the Kettle understood aliveness. The power left the mat to its dying and starting, and saw that this was good.

It considered the mites, grazing the bacterial skin in their wet patches, never traveling, never knowing that the wet patch was small. The power could have given them curiosity. Could have widened their world, taught them to walk the cold stone between seams, made them explorers.

But the mites did not need curiosity. They needed the mat, and the mat was under their feet, and the power understood that a life bounded by a wet patch was not a smaller life than one bounded by a planet. It was simply a different life. The power left the mites to their grazing.

It considered the cricket, squat and wingless, living its whole life in a warm circle a meter wide. The power could have given it wings. Could have made it brave, made it cross the cold stone and find new vents, made it a colonist.

But the cricket had no wings because it had nowhere to fly, and the power saw that this was not a limitation but a *fitness*—the cricket was shaped for its life the way a key is shaped for a lock. A winged cricket would be a cricket that had left, and a cricket that had left would be a cricket that died. The power left the cricket to its warm circle, and did not call it small.

It considered the flies, the sheet of them, the fur of them, the surplus. The power could have thinned them, could have made them rare, could have given them beauty. But the flies were not for beauty. They were for *meat*—the thing that turned bacterial pasture into the food that fed the spiders and the martins and the raptor. The power left the flies to their abundance, and saw that abundance was its own kind of rightness.

It considered the spiders, patient in their webs, waiting in the steam. The power could have taught them to hunt differently, to leave their webs, to be cleverer. But the spiders were not meant to be clever. They were meant to be *patient*—the way the raptor was patient, the way the mountain was patient, the way the whole Kettle was patient. The power left the spiders to their waiting.

It considered the pale things in the wet cracks—small, soft, damp, keeping to the film, almost certainly new to anyone who would care. The power *cared*, of course—the power cared about everything—but it did not tell them. It did not lift them out of the cracks and give them names. It left them to their condensing, and it knew that somewhere, someday, someone would find them, and the finding would be its own kind of gift. The power did not take that gift away.

It considered the martins, the commuters, the only ones who could leave a seam and come back. The power could have made them residents, could have tied them to one warm face, could have made them *loyal*. But the martins were not loyal. They were *free*, and their freedom made the seams part of a larger map, made the archipelago a country. The power left the martins to their leaving and returning, and did not call it faithlessness.

It considered the grey raptor, hanging on the updrafts, watching the fly-sheets, dropping. The power could have made it gentler. Could have taught it to eat the mat instead of the meat, made it a vegetarian, made it *kind*. But the raptor was not cruel; it was *hungry*, and its hunger was the same hunger that drove the flies and the spiders and the mites. The power left the raptor to its fishing, and did not call it evil.

It considered the thing with hooves, coming up at night, pressing its warmth into the mat, leaving the bed the frost would take by morning. The power could have kept it. Could have made it stay. Could have given it a name, a memory, a place in the economy of the warm seam.

But the hooved thing came for the heat as a body, not as food, and it left nothing but the place where it rested. The power saw that this was enough. It saw that a thing could be a visitor and still be *real*, could leave nothing and still have *been*. The power did not ask the hooved thing to stay. It let it come, and it let it go, and it did not grieve.

---

The power considered the mountain's indifference.

The Kettle did not know the power was there. The Kettle did not know anything. It ran on pressure and heat and its own weight, and the only exhaust was the mist, and the only product was the crusts and terraces and colored stains. It had been doing this since before there was anyone to notice, and it would go on doing it whether or not anyone ever stood in the steam again.

The power could have made it *aware*. Could have given it a mind, a will, a want. Could have let it know that it was seen, that it was *witnessed*, that the warmth it carried up was not just heat but *worth*.

But the Kettle's indifference was not a lack. It was a *completeness*. The Kettle did not need to know it was witnessed to be worthy of witness. It did not need to be seen to be *there*. The power saw that the mountain's indifference was the deepest kind of peace—the peace of a thing that is entirely itself, entirely present, entirely *enough*.

The power did not give the Kettle a mind. It let the mountain be mindless, and in its mindlessness it was perfect.

---

The power considered what it had not done.

It had not calmed the fractures. It had not given law to the rules. It had not made the mat permanent, or the mites curious, or the cricket winged, or the flies rare, or the spiders clever, or the pale things known, or the martins loyal, or the raptor gentle, or the hooved thing a resident. It had not made the Kettle aware.

The power considered all of this, and it was glad.

It was glad because it had given the Kettle the one gift that mattered: the gift of *being left alone*. The gift of *being what it was*. The gift of *not being improved*.

And then the power did one thing, the smallest thing, the only thing it would ever do:

It *remembered*.

Not for the Kettle—the Kettle did not need remembering. But for itself. The power held the Kettle in its attention, the way you hold a thing you love, and it *kept* it. It kept the clean fractures and the raw bone-surfaces and the warmth underneath. It kept the mat and the mites and the cricket and the flies. It kept the spiders and the pale things and the martins and the raptor. It kept the hooved thing and the bed it left. It kept the grey light and the quiet and the single drip that was an event.

The power remembered the Kettle, and in remembering it, the Kettle became *more* than it had been—not to itself, but to the power. It became a thing that was *held*, a thing that was *treasured*, a thing that was *known*.

And the Kettle, indifferent and unaware, went on running.

---

The power left.

It did not leave the way a person leaves, by walking away. It left the way a thought leaves, by becoming part of the mind that held it. The Kettle was still there, and the power was still there, and they were separate, and they were one, and neither of them noticed.

The mountain rose. The seams filled and emptied. The mat grew and died and started again. The mites grazed. The cricket sat. The flies furred the stone. The spiders waited. The martins came and went. The raptor hung. The hooved thing came in the night.

And the power, far away and everywhere, remembered.

That was all it did. That was enough.
