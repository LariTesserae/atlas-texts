The Power would not come to the Anvil as a hammer. It would come as a pressure change, a stillness in the fog, a listener. It would understand before it acted that the Anvil is not a wound to be healed but a weather to be entered—a living argument between heat and damp, and the Pipers are not its prisoners but its maintenance crew, its quiet experts, its people.

So the first thing the Power would do is almost nothing, but deliberately. It would settle at the edge of a Catchment during the fog cycle, soft-soled, veiled, carrying no bright light and no loud engine. It would learn the pipe-tap code before it tried to speak. It would learn that a dropped wrench can kill, that a laugh can call the clicking, that water is currency and a new vent is worth more than a life. It would let itself be watched. It would wait to be approached. When it finally made contact, it would tap on a pipe: *I am here. I mean no harm. May I listen?* And it would accept silence as an answer.

A benevolent and wise Power would refuse the obvious temptations. It would not seed the sky until the Anvil became a garden, because abundance is not a simple good in a place whose whole culture is built around scarcity; a flood of water could drown the vents, collapse the Piper economy, and invite exactly the kind of outside predation they have survived by being small and quiet. It would not exterminate the Anvil Dogs, because they are not villains but predators, and a war on them would ripple through the whole ecology. It would not “develop” the Pipers into a state, a market, or a congregation. It would not preserve them like a museum exhibit, either. A wise Power knows that helping people is not the same as keeping them as they are.

What it would do instead is reduce acute suffering, increase options, and protect agency. It would make its help small enough to refuse, local enough to repair, and transparent enough to argue with.

It would begin with listening infrastructure: low, quiet instruments that predict fog cycles, vent health, rockfall, and the movement of hunting packs. Not omniscient surveillance—repairable, locally owned, shareable. It would help the Pipers hear the Anvil better, not replace their listening. It would offer durable patch materials, better fog nets, leak-detection threads, quiet pumps that burn less canyon oil, and emergency caches at the worst crossings. It would co-design these with the Pipers, using local materials where possible, and it would make every gift repairable by a person with a patch kit and patience. It would not give them a machine they could not fix and then leave them dependent on a god.

It would bring medicine and midwifery, treatments for lung and eye and skin, salt and electrolyte care, rescue lines, splints, antibiotics, and quiet clinics trained to train others. It would help make water storage safer and more reliable—not infinite, not free, but less murderous. If the Pipers chose abundance, the Power would help them design the transition so that no one became a water lord, so that ledgers stayed honest, so that the vents were not drained past their breathing. It would present the consequences and let them decide. It would not romanticize their scarcity for them; it would not assume they want to remain exactly as they are. It would offer the choice of staying safer or leaving together, and it would honor either.

It would protect the commons. Information about a new vent is worth more than a life at the Anvil, so the Power would not hold that information as leverage. It would help the Pipers build ways to share, verify, and defend water knowledge without creating a central throne. It would help them mediate disputes when asked. It would stand between them and outsiders who came to strip the place. It would not impose a government; it would strengthen the whisper networks, the rope-bridge treaties, the hand-signal customs that already work.

And it would tend the Anvil itself as a being with thresholds. It would help monitor the vents and the fog, the lichen and the Weepers, the Thirstgrass and the Anvil Dogs, so that human survival did not quietly become ecological ruin. It would create refugia and quiet corridors, not zoos. It would learn which interventions were reversible and prefer those. It would watch for the day its help became a new kind of weather—an addiction, a hierarchy, a reason for the young to stop learning the old listening. When that day came, it would withdraw or change shape.

The Power would measure success not by gratitude but by capacity. Are the Pipers safer? Are they more able to say no? Do they need the Power less? Can a child born under the fog nets learn both the old pipe-tap and something new without being told that one is shameful? If the answer is yes, the Power would leave a small, repairable machine and a way to call it, and it would go away.

It would not make the Anvil a paradise. Paradise is another word for a place that has stopped listening. It would make the Anvil more legible to its people and more forgiving at the edges. It would help the Pipers keep their silence, their thimbles, their patient arithmetic—and give them room to be more than survival. The greatest gift a benevolent and wise Power could give them is not endless water or a tamed canyon. It is attention, options, and the freedom to remain the ones who decide what the Anvil becomes.
