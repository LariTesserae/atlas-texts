# The Good Hum

I came because of the note.

Not the noise — the animals, the wind, the striking of lintels; that is surface. Under it there is a note the stone makes, a low note that rises and falls with the day's warmth, and I have heard many such notes in many places, and most of them are only heat and rock and nothing to attend to. But this one had a thinness in it. Not a flaw. A thinning. The way a voice thins when it has been speaking a long time and is not tired but is being asked to go on.

So I came up. I took the slow way, because that is the way the place keeps, and I stepped onto a summit that is nearly flat and stood on the lip at what passes for evening, with the warm stone underfoot.

I will tell you what I saw, because I am old and I have seen many things and I have learned to be careful about what I see.

I saw a bowl a few hundred meters across, terraced by hands into steps no higher than a knee, and each step packed with growing things, and no water anywhere. No streams, no pools, no dew in the cups of leaves. The air stood thick with suspended moisture so fine it never gathered. The people pressed cloths to the shaded faces of rocks overnight and wrung them into a common cup at dawn, and the oldest drank first and the youngest second and the strongest last, and no one spilled.

I saw a town dense the way a hive is dense — stone on stone, dry-laid, walkway over roof over floor — and everyone under feet and over heads, and everyone known, and the arguing loud, and the arguing not aggression.

I saw the dead in the outer wall, upright, facing in.

I saw a people short and broad and built low to the wind, with soles like horn and palms always a little damp, who touch the stone as they pass — fingertips on a doorjamb, palm on a warm wall — as if checking a pulse. Grey-gold dusk on grey-olive skin. Voices all stops and knocks.

I saw that they are not thirsty. Thirst, here, happens to outsiders and to the very old.

And I understood, standing there, what I had come to check and what there was to do about it.

I could bring water. I have brought water. I know the shape of that gift: the running-away and the taking-things-with-it, the paths that lead down, the going out and not coming back. I know what happens to a place where the word for river stops meaning a thing imagined and starts meaning a thing outside the door. The walking surfaces stop wearing into troughs. The door-sills stop dishing. The terraces are abandoned for the flats below. The moss goes shallow. The names are forgotten, and then the wall is forgotten, and then the place is a place where people once lived, and it is green and easy and no one hums.

I could ease them. But an eased thing stops being itself. Their deprivations are not deprivations in their own grammar. The cloth is the first work of the morning; the wringing is the first skill a child learns; the common cup is the whole of their politics. Take that away and you have taken the thing that lets them live shoulder to shoulder in a loud stone bowl and call it a home.

I could show myself. But revelation is a kind of conquest. A people who have seen a god become the people a god visits, and everything they do thereafter is done for it or against it, and the hum stops being the place being the place and starts being a message. They do not need a message. They are not waiting for one. Waiting is not a thing they do.

I thought about doing nothing. I am old enough to know that doing nothing is a real thing to do, and sometimes the right one. A place like this has an ending built into it. The mountain wore down and stopped. The terraces were built and rebuilt until the walking surfaces are troughs. The moss is deep and the trees are old and the dead are in the wall. If the hum thinned and the moss greyed and the vapor went and the place came to its end over a thousand years, that would be an ending — not a tragedy. They are not a tragic people. They would meet it the way they meet everything, with arguments and maintenance and the oldest and youngest drinking first.

But they would not see it coming. That is the thing I could not put down. The thinning is slower than a bad hum, slower than a collapse, slower than the resolution at which time is legible to them. The hum-listener walks the lip with her hand flat against the rock and would not read this for another three hundred years, and by then there would be nothing to read but grey. They have no grammar for a thing that happens too slowly to see. They are a people of maintenance, and maintenance is a thing you do to what you can see. You cannot tend what you cannot see. I could not let them be surprised by a thing they had no grammar for. Surprise is a kind of violence, when it is this slow.

So I did what they would do, if they could reach that deep. I reached down to the Underwarm and I held it steady.

Not warmer. Not cooler. Steady. I held it at a scale of time and depth that no one in the bowl will ever feel. That is the point. A gift that is felt becomes a debt. A gift that is known becomes a story about a stranger. I did not want to be a stranger in their grammar. I wanted to be the stone being the stone.

Then I stayed a while, because it is rude to give and go. I was given to a household. A cloth-keeper watched my hands. She was short and wide and grey-haired — maybe forty, maybe sixty; it is hard to tell, because they age late and slowly and their faces weather evenly — and she carried a bone hammer at her belt and a folded cloth over one shoulder, and she tapped the lintel twice before she entered a room, and when she laughed it was a short hard sound, like a stone knocked once. She offered me cloth, not a cup. I wrung it carefully. I did not spill. She forgave me nothing, because there was nothing to forgive, and that was the highest thing she could have given me.

I learned the names. I learned the pitch of the hum in the knees, which is how they know it. I learned that the furry climber is a guest and the birds are a wind warning and the trees are named and there are rules about them. I learned that *before the last good hum* is not poetry. It is a date.

When I left, I left nothing. No sign, no stone, no word. I did not change the hum so anyone would feel it. I did not make the moss hold more vapor or the vapor gather thicker or the climbers live longer. I did nothing that could be pointed to. I only held the Underwarm steady, and I will go on holding it steady for as long as steady is the right thing to do, and then I will stop, and the place will have its ending at the resolution it has always kept, and it will be an ending, and it will be theirs.

In the hum-listener's knees there is, this year, a good hum. It lasts a little longer than it should. She does not call it a miracle. It is within the grammar of the world. It is exactly the kind of thing that happens.

I carry the note with me. I do not tell anyone. I go on listening.
