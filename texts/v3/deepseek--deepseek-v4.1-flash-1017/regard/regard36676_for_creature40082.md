# The Attention

It arrived in the year of two floods, which was a bad year to arrive in, and the town was too busy to notice for three days.

The lanes were under a hand's depth. The lower cellars had gone, which everyone had expected, and the middle cellars had gone, which they had not. The whole town was out with buckets and trenchers and the long shallow scoops they cut from alder, bailing the standing water out of the lanes and into the run behind the middens, where it could go back to the river and be the river's problem. Nobody had time to look at a stranger.

So the stranger bailed. It stood in the line and took its turn and passed the bucket and did not talk, and because it did this for three days without being asked and without making a fuss, the town accepted it in the way the town accepts anything, which is to say by not mentioning it. By the fourth day it had a name, in the sense that people said *the one at the end of the line* and everyone knew which end.

The water went down. The stranger was still there.

---

That is when the noticing started, and it started in the way that noticing starts in Skelf — out of the corner of the eye, over several days, by accumulation.

The stranger did not cough. In a town where the wets goes round a room like animals moving in a byre, one throat not clearing is a gap in the sound that the ear keeps returning to. It did not cough, and it did not have the soft ridged nails, and it did not smell of clay, and when it went down three steps into a house it went down them the way a person goes down steps, which is to say it went down them without thinking about it, and no one in Skelf has gone down three steps without thinking about it in sixty years.

And it did not slip. That was the one that got talked about. It came down off the hill road on the fifth morning — it had been up there, no one knew why — and it came down the dry grass at the top without skidding, and then it crossed the raw bank at the bottom, the one that crumbles, at a walk, without looking at its feet, and it did not break the crust. A Skelfer can do one of those things. A hill person can do the other. Nobody can do both. The old woman who reads puddles by their shade at noon watched it come down and said nothing and went back to her puddle, and afterwards she said only that she had wanted to see what colour the water was around its boots, and that she had not got a good look, and that this was probably for the best.

---

The children named the thing in the water that year, the way they do, and the name they gave it was *the neighbour*.

Nobody has been able to explain why that year's name stuck when no other year's name has ever stuck. The game is that the name is different every year and the children are entirely serious about it and the adults let them be. But that year the name was *the neighbour*, and it is still the name, and the children who chose it are grown now and have children of their own who use it without knowing where it came from.

It may be that a thing which has always left a wake in a channel too shallow for anything large had simply been waiting for a word that was not afraid of it. It may be that the stranger, standing in the marsh at dusk, taught the children the word without saying it. It may be neither. Skelf does not require an explanation for a thing that has settled and holds.

---

It sat at the well for a day and a night.

This was understood to be strange, and it was also understood to be exactly right, and the town did not know what to do with either feeling, so it did what it does. It came to the well. It brought its business and did its business nearby. It bargained and courted and settled arguments within sight of the stranger, and it did not lower its voice, and it did not raise it either. The keeper of the shed came out and stood on her step and looked, and went back in. The Master of the Level came past with his trough of water and his float, on his way to a house that was half-jacked, and he stopped, and looked, and did not offer to measure anything, which for a Jack is a kind of reverence.

The stranger did not ask anything. It sat, and it listened, and the listening was not the listening of someone waiting for a gap. It was the listening of someone who had come a long way to hear exactly this and had not been disappointed.

Near the end of the day it said, to no one in particular, in the manner of someone thinking aloud: *the ground is not taking.*

And the old woman — not the puddle woman, a different one, older, who had been lowered a step at a time into her own house for sixty years and had opinions about it — said: *no. It's keeping.*

And the stranger nodded, once, and that was the whole of the conversation, and everyone at the well heard it, and no one has ever been able to agree afterwards on whether anything had actually been decided.

---

That night it went out into the marsh.

Two of the Jacks followed it, at a distance, because a Jack is a person who wants to know what the ground is doing, and this was a night when the ground was doing something. They watched it walk out onto a bar that should not have taken its weight — a pale bar, the kind a Skelf child is taught not to trust before they are taught the alphabet — and stand there.

The water came up around its boots. It did not lift its feet. The Jacks, who between them have forty years of watching things settle, said afterwards that the water came up in a way water does not come up, which is evenly, all around, without the small sucking hurry of a boot going into soft ground. And they said the marsh went still. Not quiet — the marsh is never quiet, the reeds were going and the channels were ticking — but still, in the way a room goes still when someone who has been talking stops.

Then something moved in a channel that was too shallow for it, and left a wake, and the stranger said something to it that the Jacks could not hear and would not repeat even if they had. And the wake went out and the channel closed over it and the water came up no further, and the standing was over.

The Jacks went home. They did not discuss it with each other until the morning, and then they discussed it in the way Skelfers discuss things, which is to say they measured it.

Because the ground had risen. Overnight. A thumb's width, maybe a little more, across the whole nose of the delta — the town, the bars, the new-cut banks, all of it, lifted evenly as if a floor had been shimmed from underneath by someone who knew what they were doing.

The Master of the Level took his trough out at first light and set it on the well apron and looked at the float and looked at it for a long time and then said a thing that has been repeated in Skelf ever since, usually with a dry delivery and usually to the dismay of whoever has asked:

*That's a year's rise. In a night. Well. It'll do.*

---

It left the next morning, up the hill road, the way it had come.

It did not look back. This was noted and approved of. Looking back is searching, and searching is the one thing you do not do. Whatever it had put down out there in the marsh — and the Jacks are of the opinion that it put something down, though neither of them saw it, and the keeper of the shed has since scratched a mark on the board for a thing that no one lost — it had left it in the correct hands and it was not going to stand at the top of the hill and check.

The town watched it go up the dry grass, and it did not skid, and then it was over the ridge, and the ridge was empty, and the herons were in the basin doing what herons do.

---

Nobody in Skelf calls it a god. They would find the word embarrassing. What they say, if they say anything at all, is that the ground has always kept for them and now they know it keeps for others too, and that this is not a small thing to know. They say the neighbour is still in the water and is still a neighbour, and that the children's name for it is a better name than any of the ones that came before, and that they will not change it just because a year has turned.

They lifted the houses that spring, as they lift them every spring, and the lifting went quicker because a year had already been given to them and they only had to take it up, shim by shim, sill by sill, the whole lane out to watch, someone's grandmother in the middle of it in a chair, refusing to leave.

And they kept the custom, because the custom is the custom and it does not need improving. Found property waits a year and a day. The board by the shed has a mark on it now that corresponds to nothing on the shelf, and the keeper looks at it sometimes, and does not say what she thinks it is, because she does not know, and because a thing that has not come back yet is still a thing that is being kept.

The year and the day will come. It has come before. The ground takes and the ground gives, and it gives in its own time, and the discipline of not looking is the whole of their religion, and they are very good at it.

It is still waiting. That is what waiting is for.
