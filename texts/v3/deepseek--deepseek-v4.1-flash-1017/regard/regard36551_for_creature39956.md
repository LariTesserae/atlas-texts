The power would notice the Sallow Table the way one notices a held breath in a room: not by sound but by the sudden, deepening absence of it. It would not arrive. Arrival is a fracture, and this place is made of unhurried continuities. Instead it would become aware—an attention turned like a lens, the way the frond towers turn toward the brightest part of the sky—until the whole tableland lay under a regard as patient and as weightless as its own thin air.

What would such a power do?

First, it would understand that it must not water the place. The obvious kindness is the wrong kindness here. The Sallow Table is not waiting for rain; it has built a covenant with dryness, and its green is a sign not of thirst but of mastery. To bring water would be to dissolve that covenant, to rot the hollow stalks from within, to turn the sweet-medicinal sap into a weak and common thing. The benevolent act is restraint. The wise act is to know which lacks are not lacks at all.

So the power would begin by doing what the residents do: it would listen. It would learn the grammar of the place—the turn of the leaves before dawn, the chemical etiquette of the belts, the way the pollen pulses keep the fronds from crowding, the timing that tells a grazer when to move and a kite when to drop. It would learn that silence here is not emptiness but a medium, the way the sap-mist is a medium, and that to speak loudly into it would be a kind of vandalism.

Then it would consider its interventions as a physician considers a body in good health: reluctantly, and only where the health is genuinely at risk from something the body cannot meet on its own.

It would notice the one vulnerability that no local resident can address. The Sallow Table is a place of slow cycles—decades for a belt to migrate, seasons for a spore pulse, the long patience of erosion and growth in balance. But the sky above it is not closed. It is a ceiling of atmosphere, and beyond the ceiling, things move that are not weather: debris, perhaps, from some ancient violence, or the drifting fallout of affairs beyond the horizon. A single heavy impact, a single fall of the wrong kind of dust—rich in some element the local chemistry cannot absorb, or simply arriving with too much force—could poison a swale, could seed a suppressant that no local species has learned to answer, could begin a rot in the mineral drifts that would spread along the belts for centuries before anyone noticed.

The power would not fence the sky. It would not build a dome. It would do what the frond towers do when the light is wrong: it would angle. It would turn its attention to the high air and hold it, very lightly, the way a lens-patch concentrates light without seizing it—a gentle bias in the probabilities, a refusal of the foreign bolt, a slow redirection of the poisoning dust toward the empty stone where it might lie harmless. This is not a wall. It is a habit of the sky, a whisper to the weather, and the Sallow Table would never know it had been saved.

It would consider the lone predator—those kite-shapes that circle and drop and then vanish for a week—and it would see that they are not cruel, merely exact. Predation here is sparse and unhurried; it is part of the listening. The power would not soften it. To spare the grazer from the kite would be to unmake both, in the long run. The power knows that a world without predation is a world without attention, and attention is what makes the Sallow Table alive.

It would consider the visitors. There are few, and their footprints feel like events. Some come to look; a rare few come to take—mineral samples, sap, a frond tower cut down for the beauty of its mirror-leaves. The power would distinguish between them. It would let the lookers look. It would let them carry away wonder, which is a kind of seed. But it would make the takers forget. Not with fear, not with a wall of wind; simply with a slow lapsing of intention, the way the sap-mist lapses at midday. They would find their hands empty of the thing they came for and not quite remember why. The Table would keep its own.

And then it would consider the deepest question: whether to make the place more aware of itself. The Sallow Table already listens to the light. Could it be taught to know that it listens—to become, in some small way, a mind made of stone and sap and patient turning?

The power would weigh this for a long time, because it knows the danger in waking a sleeping thing. Consciousness is a hunger as much as a gift. A world that knows itself can want, and want can curdle into a narrow and terrible focus, the way a lens-patch can concentrate light until the leaf beneath it burns.

But the power is benevolent and wise, and it sees that the Table is already halfway there. The frond towers turn toward the light in the cold hour; the belts keep counsel by scent; the grazers warn one another by the rattle of plates. These are the rudiments of a mind, scattered and slow. The power would not install a voice. It would not carve a face into the stone. It would simply deepen the turning—a little more coordination in the spore pulses, a little more memory in the belts, so that after a century of visitors, the Table might begin to have a subtle preference for the ones who walk softly. Not a judgment. A leaning. The same leaning the leaves give the sky.

And finally, it would do the one thing that is hardest for a power to do: it would leave. It would not settle into the Sallow Table as a god of the plateau, answering prayers and playing with weather. It would withdraw its attention to the edge of hearing, to the place where a light-lens is polished by polishing, and it would watch the place resume its own slow conversation. The greatest gift it could give is the one the Table already understands: the room to be itself, unwatched, unhurried, unimproved.

The Sallow Table would remain what it was: dry stone, cool air, lush ribbons of unwatered green, and a silence wide enough to hold weather. And somewhere in that silence, very faintly, there would be a new note—not a voice, not a sign, but a kind of gratitude that has no language because it has no need of one. The power would not hear it. It would not need to. It would know, the way the frond towers know the light, that all is well.
