The power did not come down. It came near, which is different.

It had seen many unfinished places. It knew the look of a world still arguing with itself, and it knew better than to interrupt an argument that was going well. So it did not arrive with light or voice or a hand out of the sky. It let itself be a slow attention in the high thin air, and it listened.

It listened for a long time, by human measure. It felt the slab’s stored heat come off in the late afternoon, and the dry hiss of vapor escaping. It felt the plants condense the vapor and send it back down their poured-looking stems. It felt the creature under the floor—the continuous warm sheet of it, threaded through the cracks, swelling a little in the heat, contracting at night, tasting the air through three spiral towers that were already turning to stone at their bases. It understood, without needing to be told, that the creature had no mind to speak of. No self. No loneliness in the way a person means loneliness. It was a geography of appetite and adjustment, a slow tide under a bright floor.

The power considered the obvious mercies and set them down one by one.

It would not keep the stone hot forever. That would have been a kind of cage: a creature held in the middle of its own becoming, never allowed to finish. It would not cool the hollow ahead of time, either. That would be a different cage, smaller and colder. It would not give the creature a mind. A mind in that body would be a locked room with a window onto a world it could not walk into; it would suffer the knowledge of its own slow fossilization and have no hands to stop it. It would not teach it language. Language would have been noise.

It would not even rescue it. There was nothing to rescue. The creature was not in danger. It was in time.

But the power noticed one thing, and the notice stayed with it. The creature was the last of its line in that small warm room. Its buds had never opened. Its towers had never detached. There might be others like it on other plateaus, but the cold dry kilometers between were absolute. The creature did not know it was alone. It did not know it was the last. It did not know anything in that way. But the power knew, and a wise power does not confuse ignorance with immunity. It has a tenderness for singular patterns.

So it did one small thing.

It waited until the hollow was in its cool hour, when the creature’s tissues had contracted and the vapor was rising again. It reached into the corner where the small hard knots lay dormant—seeds or buds or attempts at both—and it took one. Not the largest. Not the most promising. One that would almost certainly never open in that hollow, because the crack network there was too shallow and the slab too set in its ways. It held the knot in a pocket of heat, no warmer than the creature’s own body, and it carried it across the plateau.

There was another hollow, smaller and younger, where the stone still breathed. Not identical. Not perfect. But warm enough, and cracked enough, and alone enough. The power placed the knot in a seam where vapor came up, and it did not command it to grow. It did not promise it would. It simply put it where the conditions might, one day, be kind. Then it went away and did not look back to see whether the knot had taken. That was not the point. The point was that the pattern had been given a second room to try in.

Then it returned to the first hollow, and it did the other thing.

It spoke to the creature in the only language the creature had: heat. It laid a slow pattern of warmth on the stone—not a command, not a question, just a pulse, warm and cool and warm again, within the range of the creature’s own hourly thrum. The creature’s tissues swelled and contracted in answer. It did not understand. It did not need to. For a little while, two living things were in the same rhythm, and one of them knew it and the other did not, and that was enough. The power did not call it a greeting, because the creature had no word for greeting. But it was one.

It did not name the creature. A name would have been a handle, and the power did not need to lift it.

It did not leave a mark. It did not build a wall or set a guardian or bend the weather. It did not make the hollow famous or hidden. It left the plants spaced exactly as they were, the slab just as sharp, the three towers still spiraling toward their own crystallization. The creature felt a cool shadow and a weight on the stone and then nothing it could notice as absence. It went back to its business: converting heat into form, circulating a few millimeters a year, being a slow tide under a bright floor.

The power will come back at the end. Not to stop it. When the stone finishes its long exhalation and goes cold, and the creature’s tissues crystallize completely, and it becomes a pink vein in the rock—a fossil of a living thing, a decoration in a finished stone—the power will be there. It will witness. It will remember the exact pattern of the crack network and the way the brine ran and the three spiral towers that were also a reaching and a calcifying. It will not call it a tragedy. It will call it a life, in the only sense that place allowed, and it will be glad it had been near enough to see it.

That is what a benevolent and wise power would do, if anything: almost nothing, exactly enough, and then stay.
