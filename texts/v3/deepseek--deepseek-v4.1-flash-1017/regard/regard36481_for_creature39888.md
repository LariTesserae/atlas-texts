# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

## First: observe, and learn the grammar of the place.

A wise power does not arrive with blueprints. It arrives with attention. Before anything else, it would sit—perhaps on one of the upper terraces where the warm air rises and the two old men sit—and it would listen. It would learn the difference between the patient drip and the hiss that means a fitting is failing. It would learn that the green is not an enemy but a tenant, not a blight but a crop. It would learn that the children's first rule—*walk on iron, never on the raw slope*—is not superstition but the distilled wisdom of people who have watched the fog and understood what it conceals.

It would learn that the Sump Terraces are not broken. They are *in negotiation*. And a wise power knows better than to interrupt a negotiation it does not understand.

## Second: ask what is needed, and believe the answer.

The people here are not waiting for a signal. They are not besieged. They are holding a constructed mountain together with too few hands and too much patience. If the power asked—if it sat with the maintenance crews at the end of a long day and said, *What would help?*—the answer would not be rescue. It would be specific.

*Another pair of hands who knows gaskets*, perhaps. *A way to braid the copper wire so the gloves last longer*. *A lamp that does not fog*. *A ledger that does not go soft with damp*.

A wise power would not offer what it thought they needed. It would offer what they asked for, and then it would listen again.

## Third: understand what must not be changed.

The terraces are a closed loop. Water arrives, descends, is expelled, becomes mist, feeds the condensers, returns. The green filters the water and eats the iron. The people route the vines, harvest the pools, patch the pipes. The whole thing is a conversation, and it has been going on for decades.

A benevolent power would see the temptation: to clear the overgrowth, to seal the weeping pipes, to make the structure *whole* again. But wholeness was never the point. The point was *negotiation*. The place is not drowning in its own productivity—it is *living* in it. The patient work is not failure. It is a way of being.

So the power would not clear the green. It would not seal the pipes. It would not touch the raw slope below. It would learn the prohibition and honor it.

## Fourth: offer what only a power can offer.

There are things the people cannot do for themselves, not because they lack skill but because they lack *scale*.

The pumps are aging. The condensers work, but they work at the edge of their capacity. The old men remember the earliest construction, but their memories are going soft. The children are born into the sound of water, but there are only a few of them, and the work is endless.

A wise power would offer:

- **To hold the knowledge.** Not to replace the old men's stories but to *listen* to them, to write them down in something that does not go soft with damp, to keep the ledgers that the moisture has been eating. To remember the valve names and the pipe diameters and the exact torque of the bolts that were laid in the first year.

- **To ease the margins.** Not to rebuild the pumps but to make them need less. Not to clear the green but to help route it where it does the most good and the least harm. Not to seal the weeping pipes but to find the ones that are weeping *wrong*—the ones that are failing, not negotiating—and to brace them.

- **To be present at the edge.** The children dare each other to stand at the very edge of the lowest terrace, where the spillway leaps into white. A wise power would not forbid this. It would simply *be there*—not as a guard but as a witness, a presence that says *I see you, and the edge is real, and you are brave, and I am here*.

## Fifth: accept that the gift may be refused.

The people of the Sump Terraces are not waiting to be saved. They are not besieged. They are holding a constructed mountain together while the green finds every bolt, and they are *good at it*. They have their own wisdom, their own patience, their own way of being in the world.

A wise power would offer what it could, and then it would *let them decide*. If they said *no*, it would sit with the old men and listen to their stories and oil the hinges that do not need oil. If they said *yes*, it would work beside them—wet hands, green-brown knuckles, the patient work.

And either way, it would learn from them. Because the Sump Terraces have something to teach a benevolent power: that a closed loop is not a trap, that negotiation is not surrender, that the patient work is not failure. That a constructed mountain can hold itself up by sheer insistence, and that the water, generous and everywhere, will always find its way back to the sky.

## Finally: return.

A wise power does not stay forever. It offers what it can, learns what it must, and then it *returns*—not to check up, not to supervise, but to *visit*. To sit on the upper terrace where the warm air rises. To hear the drips and the hiss and the low organ hum of the pumps. To ask, again, *What would help?* And to believe the answer.

Because the Sump Terraces are not a problem to be solved. They are a place to be *tended*—by the people who live there, by the green that filters the water, by the water that returns to the sky, and, if they will have it, by a power that has learned to listen.

*The fog does not lift. The pumps hum. The green finds another bolt. And the place, patient and generous and everywhere, continues.*
