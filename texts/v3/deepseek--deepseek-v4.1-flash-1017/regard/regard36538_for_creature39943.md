The benevolent power would come as attention before it came as action.

It would arrive without pageantry, because pageantry is a kind of weather, and this place already has weather enough. It would let itself be small—smaller than the plateau, smaller than the wind, smaller even than the Harr’s patient stillness—and it would look.

What it would see first is not a cry for help. That is the crucial thing. A lesser benevolence would see the screaming wind, the bleached stone, the solitary armored creature, and decide that something must be fixed. It would want to soften the wind, seed the fissures with streams, teach the Harr to want company, to build, to speak in sentences, to be lonely in the way that people understand loneliness. It would call this kindness. It would be a kind of vandalism.

The wise power would see instead a place that is already whole. It would see the Harr not as a creature stranded at the edge of life but as a creature that has become the edge and is at home there. It would see that the Harr does not lack. It lacks nothing the plateau has not already given it: patience, fit, a language of resonance, a body like a boulder, a mind that thinks in seasons and wind-spirals. To give it human warmth would be to give it a fever. To give it human meaning would be to fill its ears with a noise louder than the wind.

So the power would do the hardest thing a benevolent power can do: it would learn the local grammar before it spoke.

It would learn the wind’s permanent roar as a medium, not an enemy. It would learn the strata as a library and erosion as an author. It would learn the Harr’s mosaic sight—how a thousand tiny eyes do not make a panorama but a broken, precious relevance—and it would understand that to be seen by the Harr is not to be understood but to be sorted: threat, weather, prey, other, stone. It would learn the boundary law of the Harr: when two meet, the one who moves first leaves. And it would decide, quietly, to always be the one who moves first.

Then it would approach.

Not with light, not with a voice in the air, not with a gift in its hands. It would settle at the edge of a Harr’s territory, into the stone, and wait. It would wait for days if necessary. It would let the wind tear at its patience. It would let the sun bleach its intention. And when the Harr locked its jointed limbs and became indistinguishable from a boulder, the power would feel the old rule rise in its own bones—and it would move first. It would withdraw. It would leave the hollow lined with lichen and husks exactly as it found it.

But before it left, it would do one thing, and only one: it would send a single low thrum through the stone.

Not words. Words are for throats. This would be a resonance, felt rather than heard, in the Harr’s own idiom. It would mean, as nearly as such a thing can mean: *You are seen. Nothing is required.*

It would make the thrum faint enough to be mistaken for another of its kind at a distance—for that low hum that means *another presence is there, and that is enough.* It would not wait for an answer. It would not demand recognition. It would not return to check whether the Harr had understood. Understanding was not the point. The point was that the power had entered the Harr’s world without making the Harr enter its own.

After that, the power would guard the place, but only against violations from outside the place’s own logic.

It would not stop erosion. Erosion is the plateau’s own hand. It would not stop the bird from landing too close to the fissure, or the silver-winged swarm from being harvested out of the air. That is the world. It would not dam the wind or shade the sun or make the rare pothole permanent. It would not turn the Harr into a monument, a lesson, a protected species with a plaque.

But if a road came, the road would find its surveys wrong. If a mine came, the strata would seem suddenly unremarkable, the assays confused, the cost too high. If collectors came with nets and cages, they would feel a deep, unreasonable reluctance—not fear, not force, just a wrongness in the bones, a sense that they had wandered into a place that did not consent. The power would not build a wall. It would build a boundary that was mostly absence: maps that blurred at the edges, greed that lost its appetite, violence that found no purchase. It would keep the conditions intact—the thin air, the damp nights, the screaming wind, the brief bright swarms—so that the Harr could go on being exactly what it was.

And the power would remember. Not by naming the plateau or the Harr in any public record, because naming is a kind of claim. In its own memory it might hold them as *the place where endurance is enough* and *the one who is at home*. But it would not teach those names to anyone. It would not make a scripture of them. It would not return with disciples.

If someone asked the power what it had done for the Harr, it would say: *I listened. I did not take. I moved first. I made sure the wind could continue.*

And that would be the whole of its benevolence: not to lift the creature out of its world, but to refuse to be the thing that made the world smaller. It would leave the Harr still, and waiting for nothing, in the deafening, beautiful, indifferent light—and it would consider that a good deed done.
