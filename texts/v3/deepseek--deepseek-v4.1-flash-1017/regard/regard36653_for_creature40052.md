# What the Kind Power Did in the Drip-Fields

## i. It came the way anyone comes.

Through the throat of lashed hurdles, down the alder ladder, with no light of its own. It did not part the fog. It did not dry the boards before it stepped. It came in during a dry spell, when the tuners were walking the high catwalks with lamps and dripping rags, anointing the beams, and it stood in the shadow of a cribbing and watched until it understood that this was not maintenance. Then it watched a while longer, and understood that it was prayer, and it did not join. It held a lamp, once, for a tuner whose wrist had gone and who could not climb and wet at the same time; and when the tuner had finished the last of the joists and looked down to thank the stranger, the stranger had already gone quiet into the dark, because thanks are a kind of tally and it did not want one.

## ii. It learned to hear before it did anything at all.

This took longer than anything else it did, and it is the part the tuners never knew about, and it is the part that mattered. It sat at the knuckle-arguments. It learned that a tap on a healthy plank is a flat tap and a tap on a sick one is a small door left open, and it learned the difference by being wrong many times in the presence of people who were never wrong. It learned the note a gallery makes when it is content and the note it makes when it is carrying more than it admits. It learned that the ragged eastern works were not error — that the clacks and flats up there were a young voice not yet sure of its range, and that to tune them would be to silence them, the way you silence a child by answering its question before it has finished asking.

And it learned the other thing, the harder thing: that among the sour joints there were some that were sour on purpose. A wrong peg in the right place does not spoil the music. It makes the music *answer* — gives the whole a grain, a lean, a grief, something to push against. The tuners kept those. They kept them and argued about them for generations and cut tallies about them, and the tallies were a kind of love.

## iii. It found the joints that were sour by mistake, and it did not fix them.

Seven of them, it is said, across the whole basin. Load-bearing now. Cut wrong by someone dead, pegged true by someone deader, and the whole gallery above them leaning a little, the way a shoulder leans when a bone has set badly. A lesser kindness would have gone up with an auger and a fresh peg and made them right by nightfall.

What it did instead was this. It climbed to each one, and it tapped — a particular double-tap, soft, twice, the way you knock at a door you do not mean to open — and then it went on its way. And over the months that followed, the tuners found them. One by one, by ear, by argument, by a child saying *that one sounds like it is holding its breath.* Some were re-pegged. Some were left, deliberately, because when the re-pegging was mooted the oldest tuner in the western galleries climbed up, put her ear to the joint, and said, *No. It leans for a reason now. Let it lean.* And it was left, and the gallery above it learned a new note, and the new note is in the score to this day.

The power never claimed the seven. It had only knocked. The choosing was theirs, and the choosing is the whole of what a people is.

## iv. It walked the far works, and came back, and played the whole score once.

No living tuner has walked the length of it. The valley runs low and long past the rim, gallery past gallery, and the rumor of the far works comes back as a faint complicated music on the wet nights, and no one has heard it whole. The power went out along it. Down the length of the basin and out past the rim and on, gallery past gallery, for as long as it took, which cannot be counted in days here but only in drips.

And when it came back — on a wet night, when the fog was heavy and the gutters full and every surface in the valley was busy with its own arrival — it did not write anything down. It did not give them a map, or a plan, or a corrected score. Maps are stones in a stream. What it did was climb to the highest catwalk of the middle basin, and it opened its hands, and it played.

Not an instrument — the instrument was already there. It played the gutters. It set a lip here, a cord there, a stone in a channel, a shutter half-closed on a copper funnel, and it made the water go where the water could be made to go, and the valley answered, and the answer was the far works, played back. The high galleries heard the low. The western combs heard what the eastern ribs were doing and had been doing all along. For one night the whole basin heard itself — the ragged young edge and the deep old middle and the far rumor made real, all of it at once, the score nobody had ever heard whole, because it is not a score you can hear whole from inside it. You have to be outside. Someone has to walk out and come back and tell you.

It told them. And then it was quiet again, and the wet night went on being a wet night, and the tuners stood on their catwalks with their lamps shuttered and their breath going out into the fog, and some of them wept, and none of them were comforted in the ordinary way. They were *known.* It is not the same thing. It is better and it is worse and it is the only thing that ever really helps.

And the argument about what they had heard lasted three generations, and the argument is in the score now, and the far works answer a little differently because of it.

## v. It gave tools, not salvation.

It gave an oil that burns without smoke, so the high lamps do not dim the glow-film and the readers of drip-tallies do not go blind by fifty. It gave a twist of cord that does not fray at the loop, so the counterweights hold. It gave a copper mix that does not fatigue at the hammer-point, so the funnels keep their coin-ring a hundred years longer than they used to.

And then it taught the making of all three to two tuners and a child, so that the knowledge was not a gift. Gifts cannot be argued with; a gift is a small god you must be grateful to. A craft can be argued with. A craft can be improved and resented and handed down and got wrong and made right again. It taught the child first, because the child would outlive the argument.

It gave nothing that replaced the work. No lamp that did not need oil. No peg that did not need pegging. No dry. It never once gave them dry.

## vi. It tended the voiceless, and asked nothing of them.

The eels in the hide tanks — kept, or tolerated, or worshipped in some small domestic way, no one agrees. It did not free them. Free them into what — the dry? It learned what keeps a hide tank sweet, and it kept the tanks sweet, quietly, at the far cisterns where no one went.

And the long blind thing in the deeper tanks, that is mostly a rumor. It never saw it. It went down to the far cisterns on a night of the change of the wet and it freshened the water and kept the current and let no tank go dry, and it did not look, and it told no one, because a rumor that is looked at becomes a fact and a fact is a smaller thing. The creature in the cisterns lives, or does not, on its own terms, and the power's whole kindness to it was to keep its world what it was and never to require it to be known.

This is the part of benevolence that is hardest and least sung: to do good to a thing that cannot thank you and would not, and to want nothing back — not even the knowledge that it worked.

## vii. It kept the conditions.

This was the largest thing it did and the least visible. The Drip-Fields are not endangered by darkness. They are endangered by improvement. There are people outside — good people, warm people, people who mean well — who would drain the basin and roof it against the fog and let the sky in and bring in beasts and finish the works and call it saving. Such people have arrived at the rim twice in the history of the Fields. Both times they turned back at the throat of hurdles, remembering, afterward, only a green patient light and a stranger who wanted nothing of them, which is the most disarming thing in the world, and they went home and could not say why.

A lesser power would have built a wall. It built nothing. It stood at the rim on the bright dry nights when the outside weather pressed to come in, and it did not fight the weather — it simply was there, the way a tuner is there at a sick joint, listening, and the weather went around. It kept the sky out by the same means it kept everything: by attention. By refusing, with its whole patient self, to let the unfinished be finished by anyone but the ones it belonged to.

And that is the deep refusal, the one at the center of everything: it did not complete them. Completion is the one gift that would have ended the music. A finished instrument is a dead instrument. It let them stay young at the edge and raw at the ribs and always, always in the middle of the score.

## viii. It was offered a name, and it declined.

The tuners cut a new tally-beam in the middle basin, at the crossing of the two great gutters, and they left a space on it for the stranger's mark — the highest courtesy they have, higher than any word they own. To be cut into a beam at the crossing is to be answered by the whole valley forever; every drip that falls there falls through your name.

It declined. It said — and this is the only time it is known to have spoken at any length — that the beam should carry the mark of the one who taught it to hear. Not itself. The long-dead tuner whose taps it had learned from a living tuner's hands, three galleries down and two generations back, who had never been marked and was in danger of being forgotten. It spoke the name. It described the tap — the particular way that tuner had knocked, the small hesitation before the strike, a kind of courtesy even to the wood. And it said that if the beam carried that tap, the beam would answer better.

The tuners argued about this, of course. They are still arguing. But the mark was cut, and the beam does answer better, and the descendants of the dead tuner carry the crossing-mark and do not know why, and that is the whole of the reward the power wanted. To be forgotten, and to have made one thing remembered that would otherwise have gone.

It would not be a god among them. It had seen what a god would do to the score.

## ix. It left, and the leaving was the gift.

A permanent power in the Drip-Fields would have become the sky they never wanted. It would have gathered a small domestic worship at the cisterns. It would have been asked to tune, and it would have tuned, and then it would have been asked to tune the ragged edge, and it would have said no, and they would have begun to resent it, and the resentment would have got into the argument and the argument into the score, and the whole basin would have learned a new note that was not a leaning shoulder but a wound.

So it went. The way it came — up the alder ladder, out through the throat of hurdles — and it brought no light with it and it took none away. It left behind: seven knockings that became seven arguments that became part of the score. One night of the whole heard at once. Three crafts, taught. Some sweet tanks nobody has thanked it for. A rumor about a stranger that has joined the other rumors and feeds the music. And a beam at the crossing that answers a little better for a tap three generations dead.

It was seen once more, at the rim, on a wet night, standing with its back to the outside. Then it was not seen.

## x.

And it carries the Drip-Fields out with it now, wherever it goes, which is the thing no one tells you about a power that has truly listened: it cannot un-hear. The world outside is too bright and too dry and has far too much sky. The sound of rain on a roof is a small, stupid, single thing after the Fields — one note where there was a valley. It goes from place to place holding the whole score in its hearing, and every so often, on a wet night, far from the basin, it finds a gutter and a copper rim and a slate shelf, and it sets a stone, and it plays back one phrase of a tune that was never finished, to no one, to nothing, because that is what it has instead of a name.

It is still doing it. It will always be doing it. Somewhere there is a place where the water is arriving, and someone is listening, and the listening is the whole of the kindness.
