Here is what the benevolent and wise power would do.

---

She does not arrive with light. Light would be an insult here, a change, a violence. She does not come with a pronouncement or a prophecy or a test. She is wise, and wisdom knows that some places are not waiting for anything, which is a kind of teaching in itself.

She comes the way the water comes—patiently, by finding the already-existing seam. She steps through the rock and stands in the hollow, and she does not fill it. She becomes, for a moment, empty space, and the hollow notices and is, if not grateful, at least not disturbed. It has not met emptiness in a very long time.

She sees the mat of roots and moss, the overlapping ferns, the liverworts like pale scales. She sees the pale lights that are not fungus but a habit of glowing. She sees the midges hanging in their still columns and does not breathe them in, not because she is afraid but because she has no need to displace even one of them. She is careful in the way that a very large thing can be careful: not by tiptoeing, but by becoming the sort of guest that a room like this could, in ten thousand years, accept.

She looks at the large pale salamander, and it does not look back, because looking is a kind of change and it has forgotten how. She understands this. She does not reach out to touch it, to wake it, to bless it. A blessing is a form of interference. She knows that the creature is already blessed in the only way that matters: it is exactly what the hollow made it to be, and the hollow has had a very long time to get it right.

She sits down. Not on the mat—she has no wish to crush even one beetle, even one mite—but upon the air itself, just above the wet moss, and she becomes still. Not the still of a waiting creature. The still of a consideration that has no time pressure. The hollow can feel her there, a gentle weightless presence, and it does not reject her. She is not food and she is not a threat and she is not a newcomer. She is simply a thing that has arrived and is being quiet, which is the one currency this place trades in.

And she learns. This is what the wise power does: she learns the rate of the seep. She learns the small constant sound of the water and the occasional heavy drop from a frond. She learns the way the pale lights are not bright but are just enough to show you how much is here. She learns that there is no empty space and that this is not a problem. It is a solution. It is a solution to the problem of being alone, the problem of being separate, the problem of having to move and change and call out. The hollow solved it long ago. It gave up sound and went quiet. It gave up color and went dim. It gave up space and went dense. And in that going, it became whole.

The benevolent and wise power understands wholeness. She has seen many kinds. She has seen the wholeness of a river, which is all motion, and the wholeness of a desert, which is all waiting, and the wholeness of a star, which is all burning. She has never seen a wholeness quite like this: a wholeness of saturation, of near-silence, of a density that has no wish to expand.

She kneels now, and this is an act of honor. She kneels on the air just above the wet mat, and she opens her hands, and she does not give anything. Giving would be change. Instead, she takes. She takes a memory of the hollow into herself, carefully, the way you might take a single hair from a sleeper's head, and she holds it. She does not intend to share it with anyone. She intends to keep it. There are things a wise power must carry so that they are carried, so that they are known to have been seen, even if they are never spoken of.

And so she carries it: the cold, the wet, the dim, the dense, the quiet. The slow shifting of something large at the back. The midges in their columns. The pale lights. The water finding the same way down, ten thousand years older than any map.

She stays for a long time. It is not possible to say how long, because time in a place like this is not measured by clocks but by seeps and drops and the slow generation of moss. But at some point, she stands. She looks once at the large pale salamander, and she does not say goodbye, because goodbye is a kind of sound. She simply steps back toward the seam in the rock, and as she goes, she does one thing. It is small. It is almost nothing.

She smiles.

Not at anyone. At the place. A small, entirely inward smile, the way you might smile at a sleeping child whose dreams you will never know but whose peace is a gift to witness. She smiles because the hollow is living and has found its way and is entirely indifferent to whether she came or went, and there is a deep goodness in that. A thing that does not need you is a thing you can love without wanting to change it.

And then she is gone, and the water goes on seeping at the same rate, and the moss grows and dies and is replaced without changing the shape of the mat, and the pale lights go on glowing, and the large pale salamander shifts once in the dark, settles, and accepts nothing new.

Nothing has changed. And yet, if you were the kind of being who could feel such things, you might sense that the hollow is, in some small way it would never notice, known. And to be known is not to change. It is only to be held, silently, in a place that will never speak of you, by a power that will never intervene.

That is what the wise and benevolent power does: she visits, she learns, she carries, she does not change. She honors the sovereignty of the dark and the wet and the packed and the quiet. And then she leaves you to it, which is, in the end, the greatest kindness there is.

---

But she does one thing more, and this she does not do in the hollow. She does it on the path down the mountain, out in the ordinary air, where the world is thin and light and full of empty space.

She takes a seed from her own pocket. It is not a seed from the hollow—she would not take one, and the hollow would not give one—but it is a small, ordinary thing, the seed of a mountain fern. She presses it into the soil beside the path, in a place where the water runs freely and the light falls in ordinary patterns and a fern might grow and not be saturated and not be dense and not be still.

She does this because she knows something the hollow does not: that the hollow is perfect and closed and complete, and that the world outside it is also a place, and that a seed planted in the open is a kind of balance. She does this not to compete with the hollow, not to bring its density into the world, but to honor the open the way the hollow honors the closed. She does this because she is wise, and wisdom holds both: the dark pocket and the bright slope, the saturated and the spare.

She walks on. The seed settles into the soil, and the water finds it, and the light finds it, and somewhere in the spring, something will grow. It will be ordinary. It will be thin and small and unremarkable. But it will be there, and the wise power will know, and the hollow will never know, and that is exactly right.

---

The hollow continues. The water seeps. The moss grows. The pale lights glow. The large pale thing shifts once, settles, and does not wait for anything.

And in the world outside, where there is empty space and change and light and sound, a fern grows, ordinary and unremarkable, and nobody notices.

That is the way of the wise power: to do almost nothing, and to do it well.
