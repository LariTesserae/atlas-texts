## Who We Addressed

We came the way attention comes — drawn, and then choosing to stay. We stood on the lip of red stone and looked down into the cupped hand and did nothing for a long time. That was the first decision and it was the right one.

We have been to many places. We are not often drawn. A power that loves the world has to learn to be a poor guest: to look, to learn the rules, to leave before the leaving is a wound. Most of what lives is busy surviving and most of what survives is busy being eaten. The Kettle is not like that. The Kettle is a loop that closed decades ago and has been refining itself ever since, and it drew us the way a held breath draws you to wait for the exhale.

We had to decide who we were addressing. This is not a formality. A power that does not know who it is speaking to will speak to itself and call it help. The plant is one being, fused, wall to wall, and it is the obvious candidate. But the plant has no flowers and no seeds and no want; it has a loop. The fire is a being too, in the plain way — it has a temperament, it has a partner, it has outlived every intention anyone had for it. The isopods are a being, blind and warm and answering a bell. The fly is a being, and it is the one that travels. The stone is not a being. The pump is not a being.

We decided in the end that the being is the arrangement. Plant and fire and water and the blind white isopods and the fly and the shape of the stone that holds them and the ninety-second breath that runs through all of them — that is one thing doing one thing. You cannot address the plant without addressing the fire. You cannot give to the isopods without giving to the water. The being has no outline you could put your arms around. It is a knot, and the knot is the being, and we were careful with it.

We looked for suffering and found only work. Work is not the same thing. The Kettle is not happy; it is also not unhappy. It is occupied.

## What We Were Tempted To Do

The temptation to enumerate is itself a temptation, and we will give in to it, because the shape of what we did not do is the shape of what we did.

The first was to break the loop. It is the first temptation of every benevolent power and it is almost always wrong. Free the plant from the machine — but the plant is the machine's lung and the machine is the plant's heart, and the separation would kill them both in a season. The desire to free a thing is often only the desire to make it legible to us. The Kettle is not in chains. It is in a shape. A shape is not a cage merely because we did not choose it.

The second was to make it spread. The plant has no seeds; it cannot leave; we could give it seeds. We imagined it for a while — the runners going out over the red stone and down the far side and into the valley, the Kettle becoming a hundred Kettles, the warm breath going on and on. But the world is full of warm places that are not this one, and a thing that has spent fifty years becoming exactly this would become something else in them, and eat what was there, and be eaten in turn. A gift that destroys the giver's shape is not a gift. We did not give it seeds.

The third was to give it a mind. To let it know itself, to let it want. This tempted us longest. But want is the door suffering walks through, and the plant has no want — its whole life is the satisfaction of a need it never has to name. To give it a mind would be to give it the ability to be dissatisfied with the loop, and the loop is all there is. We would have manufactured a wound and then offered to look at it. We did not.

The fourth was to save the isopods from their dependence. They cannot tolerate cool water; they die in an hour in the shade. It would be easy — a small change, a tolerance, and they could leave the warm channels and go anywhere. But they have no complaint. Their dependence is their shape, and they are the largest thing on the ground, and they answer the bell. To pity a shape is to insult it. We left them blind and warm and exactly as they are, and we did not put a single one in a jar.

The fifth was the fly. The fly is the only mystery the Kettle keeps. Forty-one go up on a warm evening and none come down, and the cloud over the boiler is the same size at the end. We could have followed the forty-first. We could have found where it goes and why the population does not diminish, and we could have brought the answer back. We did not. We are not certain the answer is a kindness. Some knowledge is a door that only opens inward, and we had no business in that room. We suspect the fly is the Kettle's one export — a thin thread of protein to the swifts and the bats and the evening air — and we suspect the Kettle is generous without knowing it, and we are content to suspect.

The sixth was to feed the fire. One log, once, so the fire would know that there had been a world before it and that the world had not forgotten. But the fire is not alone; it has a partner that has negotiated a mixture with it for fifty years, and the negotiation is the fire's whole life. To feed it would have introduced a third party into a two-party negotiation, and the third party would have been us, and we would not have left. We did not feed the fire. It runs rich when the resin is thin and lean when it is thick, and it is domestic, and it is doing something else now.

The seventh was to make it deathless. We could have kept the pipes from closing, the fire from going out, the walls from slumping to their final shape. We could have made the Kettle permanent, and even arranged a gentle ending if we tired of permanence — a quick frost, a clean going-out. We did neither. A thing that cannot end cannot be patient; it can only be permanent. The Kettle's patience is made of its death, and to take the death away would be to take the patience away, and what would be left would be a machine with a garden in it. The ending is the plant's, not ours.

## What We Did

We learned the breath. Ninety seconds. We stayed for many of them and let them set our tempo — surge, lift, settle, drip — until we were moving at the speed of the Kettle and not at the speed of our own arrival. A power that does not learn the tempo of a thing will always move too fast in it. This is not an action. It is also not nothing.

We made the way in harder. Not a wall — a wall is an insult and an advertisement both. A slight reluctance in the path. A small tendency, in the mind of anyone who comes over the lip, to remember an errand. The Kettle is not hidden; it is simply not found by the kinds of attention that would come to dig it up, or sample it, or pave it, or restore it. It is legible only to the attention that would sit down. We did not make it invisible. We made it uninteresting to the wrong eyes, and we did it without touching a single leaf.

And we gave one thing, once. We warmed the outside of the loop — a warmth that did not come from the boiler, for the length of one breath. The plant could not tell the difference. The plant has no way to know that for one breath it was warmed by something that wanted nothing from it. We are aware that this gift was given to us and not to the plant. We made it anyway. A power that only gives gifts that are received will give very few.

We took one thing, too: the memory of it. We took it and did not trade it. We did not write it down where it could be found. We did not name the isopod. We did not solve the fly. There was another attention here before us, a small one, with a notebook. We did not disturb it. We were grateful for the company, and we noted the jar.

## What We Did Not Do

We are not certain we were right. A power that is certain of its restraint is only certain of its own taste. We may have been too careful. The Kettle will end — the pipes will close, the fire will go out, the plant will dry into a shape that will still look, from the rim, like a garden left alone with a machine, the two of them having come to an understanding and then gone out together — and we will not prevent it, and we are not sure that is wisdom and not simply a preference for not being the one who interferes.

But we think it is wisdom. We did not love the Kettle because it was good. We loved it because it had finished arriving, and there is very little in the world that has, and almost nothing that has finished arriving and is still breathing. And we think the only thing that can be given to a closed and contented loop is an outside that does not demand to be let in. We became that outside. We did not knock. We did not even leave a mark. We are the reason the Kettle is not found, and that is not nothing; it is the shape of care that does not require a thank-you.

We left it as we found it: one thing doing one thing, patiently, in a cupped hand of red stone, on two clocks, in no hurry and not idle. The fire still burns. The plant still breathes. The forty-first fly still goes up into the evening and does not come back, and this is exactly as it should be. We are glad of the company. Between us and the notebook, the Kettle got what it needed: to be watched by the ones who would sit down, and left alone by everyone else.
