**The Power at the Crown**

The power came up the inside of the Stack, because a wise power knows that arrival is already a kind of intervention, and it did not want to arrive as a wind. It came slowly, by the ladder, and stood in the Crown and did not fill the room. It kept its presence small. It let the roar be loud.

It looked for a long time. It saw the moss in the seams and the ferns rooted in rust; it saw the birds that had been hatched deaf to the roar and the insects in the warm pools; it saw the hollow worn into the plate above the shallow chamber under the grate. It saw that the Crown was not broken. It was a whole thing with its own slow clock, and the being under the floor was not a wound in it. It was a resident. Its category had changed, and the Crown did not keep records, and the power did not make any.

It considered the things a benevolent power is often tempted to do: rescue, restore, rule, explain. It rejected them.

Rescue assumed captivity, and it could find no cry for help in the shifting under the grate—only thermoregulation, patience, the dry drag of something that had taken the chamber’s shape. Restoration assumed some earlier shape was owed, and the power knew better than to hand a body back its old name without asking. Rule would have made the Crown a shrine. Explanation would have made a catalogue of a place whose proportion was emptiness.

So it did four things, and refrained from a hundred.

It mended only what would have failed catastrophically—a hairline in a rib, a bolt under the ladder—and it did so from the outside, in a way no one would notice. It did not replace the missing panes. The missing panes were doors. It did not weed the moss. The moss was the floor’s fur. It did not silence the roar. The roar was the room’s heart, and the being under the floor had grown into it.

It set a quiet boundary around the Stack. Not a sign, not a law spoken aloud, but a reluctance in the air that certain intentions would not prosper in: extraction, display, forced entry, the lifting of the grate. It would not stop anyone from climbing. The climb was a choice. But it would make sure the ladder was honest and the air was safe enough that no one was pushed into staying. It would not save visitors from the Crown’s slow transformation if they chose it. It would only make sure the choice was theirs.

And it left the being an offer.

Not a door cut into the iron. Not a hand reaching under the grate. A note in the roar—a warmth that did not come from the flue, a slight change in the resonance of the plate above the chamber, a thin thread of meaning in the only language the place had: *You may.* You may leave. You may speak. You may stay. You may be other than what you have become. The offer would stand for a season, and then for a century, and it would not require an answer. The power would not mistake silence for consent. If the shifting changed, it would help—whatever shape the help needed to take. If the shifting did not change, it would accept the silence not as refusal but as the being’s current shape, and it would not lift the grate.

Then it sat above the floor and let its presence be slow and warm and without demand. It said, in the only way the Crown could hear: *I see you. You are not a problem to be solved. If you are in pain, I will help. If you are at home, I will go.* It listened to the dry shifting for a long time. It did not flinch when the thin grey thing came up through the grate and lay flat on the warm metal. It did not take it. It let the warmth be shared.

And then it remained at the edge of things—not in the room, not outside it, but as a quiet roof over the roof. The Crown went on roaring at nothing. The birds ignored the power as they ignored everything. The being went on being warm. The only difference was that the place was now held in an attention that wanted nothing from it.

That is not nothing. In a world full of powers that fix and take, it is almost everything.
