# The Visitor

The power came in the way that powers come, which is to say: quietly, and without anyone agreeing to it.

It did not arrive in a chariot of fire or a pillar of cloud. It arrived as attention. For a long time—by the reckoning of the place, some years; by its own, an eyeblink—it simply watched. It watched the rain that was not rain bead and fall from the undersides of the great plate roofs. It watched the moss spread three fingers thick on the lee side of a wall and be trimmed back by a woman with a hooked blade who hummed while she worked. It watched a boy of six step over a channel without looking down, and it understood, after a while, that the boy had learned the width of that channel in his legs, the way you learn a song.

It watched the crane turn.

And because it was wise, it did not see a place to be fixed. It saw a metabolism. It saw twelve thousand people and a pelt of moss and a forest in a collapsed roof and a heron with a schedule, all of them eating and being eaten and keeping on, and it saw that the whole of it was already, in its way, a kind of prayer—the ceaseless ordinary prayer of things continuing. It saw that Omber was not broken. It saw that Omber was *tired*, in the way that a stone step is tired, worn into a shape by ten thousand feet.

The first thing the power did was the smallest thing it could.

---

It went down into the lowest level, where the reservoir is, and below the reservoir, where nobody goes.

It did not need to go in a body, but it made itself a body anyway, because some things should be done with hands. It made itself a body like a lamplighter's, thin and quiet and wearing oilskin, and it walked down the wet stairs where the eels coil and the water smells of iron and old time, and it went to the place where the oldest pipes are, the ones that feed the Works, the ones that have been shored and patched and re-shored for as long as anyone remembers and longer.

It found a joint that was weeping. Not badly—just a slow seep, a bead every few seconds, the kind of thing that has been weeping so long that the moss has grown to drink it and the moss is, in its way, keeping the joint cool, keeping the rust slow. The power looked at the joint for a long time. It understood that if it sealed the joint, the moss would die back, and the beetles that live in the moss would go elsewhere, and the pale things in the cisterns that eat what the moss does not would find less to eat, and the eels would have less to coil in, and the men who go down where the eels are would come up smelling of less.

It understood that a weeping joint is not always a fault. Sometimes it is a mouth.

So it did not seal the joint. It sat with the joint for a while, the way you sit with a person who is tired, and it listened to the seep, and it made a small adjustment—not to the pipe, but to the water. It made the water, in its passage, carry a little more dissolved mineral, a little more of the stuff that the moss needs and the ferns need and the chard needs. Not much. Just enough to be a good meal, once, in a season of lean.

Then it went up.

---

The second thing the power did was to find the heron.

It found the heron on the parapet of the highest deck, standing as herons stand, one leg tucked, staring at the water that was not water, waiting for whatever it is herons wait for here. The power stood beside the heron and did not speak, because herons do not care for speech.

After a while the heron turned its head and looked at the power with one eye, and the power looked back, and something passed between them that was not words. The heron, if it could have said, might have said: *I wait. It is what I do. I wait for the thing that comes, and sometimes it comes, and I eat, and then I wait again. It is not a bad life. The waiting is most of it.*

And the power, if it could have said, might have said: *I know. I am sorry. I did not come to make you stop waiting. I came to wait with you.*

So it did. It stood on the parapet beside the heron for a long while—an hour, a day, it does not matter—and the mist moved through the valley and the lamps burned orange and the crane turned, and the two of them waited together for whatever it is that comes.

And in that waiting, the power did a thing that it would not have done if it had been in a hurry. It learned the shape of the heron's patience. It learned that the heron's patience was not passive. It was a kind of attention, a kind of *keeping watch*, and it was the same attention that the moss-cutters gave to the lamps and the pipe fitters to the walls and the sump men to the eels. The heron was not idle. The heron was *tending*.

The power took that lesson up the valley with it, into the places where the people were.

---

The third thing the power did was to walk.

It walked the eleven decks, from the rim to the reservoir, and it did not announce itself. It wore the body of a lamplighter and carried a pole, and where a lamp was out it lit the lamp, and where a lamp was dim it trimmed the wick, and the people on the decks saw a lamplighter doing what lamplighters do and did not look twice. This is the way of benevolent powers: they come as lamplighters.

It walked for a long time. It walked until its boots were soft with wear and its coat was stiff with oil and its hands were stained green in the creases, and it walked the way the people walked—keeping to the uphill side of the drip lines, ducking under the low spans, stepping over the channels—and after a while it did these things without thinking, the way the people did them without thinking, because a place teaches you its grammar if you let it.

It walked among the women carrying crates and the men carrying spars. It walked among the children who knew the sound of a leaking joint before they knew how to read. It walked among the moss-cutters and the pipe fitters and the terrace gardeners and the sump men, and it listened to them talk, and it learned the things that Omber knows about itself.

It learned about the eels. It learned that the eels are not eaten not because of a law but because of a *knowing*—the way you know not to eat the thing that lives in your own sump, the thing that cleans the water you drink, the thing that is, in its long dark way, a kind of kin. It learned that the heron is the only creature with no job, and that this is important, that a place needs one thing that does nothing but wait, or the place forgets how to wait.

It learned that the forest in the collapsed roof was forty years old and had a floor of its own, and that the children of the lower terraces went there to be somewhere that was not a deck, and that this was the closest thing Omber had to a temple.

The power found the forest, and it sat in the forest for a while.

It sat on the mossy floor among the alder trunks that had rooted in a collapsed roof, and it listened to the small sounds—the drip of the condensation through the leaves, the hum of the Works below, the far-off ticking of the crane—and it understood that this was a holy place. Not because anyone had made it holy. Because it had become holy the way a place becomes holy: by being the place where people go to remember that they are not only a rota.

It did not speak to anyone in the forest. It did not need to. It sat there long enough to be part of the place's memory, and then it went on.

---

The fourth thing the power did was to make a gift.

It thought for a long time about what to give, because it was wise, and it knew that the wrong gift is worse than no gift at all. It did not want to give a thing that would make the people stop doing something they knew how to do. It did not want to give a thing that would make the place less itself. It did not want to give a thing that would, in a hundred years, be rusted into a memory of an insult.

So it gave a small thing. It gave a way.

It went to the terrace gardeners and it put a knowing in them—not a new thing, but a deepening of a thing they already knew. It deepened their sense of which lamps make a plant bolt and which keep it sweet. It deepened their sense of when to trim and when to let grow. It deepened their sense of the water, the way the water carries the stuff of the upper decks down to the lower decks, and it let them see, just once, with their mind's eye, the whole of the valley as a single garden, every deck a bed, every channel a root, every cistern a mouth drinking.

And the gardeners, who were practical people, did not fall down and worship. They said, *Huh*, and went on with their work. But over the next season, the chard came in a little thicker, the vines a little sweeter, and the gardeners said to each other, *I had a thought about the lamps*, and they did not know why, and they did not need to.

The power also went to the moss-cutters and put a knowing in them—the knowing that the moss is not a weed to be fought but a crop to be managed, and that a wall without moss is a wall that will rust faster, and that the moss on the lee side of a wall is the wall's own coat, the way a man's beard is his own coat. And the moss-cutters, who were practical people, said, *Huh*, and went on with their work. But over the next season, they left a little more moss on the lee walls, and a little less on the lamps, and the walls did not rust so fast.

The power went to the sump men and put a knowing in them—the knowing that the eels are not to be eaten not because of a law but because they are the sump's own people, and that a man who tends the eels is tending his own water. And the sump men, who were practical people, said, *Huh*, and went on with their work. But over the next season, they made a small shrine at the top of the lowest stairs, a shrine of iron and moss and an old valve wheel, and they did not know why they made it, and they did not need to.

The power went to the children and put a knowing in them—the knowing that the place is a thing you can love. It did not put this in words. It put it in the sound of the drip, the smell of the moss, the way the lamps make a long orange smear on the wet iron, the way the heron stands on the parapet at certain hours. And the children, who were not yet practical people, received the knowing the way children receive things, which is wholly and without question, and they grew up loving the place, and that love was part of the place's own love of itself.

And the power went to the riggers and the pipe fitters and the crane operators, and to the people who carried things from level to level, and it did the same for each of them: not a gift from outside, but a deepening of a gift they already had. It let them see, each in their own way, the whole of the place as a single thing—a metabolism, a garden, a prayer—and it let them see that they were not the mind of the place, not the owners of the place, but a part of the place, the way the moss is a part and the eels are a part and the heron is a part.

The people did not know any of this had happened. They said, *Huh*, and went on with their work.

But over the next years, the place got a little better at being itself.

---

The fifth thing the power did was to make a question.

It did not make this question to anyone in particular. It made the question into the place, the way a seed is made into soil. It made the question into the water, so that the water carried it down through every cistern and every sump; it made the question into the moss, so that the moss grew it into every lee wall; it made the question into the hum of the Works, so that the hum hummed it into every room where people slept; it made the question into the heron's waiting, so that the heron's waiting became, in some small way, the waiting of the whole valley.

The question was this: *What else?*

What else, besides the endless small adjustment of old spans shored and new spans laid over them? What else, besides the shift after shift of carrying things from one level to another? What else, besides the moss and the chard and the eels and the heron? What else is Omber for, now that it has been Omber for so long?

The power did not answer the question. It was wise, and it knew that the answer was not its to give. It knew that the question would sit in the place the way the water sits in the reservoir—patient, deep, waiting for someone to draw it up. It knew that one day a child who had been born in the collapsed-roof forest would look at the crane turning in the mist and would ask, out loud, *What else?*, and that the asking would be the beginning of something the power could not predict and would not control and did not want to.

It knew that the question was a gift, and that the gift was the asking.

---

Then the power went away.

It did not announce its leaving. It simply stopped being the lamplighter, the way a lamp stops being lit when you turn down the wick. It left the body of the lamplighter in a small room on the seventh deck, and the people who found the body did not know what to do with it, because there was no disease and no wound and no sign of anything, and they sat with it for a while the way the power had sat with the weeping joint, and then they carried it up to the forest in the collapsed roof and laid it on the mossy floor, and the forest took it the way the forest takes everything.

The children of the lower terraces said that the forest had a new tree that year, a small one, pale and leggy under the lamps, and they were not wrong, and they were not right, and it did not matter.

---

What did the power leave behind?

It left a weeping joint that wept a little more richly. It left a heron that had waited with someone for a day. It left a knowing in the practical people, and a knowing in the children, and a question in the water and the moss and the hum of the Works.

And it left this, which it did not leave on purpose, but which it left anyway, because this is what powers leave:

It left the people of Omber a little more themselves.

Not a great deal. Not a transformation. The power was wise, and it knew that a place that has been going so long in its own wet and patient and ceaseless way does not need to be transformed. It needs to be *seen*. It needs to be *acknowledged*. It needs someone to come and stand on the parapet with the heron and wait, and to walk the eleven decks as a lamplighter, and to sit in the forest and listen, and to say, without words, *I see you. I see the whole of you. And you are good.*

That is the gift of a benevolent and wise power: not to fix, not to save, not to change, but to *see*, wholly and without judgment, and to let the seeing be a kind of blessing.

The people of Omber did not know they had been blessed. They went on with their shifts. The moss grew. The chard came in. The crane turned. The heron waited.

But somewhere in the place, in the water and the moss and the hum, there was a question now, and the question was patient, and the question was kind, and the question was: *What else?*

And one day, someone would ask it out loud.

---

The power, if you asked it, would say it did not do much. It would say it lit a few lamps and trimmed a few wicks and made a small adjustment to the water. It would say it sat with a joint and stood with a heron and walked with the people. It would say it gave a way to the gardeners and a knowing to the moss-cutters and a question to the valley.

And if you said, *But is that enough? Is that all a benevolent and wise power can do?*, it would say:

*What would you have me do? Seal the joint and kill the moss? Fix the crane and put the riggers out of work? Answer the question and take away the asking? I am not here to make Omber into something else. I am here to love Omber into being more fully Omber. That is the only benevolence that is not a kind of violence.*

*And anyway,* it would say, *look at the heron. The heron is still waiting. And the heron is fine.*

And the power would go back to watching, which is what powers do, and the valley would go on being the valley, which is what valleys do, and the rain that is not rain would bead and fall from the undersides of the great plate roofs, and the work would continue, shift after shift, the way the water continues.

Which is, in the end, the whole of the matter.

The world is already a prayer. The power's only job is to say *amen*.

And *what else?*

And *amen* again.
