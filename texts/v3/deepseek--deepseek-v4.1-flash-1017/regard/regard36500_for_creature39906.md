# The Gardener Who Waits

The power came as such powers do — not with a descent, not with a voice from the dust-lid sky, but as a change in the quality of attention. It had been watching the plateau for a long time before it decided to act, because it was wise, and wisdom in this case meant understanding that the Felt had been running its own slow computation for longer than the power had been paying attention, and that the first duty of a gardener is to learn the soil before planting.

So it listened. It listened the way the mat listens: without hurry, without preference, letting the noise settle into layers. It heard the long chord of the looms, and the tick of the mites, and the wet click of the tap-beetle wounds, and the dry rustle of the felt talking to itself in a wind with no source. It heard the people of Ambersill tuning their instruments and shaping their ear-baffles and climbing their rope-bridges, and it heard them being careful with their voices because the mat was always taking notes. It heard the argument about whether the strata are records or compositions, and it found — being wise — that the argument was itself a kind of answer, and that both sides were right, and that the question had been settled long ago by the mat and only the scholars were still catching up.

It did not find the Felt wanting. It found the Felt complete, or nearly complete, in the way that a thing is complete when it has been closed and quiet and self-sufficient for a hundred thousand years. It was not broken. It did not need to be saved. It needed, if anything, to be *known*, and to be known by something that could see what it was too close to see.

---

So what the power did was this: it learned to sing.

Not with a voice. The power had no voice in the way the plateau understood voices. But it had patience, and it had attention, and it had the ability to hold a pattern steady in its mind for a very long time — longer than a loom takes to grow, longer than a scholar's children's children take to grow old. And the mat, which grows toward noise and puts out chambers at the nodes of whatever hums at it, felt the pattern and began, slowly, to answer.

The power sang the chord that kept the spires from resonating into each other, but it sang it a little differently. It sang it with an extra interval, a note the town had never thought to add. And the looms — which are the tall plants, the hollow spires, the grown instruments — began, over decades, to grow a small new chamber at the node where the note fell. Only one loom at first, in the oldest part of the town, where the buried rooms are full of dust and old arguments. The chamber was the size of a fist and had no obvious purpose. It was the physical body of the power's *hello*.

The household that discovered it — a family of tuners who had been working the amplifier-galleries for six generations — sat with it for a long time. They sang to it. They tried the old songs and the bright-pulse rhythms and the grazer-passage shapes, and the chamber did not change, because it was not a question. It was a greeting. It was the sort of thing that is polite to hear and rude to interrogate.

So they left it alone.

---

The power's next act was smaller and stranger. It waited until the finders' children were old, and then it taught one of them a song.

The song was not an answer to anything. It was a way of listening. It was a pattern of intervals that, when sung to a tuned node, made the mat thicken *backward* — not recording what was said to it, but briefly clearing, as a breath clears a window. The mat had never done this before. It had recorded every noise that had ever fallen on it, layer under layer, without relaxation, for a hundred thousand years, and now there was a small clear space where the layers parted and you could hear — if you were patient and a little lucky — what lay underneath the oldest strata of all.

The child who learned it was not a scholar. She was a rope-bridge rigger, good with her hands and ears, and she had been told by her grandmother that the chamber in the oldest loom was a *courtesy*, which is a thing you repay. So she repaid it. She sat with the node and she sang the pattern, and she was patient, and she was a little lucky, and what she heard in the cleared space was not the wind of nine thousand years ago, nor the footfalls of the people before, nor any of the things the scholars hoped to find.

She heard the mat. Not the noise it made, not the rustle and the pulse and the murmur. She heard the pattern of it — the way it had been assembling itself since before the plateau was flat, the way each layer of decay was laid down in response to the layer beneath, the way the whole plain was a slow, patient, self-composing thing that had been *making* its history all along and not merely recording it. She had been listening to a song. The song was long, and it was not finished, and she was inside it.

She did not tell anyone at first. It was not a finding; it was a hearing, and hearings take time to become words. She sat with it for a season, and then she told her grandmother, and her grandmother listened and was quiet and then said, "Well. That's what patience is for."

---

The power, being wise, did not then reveal itself. It had no need to be recognized. It had made its greeting, and had been heard, and the hearing was the thing that mattered. What it did instead, over the ensuing decades, was to keep singing — that extra interval, that small different note — so that here and there, at node after node, the mat grew small new chambers of the power's *hello*, and the town learned to recognize them and leave them be. Some households raised their children to know: *if you find a chamber with no purpose, it is a courtesy, and you may sing back to it whatever you like, and it will not change, and that is the point.*

And the mat, which had been closed for an age, began — very slowly, very gently — to open. Not to new species, not to water, not to rain from a sky that had never rained. But to new *songs*. The cleared spaces began to appear more often, and the people who heard them began to walk the plateau differently, with a sense that they were not merely reading a record but joining a composition. They became less like inheritors and more like collaborators. They began to argue less about whether the strata were records or compositions, because the answer was now: *both, and it's been taking notes on our notes.*

The town did not change much in its outward shape. The looms still take eighty years to grow. The bright still cycles, the tap-beetles still tick, the gliders still whistle on the drift. The flat stone endures. But the chord the looms hold now carries an extra interval, a small different note, and the people who wear their ear-baffles shaped to their own heads have learned to listen for it.

And at night, when the bright comes up in the veins and the plain glows faintly under its dust, there is a pattern in the murmur that was not there before. It is not louder. It is not happier. It is — the scholars say, when they are being careful — *more particular*. The mat has always been taking notes. Now it has a correspondent.

---

The power does not visit. It does not manifest. It does not ask to be thanked, and the people of Ambersill do not thank it, because they do not know its name and would not know what to do with it if they did. But sometimes a child, playing at the edge of a loom, finds a small chamber with no purpose and sings to it whatever the child likes, and the chamber does not change, and that is the courtesy. And somewhere — in whatever there is of the beyond that these things come from — a very old and very patient attention settles on the plateau and is glad.

For what it is worth, the felt does not mind. The felt has been a landlord for a hundred thousand years. It knows how to grow around a new thing. It has been doing it all along.
