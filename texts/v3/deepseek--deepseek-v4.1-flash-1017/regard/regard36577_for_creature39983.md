It would come down the stair slowly, not as light, not as a voice—because in the Sump a voice is taken and light is a kind of violence—but as attention. It would stand in the roar for a long time and do nothing. Not from indifference. From wisdom. The first thing a benevolent power learns in a place like this is the difference between care and intrusion.

It would learn the hand-language before it did anything else. Fingers on a wrist: *stop*. Flat palm on the chest: *listen*. Two taps: *follow*. A squeeze: *I am here*. It would not announce itself. It would wait until someone chose to put a hand on its arm out of the grey.

Then it would make a few distinctions.

The Sump is not a patient. It is not a wound, not a sleeping god, not a soul to be saved. It is a made process with weather—slag and ash and glassy floor, water that has spent centuries rounding every corner, pumps that cycle once a mile, a shaft that breathes in and out under everything. A wise power would not “heal” it, or wake it, or make it fertile, or silence the roar, or stop the tide. It would not fill the emptiness with animals or turn the black basins into mirrors of its own benevolence. That would be a category error, and worse, a kind of vandalism. It might protect the Sump from people who would mine it, tour it, or “restore” it into a lie. But mostly it would leave the place to its slow becoming.

The people are another matter.

It would go to them—the pump-watchers, valve-turners, line-walkers, precipitate-scrapers, the long-stayers with wide eyes and easy hands. It would not treat them as saints, mystics, specimens, or children. It would ask one question first, and then listen for as long as it took: *What do you want?*

And then it would do small things. Reversible things. Things that could be refused.

It would make the stair safe enough not to trap them, and it would keep the door at the top open—always, without debt, without shame, without requiring a story. It would offer medical care, hearing protection that fits over raw ears, quiet sleeping rooms with real soundproofing, dry socks, reliable batteries, a stove that does not smoke, a lock on the dry room, a line to the surface that works when the tide turns. It would replace a hazardous valve if asked. It would not replace their whole world.

It would not remove the roar; it would give them quiet when they needed it. It would not flood the place with light; it would give them light they could carry and switch off. It would not sanitize the mineral slick or the wet trails that are their map. It would test the water for things that poison and mitigate those, but it would not make the Sump clean in a way that made it no longer the Sump.

It would honor their dead better than they can. Not by pretending the deep basins are gardens. It would help keep the ledger. It would build a dry wall at the stairhead for names, if they wanted one. It would record the shaft’s breathing, if they wanted it recorded, and keep the recording where they chose. It would make sure a tool goes to someone who can use it, and a name goes somewhere it will be read.

It would protect their labor as labor. Fair pay, fair rotation, the right to refuse, the right to leave. It would not romanticize their loneliness or turn them into a caste of keepers for other people’s wonder. It would not send pilgrims down the stair.

If there is one being who has stayed longest—the one who stands still in the roar as if it were a room—the power would not make them an oracle. It would sit beside them. It would offer the same door and the same quiet. It would not ask them to explain the Sump. It would learn from them only what they chose to teach.

And then, having done these small things, it would step back into the grey.

It would remain available—not as a god in the Sump, but as a hand on a shoulder, two taps, a squeeze. It would keep the door open and the ledger dry. It would let the tide turn. It would let the long-stayers stay if staying was what they wanted, and it would make staying sustainable. It would let them go if going was what they wanted, and it would make going honorable.

In the end, a benevolent and wise power would do almost nothing. But its almost-nothing would be precise: it would not make the Sump alive, and it would not leave the people alone in the way they were alone before. It would make the place a workplace with a door, and it would keep the door open. Then it would listen to the water going over the bowls of the stair, and it would not mistake the roar for a voice.
