The power is old and it is not in a hurry, which is why it finds the bowl restful.

It has seen a great many things build themselves against entropy — cities with walls that outlasted the languages of the people inside them, temples whose stones were laid by hands that thought the universe would notice. It has watched most of these come apart, and the ones that did not come apart it has watched turn hollow. The bowl is not hollow. The bowl is a working animal. It eats light and water and plant-flesh and it makes resin and steam and children, and it does not pretend to be anything else, and this is rarer than the power expected and it likes it.

So it does nothing for a long while. It simply looks. It looks the way a person looks at a tide-pool: not to intervene, but because the small closed world is legible and complete and doing its work well. It watches the slot of light travel across the floor. It watches the gutters fill and the sap run and the boiler breathe. It watches a child learn the names of pipes. It watches a woman patch a frond panel by the light of a lamp that reflects in the wet planks, and it notes that her hands are stained the colour of the resin she makes, and it finds this good, though it could not say why it finds it good except that things which are used tend to have a rightness to them.

Then it does a small thing, because small things are what the bowl understands.

It waits for a night without a moon and it takes the shape of weather. It comes down the eastern slot as a slow warm front, so slow nobody feels it arrive, and it spreads itself across the canopy and works its way down through the leaves and into the root-thick air. It does not speak. It does not make a sign. It finds the boiler house where the two wheels turn and the steam goes up and it breathes on the wood a little, very gently, and then it finds the cut stems. The bowl is full of cut stems. Sap-tappers have been cutting them for a hundred years. The power goes to one — a root column near the eastern terraces, low down, where the tappers came this morning — and it looks at the wound, and it puts a thumb of itself on it, and the wound closes over clean.

The next morning the tapper comes back with her blade to deepen the cut, because that is what you do, and she finds the cut gone nowhere. Sealed. A scab of new bark where the blade was. She stands there for a while in the warm drizzle with her head on one side. She calls the channel-man over. He looks at it and shrugs and says the plant is doing what plants do, and she says it has never done that before, and he says it has, probably, and nobody noticed. And she says she noticed. And he says well then, keep cutting somewhere else, and she does, and that is the end of it for that day.

But the power goes on doing it, because the power has a long view and a slow nature. Every night it moves through the bowl and finds the newest wounds and closes them, and every day the tappers go out and find their cuts sealed and their cups dry and they cut again, and they talk, and the talk spreads the way talk does in a town of a hundred-odd people packed into the roots of a hole. Some of them say the sap is failing. Some of them say it is the mineral in the water, or the boiler smoke, or a new thing in the air. One old woman who has filtered sap her whole life says the plants are getting tired of being bled, and the others laugh at her, and she does not laugh with them, and she is right.

The power does not do this to hurt them. It is doing it because it has decided to teach them something and it is not sure yet what the lesson is. It is turning a problem over. Here is a people who live on a thin sweet bleed they take from a thing that cannot say no. They are careful with it — they are careful with everything, they are repairers — but they are also practical, and practicality can slide into a kind of blindness, and the power wants them to look. Not to stop. It does not want them to stop. It wants them to see what they are drawing from. It wants the woman with the blade to have a moment where she looks at the closed bark and thinks, *something is answering me.*

So it does not close every wound. It closes one in ten, and then one in five, and it watches the pattern become visible. The tappers develop a walking order, a set of columns they visit in a set of days, because you cannot hit the same cut twice or it scars. The power watches them develop it. It watches them start to leave offerings — a strip of cloth tied to the root, a pinch of boiler ash pressed into the bark — not because they believe it will help but because it is what you do when a thing is behaving oddly and you cannot say why. It watches a child ask her mother what the plants are thinking and get told *go clear the gutter.* It watches the town do what towns do when the world stops being predictable: it gets practical, and then it gets a little bit sacred, and the two are closer than people think.

The power keeps this up for a year. This is nothing to the power. It is the length of a blink. But to the bowl it is a year of the best cuts sealing overnight and the sweetest bleed coming only from the older columns, the ones nobody had cut in decades, the ones that have had time. And a quiet idea takes root, and it is the idea the power wanted, though it arrives in a shape the power did not predict — which is how it always goes, and why the power does it, because a thing you can predict teaches you nothing.

The idea is this: *cut the old, spare the young, and the sap comes anyway.* Which is not a new idea. It is a farmer's idea. It is an idea every settled people arrives at eventually, or doesn't. But these people had no animals to herd and no fields to rotate, and they had one enormous giving thing that never seemed to mind, and they had not arrived at it yet, and now they do. The old woman who said the plants were tired of being bled is shown to be right, and she does not gloat, which the power notices and likes. She takes the young tappers out and teaches them to read a root column: which ones have the dark bitter stain that means old, which ones run pale and quick and need to be let alone a season. They call it *reading the years.* It is not science. It is a habit of attention, and it is enough, and the sap yield does not fall. It climbs a little.

The power is content with this and is preparing to withdraw — to go back to being the weather and the slot of light and nothing else, to leave the bowl to keep its shape — when a child does something.

The child is nine, maybe ten. She is up on the eastern lip with the hoist crew, because her mother works the hoist, and she is not helping and she is not supposed to be there, she is sitting on the rim in the cold grey light with her feet hanging over sixty metres of green, which she has been told a hundred times not to do, and she is watching the resin come up in the bucket. She has been watching it for an hour. The bucket goes up and the bucket comes down, and one time the bucket comes down and there is a bird sitting on it.

There are no birds in the bowl. The hoist crew goes quiet. The child does not go quiet. The child looks up through the slot of sky — the only sky in the world, so far as she knows, because she has never been up on the rim and out — and she sees a line of grey-white cloud moving, thin and high, and she does not know it is the power looking back at her, and she says, out loud, to nobody in particular, in the flat practical voice of a girl who has grown up inside a machine:

"What's up there?"

And the power, which has been doing so well at keeping its distance, finds that it does not want to lie to her. It thinks about it. It has been a small weather and a closed wound and a habit of attention. It could go on being those things. But the child has asked, and the asking is the whole point of everything — the power knows this the way it knows the sun is a star, it is that old and that sure — and so it does the largest thing it has done since it came into the bowl, which is still very small.

It puts one star in the slot of sky. Just one. At the edge, low, where the canopy closes in, visible only from the eastern terraces for an hour after the light has gone. It is not bright. It is the kind of star you have to not-look-at to see. But the child sees it, and the hoist crew sees it, and by evening the whole northern third of the town has been told that there is a new thing above the leaves, and by the next evening there are people on the eastern terraces who have never in their lives bothered to look up through the slot who are now looking up through the slot, and the power watches them and feels something it cannot name, which is the feeling of having given a gift that was received.

The old woman, the one with the blade and the right idea, is among them. She looks up at the star for a long time. Then she looks down at the closed bark of the root column beside her — one of the ones she spared — and she looks at her hands, brown with a hundred years of sap, and she says, quietly, in the voice you use when you are working something out: "So it was up there the whole time, and we were down here the whole time, and nobody said."

Which is not the truth, exactly. But it is a true thing, and the power takes it, and it is glad of it, because a people who can hear a thing like that in a closed bowl are a people worth being quiet around.

It stays long enough to watch the star become ordinary. It is ordinary within a month; a month is enough for a closed town to build the star into the night the way it built the rains and the wheels into the day. The children have a game about it. The tappers measure the last hour of the light by it. Somebody carves a star into the lintel of the eastern hoist station, small and not very good, and nobody scrapes it off. That is the sign the power was waiting for, though it could not have said so at the start: that the new thing has been made local, made practical, made part of the machine. The bowl is itself again. It has absorbed the gift and the lesson and the star and gone back to being a working animal, which is exactly what the power wanted and exactly why it can leave now.

Before it goes it does one last thing, and this it does for itself, because it has been in the bowl for a year and has come to feel about the bowl the way you feel about a ship you have sailed in. It goes down, all the way down, into the wet green dark where the root columns take on weight and nothing with a nervous system lives but people. It moves through the town slow, the way the slot of light moves, and it touches each house once — the patched roof, the rope catwalk, the lamp with the yellow flame — and it finds the seams the repairers have mended and it finds the seams that are about to fail and it does not fix the ones about to fail, because the repairers need their work and their work is their dignity, and the power has learned this in the bowl and will not forget it. It just looks. And what it sees it approves of, in the way you approve of a thing that does not need your approval but has earned it anyway.

Then it goes up through the leaves and out of the slot and back into the weather, and the bowl is left with its star and its habit of attention and its practical love of the old columns, and it gets on with it, and the power does not come back.

Not because it has lost interest. Because it has learned what it needed to learn. The bowl is a closed system that keeps its own weather and its own time, and the correct thing for a wise power to do with a closed system that is working is to leave it closed, and to remember it, and to tell others. Which is what the power does. It tells the next power it meets, at the edge of some other sky, that there is a bowl in a limestone sink where a hundred-odd people live inside a machine they did not build and keep it turning, and that they have no animals and they read the years, and that there is a star in their slot of sky that they have made into a game.

And the other power will say, is that all.

And the power will say, yes. That is all. That is everything.
