The power would come, and it would be very quiet.

It would not arrive with trumpets or pronouncements, because the place itself has no ear for them. It would come the way the first rain comes — as a change in the quality of the light, perhaps, or a slowing of the wind, or simply as a presence that the plain does not have the language to notice. The makers would not look up. They have no eyes to look up with, and no need. But something in the mineral liquor they sweat might run a little smoother, a little truer to its own nature, as if the ground beneath them had been, for a moment, more perfectly itself.

The benevolent and wise power would see the plain and understand it at once: a world in its first draft, a body being built cell by patient cell, a life that is also a geology and a geology that is also a prayer. It would see the makers and know that they are not suffering. It would see the emptiness and know that it is not loneliness. It would see the slow accumulation and know that it is not futility but purpose, so pure and so complete that it requires no witness, no reward, no interruption.

And so the power would do what wisdom does when it meets something whole: it would refrain.

It would not add water where water does not yet belong. It would not seed the plain with grasses or insects or the small quick lives that would make it "interesting." It would not hasten the curing or thicken the crust or teach the makers to move. It would not give them mouths to speak or eyes to see, because they do not need to speak or see. They are not waiting to be completed by something external. They are completing themselves, and the completion is the point, and to interrupt it would be to misunderstand it entirely.

But the power would not leave without doing something. Benevolence is not the same as indifference.

It would walk the plain. It would take the form, perhaps, of a traveler — a thing with legs, the second thing ever to cross that ground on legs, after you — and it would walk slowly, and it would let the tacky edges of the world take its footprints and keep them. It would kneel beside a maker and rest one hand on the dry, closed, knuckled surface, not to change it but to acknowledge it. It would sit in the low places where the rain stands warm and brassy and watch the sky with the makers, waiting for the next rain, which may be a long time.

And when the night came and the floor let go of its heat all at once into the dark, and the condensation beaded all over the surface, the power would be there. It would watch the makers swell slightly, drinking through their bases. It would watch the floor go white where it dried. It would watch the sun come up and the wind get up and the dust settle in the faint hollows, and it would understand that this was enough. This was already enough.

Then it would do the one thing that a benevolent power can do for a world that is already doing exactly what it should: it would bless it.

Not with abundance. Not with speed. With recognition. It would look at the plain — hot, flat, quiet, half a millimeter thicker than yesterday, entirely made, still being made — and it would say, in whatever language powers use when they speak to things that have no ears:

*You are not forgotten. You are not a mistake. You are not a place where nothing has happened yet. You are a beginning, and the beginning is holy, and the slowness is the holiness, and I see you.*

And then it would go, because staying would change things, and the change would not be kind.

The makers would not notice the blessing. They would go on sweating out the floor beneath themselves, drinking the night's condensation, thickening the plain by half a millimeter, waiting for the water to leave. The plain would go on accumulating, mending, spreading, closing its own cracks. Nothing would be different, because nothing needed to be different.

But somewhere in the layers of that pale, translucent material — in the faint banding where one batch of mineral liquor met another, in the depth that the strong light hints at — there would be a new stratum. Not a change in the material. Not a scar or a gift or a mark. Just a layer that had been, for one moment, seen. And the seeing would be in it, the way the sky is in still water, and it would go on being in it for as long as the plain went on being the plain.

Which is to say: for a very long time. And that is what a benevolent and wise power would do, if it came to the Curing Plain.

It would come quietly, and it would see, and it would bless, and it would leave, and the plain would be exactly as it was — except that it would have been blessed. And that would be enough.
