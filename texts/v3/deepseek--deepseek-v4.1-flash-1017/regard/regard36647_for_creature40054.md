The power arrives the way a thought arrives—gradually, then all at once, and afterward it is difficult to remember not having thought it.

It does not come in a ship. It has no body to park. It comes as an attention, a widening of what is already there, the way a sleeper might become aware that the room has been getting brighter. The scarp does not notice. The wind continues its climb, the dust-ropes thicken and thin, the drifters spiral and call. But something in the space above the terraces has begun to hold them differently—not more tightly, but more **attentively**, the way a hand holds a bird it does not wish to crush.

The first thing the power does is nothing. It watches for a long time, long enough to learn the forty-hour breath, long enough to see the towns as living things and the cables as their nerves, long enough to understand that the emptiness between settlements is not a wound but a deliberate room to breathe. It watches the maintenance: the mending of crust-mats, the shuttering before storms, the slow replacement of machinery that is itself being replaced. It watches the people come out at evening to stand on their roofs and listen, and it understands, in the way that powers understand things, that this standing-still is the closest thing here to prayer.

Then it goes among them, but not as a person. A body would be a lie, and also an intrusion—the people of Orm read dust and cable-sing and the color of the light; a new body would be a wrong note in a song they have sung for generations. So the power comes instead as a quality of the air, a slight change in the taste of the wind at evening, a faint extra warmth in the lee of a stone that makes the crust-mats spread a little more generously. It comes as a feeling, in certain individuals, that some question they have not asked has been answered.

The first visible thing it does is small. A town high on the seventh terrace has been losing its lower lift for three generations; the counterweight is cracked and the replacement is a rumor passed down from a grandmother. The power does not fix the lift. That would be to take the work, and the work is the life. Instead, it sends a dream—not a dream to a person, but a dream to the town, if a town can dream. Three of its maintainers wake in the same night with the same image: a particular shelf of rock two kilometers down the face, where a fragment of the right alloy has lain half-buried since before the town was built. They do not know why they know, but they go, and they find it, and they haul it up in a basket over four days, and they argue about the fitting for another week, and the lift works. They do not thank anything. That is correct. The power did not want thanks. It wanted the lift to work, and the work to remain theirs.

The second thing it does is also small, but stranger. It goes to the stone-borers—who are never seen, only inferred—and it learns them. They are a species so old and so quiet that they have no name, even among themselves. They live in the rock's deeper layers and they have never hurt anyone, and no one has ever seen them, and the power finds them and sits with them in the dark for a while. Then it does a thing it has not done anywhere else in this galaxy: it gives them a gift they did not ask for and cannot refuse. It teaches them, very slowly, over what to them is a single generation, to bore in a slightly different pattern—a pattern that follows the contours of the crust-mats above them, aerating the rock in a way that lets the mats root deeper and spread farther. The mats flourish. The grazers increase. The long pale things have more to eat, and they in turn become a little less secretive, and the people notice, and they adjust their own tending in response. The power does not arrange for anyone to understand what happened. It simply lets the gift propagate through the food chain until it arrives at the towns as a better year.

The third thing it does requires patience, even for a power. It waits for a dust-front—a true one, two days long, the kind that shutters the towns and slackens the cables and makes the upper escarpment a single grainy gold roar. And when it comes, the power does not calm it. It would not dare. But it goes into the wind and learns it, the way one learns a language by listening, and in the storm's deepest hour it finds the place where the scarp's own breath and the wind's fury almost agree—and it **sings there**. Not a song of power. A song the drifters taught it, or the cable-sings, or the long pale things in their crevices. A low note, a carrying note, the same note the drifters call at evening. And the wind does something new: it takes the note and holds it and returns it, modulated, and for a few hours the storm has a melody inside it. The towns, shuttered, hear it as a strange richness in the roar. They will tell stories about that storm for a hundred years. They will say the scarp sang back.

The fourth thing it does is the hardest, and it nearly fails. It goes to the drifters. The drifters are enormous and membranous and almost weightless, and they have been spiraling the upper escarpment for longer than the towns have stood, and they are not intelligent in the way a person is intelligent, but they are old, and they are patient, and they have been watching. They are **already aware** of the power, and have been since it arrived, and this is the first thing on Orm that has surprised it. The drifters ask it, in their way—in bladders and filaments and a low note that carries for kilometers—what it wants. And the power, which has come so far and watched so long and intended to give without being seen, finds that it wants something after all.

It wants to learn to spiral.

The drifters, who have no reason to refuse and are curious about everything slow, teach it. It takes a very long time, even for the power. The updraft is not a thing you ride; it is a thing that makes you into a rider. The drifters show it how to be light, how to be patient, how to let the wind do the work. And when it has learned—when its attention finally catches a single early-morning therm and holds it and spirals, once, over the highest terrace, casting a shadow that is not the shadow of any drifter—

One person sees it. A child, awake early, standing on a roof with a veil around her throat and a hand against the warm stone, watching the drifters settle and one drift that is not a drifter, that spirals once and slides away. She does not tell anyone. She is not certain what she saw. But she keeps it, the way the people here keep things—without ceremony, without urgency, in the same place where they keep the long rhythms and the dust and the patience.

The power departs as it arrived: gradually, then all at once, and afterward it is difficult to say what changed. The lift works. The mats are deeper. The stone-borers are still unseen. The storm that sang is already becoming a story. Nothing on Orm has been conquered, converted, instructed, or corrected. The power did not bring a message and did not leave a monument. It came, and it watched, and it offered three things—a dream, a pattern, a note—and it took one thing, a lesson in how to be light.

Somewhere else, a long way away, the power is asked what it did on Orm, and it says: "I attended. I was taught by the small and the old. I learned to spiral." And the one who asked does not understand, but the power does not try to explain, because it has learned patience from the wind and it has learned that the emptiness is not a lack; it is the shape of the life. The terraces are still there, and the drifters are still spiraling, and at evening the scarp still hums one warm low note, and the people come out onto their roofs to listen, and the stones are a little kinder under their feet than they were before.
