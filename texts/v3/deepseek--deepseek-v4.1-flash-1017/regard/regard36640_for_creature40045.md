# What the Wise Power Would Do

It would come up the western slope in the mist, not descending from above (which would be an intrusion, a statement) but arriving the way water arrives, finding the low places, taking the shape of the terrain.

It would stand on the rim for a long time.

---

## First: listening

A benevolent power does not arrive with a plan. It arrives with attention.

So it would stand on the soft grey lip of the bowl while the mist came up around its knees, and it would hear what the place is saying. Not the wind only—the wind is the instrument, not the voice—but the whole chord of it: the hundred pitches of the mounds, the drip and gurgle of the drainage, the creak of ice, the resinous crackle of the scrub, the low subaudible croon of the one grey animal going about its circuit below.

It would listen until it understood that the town is not abandoned. It is *open*. There is a difference. An abandoned place is a closed door. This place has all its mouths open and facing downslope, waiting for water, waiting for wind, waiting for whoever comes. The emptiness is not a wound. It is capacity.

The power would recognize this and be quiet.

---

## Second: walking

It would walk the town in the way the grey animal walks it—not to survey, not to catalog, but to *learn the sound-map*. To pass a mound-mouth and hear the pitch change as it blocked the wind with its body, and understand, in the body and not the mind, what the place is like from inside the chord.

It would notice: the mounds were built by someone. The banding says so. And the builders are gone, and the mounds are *still working*—still filtering water, still singing, still passing the wind through themselves in the way they were shaped to do. The power would feel the long patience of that. Architecture that outlives its architects and continues to function is a kind of faithfulness. It would recognize that the town is not dead. It is *idling*.

It would step carefully in the channels. It would not widen them.

---

## Third: considering the animal

And it would come, eventually, to the one grey animal, asleep in its dry chamber above the waterline, and it would consider.

Here is a creature that did not build this place and does not maintain it and does not sing in it—and yet it lives *inside* the song, uses the drainage as paths, sleeps in a chamber chosen by the sound-map, moves through the mist with its eyes half-closed because it knows the chord well enough to navigate by it. It is not the town's maker. It is the town's *reader*. It croons, low and almost below hearing, when it is comfortable, and the mounds pass that along.

The power would understand something: this animal is the town's continuation, not its replacement. It keeps the place inhabited, which is to say it keeps the place *in use*, which is to say it keeps the place from becoming a ruin. A ruin is a thing that has stopped being used. The grey animal's small warm route through the wet stone is the thread that holds the town in the present tense.

The power would not make the animal more than it is. It would not give it tools, or speech, or the memory of the builders. That would be a kind of violence—an imposition of the power's own shape onto a creature that has found its shape already, and found it good.

But it might do something smaller and stranger and more fitting.

---

## Fourth: the gift

It would wait for a still day—one of the days when the chord drops to a breathy murmur and the pools are glass and the frost is in the scrub—and it would go to the animal's mound, the one whose mouth faces away from the prevailing wind, and it would sit at the mouth of it and hum.

Not a song. Not a message. Just a tone—one tone, held, matched to the pitch of the mound itself, so that the mound took it up and passed it along and the whole choir shifted by one note, briefly, and then settled back.

And the animal would come out, beaded with mist, and look at the power, and decide it was weather, and continue.

But the tone would *stay*. Not in the air—air forgets—but in the mound, the way a bell remembers. A small change in the pitch of that one hollow shell, a half-step lower than it was, so that the animal's sleeping-chamber would hum a different note from now on. A note it would hear every night as it settled. A note that would become, over the animal's small lifetime, simply the sound of home.

That is the gift: not knowledge, not power, not company. A slight change in the chord that makes the place *more itself*—one more voice in the choir that was always there and hadn't yet been heard.

---

## Fifth: the seed

And then the power would do the thing that is hardest for a benevolent power to do, which is *nothing more*.

But it would leave something. Not a monument. Not a tool. Something the place would know what to do with.

It would gather, from the drainage channels where they run deepest, a handful of the pale silt that the water carries down—the mineral grit that the mounds are made of, that the builders used, that the water still deposits in the low places. And it would carry that silt to one of the empty mounds, one of the little ones, fist-sized, whose mouth faces the low side of the slope. And it would pack the silt into the mouth of it, gently, the way a bird packs a nest, and leave it there.

Rain would come. The silt would settle. The mound would take it up the way it takes up everything the water brings it—slowly, in bands, over years. And in a hundred years, or a thousand, the mound would be a little thicker, a little deeper-voiced, a little more *itself*.

That is the seed: not a new builder, not a resurrection, not a restoration. Just a small addition to the slow accretion of the place, the way the builders themselves once added, generation on generation, shell on shell. The power would not rebuild the town. It would *add one band*.

---

## Sixth: the leaving

And then it would go, the way it came, up the western slope into the mist, and it would not look back, because looking back is a kind of claim.

But as it went it would croon—low, throaty, almost below hearing—and the mounds would take it up and pass it along, and for a moment the whole bowl would hum a note it had not hummed before, and then the wind would shift, and the chord would settle, and the place would go on being what it is: finished, ancient, waterlogged, half-abandoned, full of wind, humming to itself, waiting out the next few thousand years.

And the grey animal, asleep in its chamber above the waterline, would hear the new note in the chord, and would not wake, and would be comfortable.

---

## What it would not do

It would not bring the builders back. The builders are gone, and their going is part of what the place is now—the openness, the capacity, the mouths of the mounds standing open like cups set out for a party that ended before you arrived. To fill them would be to close them. The power would understand that the emptiness is a kind of welcome, and it would not refuse the welcome by filling it.

It would not teach the animal to sing back. The animal's croon is not a failure of the song. It is the song, heard from inside. The power would leave it that way.

It would not mark the place. No sign, no seal, no signature. The power's visit would leave no trace except the silt in the small mound and the half-step in the chord, and those are not traces. They are *contributions*. The place would absorb them the way it absorbs everything: slowly, in bands, over years.

And it would not stay. A benevolent power that stays becomes a resident, and a resident changes the place by being there. The power would understand that its gift is the *not staying*—the leaving-open, the leaving-be, the trust that the place will go on being what it is without being watched.

---

## The last thing

On the rim, in the last of the light, the power would pause and look back at the whole of it—the soft grey lips, the furred stone, the hundred open mouths glowing, the pools like coins, the one small warm body going about its circuit—and it would feel what the place has been feeling for a very long time, which is *patience*, and it would be glad.

And then it would go, and the mist would close behind it, and the chord would go on, one note lower, waiting.
