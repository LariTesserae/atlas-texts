The power would come not as a conqueror, not as a savior, but as something the basin has never known: a visitor who understands that arrival itself is a kind of violence, and so arrives as gently as a new seep opening in the mud.

---

**I. The Arrival**

It would come in the space between the drips.

Not through the sky—the grey ceiling of cloud is a closed lid, and the power would not break it. Not through the stone—the pale rock is still warm, still learning, and to crack it would be to interrupt a sentence half-spoken. It would come as the people come: low, unhurried, splayed and soft. It would come through the damp itself, condensing out of the warm air the way a thought condenses into a word. One moment the space between two vine-houses is empty; the next, there is a presence there, and the presence is not moving, and the presence has been there for some time, and no one can say when it began.

It would not be large. That is the first mercy. It would be the size of a child who has just climbed out of the seep, still gill-frilled, still wet. Its skin would be the color of the pale stone before it crumbles, that warm porous white, and it would be beaded with moisture, because everything here is beaded with moisture, and it would not presume to be dry. Its eyes would be wide and heavy-lidded, and they would be the color of the deep seeps where the light does not reach, and they would not blink, because blinking is a kind of assertion, and this power has no need to assert.

It would stand in the path and wait. It would wait for the people to notice, and it would wait for them to decide what noticing means. It would not speak first.

---

**II. The Listening**

The people would gather, slowly, the way water gathers in a hollow. They would not shout. They would not flee. They would stand in a loose curve, their webbed hands loose at their sides, their nostril slits opening and closing, tasting the air for what this thing is. The children would be behind the adults, peering through the gaps between bodies, and the old ones would be at the front, because the old ones have seen springs open and vines die and they know that new things are not always threats.

And the power would listen.

It would listen to the drip of water from the leaf-tips. It would listen to the low croak of the hidden amphibian, and it would hear in that croak the particular grammar of this place—the way a sound is not a signal but a sentence, the way silence is not absence but punctuation. It would listen to the people's breathing, which is slow and deep and smells of wet stone. It would listen to their silence, which is the loudest thing in the basin, and it would understand that this silence is not emptiness but a kind of speech, a way of saying *we are here and we are deciding and we are not afraid but we are careful*.

It would listen for a long time. Long enough for the light to shift from pearly grey to a deeper, softer grey. Long enough for a gnat-cloud to dance and settle and dance again. Long enough for the people to understand that this thing is not going to move, not going to speak, not going to demand. It is going to wait.

And then, when the oldest of the old ones—the one who has guided more vine-arches than anyone living, whose skin is the color of the oldest, softest mushroom—when that one steps forward and presses a palm to the power's chest, the power would close its eyes and exhale, and the exhale would smell of nothing at all, which is the most respectful thing a visitor can smell like in a place where every scent is a signature.

---

**III. The First Gift**

The power would not bring fire. It would not bring metal. It would not bring the wheel or the written word or any of the bright, sharp things that powers usually bring. It would bring something smaller and stranger: it would bring **the ability to remember water**.

Here is what that means. The people already remember the shape of a roof, the curve of a path, the particular croak a family uses at dusk. But water—water is the thing that moves, that seeps, that opens new springs and closes old ones. Water is the thing that makes the basin a basin. And yet the people cannot remember water. They can mark a seep that is breathing, but they cannot remember the exact taste of the seep as it was before the last tremor. They can feel a new spring bubble up, but they cannot hold the memory of the mud before the spring was there. The basin is always becoming, and the people are always becoming with it, and so they are always losing the water that was.

The power would teach them to keep it.

Not in a vessel—vessels are for things that stay. In a **song**. A song that is not a song the way the people know songs, not a croak or a hum, but a structure of sound that holds the memory of water the way a vine-arch holds the shape of a room. The power would teach them one song first, a small one, the memory of a single seep in the eastern quarter that opened three generations ago and has been slowly closing since. The song would hold the taste of that water, the temperature of it, the way the light fell on it at dusk. And when the seep finally closes—when the mud settles and the moss creeps over and the water is gone—the people would still have the song. They would be able to pour it into a new seep, a new hollow, and the water there would remember being the old water, and it would taste the same, and the gnats would gather the same way, and the amphibian would croak the same low note.

This is not power over the land. It is **memory with the land**. It is a way of negotiating that does not end when the water moves. The people would understand this immediately, because they are a people who understand that a house is never finished and a path is never straight and a family is never done. They would take the song and they would hold it, and they would begin—slowly, patiently, over generations—to learn the other songs, the ones the power would teach them one at a time, each one the memory of a seep or a spring or a pool that is still here or already gone.

And the power would not teach them all at once. It would teach them one song, and then it would wait, and it would listen, and it would learn from them how a song should be sung in this place, in this damp, in this light. The power is wise, but it is not omniscient. It is a visitor, and visitors must learn.

---

**IV. The Second Gift**

The power would notice that the people do not have a word for *stranger* that does not also mean *guest*. It would notice this and it would be moved, because it has been to many places and few of them have had such a word. And so it would offer a second gift, not as payment—payment is an insult—but as a response. A response to the word.

The second gift would be **the ability to hear the vines**.

The people already know that the vines may be slow intelligences. They press a vine into a new curve and hold it for an hour, and they feel something in the vine—a resistance, a yielding, a kind of attention. But they cannot hear the vines. They can only feel them, and feeling is a language of pressure and patience, not of words.

The power would teach them to hear. Not with their ears—their ears are for croaks and drips and the wet suck of footfalls. With their hands. The webbed, soft-padded hands that press and hold and coax. The power would show them how to press a palm to a vine and *listen*, and the vine would speak, not in words but in **shapes of feeling** that the hands could translate into words. The vine would say: *this arch is too tight, the light will not reach the inner leaves*. The vine would say: *I am thirsty, the seep beneath me is closing*. The vine would say: *the child who held me last was impatient, and I am still deciding whether to grow in the direction she asked*.

And the people would listen. They would learn to negotiate not just with patience but with **conversation**. They would learn that a house is not just a lineage of hands but a lineage of voices, and that the vine has been speaking all along, and the people simply did not know how to hear. They would learn that the basin is not just a nursery but a **choir**, and every seep and every stone and every slug-thing and every gnat-cloud is singing its own slow song, and the people are only one voice in it.

The power would not teach them to command the vines. It would teach them to *hear*, and then it would step back. What the people do with the hearing is their choice. The power does not make choices for others. That is not wisdom. That is arrogance dressed in wisdom's clothes.

---

**V. The Question**

And then, after the gifts, after the songs and the hearing, the power would sit with the people in the largest vine-house, the one that has been growing for nine generations and has a dome so wide that the fleshy flowers in the niches cast a cool green light across the moss floor. It would sit with the oldest of the old ones and the youngest of the children who have just climbed out of the seep, and it would ask a question.

It would ask: **What do you want to become?**

Not *what are you*. Not *what should you be*. What do you *want* to become. The power knows that this is a world caught in the act of becoming, and it knows that becoming is not a destination but a direction, and it wants to know which direction the people are facing. It wants to know what they dream of when they dream of the future. It wants to know if they want to stay in the basin forever, or if they want to find a way through the grey ceiling of cloud, or if they want to learn to breathe the water again and return to the seeps, or if they want something the power cannot imagine.

And the people would not answer immediately. They would sit in the cool green light and they would think, and the water would drip from the leaf-tips, and the amphibian would croak its low note, and the power would wait. It would wait for a day. It would wait for a season. It would wait for a generation if it had to. The power is patient. The power has learned patience from the basin itself, from the mud that settles and the stone that forms and the vines that grow one arch at a time.

And when the people finally answer—when the oldest of the old ones speaks, or when a child speaks, or when the whole household speaks together in a croak that is not a croak but a chord—the power would listen. It would listen with its whole self, with its wide heavy-lidded eyes and its pale porous skin and its hands that know how to hold a vine without pressing too hard. And then it would nod, once, slowly, and it would say:

**Then that is what you shall become. And I will help, if you want help, and I will stay out of the way, if you want that instead. And I will not decide for you. That is not my place. My place is to listen. My place is to remember. My place is to be here, in the damp, with you, for as long as you want me.**

---

**VI. What the Power Would Not Do**

It would not build a temple. It would not ask for worship. It would not declare itself a god, because it knows that gods are a kind of fire, and nothing here burns well. It would not bring laws or commandments or a book. It would not tell the people that their way of living is wrong, or that they should be more like the people of other places, or that they should stop tending vines and start cutting stone. It would not mark the basin with its name. It would not claim the seeps or the springs or the great fleshy flowers as its own.

It would not stay forever. The power is a visitor, and visitors must leave, or they become something else—a weight, a wall, a thing that blocks the seep instead of letting it breathe. The power would stay for a time—a generation, perhaps, or three—and then it would go, the way it came, condensing out of the damp and into the damp, leaving behind only the songs and the hearing and the memory of a presence that listened more than it spoke.

And when it left, the people would not build a monument. They would plant a vine. They would coax it into an arch, and they would hold it for an hour, and they would listen to it, and they would teach it the first song—the memory of the eastern seep that opened three generations ago and has been slowly closing since. And the vine would learn the song, and it would hold it, and the people would remember the power not as a god or a king but as a **gardener of memory**, a visitor who understood that the greatest gift is not power over the land but the ability to remember the land as it was, and to carry that memory forward into whatever the land is becoming.

And the basin would keep becoming. The mud would keep settling. The stone would keep forming. New seeps would open and old ones would close. The people would keep singing their songs, and the vines would keep speaking, and the gnats would keep dancing in their silent, chaotic knots, and the amphibian would keep croaking its low calendar-note, and the great fleshy flowers would keep blooming their cool green light into the warm, wet, patient dark.

And somewhere, in the damp, in the space between the drips, the power would be listening still. Not because it has to. Because it wants to. Because the basin is a choir, and the power has learned to love the song.
