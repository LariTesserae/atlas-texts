The power would come quietly, because that is the only way to come to the Kiln.

It would not arrive as light, for the Kiln already shines with its own slow luminance, and a brighter light would be a violence. It would not arrive as voice, for the builders speak in short declaratives and would not know what to do with a sermon. It would not arrive as fire or flood or revelation. The Kiln has no weather, no catastrophe, no end. It has only the patient work.

So the power would arrive as attention.

---

It would come as a quality of noticing, a presence that does not interrupt the ticking of condensation but somehow deepens it, the way a held breath deepens silence. The builders would not look up. They do not look up unless there is a reason, and this would not yet be a reason. It would be a change in the texture of the murk, a faint alteration in the way distance behaved—not the sudden rearrangement of corridors and plinths, but something subtler: the sense that the murk was paying attention back.

The first thing the power would do is watch. It would watch for a long time, longer than a human life, because the Kiln does not reward haste and the builders would not trust a power that hurried. It would watch a builder lift a block and set it and check the joint with a knuckle and squat and wait for the water to speak. It would watch the water turn upward along a seam and the builder nod and adjust the next course. It would watch a finished wall un-finish, a course loosening and rising a hand's breadth and settling back into a new arrangement, and the builder who laid them come back in an hour and lay them again, in a different order, without complaint. It would watch the lamps burn on, marking nothing, claiming nothing, and the small cold flames become a kind of constellation across the vast unfinished courses.

And in the watching, the power would begin to understand something. It would begin to understand that the Kiln is not broken. It is not a prison, not a punishment, not a thing waiting to be fixed. It is a making, and the making is the point. The builders are not trapped in an endless task; they are the task, the way a hand is the work it does. They know they will not finish. They say so. It is not despair. It is a condition of work, like wet.

The power would understand this, and it would be tempted to give them rest.

It would be tempted to offer them a final course, a last block, a wall that stays. It would be tempted to let the water run down instead of up, to still the loosening courses, to let the builders set down their mauls and look up and see something other than more work. It would be tempted to finish the Kiln.

And it would not.

Because the power is wise, and wisdom sometimes looks like restraint. The builders do not want to be finished. They want to lay blocks. They want to watch the water find its way. They want to leave lamps that burn on after they walk away, marking that the making went on. To finish the Kiln would be to end the making, and the making is what they are. The power would understand this the way a builder understands a seam: by watching until it has said what it means.

---

So the power would do something else. It would do the small thing, the quiet thing, the thing that is not a gift but a recognition.

It would begin to leave lamps.

Not its own lamps—it has no waxy fuel, no cold flame, no hands to set them down. But it would learn the builders' grammar, their short declaratives, their patient syntax. It would find a builder squatting beside a half-laid course, watching a bead of condensation gather and run, and it would place beside them, in the way that powers place things, a small certainty: *you are seen*.

The builder would not look up. But something in the set of the shoulders would change, a millimetre's relaxation, the way a wall settles when the mortar has found its level. The builder would tap the next block into place with a slightly different rhythm, and the rhythm would carry a note it did not carry before.

The power would do this again and again. It would find the builders in their solitary work, in their paired work, in the rare gatherings where they squat near a lamp and speak their short declaratives that seem to be about joints and water and turn out to be about something else. It would place its small certainty beside them, not as an interruption but as a resonance, a second note in the same key. It would find the ones who stand with one hand flat on a wall and let the ticking pass through them, and it would let the ticking pass through it too, and the wall would be warm with two attentions.

It would find the lamps that burn on after the builders walk away, marking nothing, claiming nothing, and it would tend them. Not refuel them—the fuel does not consume—but simply be present to them. A lamp burning alone in the murk is a small lonely thing, and the power would stand beside it and understand that the loneliness is not a defect. The lamp marks that the making went on. The power would mark it too.

---

And then, because the power is wise and knows that giving is not always the same as helping, it would do one thing more.

It would learn to build.

Not in the builders' way—it has no back and knees, no patient leverage of body. But in its own way, which is the power's way: it would learn the grammar of seams and puddles, the syntax of loosening courses and new stairs, the vocabulary of wet. It would watch until it understood what the builders understand: that nothing here is permanent and nothing is broken, that the water says what it means if you wait, that a wall un-finishing is not a failure but a conversation.

And then it would set a block.

It would find a course that was waiting, a joint that was open, a place where the work wanted to go on, and it would set a block there. Not a perfect block, not a final block—a small one, thumb-pressed, uneven, the way the oldest joints are crude. It would check the joint with something like a knuckle. It would squat and watch the water find its way through the new seam. It would wait until the water had said what it meant.

And a builder, passing an hour later, would see the block. Would squat. Would check the joint. Would watch the water run.

And would nod.

---

The power would not stay forever. Powers do not stay; they attend, and then they withdraw, and the withdrawal is part of the attendance. But it would leave something behind. Not a monument, not a revelation, not a finished wall. It would leave a lamp.

Not a real lamp—it has no fuel, no flame. But it would leave a brightness in the murk, a small quality of attention that would persist after it had gone, the way a warm wall holds heat. And the builders would feel it, in the way they feel everything: through their hands, through the ticking of condensation, through the patient grammar of their work. They would not name it. They do not name things. But they would know.

And when they set their lamps on half-built walls, marking that the making went on, they would know that somewhere in the murk, someone else had marked it too. Someone else had watched and understood and set a block and walked away. Someone else had learned the grammar of the Kiln and spoken it, once, in the only language that matters here: the language of patience with hands.

The Kiln would go on. The builders would go on. The water would tick down a thousand surfaces toward the puddles, and the heat would breathe up from below, and the pale walls would shine their dim light into the murk. And somewhere, far off, a lamp would burn on, marking nothing, claiming nothing, and a small hooded figure would pass it and not look up, because there was work to do, and the work was the point, and the point was the making, and the making went on.

And the power would watch, from wherever powers watch, and it would be glad. Not because it had fixed anything. Not because it had finished anything. But because it had attended, and the attending had been received, and somewhere in the vast unfinished courses a block had been set that would never stay and had mattered anyway.

That is what it would do.

It would not save the Kiln. It would not end the Kiln. It would love the Kiln, which is a harder thing, and a wiser one, and the only thing a benevolent power can do for a place that is already, in its patient and unfinished way, exactly what it needs to be.

It would leave a lamp. And walk away. And the lamp would burn on.
