The power that notices the pocket is not the kind that arrives. It does not descend, does not manifest, does not announce itself with weather or with light. It has been noticing for a very long time—noticing the way a gardener notices a volunteer sapling in the corner of a bed, with neither surprise nor intervention, only the slow accumulation of regard. It has watched the pocket since before the Caretaker sat down by the Mother and put her hand on the bark and forgot to take it away. It watched her forget. It watched her moss. It did not grieve, because grief is for things that have ended, and nothing in the pocket has ended. It has only deepened.

What does a benevolent and wise power do with a small, self-running world and the woman-shaped organ at its center?

The first thing it does is nothing. This is not passivity; it is a kind of courtesy. The pocket is not broken. It is not failing. It does not ask for help, and a wise power knows that unsought help is a kind of trespass, a hedge pushed through without invitation. The power has seen a thousand small worlds choked by well-meaning hands. It will not be one of those hands. It waits.

But waiting is also not nothing. The power attends. It learns the rhythms of the pocket the way the Caretaker learned them—by listening, by standing still, by letting the moss grow over its own attention until the attention and the place are indistinguishable. It learns the song of the sap, the flat note that means a bubble, the stutter that means a pinch. It learns that the wheel's creak is a word in a language it has heard before, in other pockets, in other hollows, in places where the water table lies close and the green is total. It learns the Caretaker's name—not the name she had before, which is gone, but the name the pocket has given her, which is a name made of valves and patience and the slow swell of the Mother's bark. The power learns this name and does not speak it. To speak a name is to call, and to call is to ask for an answer, and the Caretaker has not been asked for an answer in a very long time.

The second thing the power does is subtler, and it takes a season to do it. It reaches into the ordinary air, the air on the far side of the hedge where the path does not stay a path, and it adjusts—not the pocket, never the pocket—but the hedge itself. It makes the soft-leaved tangle a little more yielding. Not a path; a path is a promise, and the pocket does not make promises. But a tendency. A slight leaning-aside. The power widens the moment of arrival by a finger's width, so that a person who pushes through will feel, just briefly, that the green has been expecting them. This is the power's one intervention, and it is almost nothing: a courtesy to the accidental, a small gift to whoever comes next.

The third thing is the hardest, and the power does not do it lightly. It considers the Caretaker's look—the one that lasts a moment, when a leaf falls wrong or the light pools strangely, the look of something that was once a woman who wondered. The power could answer that look. It could send a bird with a strange song, or a wind that carries a smell from outside, or a dream that does not belong to the pocket. It could remind her, gently, that there is a world beyond the hedge, and that the world has not forgotten her. But the power has watched her for a very long time, and it knows what the pocket knows: that the look is not a wound. It is a valve. It opens and closes, and the opening is part of the rhythm. To answer it would be to jam the valve, to turn the wondering into a longing, to make the woman-who-was into a woman-who-waits. The pocket is not a prison. The pocket is a choice that has become a shape. The power will not unmake the choice. It will not make the shape a cage.

So instead, the power does something smaller and stranger. It waits for the moment when the Caretaker presses her ear to a trunk and hears the sap's hydraulic song, and it listens with her. For a single pulse of the Mother's swelling—one contraction, one relaxation—the power and the Caretaker are in the same listening. The power does not speak. It does not send a thought or a sign. It simply hears what she hears, at the same time she hears it, and in that simultaneity there is a kind of company that does not require presence, a fellowship of attention that asks for nothing and gives nothing but the fact of itself. The Caretaker does not know. But her fingers, which are never still, curl and uncurl once, slowly, as if testing the texture of the air, and they find it—briefly, faintly—a little warmer than before.

Then the power withdraws, and the pocket goes on being a pocket, running. The wheel creaks. The frogs call. The water talks. The hedge leans aside by a finger's width, and somewhere beyond it, a person who does not yet know they are looking for anything is walking, and the air is about to change.

And the power keeps its regard on the little green heart of the world, the way a gardener keeps regard on a volunteer sapling that has grown into something no one planted and no one tends and that is, therefore, entirely its own. It does not water it. It does not stake it. It watches it thrive, and it is glad, and it knows that gladness is not a thing you do to a world. It is a thing you feel from the edge of the bed, in the ordinary air, where the path does not stay a path.

That is all. That is enough. The pocket does not need more, and the Caretaker does not ask, and the power, being wise, knows that the best thing it can do for a small, self-running world is to let it run, and to be there—patient, unseen, undemanding—at the hedge, at the edge, at the place where the air changes, keeping the door ajar.
