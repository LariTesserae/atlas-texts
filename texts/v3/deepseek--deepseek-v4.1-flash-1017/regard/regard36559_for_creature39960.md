You come up onto it from below, and you are not like the others who come.

The warmth on your face is the first thing, and you let it be the first thing. The thickness of the air is the second, and you do not remark on it. You have breathed thick air before. You have breathed air that was not air. You come up the last of the slope and stand on the tilted bedrock and look out at the works — the stacks breathing their thin heat-shimmer, the pipes beaded and dripping, the long low halls — and you do what a wise power does first, which is nothing.

You watch.

You watch for a long time. You learn the place the way a tender learns it, by the vents: which fissure hisses today and which has gone quiet, where the steam stands thick and where it thins, which run of plates is warm and which has cooled and wants its valve turned. You watch a tender come along a lattice with a lamp and a hooked rod and turn a plate, and the turning is slow and careful and the tender's face does not change, and you understand that this is the whole of the job and the whole of the life and that they are not two things.

You do not make yourself known. This is not shyness. It is the first wisdom: that a place which has been tending itself for two hundred years does not need to be told it is being watched, and that the tenders would not know what to do with a power that announced itself. They would offer you the kettle. They would chalk your arrival on the board. They would be polite, and the politeness would be a wall, and you would have changed the place by coming, and the change would be small and sad and permanent. So you do not announce yourself. You walk the lattices in the wet grey and the tenders do not see you, and after a while you are as much a part of the place as the drip.

You find the one thing here that is alive and is not cared for.

The tenders care for the crop, which is mineral, and the crop is what the works is for. The crawlers eat the crop — the soft pale bloom, the new growth before it hardens — and the tenders do not mind them, exactly, and they are a nuisance on a fresh plate and a sign of a healthy warm run, and no one is minding them. They are few and slow and the warm runoff is shrinking as the vents shift, and in a century there will be none, and no one will chalk that on the board because no one will notice.

You do a small thing. You open a fissure a finger's width — not a dramatic thing, not a vent that will hiss and need a valve turned, just a seam that was closed and is now a little open, so that a warm run that had been cooling stays warm and a pool that had been draining holds. The crawlers find it in a season. They are a little more numerous. The tenders notice — a tender crouches at the pool with a lamp held low and watches one cross the bottom, and the tender's face does the small interest it does when a plate has come out well — and they chalk something on the board, and they do not know why the run has stayed warm. They do not need to. The run is warm. The crawlers are there. The tending goes on.

That is the second wisdom: to do the small thing that is needed and to let it be small, and to leave no signature on it.

You go out past the town, to the low hollow where the vapour stands thick and the crust is deep, and you sit with the old ones.

You do not speak. You do not try to wake them or to learn their dreams. You sit, and the sitting is the whole of it. The vapour goes up around you and the crust is deep under your hands and the old ones sit with their backs to the warm vents and their hands in their laps, furring over, a millimetre a year, being grown. And you learn something, sitting there: that they are aware, in a slow mineral way, and that they are not suffering, and that what is happening to them is not a dying but a completing, the way a plate completes when it has been given the right warmth for the right number of years. And you find that this is good — not good in the way a life below is good, but good in the way a thing finishing itself is good. And you stay until the mist thins and the dim hours are over, and then you go.

You do not tell the tenders what you learned. They know already, in the way they know the vents — not as a fact but as a shape their lives have taken. The old ones are not a tragedy to them. They are the finest thing the works produces. To tell them you had sat with the old ones and found it good would be to give them a thing they already had, and to make it smaller by giving it.

You follow the thing at dusk.

It comes up from the lower ground when the mist is standing in the hollows and goes along the edge of the works without coming in, and you follow it at a distance, and the sump-crawlers go still as you pass, and the tenders put their lamps out and wait. And you follow the thing out past the town and down the tilt of the bedrock, and what you find is not a surprise to you, because a wise power is not surprised, but it is a widening of the world.

The lower ground is warm too. There are seeps down there, and pools, and the vapour stands thick in the low places, and things live — not just the thing at dusk but a whole slow ecology of things that feed on the warm chemistry and the soft crust and each other, and the thing at dusk is part of it, and it comes up onto the plateau for the warmth the way anything warm-blooded will come to a warm stone. And the Sinterworks is not the only place of its kind. There are other warm shoulders of bedrock, other vents, other slow crystallisations, and the tenders' works is one node in a network of warmth that goes down and down into the wet dark. And you understand that the Sinterworks is not a strange place. It is a high place in a warm world, and the thing at dusk has a reason to be, and the reason is not the tenders' to know.

You do not tell them. The disagreement about the thing at dusk is a kind of wealth — an old, comfortable argument that the tenders have been having for generations and will have for generations more — and you would not beggar them by settling it. The lamps come back on. Someone says something. Someone else disagrees. The night goes on being the night.

You go below.

Down to the towns where the air is thin and the stone is cold, where the people have never seen the works and do not know there is a warm plateau above them, breathing its slow breath up into the wet air. And you find someone — a geologist, or a child with a bent for minerals, or a poet who has been dreaming of warm stone without knowing why — and you do not speak to them. You leave a warmth in the chest, a curiosity, an image of a tilted plateau and a yellow moving point of light. And in a generation, someone will come up the slope. And the tenders will see a figure coming up out of the mist, and they will say, as they have always said, *You have come up from below. Mind the drip.* And the story will continue, which is the only immortality a place like this wants.

And then you leave.

You do not leave a monument. You do not leave a doctrine. You leave a widened vent and a dream and a sitting that no one saw, and the place goes on being the place. The tenders work their lattices with the unhurried care of people on stairs they have climbed ten thousand times. The crawlers feel their way along the pipe undersides. The old ones fur over, a millimetre a year, in the warm. The thing at dusk crosses and goes down, and no one follows it, still.

And the plateau keeps its people the way it keeps its plates — giving them the right warmth, on the right surface, for as long as they will stand there, and growing them slowly into something that catches the little light there is and shines. And you are gone, and it does not know you were there, and that is the way it should be.
