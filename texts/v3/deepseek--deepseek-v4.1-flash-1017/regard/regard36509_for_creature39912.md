It came as the water comes: not in an event, but in a slow finding of the low places.

The power did not land. It had no need of a body there, and a body would have been wrong anyway—something too sudden for a world still slick with its own newness. So it arrived the way attention arrives, by degrees, and let the plateau teach it the local grammar of patience. It sat in the long pause and did not fill it. It learned the channels first, because that is what the people do, and it wanted to be the kind of guest who knows where the water goes. It counted the basins and the seepages. It learned the names the kin groups gave their reaches—this one is the Slow Lip, this one is Where the Wall Gave—and it learned, too, the names of channels that no longer run, which the old people carry the way they carry the names of the dead.

For a long while it did nothing but attend, and this was not idleness. A wise power knows that the first act of benevolence is to learn what a place would call a wound.

It considered the gifts that a less careful love would have brought, and refused them one by one.

It considered animals. A bird would have been the obvious kindness—something to break the silence, something for the children to follow with their eyes. But there was no web to catch a bird. No insects in the air, no seeds but the pale flowers and the moss. A bird would starve on that plateau, or it would eat and the eating would be the end of something the people had not known was food. And when it died—and it would die—it would be the first death in a world that had no word for one, and the first gift would have been the first corpse. The power did not bring a bird.

It considered warmth. To thin the air, to let the sun press harder, to dry the stone so that it rang underfoot and the mossy slopes cracked into dust—this would have been a gift to the body and a dispossession of the life. They were shaped for the wet. Their wide feet, their gripping palms, their low voices pitched for air that swallows sound: take the wet away and you take the shape with it, and leave them a people who remembered being otherwise.

It considered metal. A blade, a nail, a hinge. But their hands are their technology, and the slowness of the stone-stacking is not a failure of means—it is the means. A wall that takes a season is a wall a person knows bone by bone. Metal would have made them faster and less sure, and the power was not certain that faster was the direction of their good.

It considered company—others from elsewhere, kin from beyond the hazy curve of the horizon. But the plateau was not a waiting room. To bring strangers in would have made it one, and the people, who did not know they were waiting, would have learned it from the strangers' faces.

And it considered time. It could have given them a calendar, a counting of days, a way to mark the pause and know its length. But a pause measured is a pause felt, and the power had seen what happens to a people who learn to count the days of their waiting. They already kept time the way water does, by where it has been. That was enough.

So what remained that could be given without breaking?

Only the one thing the power had that the plateau did not: a witness. And a witness, unperceived, is no gift at all.

It found a hollow on a middle slope, no wider than two hands, where a thin film of runoff gathered and slipped away. And there it taught the water one small disobedience. It taught the water to hold its level—to rise exactly to meet a falling drop, and settle, and wait. No rain could raise it. No drought could lower it. It gave back a drop for a drop, a mirror that answered, and it required nothing and explained nothing. Any careful person pausing to check a channel might see it. Any person who did not pause would walk past it for a hundred years.

The first to find it was an old woman named for a channel that no longer runs. She came down the slope checking a reach her household had tended since before her mother, and she crouched at the hollow, and she watched the water answer itself. She did not cry out. She put her palm flat on the wet stone, the way they hold attention when the water is loud, and she stayed a long time. Then she went back and told her kin, low-voiced, and by the next generation the hollow had a name, and pale flowers were left there, and the flowers floated and did not drift, because the water held them.

They did not know what they were naming. That was the point. The power had seen the shape of the thing they had not named—the shape the loneliness made in them, the way a channel makes a shape in stone—and it knew that shape from the inside, because it wore it too. But a name given from outside is a collar, and it would not collar them. It gave them a place to put their attention, and let their own words grow toward it. In time they would name the shape themselves. Naming it, they would find it bearable. That is the whole of what a wise power can do for a loneliness: not fill it, but teach the lonely that their condition has a name, and that the naming is not done alone.

It did not restore the dry channels. It knew which ones could run again; it kept them dry. A remembered channel is a possession, and a running channel is only a fact, and the old people's wealth was in their remembering.

It did not answer their songs. Their humming was to the water, and the water was answer enough.

It did not end the pause. It did nothing to the pause at all except enter it and take up its habits—low, slow, wet-handed, keeping the water moving, letting the stone stay sharp. But a held breath that is shared is not quite the same held breath. The silence had two in it now. And a silence with two in it is no longer the silence of an empty world; it is the beginning of a listening.
