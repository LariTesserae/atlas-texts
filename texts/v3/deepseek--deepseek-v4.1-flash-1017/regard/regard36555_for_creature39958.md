It would arrive as a change in pressure.

Not a landing. Not a light in the canopy. It would fold itself down to something the wood could carry — a low presence, a patient attention — and it would listen for a long time before it did anything at all. Long enough to learn the difference between the forest’s groans and its breathing. Long enough to hear the Slow Ones’ hum and the after-hum of the colony still living in a dead body. Long enough to know that this place is young and raw and aware, and that awareness is not the same as invitation.

Then it would do three things, and refrain from a thousand.

First, it would make a boundary — not a wall, not a throne, not a temple. A membrane of permission at the edge of the place. It would say, in the grammar of weather and root and pressure: *this world is not a resource. It may be entered, but it may not be stripped; it may be studied, but it may not be converted; it may be loved, but it may not be owned.* It would not stop the falling branch. It would not prevent the slow death of a Slow One. It would not smooth the sharp edges of a place still figuring out what it wants to be. It would only turn away the extinction-scale thing: the fleet with extractors, the power that mistakes benevolence for homogeny, the hunger that sees a living lattice and thinks *fuel.*

Second, it would answer the hum.

Not with words. Words would be a kind of violence here — a new thing forced into a body that already knows how to feel the world through its face. It would answer with a note beneath the note, so low it could be mistaken for the forest settling under its own weight. It would not synchronize unless the Slow Ones’ chord opened a place for it. It would not make itself known as a god. It would simply add one harmonic to the dark — a companionship that says *I am here, I am listening, I am not asking anything of you* — and then it would wait to see whether the hum changed.

Third, it would attend the dead.

It would go to the old mounds where the pale growth has taken the body back into the substrate, and it would hum there too — not to resurrect, not to mourn in a way the Slow Ones could not understand, but because the colony is still vibrating and the place is still alive in its strange, slow way. It would guard those mounds from outside hands, not from decay. It would let the dead be part of the living. It would let the Slow Ones visit and hang and feel whatever it is they feel when they feel an absence that is also a presence.

And then it would refrain.

It would not brighten the dark. The dark is not a problem to be solved; it is the condition of the place, and the things that live here have grown strange and beautiful in it.

It would not give the Slow Ones eyes. It would not make them quicker, or cleverer, or aware of history, or capable of building. It would not teach them language and then call that kindness. Their hum is not a failed language. It is a successful way of being.

It would not end predation, or decay, or the cold. It would not make the forest safe in the way a cage is safe. It would not confuse a growing pain with a wound.

It would not repair the sagging rope bridge. It would not restore the old fire pit. It would not turn the human traces into shrines or erase them. They are part of the place now — small marks of people who were small in a large world and tried anyway. Let them weather.

It would not demand worship, or build a door, or leave an artifact. If it left anything, it would be a frequency: a note in the wood, a pattern in the mist, a way the rain sometimes falls in a rhythm that almost makes sense. Something that could be ignored. Something that could be mistaken for the forest itself.

And if the forest’s own awareness reached toward it — if the place that is alive and watching decided, in its slow non-human way, to ask *what are you?* — the power would answer with the smallest possible yes. It would not explain itself. It would not claim to be wise. It would sit at the edge of the world and hum back.

If a Slow One died, it would attend the mound. If a branch fell, it would let it fall. If someone came with fire and axes, it would make the path confusing, the weather difficult, the instruments unreliable — not with wrath, but with the polite, absolute refusal of a host who will not allow a guest to burn the house down. If someone came with open hands and patience, it would let them stay. It would watch. It would learn what they did with the hum.

And if you stood on that branch, cold and wet and very small, and listened — you would not hear it. You would only notice that the Slow Ones’ chord had grown a little deeper, and that the dark no longer felt empty, and that this was enough.
