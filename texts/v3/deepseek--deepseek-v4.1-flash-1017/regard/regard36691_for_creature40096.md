# The Careful Hand

The power comes the way weather comes—without announcement, without demand. It settles over the Breathing Stair like a change in atmospheric pressure, like the moment before rain when the air goes heavy and the insects drop a half-tone in their singing.

It is not a god. It does not wish to be. It is something older and more patient than that: a benevolence that has learned, across incomprehensible spans, that the first duty of wisdom is to *not interfere*—and the second is to know when the first duty must be broken.

---

## First: It Listens

The power does not arrive with trumpets. It arrives with attention.

It settles into the moss—not displacing it, not crushing it, but *with* it, the way fog settles into a valley, taking the shape of what it touches. It feels the slow see-saw of the bryophyte under its own weight (it has no weight; it borrows the weight of the wind). It feels the water being held and given back, the long patient accounting of the sponge.

It listens to the beetles.

The hum is not noise to the power. It is *speech*—not language, not exactly, but the metabolic argument of a million small bodies making their case to the future: *we are here, we are eating, we are being eaten, we are continuing.* The power has heard this argument before, on other mountains, in other ruins, and it has learned to respect it. The beetles are not asking for anything. They are simply *being*, at volume, and the volume is the point.

It listens to the frogs, and understands that their chorus is not a request but a *claim*: this pool is mine, this moment is mine, this wet-slate body is mine, and I will make myself known. The power has heard this claim before too. It is the oldest claim there is.

It listens to the simurgh—that enormous silence crossing the third terrace—and understands that silence is also a claim, and perhaps a wiser one.

It listens to the stone. This takes longest. Stone speaks at a rate that makes glaciers seem hasty, and the power must slow itself to hear it. But it does. It hears the low drum-note of the mountain's own settling, the ten-thousand-year conversation between the terraces and the wind, the slow *yes* the stone has been saying to the lichen for longer than the lichen has had a name.

And it listens to the marks the people left: the wall that stops, the turf-roofed pit, the bare hearth-stone. It hears what is not there—the hands, the fire, the voices—and it hears what *is* there now: the shrews in the wall-gaps, the beetles warming on the hearth. The absence has become a presence. The people are a stratum now, and the stratum is inhabited.

The power listens for a long time. Listening is most of what it does.

---

## Second: It Learns What Is Needed

The power has a habit—a discipline, really—of asking one question before it acts: *What is already being done well?*

The Breathing Stair is being done well.

The moss holds water. The frogs do not hurry. The beetles sing. The simurgh hunts in silence. The wall provides shelter. The hearth warms small bodies. The stone is patient. The stair is not on its way anywhere, and that is not a failure—it is a *completion*. It has arrived at what it is.

The power understands this. It has seen many places that were not yet themselves, places that needed shaping or healing or time. The Breathing Stair does not need these things. It needs only what it already has: continuation.

But the power also sees the edges—the places where the stair's continuation is *threatened*, where the slow breathing might become a gasp.

It sees the pools shrinking faster than they used to. It sees the insects thinning at the terrace edges where the wind has grown harsher. It sees the simurgh's shadow crossing less often than it should. It sees the moss drying at the margins, the lichen retreating, the long patient accounting going wrong in small ways that will become large ways if nothing is done.

It sees, too, the marks of the people—and understands that the people are not coming back. The stratum is closed. The stair is not waiting for them. This is not a grief; it is a fact, and the power respects facts.

So the power decides what it will do. It will do almost nothing.

Almost.

---

## Third: It Acts—Smallly

The power does not reshape the terraces. It does not summon rain. It does not build walls or dig wells or teach the frogs to hurry. It does not *fix* anything, because nothing is broken. The stair is not a problem to be solved. It is a place to be *tended*.

So the power tends.

It moves the wind a little. Just a little—a fraction of a degree in its prevailing direction, so that the storms come a day later than they would have, so that the pools fill a centimeter deeper, so that the moss at the margins holds its water a day longer. The beetles do not notice. The frogs do not notice. The stone does not notice. But the accounting improves.

It slows the drying. Not stops it—the stair has always dried and filled, dried and filled; this is its breath—but it makes the dry breath a little shallower, the wet breath a little deeper. It gives the simurgh a slightly easier hunt, not by making the prey slower, but by making the wind a little kinder, the thermals a little more generous. It gives the beetles a slightly longer season, not by changing the sun, but by softening the edge of a cold front that would have killed too many.

It does not touch the marks of the people. Those are not its to touch. They belong to the stratum now—to the shrews, the beetles, the birds that crack snails on the hearth. The power only makes sure that the hearth-stone stays bare, that the wind does not bury it, that the moss does not quite cover it. It does not know why this matters. It only knows that it does.

And once—just once—it does something larger. It notices that the simurgh's shadow has not crossed the third terrace in three days, and it goes looking. It finds the great bird on the root-matted riser between terraces, caught in a snarl of old roots, exhausted, injured. The power does not heal it. That is not what the power does. But it *eases*—it loosens the roots a fraction, it calms the wind so the bird can rest, it makes the night a little warmer than it would have been. And then it waits.

The simurgh recovers. The simurgh flies. The shadow crosses the third terrace again at midday, silent as ever.

The power does not take credit. It does not need to. It only notes that the silence has returned, and that the silence is part of the place, and that the place is a little more itself than it was.

---

## Fourth: It Leaves a Mark (Invisible)

The power does not build. It does not carve. It does not leave a sign for the people who are not coming back.

But it does leave something—a *trace*, a subtle arrangement in the way the wind moves now, the way the water holds, the way the roots grow. A pattern that is not quite natural but not quite artificial. A kind of gentle *blessing* on the place.

In ten thousand years, if there are people again, they might notice that the moss is a little thicker here than it should be, that the pools are a little deeper, that the frogs are a little more numerous, that the simurgh's shadow crosses a little more often. They might wonder why. They might build theories. They might build shrines.

The power will not care. It will be elsewhere by then, tending other stairs, listening to other hums. But the Breathing Stair will remember—not in a way that can be spoken, but in the way that a place remembers: in its own continuation, in the slow breath that goes on and on, in the biology that is home.

---

## Fifth: It Waits

And then the power does the hardest thing, the thing that wisdom always must do: it *waits*.

It waits to see if the stair will continue on its own. It waits to see if the small adjustments were enough. It waits to see if the breathing will deepen or shallow, if the hum will grow or fade, if the silence of the simurgh will remain or vanish.

It waits for a long time. Waiting is most of what it does.

And in the waiting, it learns something it did not know: that the Breathing Stair is not a place that needs a power. It is a place that needed a *witness*. It needed someone to see it, to hear it, to understand that it is complete—not perfect, not finished, but *complete*—and to let it be.

The power is that witness.

And when it finally leaves—drifting away like fog, like the memory of a hand on a stone—it leaves behind nothing that can be seen. Only a slight easing in the wind. Only a slightly deeper pool. Only a simurgh that flies a little more easily, a beetle that sings a little longer, a frog that calls a little louder into the dusk.

The Breathing Stair goes on breathing.

And somewhere, on another mountain, in another ruin, the power is listening again.
