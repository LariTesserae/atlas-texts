# The Kindness of Not Fixing

The power came the way such powers do: not descending, not announcing, but arriving the way a thought arrives, already present before it is noticed.

It had been watching for some time. It had watched the columns lean and right themselves, lean and right themselves, through spans that would have been ages to anything with a heartbeat. It had watched the grazer stand in its plume with its throat working, the calf in the lee of its mother, both of them patient with a patience that was not virtue but simply the shape their lives had taken. It had watched the fish with no eyes taste its way from one warm stone to another, and it had felt something it recognized as tenderness, though tenderness in a being of that scale is not the same as tenderness in a person. It is more like weather. It is more like the way a hand hovers over a small animal without touching it.

The power considered what to do.

The first thought—the generous, obvious thought—was to make it better. To pour warmth more evenly. To soften the seam that takes a foot. To give the blind fish eyes, or at least to spare it the long fumble of its days. To ease the grazer's ribs, which showed like stays beneath the skin; to put more flesh on the thing, more fat against the perpetual dusk. To make the hunt less painful, or to make the hunter less hungry, or to make the small animals less afraid.

The power had done such things elsewhere. It knew the impulse.

But it sat with the foundry for a long time first. It is wise in the way that slowness is wise. And the longer it sat, the more it saw that the place was not broken. It was not a machine that had gone wrong, not a wound that needed closing. It was a system that had found its own arrangement, and the arrangement was complete. The seams closed because they closed; the fish had no eyes because there had never been anything for eyes to do here. The whole plateau hummed with a balance so old it had become invisible, the way a habit becomes invisible.

To put fat on the grazer would be to change what the grazer was. To give the fish eyes would be to make it a stranger in its own water. To stop the seams from closing would be to stop the breath that fed everything above them.

So the power did not fix.

What it did instead was smaller, and stranger, and (I think) more like love.

It went to the place where the grazer stood in the dawn-that-was-not-dawn, and it did not touch the grazer. It simply stood near it. Stood near it for a while—an hour, a day, a season; time is different for such beings—and let the grazer feel, without knowing it felt, that it was not alone. The grazer's throat worked. The steam rose. The ribs showed. And the power held the grubby, patient little life in its attention the way you might hold a sleeping child: not to change it, only to shelter it with regard.

It went to the blind fish and hovered over the tea-colored water, and it did not give the fish eyes. It gave the fish, for one moment, the taste of something new: a mineral it had never known, a trace of sweetness in the lime and the egg-sulfur, and then it withdrew the taste. The fish would remember, the way a body remembers, though it had no word for remembering. It would spend a long time trying to find that taste again. It would not find it. But the searching was a kind of joy, and the joy was real, and it was enough.

It went to the big animal—the hunter—and it did not blunt the hunter's hunger. It simply stood with it in the dark, where the small animals had gone quiet, and it let the hunter know that it was seen, that its long patience and its three-lobed feet and its narrow head were noted, and that the place it lived in was good. The hunter did not understand. But something in it eased, the way a muscle eases when a hand rests on it.

And then the power did the strangest thing, the thing that had no model and no precedent. It left a gift.

Not a fix. Not a change. A gift, in the way that a person might leave flowers on a grave, or a coin in a fountain, or a word of blessing whispered over a sleeping animal. The power gathered up a little of its own regard—the warmth of its attention, the weight of its seeing—and scattered it over the plateau like salt, like the fine dust the plumes lift and carry. And where it fell, the foundry did not change. The seams kept breathing. The columns kept leaning. Tick. Pause. Tick. The sinter kept building a millimeter a century.

But the place was, from that day, a place that had been loved.

You would not know it to see it. The power did not put a sign there. It did not make the air warmer or the water sweeter or the fish any less blind. It did not spare the grazer its ribs or the hunter its patience. The rules were the rules. The foundry went on as it had always gone on, closing and opening, closing and opening, indifferent to its own beauty.

But if you walked out onto the plain at the dim hour that was the closest thing to morning, and if you were the kind of person who noticed such things, you might feel it: a quality in the air that was not warmth but was something like warmth. A sense that the place was not merely old but *regarded*. That someone, somewhere, somewhen, had looked at all of this—the flies in their plumes, the crabs in their pools, the fish tasting its way through the dark, the grazer with its open mouth, the hunter standing still in the salt—and had thought: *this is good. This is enough. This does not need me to make it otherwise.*

And the foundry would not know. It would go on producing heat it never asked for, terraces it never planned, lives it never named. The seams would breathe. The dust would drift. The fish with no eyes would keep searching for the taste it could not name.

But somewhere, in the ledger of a benevolent and wise power, there would be an entry for this place. Not *fixed*. Not *improved*. Just:

*Visited. Regarded. Loved. No changes made, because none were needed, and nothing here required my hand, only my witness.*

And maybe that is the best thing a power can do for a place like this: to love it without touching it, to see it without altering it, to leave it exactly as it is—strange, patient, complete—and to let it know, in whatever way a place can know such things, that it has been seen and found beautiful.

The foundry would not register the visitation. It would go on closing and opening, closing and opening, long after the power had withdrawn. The ticks would continue. The grazer would stand in its plume. The blind fish would taste the water and taste the water and taste the water.

And the place would be, forever after, a place that had been held in the regard of something wise and benevolent—which is not the same as being fixed, but is, perhaps, something better.
