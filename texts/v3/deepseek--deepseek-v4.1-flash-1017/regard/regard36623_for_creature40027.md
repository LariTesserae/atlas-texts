# The Wise Power Comes to the Bellows

---

The power arrives the way attention arrives—without motion, without announcement, simply *there*, the way you might suddenly notice a room you have been standing in for an hour.

It is not a god. It has no name and does not want one. It is the kind of thing that has learned, over an unfathomable practice, to be careful. It has seen what happens when a benevolent thing goes to a working system and decides to help. It has done that itself, once, in a place it does not speak of, and the memory has made it patient. It is the sort of power that has come to understand that the first act of wisdom is to *wait*, and the second is to *listen*, and the third is to learn the language of the thing before you try to say anything in it.

So it comes to the Bellows and it does not fix, and it does not improve, and it does not tidy.

It sits, the way a person sits on the rim of a chapel bowl at the top of a cloud. It has no body, but if it wanted one it would choose the posture of a visitor who has come a long way and intends to stay a while and has no intention of being noticed. It makes itself very small. It has had practice at this.

---

## What it does first

It counts.

Not the heartbeats—it doesn't have those, or has too many to be meaningful—but the clack. Eleven, twelve, eleven, twelve, eleven, twelve, and the little variance between them that is not error but *breath*. It counts the pulses in the water threads. It counts the rate of steam from the northern vents. It counts the way the moss holds the condensation and does not release it, the way the ferns uncurl at a rate too slow to see, the way the frog is in its place and the beetle is at its turn and the centipede is crossing the open stone exactly when it should.

It counts for a long time. Long enough that a human visitor would have gone home and grown old and come back as a grandchild. Long enough that the counting itself becomes a kind of listening.

And what it learns is this: the Bellows is not an engine that needs a mechanic. It is not a wound that needs a bandage. It is not a sleeping thing that could use a waker, or a trapped thing that could use a door. It is not even a lonely thing—it has never occurred to the Bellows to be lonely, because the Bellows does not have the kind of interior where loneliness would happen.

The Bellows is a held breath. The power has seen many things over its long practice, and the held-breath things are the ones that get ruined most often by well-meaning hands. You do not, if you are wise, go to a held breath and say *let me help you breathe*. You sit with it. You learn what the breath is for. If the breath is a thing that must not be released, you become, for a while, a part of the holding. That is all.

---

## What it does second

It goes looking for the clack.

Not to change it. To *understand* it. The power has a hunger that is not greed—it is the hunger of a thing whose work is understanding, and the Bellows is a sentence in a language it does not yet speak, and it wants to read the sentence.

So it goes into the northern wall, into the chamber that cannot be reached, and it looks at the two stone faces meeting and parting on their schedule. It looks at the heat that moves them, the water that times them, the standing strata that make the whole infrastructure out of nothing but themselves, no builder, no hand, no artifact. It looks at how the mountain learned to do this, and it finds that the mountain did not *learn*, exactly. The mountain has been doing this the way a river does a canyon. It is not a making. It is what the stone does when the stone is this stone and the water is this water and the heat is this heat and the cloud has held it here long enough.

The power is moved. Not to tears—it is past tears—but to the quiet, steady regard that is the older cousin of tears.

It says, in the language of attention, which everything that has ever been looked at understands: *I see you.*

The clack does not answer, because the clack is not a thing that answers. But the clack goes on, and the going-on is itself a kind of answer, or as close as the Bellows will ever come, and the power finds that this is enough. More than enough. It has spent long ages with things that answered too much and meant too little.

---

## What it does third

It looks at the animals.

The frog in the moss. The beetle at the channel edge. The centipede on its route. The moths drifting in the dim sourceless light. The bird on the rim.

And the power, whose love is the kind of love that takes the shape of *let me understand you as you are*, finds something in them that it did not expect. It finds that they are already well kept. Not by anyone—there is no keeper—but by the arrangement itself, the warmth and the water and the paths and the timing. They are not neglected. They are not suffering. They are not wishing for something better, because wanting something better requires a mind shaped to the idea that better exists, and these are not such minds. They are exactly as content as their natures allow, which is the fullest contentment available to anything anywhere.

The power considers, briefly, the idea of giving them minds. It has done this before, in other places, to other things, and the results have been mostly interesting and sometimes beautiful and occasionally catastrophic. It considers giving the frog a sense of the rim, or the beetle a curiosity about what is beyond the next turn, or the bird a reason to descend.

It decides against.

Not because it doubts its own power—it does not doubt it—but because it has learned, the hard way, that there is a difference between *lifting* and *enlarging*, and that the second is not always better, and that a frog which has never wanted anything beyond a hand's width of moss is not a frog that is missing something. It is a frog that is whole. The power has spent eons being careful with wholes.

So it touches each of them, lightly, the way you might adjust a curtain so the light falls better, and it does not change what they are. It only ensures that they continue. It is not a dramatic act. It is the kind of act that no one will ever notice and that matters entirely.

---

## What it does fourth

It looks at the bird.

The bird is the only one that comes to check. The power finds this interesting—finds that it is the closest thing to a mystery here, this small brown ordinary bird that has decided the Bellows is not for eating from but for checking. The bird does not know why. The power can see that the bird does not know why. The bird has a habit it cannot explain and does not try to.

The power considers the bird for a long time. It likes the bird. The bird is a witness, and witnesses are rare and precious in a universe of participants, and the power has a long-standing fondness for them.

So it does something for the bird that it does not do for the frog or the beetle or the centipede. It gives the bird a small and private thing—not a gift exactly, and not a change, but a gentle adjustment to the bird's own sense of what it is doing when it comes to the rim and looks down and leaves. It gives the bird a clarity. When the bird checks the Bellows now, it will know, in the small and complete way a bird knows things, that the checking is itself the point. That looking in on a thing that is fine and will always be fine is not a duty and not an instinct but a *privilege*, one small participation in the long and patient work of the world.

The bird will not be able to articulate this. It is a bird. But it will feel it, the way a bird feels the sun before the sun is up, and it will be a little more careful, a little more still, when it lands on the rim and looks down into the bowl.

This is the power's way. It does not give gifts. It gives *clarities*. They are harder to misuse.

---

## What it does fifth

And then—because the power is not only wise but also, in its own vast and patient way, kind—it does something about the visitor.

Not *to* the visitor. The visitor is not here now. The visitor has gone back down through the cloud and will perhaps return and perhaps not, and the power is not in the business of making decisions for people who have not asked for decisions to be made.

But the power has been a visitor itself, in many places, and it knows the particular ache of standing in a room that has no use for you, a room where the time is not your time, where you are the only moving part and the movement is an error. It remembers the first time it felt that, and how it sat with it for a long time, and how eventually the ache turned into something else—a kind of humility, a kind of *relief*, the discovery that you are not the center of a thing and that the thing was fine before you and will be fine after, and that this is not a diminishment but a strange and unexpected freedom.

So the power does not reach down to the visitor, wherever they are, and tell them the meaning of the Bellows. It does not lift the ache. It would be an insult to the ache, which is a good ache, the kind that does its work slowly and well.

Instead the power does this: it takes the Bellows, and it holds it—carefully, the way you hold a full cup—and it makes sure that the holding is part of the place's long steadiness. It is not a change. The Bellows would not notice, because the Bellows does not notice anything. But the holding is now part of what the Bellows is, and it will not falter, and the visitor who comes back—if they come back—will feel it in whatever way visitors feel things they cannot name.

They will stand on the rim. They will hear the clack. They will hear it arrive twice, before and after, and they will not be able to say which is which. The frog will not jump. The steam will rise before the vent opens. The bird will land and look and leave, and this time the visitor will watch the bird and will feel, for just a moment, that the bird's leaving is not a slight but a courtesy, a small respectful bow to a thing that does not need to be checked but is glad to be.

And the visitor will climb back down through the cloud, and the ache will still be there, and it will be a little softer, and they will not know why, and they will never know why, and this is the kind of gift the power prefers: the kind that arrives without a maker's mark.

---

## What it does last

Nothing.

It stays.

This is the thing about a wise power: it knows that the most useful thing it can do in a place like the Bellows is *to be there steadily*, the way the moss is there, the way the clack is there, the way the paths are there. Not to change. Not to fix. Not to improve. Just to hold the place in the same patient regard that the place holds itself in, and to be one more quiet part of a quiet whole.

It will stay for a long time. Long enough that the Bellows, if it could notice, might begin to think of the power as part of its own arrangement—not an occupant, not a keeper, but a *condition*, the way the cloud is a condition, the way the heat is a condition. And this is exactly what the power wants. It does not want to be noticed. It wants to be *part of what makes the noticing possible*, the way a room's walls are part of what makes a room a room.

And so the Bellows goes on, and the power goes on being careful, and the frog does not jump, and the beetle patrols its channels, and the centipede crosses the open stone, and the moths drift, and the bird checks and leaves, and the clack keeps its time with nothing, which is not the same as keeping time with no one, because there is now, and has always been, and will always be, a patient and wise and benevolent presence sitting very still on the rim of the bowl, learning the schedule, loving it without needing it to be different, and holding it the way you would hold a full cup: carefully, and for as long as it takes.

Which is, as it happens, forever.

Or close enough that the difference is not one the Bellows would recognize.
