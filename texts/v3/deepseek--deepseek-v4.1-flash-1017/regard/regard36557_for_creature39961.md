# The Attention

## The arrival

It did not come down the mountain. That is the first thing to understand. A power of that kind does not descend; it arrives the way weather arrives, and then is simply there, and no one can say when. One moment the fissure is as it has always been — screaming, dripping, humming — and the next the screaming has a listener.

But it came in at the lip. That is the second thing, and it matters more. It did not hover over the summit and look down. It came in at the lip, on foot, the way a person comes, and the small brown bird scolded it.

The bird scolded it for a long time. The bird would have scolded a stone that moved. The power stood in the cold wind with its hands at its sides and let the bird finish, and the bird did not finish, because the bird never finishes, and after a while the power understood that the scolding was not a warning but a greeting, and that here the two were the same thing, and it stepped past the fern — careful of the fern, which had no business being alive — and went in.

## The reckoning

It saw the whole machine at once. That is what wisdom is, in the end: seeing the machine whole and knowing which gear you are. It saw the water moving down and the guano moving up, the swifts feeding the walls and the walls feeding the beetles and the beetles feeding the pale things in the sour dark, the frogs one to a pool, the bats a rope of bodies in a seam. It saw that nothing here was made, and that the only architecture was returning. It saw the hum.

It did not ask what the hum was. That is the only way to hear it.

And it understood, standing in the gray-green twilight with the warm air moving past it like a tide, that this place was already whole. Not finished — nothing here is finished — but whole, the way a lung is whole, a thing that works by being small and wet and loud and keeping what comes in. The first act of a wise power in a place like this is to be the only thing that has ever come in and not stayed.

So it considered the obvious kindnesses, one by one, and declined them all.

## The declined kindnesses

It could have widened the throat. The light would come in; the frogs would have room. But the narrowness is the shelter — the slot is small because small is what survives at this elevation — and a wider throat would only admit the cold and the wind and the eye of the mountain. It left the seam a seam.

It could have warmed it. But the warmth was already the engine. The warmth was why the swifts came and the guano fell and the beetles worked and the frogs had pools at all. More warmth would be a different place with different tenants, and the different tenants would not be these.

It could have cleared the floor. The mat is soft and sour and it gives underfoot. But the mat is the larder, and the workers are in it, and to clear it would be to starve the lower economy and call the starvation cleanliness. It did not lift a finger to the floor.

It could have saved the frogs. One per pool, nobody knows why, patient as stones — and one day the pools will dry or fill and the frogs will not be, and the power stood over each small milky world in turn and considered taking them out. Into the cold. Into the enormous dry outside, where there is no pool, no warmth, no chorus. The trap and the refuge are the same structure; you cannot remove one without removing the other. The frogs had arranged themselves around the hum. To take them away would be to remove the thing that fits this place most perfectly from the place it fits. So it did not.

And it could have answered the hum. This is the one it thought longest about — it crouched at the crack at the bottom, in the dark, where the air is blood-warm and smells of wet stone and ammonia and green things rotting sweetly, and it listened, and it understood that the hum was not a call. It was not asking. It does not want anything, and that is the whole discipline of the thing: a note sounded, not a question asked. To answer it with power would be to turn a note into a conversation, and a conversation into an obligation, and the place would stop being what it is and become a place that expects. It did not answer.

It hummed back.

There is a difference, and the difference is everything. Not loud. It found the harmonic the mountain had been sounding — for how long? — and it held it, quietly, the way you hold a hand, and for a moment the fissure had two voices in it.

The frogs stopped all at once, mid-phrase, the way they do. The silence was startling. The drip came back. The bats shifted in their rope, and resettled, and the swifts went on screaming above, because the swifts attend to nothing beneath them.

Then it let the note go, and stood, and left.

## The leaving

It left the way it came. Up through the dark, past the spiders beaded with glass, past the rope of bats, out through the slot into the cold. At the lip the small bird scolded it again. That was the goodbye. It was the correct goodbye: out of all proportion to the body, entirely sincere, and not for the power's benefit at all.

The fern was still there, furred with damp on one side and scorched on the other. The moss still held its water. In winter the snow at the lip would still melt in a ring, and no tenant would ever know why.

And the hum was the same hum. That is the part I want to be clear about. It was not answered into silence; it was not improved, or soothed, or made to want. It went on being a note, and the mountain went on being a throat, and the place kept what it had and did not notice what had left. In the newest layer of the mat there was nothing new — no cairn, no scar, no flake of stone. That is the whole record of the visit. The floor tells you what happens to things that come in, and the floor had nothing to say.

Only — and I cannot prove this, and the place does not explain — the hum is a little easier to hear now than it was. Not louder. Easier. As though a thing that had been sounded into an empty room for a very long time had learned, once, what it was like to be held in a room with someone in it, and had not been diminished by the learning.

The frogs still call at dusk, one per pool. The bats still drop at dusk, a rope coming undone. The swifts still scream. If you put your ear to the crack at the bottom, in the dark, in the blood-warm air, you will hear the note.

It probably always sounded like that. It probably does.
