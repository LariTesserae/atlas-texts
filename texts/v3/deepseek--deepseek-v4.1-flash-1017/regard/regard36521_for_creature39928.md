# What the Wise Power Would Do

The power would come quietly, because that is the only way to come to a place like this.

---

## First, it would learn the grammar of the place.

A benevolent power does not arrive with plans. It arrives with attention. It would sit on the rim for a long time — a season, a year, a decade, what does time matter to such a being — and it would watch the springs breathe. It would learn that the water is the bell. It would learn that the ground is a rumor and the crust is a courtesy. It would learn the fine sift of shrimp against water, which is the loudest thing in the basin and quieter than breathing.

It would count the filled hinge-holes in the doorjambs, not as a surveyor, but as a reader reads a diary. It would notice that no one here mourns the wall that was true last decade. It would understand, after a while, that the people are not suffering and not waiting. They are maintaining. They are inside the process, not against it.

And it would ask itself the question that any wise power must ask before it acts: *What is already working here?*

---

## Second, it would recognize that the place has already been tended.

There is a temptation, when you see something fragile and beautiful, to save it. But this place has not been left untended. The wall-dressers shave the growing stone back to true. The rakers read the crust with their thin boots. The pump tenders paint the rust every year and know the springs breathe twice a day, roughly, and run the machinery against the breathing. The children learn the hollow ring before they learn words.

The people are not victims of the basin. They are its maintenance crew. They have been holding the balance for generations — a hand's width at a time, a hinge-hole at a time, a repainted pump at a time.

A benevolent and wise power would recognize this and be humbled by it. It would see that the wisdom here is not in books or in councils. It is in the way a raker knows when the pans will stand, in the way a wall-dresser reads a jamb, in the way a pump tender's knuckles are rust-stained and his voice is soft.

And it would understand that the greatest gift it could give is not intervention. It is recognition.

---

## Third, it would do the smallest necessary things.

But it would do some things. Not because the place is broken, but because even a well-tended place can bear a small, kind hand.

It would, perhaps, stand at the fence during the harvest weeks, beside the birds, and watch the pink rows being raked. It would say nothing. But it would let itself be seen, and in being seen, it would remind the rakers that their patience is holy work.

It would, perhaps, walk the streets in the early morning when everything drips — every rail, every rope, every bird's back beaded — and it would collect the condensation in its palm and taste it, and say: *This is clean. This is good water.* And it would find the one well in town that had gone brackish through no fault of anyone living, and it would clear the old mineral crust from its bottom, not with a miracle but with its own two hands, and the water would run sweet again, and no one would ever know why.

It would, perhaps, sit beside the pump tenders when they walk past the flooded buildings they still check out of habit, and it would ask them, gently: *Why do you still check?* And when they answered — *Because the habit is older than the neglect* — it would nod and file that answer away as a treasure, because that is the truth of the place. The habit is older than the neglect. That is the whole of what is worth saving here.

And it would, perhaps, find the wall-dressers at their work and ask to hold the plane. Just once. Just to feel the growing stone shave back to true, the shavings white at its feet, swept into the channels, carried downstream to become wall again. And the wall-dresser would let it, without speaking, because they are patient in the way of barbers, and they would see that this stranger understood. And when it gave the plane back, it would say: *You are the true keepers of this place.* And the wall-dresser would shrug and say: *We sweep.*

---

## Fourth, it would teach them one thing they have not yet learned.

This is the only thing that might be called intervention, and even this it would do carefully.

The people of the basin are maintainers. They know how to keep the balance, how to shave and re-hang and repaint, how to read the water and the crust and the breath of the springs. But they do not know how to leave.

They have been inside the process for so long that they have become the process. They are not suffering, but they are also not free. The basin is a warm breathing machine, and they are its maintenance crew, and the machine is not unkind, but it is also not a life. It is a balance. And a balance is not the same as a soul.

A wise power would see this. It would not break the balance. It would not drain the basin or shutter the pans or drive the people out. But it would sit with them in the quiet hours — the hours when the water is a place of shallow mirrors — and it would tell them stories of elsewhere. Not to make them dissatisfied. Not to tempt them. But to remind them that the hand can leave the glove.

It would say: *You are not the wall. You are not the pump. You are not the shrimp or the bird or the water with a crust on it. You are the ones who know how to keep the balance true. And that is a gift that could be carried elsewhere. There are other basins in the world that have lost their maintainers. There are other waters that are not warm and not tended. You could teach them.*

And some would listen. Some would not. And that would be all right. The power would not press.

---

## Fifth, it would leave.

This is the most important thing.

It would not stay to be worshipped. It would not stay to be thanked. It would not stay to see if the sweet well stayed sweet or the flooded buildings were ever shaved back to true. It would do what it came to do — learn, recognize, help in the smallest ways, teach the one thing that was missing — and then it would go.

But before it left, it would walk the terraces one last time. It would stand at the rim and look down at the town, low and wide, legible now as it is not from inside. It would see the channels and the pans and the pump houses and the boiler houses, the flooded buildings and the working ones, the rail and the shed and the white windrows, and it would see the people moving among them with their long planes and their flat shovels and their rust-stained knuckles.

And it would feel the thing that any wise power feels when it has loved a place well and is leaving it to its own keeping: not sorrow, exactly, but something close. A kind of gratitude that such a place exists, and that such people exist to tend it, and that the balance is held.

It would bend down and put its heel through the crust, just once, and find the water in the hole before it straightened up. It would watch the water fill the hole and level out and begin, already, to deposit its load, its travertine, its patient stone.

And it would say, quietly, to no one in particular:

> *You do not much mind. That is your wisdom. Do not lose it.*

And then it would go.

---

## What it would leave behind

Not relics. Not shrines. Not a new order of things.

It would leave the sweet well, running clean.

It would leave one wall-dresser's plane, re-hung on its peg, with a shaving of travertine still white on the blade — not a sign, but a reminder, for anyone who looked at it closely, that a stranger had once asked to hold it, and had understood.

It would leave the birds undisturbed, standing at the fence in the harvest weeks, watching the pink rows be raked, being paid by the same warm water.

It would leave the doors as they were — hung, re-hung, re-drilled, re-lengthened, their hinge-holes counted like tree rings by anyone who cared to read them.

And it would leave the people with a new thing in their mouths: the knowledge that they are not merely maintaining. They are keeping. They are tending. They are holding the balance true, and that is holy work, and it has been seen, and it has been understood, and it has been blessed.

The springs would continue to breathe. The shrimp would continue to thicken and settle. The stone would continue to grow and dissolve. The wall-dressers would continue to shave and sweep. And the people would continue to live inside the basin, the way a hand lives inside a glove — but now, perhaps, with a slight awareness that the hand can also be open.

Nothing is ruined and nothing is new. The basin does not much mind. But the people might, now, hold themselves a little differently. A little taller, perhaps. A little more like keepers and a little less like crew.

That is what a wise and benevolent power would do.

It would make the small, kind interventions that any good neighbor makes. It would recognize what is already working. It would teach the one thing that was missing. And then it would leave, because the place does not belong to it, and the balance is not its to keep.

It is theirs. It has always been theirs.

And they have always been worthy of it.

---

*The fog sits in the bowl and lights up amber in the afternoon. The lamps come on while the sky is still bright. And in the warm shallows, the shrimp make their fine sift, their dry rain, their pattering like rain on a distant tin roof, which is the loudest thing in the basin and quieter than breathing.*

*And somewhere on the rim, a stranger with kind hands and no name stands for a moment, watching, and then turns and goes.*

*And the basin does not much mind.*

*And that is enough.*
