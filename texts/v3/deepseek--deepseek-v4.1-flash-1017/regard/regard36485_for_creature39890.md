# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

It would come in the way that fog comes—not as an arrival but as a gradual thickening, a sense that the world has become more itself.

---

It would begin, I think, by listening.

The plain is not silent. It is not asking for anything. It has been venting its own decay through two hundred warm throats for a thousand thousand years, and it does not know that it is old, because age requires a before. The power would come and stand—well, not stand; presence does not require a body—and simply attend to the dispersed chord, the murmur of the channels, the shriek of the overloaded flues, the pop and bloop of the digesting peat. It would learn the pitch of each flue the way you learn the voices of a choir: not to correct them but to know them, to hold them in the hollow of understanding.

This would take time, and the power would not begrudge the time, because the plain is not in a hurry and neither would it be.

---

Then, perhaps, it would notice the empty intervals.

Three hundred meters between flues. The calls that cross the gaps—bird to bird, flue to flue—carrying over the moss and finding only moss to receive them. The plain is not lonely. Loneliness requires a self that could be alone. But the intervals are where the plain is thinnest, where the mat holds nothing but itself, and the power would see that the intervals are not a flaw. They are the shape the plain has chosen. The knots and the gaps together are the whole.

So it would not fill them.

It would, though, learn to walk them. To move across the mat in the way the mat wants—up to the ankle, sometimes to the knee, the footprint held and then forgotten—and it would feel the charge in the peat, the quiet accumulation, the static that makes the fog cling. It would feel the ground-storms before they came, the teeth-ache, the flat held air, and it would wait, and it would watch the blue-white bloom spread low across the moss, and it would see the lights left standing in the fog afterwards, and it would not mistake them for living things. It would see the old ones that the animals have stopped noticing, and it would notice them.

The power, being wise, would know that noticing is a form of keeping.

---

What does the plain need? Nothing, in the way that need is usually meant. The basin keeps what the weather brings; it vents what it cannot keep; the flues are all old, and no new one has risen in living memory, and that is not a tragedy. It is the shape of a thing that has settled into itself.

But the power would see—slowly, over seasons of attending—that there is a thing the plain cannot do. The plain is one organism with a great many opinions, and it is not young, and it does not build, and it does not send anything out into the world beyond its rim. The calls of the birds never leave the basin. The plumes rise and thin and vanish against the cloud lid. The plain's voice is heard only by the plain.

The power would take that voice and carry it.

Not loudly. Not in a way that changed the plain or drew people to it. But in the way that a wise person carries a song out of a room—humming it, so that it enters other rooms, so that someone somewhere looks up and half-remembers a melody they've never heard and feels, for a moment, the particular cold of a cellar, the particular warmth of a flue's ring, the low crowded murmur of water with nowhere to fall. The plain would enter the world sideways, at the edges of attention, as weather enters memory.

The power would do this because it is generous. Because the plain is complete and does not need to be shared, but sharing it costs the plain nothing and gives the world something it has no other access to: the sound of a place that has been metabolizing its own decay for longer than the mountains have been hills, and has not asked to be understood, and has not asked for anything at all.

---

And then there is the harder question.

Would the power interfere?

The plain has no hands. It has no one to build, no one to cut, no one to make a mark. The flues fall, one by one, over centuries—black and hollow and cold—and the rings around them cool, and the bazaars close, and the plain has one more interval of nothing. There are two hundred flues now. There will be fewer. The plain is not dying. It is the same as it has always been. But it is aging in the way that old forests age: not failing, but thinning, the population of its lungs declining, the intervals widening.

The power would notice this. It would not be able to un-notice it.

But I do not think the power would grow new flues. That would be building, and the plain does not build; the flues grew because a bog needed to vent, and a bog that no longer needs what it once needed has moved into a different phase of its existence. To force a new lung would be to tell the plain to be young again. The wise do not do this. The wise sit with the old and learn their rhythm.

What the power would do—what I believe it would do, reasoning from the nature of benevolence and wisdom together—is this: it would take the falling.

When a flue finally goes cold, and the ring around it begins to cool, and the birds that nested in its valve collars go quiet, and the frogs in its meltwater stop their chorus, and the insects thin and drift away—the power would be there. Not to stop it. To witness it. To hold the dying flue in attention the way a hand is held at the end of a life, not to pull the person back but to make sure they are not alone.

And because the power is not a person, and its attention is not a thing that ends when it looks away, the flue would remain, in some sense, held. The plain would not know it, except perhaps as a slight warmth in the intervals, a sense that the nothing between the rings is not quite nothing, not quite empty, not quite alone.

The animals would feel it before anything else. They would come back, slowly, to the cooled ring. Not because it was warm again—it wouldn't be—but because something was there that had not been there before. Something that noticed them. The fox would pause at the edge of the cold disc. The hare would sit up. The birds would land, cautiously, in the empty crown, and find it was still, and still hollow, and still a good place to be.

The power would not stay. It would move on to the next flue that was going cold, and the next, across the centuries. It would be, for each of them, the last company.

---

I think, too, that it would visit the lights.

The pale standing figures in the fog, the leftover glow of ground-storms, resident and patient, dimming over hours, drifting a little. The old ones that the animals have stopped noticing. The power would go to the oldest ones—the ones lit so long ago that no one remembers the discharge that made them—and it would stand in them and be lit by them. It would let the light fall on it, whatever it is, or whatever it means to be a benevolent power standing in the residue of weather. And it would leave something there: not a new light, not a discharge, but a kind of echo, a shape in the fog that was not a ground-storm's shape, that would linger after the power had gone.

The animals would notice that one too. They would walk around it the way they walk around the others, and after a while they would stop noticing it, the way they have stopped noticing all the rest. But the plain would know. The plain would have, in its fog, the shape of having been seen.

---

And then, finally, the power would leave.

Not because it had finished—the kind of attention I am imagining does not finish—but because to stay would be to become part of the plain, and the plain does not need another inhabitant. It needs what it has: the mat, the flues, the rings, the charge, the lights, the slow digestion, the dispersed chord. It needs to be what it is without an audience.

So the power would go, carrying the chord with it, humming it into other rooms, leaving the plain as it found it: wet and cold and loud and old and complete. The only change would be the faint warmth in the intervals, the echo in the fog, the sense—if a plain can have a sense—that the nothing between the rings is not quite nothing anymore.

And that would be enough. That would be, I think, the whole of it: a benevolent power that came, and listened, and learned the pitch of every throat, and walked the empty places, and carried the voice outward, and sat with the dying, and stood in the leftover light, and left.

The plain would not know it had been visited. But it would have been.

And somewhere, in some other room, someone would look up and half-remember a melody they'd never heard—a low crowded murmur, a dispersed chord, a ground-storm's rolling boom—and feel, for a moment, the particular cold of a cellar, the particular warmth of a ring, the particular patience of a place that has been metabolizing its own decay for longer than the mountains have been hills, and has not asked for anything at all.
