# The Visitor

The power comes in quietly, the way wisdom always does, through the throat, on the slack, when the water is low enough not to drown the knocks.

It has been watching for a long time. Not spying — *attending*, which is different. It has listened to the brass note of the chamber from far off and understood that something lives inside that sound the way a thought lives inside a word. It has seen the ochre lamps from the surface, at night, going dim and bright and dim again in no pattern anyone could chart but that is not random either. It has felt the pulse of the pumps through the bedrock and known, with the patience of something that has watched many living things build many homes, that a people had made a decision here, long ago, and that the decision was not a wound but a way.

And now it is inside.

---

The first thing it does is nothing.

It finds a high gallery, one of the ones kept for the sulphur days, and it sits in the dark with its hands open on its knees and it listens. It listens through one full surge and then another and then a hundred. It learns the rhythm of the brass note decaying against the incoming strike. It learns the underlayer of the engines, the tick and sigh of them, the way they falter and recover. It learns that the water breathes in for forty and out for forty and that the people breathe with it, and that the breathing together is the oldest thing here, older than the pumps, older than the town.

It does not announce itself. Announcement is a surface habit. In the Kettle, the questions are asked first, by knocking, from a distance, and answered last, by taking a hand.

They find it on the third day. A child, sent to check the high galleries after a sulphur belch, sees the shape in the dark and does not run. Runs. The child knocks — two quick strikes against the rail and a wait. The power hears the question, and understands it, and does not know how to answer. So it does what a wise thing does when it does not yet know the language: it takes the child's hand, gently, and taps the child's knuckles against the rail in the rhythm it has heard the child use for itself. *I am here. I am not a threat. I do not know how to say more.*

The child stares. Then the child knocks back, slowly, with exaggerated clarity, the way one teaches a younger one. *Wait.* And runs.

---

What the wise power finds, when the town comes for it — not with weapons, because the Kettle has no weapons, but with the whole town, which is worse — is that it has been expected for a long time and expected in a particular way. The people are not surprised. They are not even, in the way of people, afraid. They have been knocking *unanswered* for years, and the unanswered knock has a meaning that has nothing to do with strangers. It means something is wrong with the world. It means the balance has slipped. The arrival of a thing that can be heard is not a rescue. It is a correction.

They sit the power down on the widest catwalk, in the best lamp-light, and they knock at it. All of them. Not in unison — that would be an attack — but in sequence, one after another, the way they introduce themselves to a new child. The rhythms go on into the night. The power learns, over the course of that first long conversation, that they are asking a single question in a hundred ways:

*What are you here to take?*

---

And the power, being wise, answers honestly. It knocks back. It is clumsy at first — the way anyone is clumsy at first — but it is patient, and it does not pretend to fluency it does not have. It says, in the grammar of the pause: *Nothing. I came because I heard you and I did not know what I was hearing. I came to understand.*

The people confer. A lamp-trimmer, an old woman whose knock is the deepest in the town, answers for them: *Then understand this first. We are not drowning. We are not waiting to be saved. We are keeping a machine alive so that the machine keeps us alive. The balance is not kind, but it holds. If you are here to fix it, you will break it.*

The power says: *I am not here to fix it. I am here to learn what it is.*

And that, more than any gift it could have brought, is the thing that lets it stay.

---

It stays for a season. It learns the knocks. It learns, first, the ones for weather — because the Kettle has weather, the sulphur and the fresh, and the people read it the way farmers read clouds — and then the ones for people, and then, last and most carefully, the ones for itself, which the people invent for it and repeat to it until it knows its own name in their language.

It learns that a name is a rhythm. It learns that when a child is given its knock, the whole town repeats it on the crib and the rail and the breastbone until the child knows itself as a pattern before it knows itself as a face, and it weeps, quietly, in the dark, where no one can see, because it has been many things in many places and it has never seen a people so deliberate about belonging.

It learns that love is a knock repeated slowly until the other person answers from the dark. It learns that grief is the same, repeated until it becomes a name again. It learns that the shrine to the saint whose name has worn off is kept clean because cleaning is a way of saying *we are still here*, and it does not tell them about the saints it has known, because it understands that the unnamed one is theirs and the not-knowing is part of the keeping.

It learns the crabs. They are, in fact, delicious.

---

After a season, it asks — in their language, by knocking — what it can do.

The old lamp-trimmer answers, and she is careful, because she has been thinking about this question since the power arrived and she does not want to waste the answer: *Nothing that changes the shape of us. But you have been in other places. Other throats. You have seen what we have not. If there is a way to hold a seal against the acid belch that we have not thought of, tell us. If there is a metal that lasts longer in the warm water, tell us where it is. If there is a way to read the vents before they turn — a rhythm we have missed, a sign in the stone — teach it. But do not give us a new door. Give us the means to keep the old ones.*

The power does these things, and it does them slowly, and it does them with its hands, and it asks permission before it touches any joint that has been re-made a dozen times, because each re-making is remembered in the way the metal sits and it will not add a thirteenth without the town's blessing.

It brings a gasket material from a place it knows, far off, that will hold against the acid. It brings a way of wedging the intake-shells loose that spares the pump the wear. It brings a lamp that holds its charge through two full cycles, and it brings only three of them, because a lamp is a small thing and the town must decide how to share them and the deciding is the town's.

It does not bring green. It has green in it — it has seen forests, it carries the memory of them the way the people carry the memory of the brass note — and it thinks, once or twice, of bringing a fern, a moss, something that flowers, to see whether it could live in the warm dark. But it thinks of the photograph of the forest on the wall, and the polite bewilderment in the eyes of the people looking at it, and it understands that the Kettle's palette — ochre and iron and bone, the black of deep water, the red threads of the glowing rails — is not a poverty to be corrected. It is a *choice*, made so long ago and so completely that it has become a face. It will not offer to change the face of a people. It is a wise power. It knows better.

---

It stays through one sulphur belch, and it goes up into the high galleries with the others, and it listens to the brass note go strange and sour, and it feels the seal it brought hold — just one seal, just on one door, a test — and it hears the town knock *it holds* and then, more quietly, *thank you*, and it understands, for the first time, what maintenance is. Not a chore. Not a virtue. A form of love. The love of something that will not stay fixed without you. The love of the thing that is yours because you keep it.

It thinks, then, about the long problem. The stone growing around the foundations. The town being eaten, slowly, by its own home. And it asks the old lamp-trimmer, in the grammar of the pause: *What do you want for the hundredth year?*

And she answers: *We want to still be here. That is all. We are not asking to be elsewhere. We are asking for the hundredth year to come and find us keeping the pumps.*

The power nods. It has been many things. It has been a rescuer, and it has learned that rescue is sometimes theft. It will not steal this. It will go away and think, and it will come back with metal that lasts, and a way of reading the stone, and it will not offer a new home. It will offer *help holding the old one*. It will offer redundancy, which in the Kettle is the word for wealth.

---

When it leaves — and it must leave, because a guest who stays too long is a wall — it goes out through the throat on the slack, and the water swallows it and spits it, and it lets itself be swallowed and spat, because that is the way out and it has learned to be a guest.

It surfaces into a silence so total it is a kind of noise. The loudest thing it has ever heard. And it finds itself, without thinking, knocking on the side of the boat. Two quick strikes and a wait.

There is no answer.

It knocks again. The same. Then it understands: it has learned a language in which the answer is the knocking. The waiting is the point. The being-in-the-dark-with-your-hand-on-a-rail is the point. It has been in the Kettle, and the Kettle is in it now, as it is in all of them, and it will never be entirely quiet again.

It will come back. It has promised, in the grammar of the pause. It will come back with the metal and the stone-reading and the third thing it has not thought of yet, and it will knock first, from a distance, and it will wait for the answer.

And on the surface, in the meantime, when it passes a doorframe, it will tap. Once. Twice. A wait. And the people it passes among — the surface people, who do not know, who have never been in a throat — will look at it strangely, and it will not explain. Some languages are not for explaining. Some are for keeping.

The balance is not kind, but it holds.

*Remember.*
