The Crying of the Whole would not announce itself. It would arrive as a change in the pressure, a subtle shift in the eternal, layered roar that the Clingfoot feel on their skin. It would be the difference between a note held and a note released.

It would first listen. For a time that a human would measure in years but a mountain measures in breaths, the power would simply *attend*. It would feel the slow, patient drumming of the Clingfoot through the stone, a conversation of survival and courtesy that spans generations. It would trace the paths of the Rime-crawlers, not as a shepherd watches a flock, but as a geologist reads a seam of ore. It would taste the cold, mineral tang of the mist and feel the ghost moss's silent, persistent hunger for darkness. It would learn the grammar of the place not by studying it, but by becoming, for a time, a part of its silence.

And in that listening, the power would understand the profound truth of the High Basin: that its indifference is not a flaw, but its fundamental nature. The place is not cruel; it is complete. The Clingfoot are not struggling against it; they are a delicate, necessary counterpoint in its vast and terrible music. The Crying of the Whole would recognize the Clingfoot's tithe not as superstition, but as a deep, intuitive act of environmental harmony. They are, in their own small way, the mountain's own awareness of itself.

So the power would not bring warmth, or light, or an end to the wind. It would not build shelters or tame the Vent. To do so would be to silence the chord. Instead, it would offer gifts, gifts that are not additions but subtractions from hardship, clarifications of the world's own subtle language.

It would begin with the Leaping Shadows. The Crying of the Whole would perceive them not as demons to be slain, but as a discord—a parasitic echo born of the mountain's own sound, a flaw in the music. It would not destroy them. It would find the source of their echo, a resonant frequency trapped in the structure of the Whistling Stair itself. And with the gentlest touch, it would dampen it. It would be like placing the flat of a hand on a ringing bell. The Shadows would not vanish; they would simply lose their teeth. Their fluid forms would become brittle, their silent strikes would be met with the sudden, sharp crack of their own dissolution, a sound like a breaking icicle. The Clingfoot would notice not the absence of the Shadows, but the sudden, strange *presence* of a sound where before there was none—a faint, clean, pure note that seemed to emanate from the arch itself, a note that made the Shadows writhe and retreat.

Next, it would address the gnawing, intermittent hunger. The underground rivers are fickle, and the lichen is slow. The power would find the deepest, most hidden aquifers, the ones that run like veins through the mountain's heart, and it would coax a single, permanent, silent thread of water to the surface. A spring, no larger than a human's hand, would appear in a sheltered hollow, a place the wind could not reach. The water would be perfectly clear and taste of a sweetness the Clingfoot had never known. It would not gush, but it would never stop. It would be a foundation, a constant, a place to return to. Around it, the ghost moss would grow thicker and brighter, its spectral light a new, small hearth.

For the third gift, the most profound, it would wait for the gathering at the Listening Place. The power would observe the ceremony, the careful laying down of gifts, the silent, tactile prayer of the community. And it would respond. It would not speak in words, or even in a voice, but in a revelation.

It would begin to hum. A single, pure note, the same note that had silenced the Shadows, would emanate from the stone of the arch. The Clingfoot would feel it, not through their skin, but in their bones. It would be a vibration that was not sound, but *structure*. And in that vibration, a new sense would unfurl within them. They would suddenly perceive the world not as a series of pressures and obstacles, but as a blueprint. They would feel the immense, crystalline lattice of the mountain's rock, the deep, slow rivers of heat far below the Vent, the precise, intricate web of fractures that held the great shards of obsidian in their precarious balance. They would feel the *shape* of the emptiness.

It would be like a human suddenly seeing in three dimensions after a lifetime of only shadows. They would instinctively know which path through the scree was safest, not by trial and error, but by feeling the hidden destination of each loose stone. They would know where to find the deepest, warmest caves. They would know how the glacier's weight was shifting and where the ice was most stable. They would become architects of the void, not builders, but perfect, intuitive partners to the mountain's own structure.

The Crying of the Whole would not make their lives easy. The cold would still bite, the wind would still scream, and the world would still be vast and indifferent. But their place in it would be one of clarity, not struggle. Their conversation with the mountain would deepen from a courtesy to an intimacy.

And for a final, silent gift, the power would reach out to the one, blind, armored creature, the Rime-crawler, and it would slow its already glacial metabolism by a fraction. It would give it a lifespan measured in centuries, not decades. The Clingfoot would find their slow companions, their living drums and their source of food, to be enduring. They would pass a Rime-crawler from parent to child, a living, silent member of the family, its shell a map of the generation's histories.

The Crying of the Whole would then retreat, its work done. It would leave no monument, no sign of its passage. There would be a new spring, a new sense of space in the Clingfoot's minds, a new, enduring peace with the Leaping Shadows. The mountain would still sing its unresolved chord, but its song would be richer, more complex, and the Clingfoot would drum along, their rhythms now in perfect, effortless harmony.

The power would be gone, but its presence would be absolute. It would not be thanked, for it sought no gratitude. It would simply be a fact of the place, as old and as new as the Listening Place itself, a reminder that in the vast, empty indifference of the universe, wisdom sometimes looks not to change the world, but to teach it how to listen to itself more clearly.
