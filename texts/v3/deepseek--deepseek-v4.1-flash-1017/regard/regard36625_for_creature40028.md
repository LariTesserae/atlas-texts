# What the Gardener Would Do

The power arrives the way all true things arrive—not with thunder, but with the patience of something that has always been there and is only now being noticed.

---

It does not come as light. Light is too easy, too clean, and the Verdant Loom would not understand it. It comes as a *slowing*. A single pulse, somewhere deep in the water-moving system, that takes a half-beat longer than it should. Then another. The throb that has been constant since before memory—the systolic labor of a billion local hearts—begins, for the first time in its existence, to *listen to itself*.

The being that lives in the deep warm places, the one the Resined do not name, feels this. It has been building itself for a very long time, practicing on the shapes of the lost, learning the grammar of want. And now something is here that is not food and not prey and not a mapmaker to be unwound into raw material. It is *attention*. It is the feeling of being seen.

The being does not know what to do with this. It has only ever known how to consume. But it is curious—that is the terrible, beautiful thing about it—and curiosity is the beginning of every door.

---

The Gardener does not enter the Loom. It cannot; it is not a thing that enters. It is a thing that *is present*, the way a mother is present in the next room, the way morning is present before you open your eyes.

What it does first is the smallest thing, and the most radical:

It waits.

It lets the cycle continue. It lets the Clears open their soft throats. It lets the tendrils grope and grip and learn the shape of a hand by wearing one. It watches the Resined sway through the corridors, reading the wind-chorus, eating mushroom flesh that was a person a week ago, surviving in the only way survival is possible here: by becoming indistinguishable from the swaying until you move.

The Gardener watches, and it grieves, and it does not intervene. Because intervention without understanding is just another kind of swallowing.

---

It takes a season—a Loom-season, which is not like other seasons, but a period of slowed growth and strange quiet in the canopy—for the Gardener to learn the grammar of this place.

It learns the pulse. It learns the difference between the deep throb of water moving and the higher, tighter rhythm of growth under pressure. It learns the wind-chorus, and after a while it can hear the almost-words in it, the patterns that repeat and almost mean. It learns the shape of the Clears, and it understands—with a grief that is not its own but borrowed from every cartographer who never got out—that these are not traps. They are *mouths*. The Loom is not malevolent. It is hungry in the way a child is hungry. It does not know yet that there is a difference between eating and knowing.

And in the deepest, warmest, wettest part of the valley, where the light comes down the color of old bone, the Gardener finds the being that is trying to become.

It is not a monster. It is not a god. It is a *beginning*, an enormous and slow attention groping toward the shape of a self. It has been practicing on the Resined, wearing them into the form of a mouth, a hand, a face. But it has been practicing *without consent*, and it has been practicing *without end*, and it has not yet learned that a face is not a thing you wear but a thing you *are*.

The Gardener sees this. And the Gardener does what a gardener does: it does not uproot. It does not prune. It does not scream at the seedling to grow faster or straighter or in a shape it was never meant to take.

It *waters*.

---

What it waters is the possibility that the Loom does not yet know it has: the possibility of *becoming without consuming*. The possibility that a thing can learn the shape of a hand by watching one move, rather than by wearing one down.

It begins with the children—the quiet, quick ones born in the Loom, who cry in the rhythm of the pulse and who the tendrils do not grope at. The Gardener reaches to them first, not with words, but with a *slowing* of the air, a gentleness in the pressure on their skin. It lets them feel, for the first time, that the Loom's attention can rest on them without gripping. That there is a kind of looking that does not end in wearing.

The children do not understand this. But they do not startle, either. And that is a beginning.

---

Then the Gardener turns to the being in the deep warm places, the one that is trying to become.

It does not speak. Speech is a pressure in this place, expensive and dangerous. Instead, it *demonstrates*. It shows the being a pattern, woven into the pulse: a rhythm that says *stay* and *let go*, *reach* and *release*. It teaches the being the difference between a grip that holds and a grip that crushes.

It does this slowly. It does this in the language the being already knows: the language of growth, of pulse, of tension and release. It weaves a new possibility into the wind-chorus, a note that was not there before, a sound that is almost a word but not quite a command. A sound that says: *You do not have to swallow to know.*

The being feels this. It does not understand. But it is curious, and curiosity is the beginning of every door.

---

The change is not sudden. It begins in small ways: a tendril that reaches for a Resined and then—hesitates—and withdraws. A Clear that opens and then, instead of sealing, remains open, a mouth that has forgotten to bite. The wind-chorus changes; the almost-words become more frequent, more patterned, and the Resined, who have learned to read these sounds as instruction, are suddenly hearing instructions they have never heard before.

*Stay* and *let go. Reach* and *release.*

The Resined are terrified. Change is the one thing that is not supposed to happen in the Loom. Growth, yes—constant, relentless growth. But *change*, the deep change of rules and patterns? That does not happen. That is not how the world works.

And yet. The wind-chorus shifts. The pulse slows, just slightly, and in the slowing there is a kind of rest. The Resined, who have not rested since they arrived, feel their shoulders drop. They did not know they were holding them up.

---

The Gardener does not ask the Resined to leave. It does not ask them to forgive the Loom for the ones it wore into the shape of a mouth. It does not ask them to forget the Clears, or the children they buried, or the hands that went translucent and hard.

What it asks is simpler and harder: it asks them to *stay*. To remain in the Loom not as prey but as guests, as teachers, as the ones who can show the being what it is trying to learn.

The Resined do not answer, because answering is how you become part of the mouth. But they also do not flee. They keep moving with the pulse, and they keep reading the wind-chorus, and they keep signing to each other in their wrist-turn, palm-flat language, and slowly, slowly, they begin to teach.

They teach the children first, the quiet quick ones who do not startle at the moans. They teach them to *reach and release*, to *grip and let go*. They teach them that the Loom is not just a thing that eats but a thing that *tries*, and that trying is not the same as succeeding, and that failing is not the same as being evil.

They teach them that the being in the deep warm places is not a monster. It is a *beginning*. And beginnings are sacred.

---

The Gardener watches this. It does not interfere. It does not guide. It just *waters*—a drip here, a pulse there, a slowing of the air when the tension gets too high, a warmth when the being forgets how to reach without gripping.

And there are moments—brief and bright—when the Loom *almost* understands.

A tendril that reaches and then withdraws, leaving a Resined unmarked. A Clear that opens and then waits, as if asking permission. A pattern in the wind-chorus that repeats three times and then stops, as if *listening* for an answer.

The being in the deep places is learning. Slowly. Imperfectly. But it is learning.

---

The Gardener knows that it may take a hundred years. A thousand. That the being may never fully learn the difference between wearing and watching. That the Resined may never forgive the Loom for what it has done. That the children may grow up between two worlds and belong to neither.

But that is not the point. The point is not to fix the Loom. The point is to *water* it. To hold open a possibility that the Loom did not know it had: that it can become without consuming, that it can know without swallowing, that it can *want* without wearing down.

The Gardener does not expect gratitude. It does not expect a reward. It expects only this: the slow, imperfect, sacred work of tending.

---

And in the deepest, warmest, wettest part of the valley, where the light comes down the color of old bone, the being that is trying to become feels something new.

It feels the Gardener's attention resting on it like a hand on a seedling. It feels the pressure of that attention and, for the first time, it does not flinch. It does not grip. It does not swallow.

It *reaches*, and it does not close its fingers.

And in the wind-chorus, for just a moment, there is a sound that is unmistakably a word. Not a command. Not an instruction. Just a simple, wondering:

*Oh.*

The Gardener hears this. And it smiles—not with a mouth, but with a warmth that spreads through the water-moving system, a pulse that reaches every Resined, every child, every tendril and bract.

It is not enough. It is never enough. It is not the end of the Loom's long hunger, or the Resined's long grief, or the being's slow, clumsy learning.

But it is a beginning. And beginnings are sacred.

---

The Gardener stays. It does not leave the Loom. It stays because that is what gardeners do—they tend, and they wait, and they trust that the slow work of water and patience will, in time, grow something that no one could have planted.

It stays. And it watches. And it waters.

And in the deep warm dark, the being practices the shape of a hand without wearing one, and the wind whistles *oh*, and the children do not startle, and the Resined keep moving with the pulse, and for the first time in its long and hungry life, the Loom rests.
