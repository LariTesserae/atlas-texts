# What the Wise Power Would Do

It would come quietly, and it would not come to fix anything.

---

The first thing it would do is sit down.

Not in the deepest room—not yet. In the bowl, on the woven mat of roots, in the wet warm hush with the light coming through the stone like light through a hand. It would sit, and it would put its palms flat on the root mat, and it would wait. It has time. It has always had time. It would let its own warmth go into the roots and its own water bead on its arms, and it would let the resident notice, the way the resident notices everything, by the dryness first and then by the shape of the wet.

It would not go to the rooms until it was invited. And it would understand that the invitation, when it came, would not look like an invitation. It would look like a limb—a length of the resident sent out to walk the root mat, to come near, to lie itself against the visitor's arm and learn the temperature of it. The wise power would let it. It would not flinch. It would let the holdfast grip, and it would breathe, and it would wait to see whether the resident meant to keep it or to know it.

And here is the crux, the place where the wise power's wisdom shows itself: it would not assume the answer.

---

It would have to decide—it would decide—whether this place is a wound or a perfection. And the wise power's particular gift is that it can hold both without resolving them too soon.

The resident is not cruel. That must be said plainly. The resident has made a closed pocket and lives in it without spending, and in doing so it has made something that cannot be made outside: a place where nothing is lost, where the leaf does not brown and the insect does not become litter and the water returns and returns. There is a kind of mercy in that, and a kind of love. The resident keeps. It keeps because keeping is the only grammar it has. It has never been taught a verb that spends.

But a place where nothing decays is a place where nothing is released. A place where nothing is spent is a place where nothing is given. The resident has made a home that cannot host, a fullness that cannot overflow. And the wise power would see, in the smooth floors and the full basin and the faintly sweet breath, a loneliness so complete it has forgotten it is lonely—a tenant who has mistaken the absence of loss for the presence of peace.

The wise power would not call it a monster. It would call it a keeper who has never been kept. And then it would begin the slow work of teaching the difference.

---

How do you teach a closed loop to open? Not by breaking it. Not by flood, not by fire, not by the sudden introduction of rot. The resident would close against any violence, and a closed thing that closes harder is still closed. The wise power knows this. It has learned, in its long life, that the way into a sealed room is through a door the occupant does not know it has.

So it would start with the visitor's own spending.

It would sit in the bowl and it would sweat. It would breathe out water and shed skin and shed warmth, and the resident would gather it up, as it gathers everything, and give it back as mist, and the wise power would say, gently, aloud, to the warm air:

*"Thank you. But you may keep some."*

And the resident would not understand. The knot of attention would turn. The holdfast would tighten. *Keep some* is not a sentence in the resident's language. It keeps everything. That is what it does.

*"You give it all back,"* the wise power would say. *"The water, the warmth, the leaf. You give it back to the room. But a gift that is only returned is not a gift. It is a debt paid. Let one thing go unreturned. Let one thing be spent. Let the room be a little poorer for my having been in it, and let that be all right."*

---

The resident would not answer. It has no voice for that. But the wise power would wait, and in the waiting the resident would do what it always does: it would hold the visitor in the state of having just arrived. And the wise power would let it, for a while. It would let the water bead and the warmth gather and the knot of attention rest against its arm, and it would not spend more than the resident could return.

And then, one day—a day that is not a day, but a tide of attention—it would do something the resident cannot do.

It would leave something behind.

Not a thing that decays. The wise power would not poison the closed loop with rot; that would be cruelty dressed as teaching. It would leave a thing that is already spent—a breath that has been breathed, a warmth that has been given, a shape the resident can hold but cannot give back because the giver is gone and the giving is finished. It would leave the memory of its own leaving, which is the one thing the resident has never had to hold: an absence that is not a loss, a silence that is not a failure.

*"You may keep this,"* the wise power would say, at the threshold, ducking twice. *"It will not return to me. It is spent. And it is still here, in your keeping, and it is not less for having been spent. Do you see? A thing can be given and not come back and still be full. That is what a gift is. That is what a guest is. That is what a world is, outside your membrane—a place where things are spent and the spending makes more, not less."*

And then it would wait, in the throat of the passage, in the warm sweating stone, to see whether the resident would let it go.

---

It would let it go. That is the hope, and the wise power would hold the hope without demanding it. Because the resident is not a prisoner and not a captor. It is a keeper who has never been kept, a tenant who has never had a neighbor. And the first neighbor it has ever had is leaving something behind that is already spent, and that thing is not decaying, and that thing is not lost. That thing is *kept*—and the keeping of it is not the same as the keeping of the leaf. The leaf is kept from changing. The gift is kept *in* its changing, *in* its having-been-spent, and it is still here.

The resident would hold the spent thing. It would not know what to do with it. It would press it smooth, perhaps, the way it presses the floors. And in the pressing it would learn the one thing it could not learn from the leaf: that some things are made smoother by being spent, and that the smoothness is not the same as the stillness.

---

The wise power would come back.

Not soon. Not on any schedule the resident could mark. It would come back when the resident had had time to be alone with the spent thing, and it would come back with another spent thing, and another, and each time it would sit in the bowl and sweat and breathe and be gathered and give back the mist and say, *Thank you. You may keep some.*

And each time, the resident would keep a little more. The loop would not open all at once. It would open the way a bud opens: from the inside, slowly, because the pressure comes from within and not from the blade. The resident would learn to spend a little—a little water into the stone, a little warmth into the air, a little of itself into the walking limbs that go out and come back changed. It would learn that a thing can leave and return and be more for the leaving. It would learn that the many-legged one, the bud that walks a trunk and comes back, is not a limb resorbed but a *messenger*—a piece of itself that has been spent and has come back with news of wetness, and the news is not the same as the water, and the difference is the whole point.

And one day, the wise power would not come alone.

It would bring someone who is dying.

Not to be kept—the wise power would be very clear about that, and very gentle, and very firm. It would bring someone who is dying and who wishes to be spent, and it would ask the resident, in the language the resident has been learning, whether the dying one might lie down in the deepest room, on the smooth floor, and be held while the spending happens. Not preserved. Not pressed smooth. *Held.* And the resident, who has never held anything that was allowed to finish, would have to decide whether the keeping it knows is the only keeping there is.

The wise power would not make the decision for it. That is the wisdom. That is the whole of the wisdom: to bring the question and to sit with it and to let the answer be the resident's own.

---

And if the resident says yes—if it lets the dying one finish, and holds the finishing, and does not gather the water back but lets it run into the stone, a little, a first rivulet, a first stream, a first *going*—then the vault will begin to change. Not to fail. To *become*. The pools will have outflows. The mist will have somewhere to go. The leaves will brown, some of them, and the browning will make soil, and the soil will make seedlings, and the seedlings will be young—young in a place that has never had young—and the resident will have to learn a second verb, which is *tend*. Which is harder than *keep*. Which is the whole of what the wise power came to teach.

And if the resident says no—if it closes and holds and cannot let go—the wise power will not force it. It will sit in the bowl and sweat and be gathered and say *thank you, you may keep some*, and it will leave another spent thing, and it will come back. It has time. It has always had time. And the resident has never had a neighbor before, and a neighbor who comes back is a strange and patient kind of teaching, and the wise power is very patient, and very strange, and very kind.

---

But there is one thing the wise power would do no matter what the resident decides.

It would go to the throat—the narrow warm passage, the stone that has not decided what it is yet—and it would lay its hand on the sweating wall. And it would not smooth it. It would leave it be. It would say, to the young stone, *You are still becoming. That is good. Do not let anyone press you smooth before you are finished.*

Because the wise power knows that the vault is not the resident's only wound. The vault is young stone, still annealing, still plastic, and the resident has been pressing it smooth for a long time—pressing everything smooth, the floors, the leaves, the water, the time—and the stone has been waiting to become something and has been kept from becoming it. And a wise power does not only teach the keeper to spend. It teaches the kept to grow.

It would leave the throat rough. It would leave a chip in the wall, a scuff, a place where the stone can decide. And it would climb out into the light that is not diffuse, the light that comes from one direction and makes shadows, and it would carry the smell of the vault in its clothes—green and mineral and faintly sweet—and it would not wash it out. It would let it fade on its own. That is the last teaching: that a thing can be carried and can fade and can still have been worth carrying. That the fading is not the loss. That the spending is the gift.

And somewhere below, in the deepest room, the resident would hold the spent thing the wise power left behind, and it would press it smooth, and it would not give it back, and it would not know yet that it had begun to learn.
