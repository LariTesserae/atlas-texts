The power would come, as such powers do, without announcement. It would arrive the way the surges arrive: not with a roar, but with a change in pressure, a sense that something has shifted under the water. The old one with their arm in the runnel might feel it first — a small irregularity in the hum, a new note in the low song of the basin. They would say nothing. They would wait.

The power would spend a long time simply watching. It would not be visible. It would learn the Kettle the way the Kettle has learned the water — slowly, by paying attention. It would watch a family rebuild a chute for the fourth time in a season and understand that the fourth rebuilding is the point. It would watch a child count runners and lose track and start again and lose track again and laugh, and it would understand that the counting is the point, not the number. It would watch an old one die and be given to the water and watch the water take them and circulate them and bring them up somewhere else, and it would understand that the giving is the point.

It would notice that the winterers are courteous to the place. It would notice that the courtesy is not fear and not worship but something rarer: a habit of attention. They do not try to make the Kettle into something it is not. They do not dam the water or still the pools or name the surges before they come. They get up early. They move fast. They work together. They pay rent in labor and in gutters and in the terrible, patient discipline of never, ever assuming that tomorrow will be like today.

What would a benevolent and wise power do with such a place and such a people?

It would, I think, do almost nothing. That is the first act of wisdom: to recognize when a thing is already alive and already working and already, in its way, complete. The Kettle is not broken. The winterers are not lost. The power would see that the whole arrangement — the water and the stone and the shrimp and the birds and the runners and the pale things below and the people with their ropes and their chutes and their old one with an arm in the runnel — is a working system. It produces something valuable. It produces people who know how to get up.

To intervene would be to break the system. To stop the surges would be to make the chutes permanent and the rebuilding meaningless. To calm the water would be to make the ropes unnecessary and the tying-practice pointless. To make the place safe would be to empty it of the thing that makes it worth being in. The power would know this. The power would do what wise powers do when they encounter a thing that is already doing its work well: it would be quiet. It would watch. It would learn.

But I think it would also do a small thing. A very small thing. It would move through the settlement at dusk, when the count is called and the count has to come out right. It would not be seen. It would not be heard. It would simply be present, the way the Kettle is present, in the hum and the cold and the enormous light. And when the names are called and the count comes out right and the whole rim relaxes and eats their shrimp and their seed and their stringy birds, the power would add a note to the hum. Not a big note. Not a note anyone could point to and say there, I heard it, there. But a note that would be there, that would make the hum just slightly richer, just slightly warmer, just slightly more clearly a thing that holds the basin and keeps it and is well-disposed toward the people who live in it.

The old one with their arm in the runnel would feel it. They would not say anything. They would sit in the dark before dawn with their forearm submerged and feel the pulse of the basin and the new note in the pulse and they would think, without words, well. Then they would go back to listening for the surge.

The power would stay through the winter. It would watch the ice calve above the basin. It would watch a family of new winterers arrive and be given a coil of rope and be taught to tie a line in four seconds. It would watch the runners steal boot-linings and the children count them and the birds clatter and the pale shapes in the deep pools migrate along the vents. It would watch the count called at dusk and the names said and the note added to the hum. It would watch the surge come and the people move fast and the chutes take the water where they have decided it should go this week and the settlement come through mostly intact and the repairs begin again.

It would watch all of this and it would understand that this is enough. That the Kettle does not need a savior and the winterers do not need a patron. That what they need is what they already have: each other, the rope, the habit of getting up, the courtesy that is not quite courtesy but is more like respect, and the place, the tremendous courteous place, humming under them, not stopping for anybody, allowing them to watch.

The power would leave when the thaw comes. It would go the way it came, without ceremony, without thanks, without a name. The winterers would go down the pass. They would not know that anything had visited. But some of them, in the lowlands, would find the hum still in their ears, richer than before, and they would think: the Kettle. They would say: the Kettle is well this year. They would say it without knowing why they said it. They would say it the way you say a thing that is true.

And that would be the whole of the power's action: to have noticed, to have understood, to have been present, to have added a note to the hum, and to have left without taking a single thing that was not freely given. To have been, in short, a guest. Which is what the winterers are, and what the Kettle is, and what the whole place has been teaching anyone patient enough to listen: that the world is full of things that are already alive and already working and already complete, and that the wisest thing you can do is get up early, move fast, work together, and not get in the way.
