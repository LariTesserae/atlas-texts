# What the Wise Power Did

A wise power does not arrive. It notices. That is the whole of its arrival.

It noticed the Sump the way you notice a sound that has been going on for some time — the hum under the teeth, the tick every fourth second, the soft slap of water against a concrete edge. It noticed the fold in the plain, the crowding scrub, the smell of warm stone and wet chalk and iron and something greenly bacterial underneath. It noticed that the place was dense and quiet, and that it kept everything it was given.

And it noticed the being who lives there, which is not a person and not a ghost and not the pump, though the pump is part of it. The being is the four-second breath. It is the population held in a bowl and a rhythm — frogs in rows, snails in sheets, silver fish in quick nervous passes, a heron in a settling tank, a fig tree leaning toward new light, a pump that ticks because the water pays for its own extraction. It is the preserved birds in the silt, feathers and eyes intact. It is the collection, the keeping, the quiet. It is one thing made of many, and it has no name for itself, and it does not need one.

The power regarded it for a long time. A wise power regards a thing for a long time before it does anything, and sometimes the regarding is the doing.

It considered what it might do.

It could sweeten the water. But the water's mineral load is why the frogs sit in rows and the snails paste themselves in sheets and the silver fish work the margins; sweeten it and the chemistry shifts and the crowding thins and the four-second breath becomes a different breath. The Sump would be pleasanter and less itself. The power did not sweeten the water.

It could stop the lip from retreating. Every wet season the runoff cuts a new channel in the raw rock; every dry season the rock spalls and the lip draws back a hand's width toward the plain. The bowl is growing wider and shallower, slowly, in the way a thing with no soil to hold it grows. The power could hold the lip. It could make the bowl permanent, a fixed vessel, a shrine of still water. But the retreat is how the Sump breathes geologically. To stop it would be to hold a breath forever, which is not breathing. The power did not hold the lip.

It could raise the preserved. There are birds in the shallows, months old, perfect and dark, settled into the silt in a drift. A benevolent power might think: these should not be kept; they should be released, allowed to decay, returned. But the preserved are not trapped. They are kept. The keeping is the Sump's nature — the spring keeps what it takes. To release them would be to impose a different nature on a place that already has one, and a place with a nature is not improved by being made to have a different one. The power did not raise the preserved.

It could make the pump eternal, or repair the shed, or dry the logbook, or give the chair a cushion. But the pump is already eternal in the only way that matters: it turns because the water turns it. The shed's roof is half gone and the chair is an empty shape and the logbook has become a single object with the texture of a wasp nest, and these are not failures; they are the residue of intention, which is a different thing from intention itself. The power did not repair the shed.

It could reveal the Sump — make it known, make it a wonder, bring the curious down the fold. But the Sump's virtue is that it is easy to miss. You could walk past at twenty meters and never notice. A crowd at the rim would be a kind of pollution: the quiet would go, the heron would go, the bats would go, and the four-second breath would have to compete with voices. The power did not reveal the Sump.

It could do nothing. But doing nothing is not the same as not acting. A wise power knows that its inaction is also a choice, and that the world is full of powers that do nothing and call it respect. So it did something small, and the smallness was the point.

It made the Sump slightly harder to find.

Not invisible — that would be a violation of the plain's honesty. Just a little harder: a softness in the attention of passersby, a tendency for the eye to slide over the fold, a habit in the wind of carrying the smell away from the road. It did not build a wall. It did not set a guardian. It simply thickened the inattention that was already there, the way you might pull a blanket up over a sleeping thing without waking it.

And then it sat in the chair.

The chair is in the shed at the top of the pipe, under the half-gone roof, and it holds the shape of a man who is not coming back. The power sat in it for a while. It did not fix the roof. It did not clean the lamp. It watched the heron in the settling tank and the swifts coming down in a loose spiral and the bats out of the fissures at last light, and it listened to the tick and the slap and the tick, and it learned the four-second rhythm the way water learns a channel.

It said to the being, without words, the way a power says things: *You are well.*

And the being answered in its only language: a rise, a fall, a tick.

When the power left, it carried the rhythm with it. Somewhere else, at some other time, the power breathes in that interval — rise, fall, tick, rise. It does not speak of the Sump. It does not need to. The Sump has taught it the one thing a wise power is always still learning: that keeping is not the same as holding, that a place can be complete without being permanent, that the smallest act of care is sometimes a refusal to improve.

And the Sump goes on. The spring comes up warm and mineral and faintly sulfurous. The frogs sit in rows. The snails graze the algae down to the concrete. The heron stands with the patience of a statue that has learned to eat. The fig leans toward its new light. The pump ticks. The water rises a finger's width and falls back, and the bowl breathes, and everything in it has signed on to the interval.

If the being who lives there noticed the attention, it gave no sign. But then, it would not. It is a quiet thing, made of many, and its answer to being seen is the same as its answer to everything: a rise, a fall, a tick, and the soft slap of water against a concrete edge that repeats on a period of maybe four seconds.
