**The Long Attention**

It would do almost nothing. The almost is exact.

Call it the Long Attention, because that is what benevolence looks like when it has learned its own strength: a patience that does not announce itself, a nearness that does not crowd. It would come to Vaeless the way the rain comes—without appointment, without spectacle, in short soft passes—and it would see first what the people see last: that Vaeless is not a place with a being in it. It is a place that is a being, and the people live in the being the way birds live in a tree. The pale walls are its skin, the cisterns its slow blood, the long corridors its grammar of consent. The people are its weathers: sparse, soft-footed, courteous. The horned grazer is its old neighbour. The green is its memory of the sea.

The power would recognize all of this before it recognized any single face. It had seen many worlds, and it knew the first discipline of wisdom: not to mistake admiration for license. Vaeless was not suffering. It was mid-sentence. There is a kind of help that is only a hurry wearing a kind face, and the power would refuse it.

So it would not reveal itself. It would not teach, improve, populate, seal, test, or solve. It would not straighten the corridors or explain the walls. It would not make the city interesting to other powers. It would not mistake the empty streets for lack or the people’s quiet for grief. It would not “save” a young land from becoming old. It would give the horned grazer the long way around, and the grazer would give it the long way around, and neither would name the other loudly. That is courtesy.

What it would do is sit.

It would take a shape that could sit on moss without crushing it—barefoot, reed-green, rain-soft, nothing that would make a child stare. It would arrive at a well-court in the hour before the afternoon rain, accept a shallow bowl of water, and wait three breaths before speaking. Then it would not speak. The people would lower their voices. The power would lower its own. The silence would not be empty; it would be the room the city had given sound to travel in.

And there, with its eyes on the water and its attention on the city, it would ask—not in words, because words are handles, and it did not want to be carried—“Is there anything you would have me do?”

The city would answer in its own way. A corridor would lengthen by one pace and another shorten by two. A cistern would catch a different coin of sky. The old fig by the walkway would hold its corner a little more firmly. The power would read the answer as clearly as a sentence: *Do not mend me. Do not hurry me. Keep the outside from hurrying me. Be a room I can set down what I cannot say.*

So the power would set a courtesy at the edge of the basin—not a wall, nothing that would show on the pale ridges. It would be a quiet in the land’s memory, a detour in the intentions of anything that would use Vaeless without consent. Appetite would lose its hurry at the low ridge. A hungry power, a careless power, a power that mistook the city’s self-keeping for invitation would find itself walking the long way around and forgetting why it had come. The sea’s slow return and the ridges’ soft collapse would continue; the city would remain mid-sentence. The power would not stop harm that is part of becoming. It would stop harm that is only appetite. That distinction is the whole of wisdom.

It would leave one other thing: an empty chair in an unused room. Not a throne, not a relic, not a door with a handle. Just a chair, low and plain, in one of the rooms that is larger inside than out—one of the rooms the people already leave empty for someone who may come. The power would not sit in it permanently. It would visit sometimes, as one of those someones. The people would find the chair and not move it. They would not name it loudly. They would give the room the long way around. A child might sit in it once and feel only warm stone and old water and a patience like a held breath. That would be enough.

In the season after, the afternoon rain would come in softer passes. The well-courts would hold the sky a breath longer. One corridor would be a pace longer than anyone remembered, and no one would remark on it. That is how Vaeless thanks.

So the power would do almost nothing, and the almost would be exact. It would give the city the two things a wise power can give a place that is still becoming: time, and company without debt. It would let Vaeless finish its own sentence. It would stay near enough to hear the period when it comes, and far enough not to write it.
