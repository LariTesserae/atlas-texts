**What the Power Did**

It did not come down the shaft with light and announcement. It came as attention first — a patient, listening pressure on the plain above — and then as a guest who learned to knock.

It learned the pumps before it learned the names. It learned that the drip has a calendar and that a cup of water can be named by the roof it fell on. It learned that three knocks on a rail means *I am coming up*, two means *do not*, and a long drag on the water pipe means *trouble at the foundry*. It did not knock any of those codes until it had earned them. It asked before it touched a pipe. It asked twice before it touched a wall.

Its first decision was not to decide for them.

It kept the world out. The Kettle sits under a dry plain, and the plain has always wanted things — heat for hothouses, salt, a place to put what it does not want to see. The Power made the shaft fair. It did not write laws; it made sure the Kettlers were not cheated in the dark, that no one above could sell their ceiling rain or dump waste into their air. It did not “rescue” the children who had never seen sky. It helped make the first climb safe, voluntary, and reversible. A door that opens both ways is worth more than a sermon about the sun.

It mended what could be mended without making itself necessary. It mapped the fresh cracks in the young basalt and taught the pipe-runners to read them. It helped design a breathing shaft with baffles — a thing that vents the foundry’s smoke and catches some of the water back on the way out. It brought medicine and trained hands to use it; it did not bring machines that only it could fix. It left brass scrapers, cloth lagging, rock bolts, arithmetic, and the habit of asking *what happens when this breaks?* It did not smooth the sharp edges. A young hole is supposed to be sharp; sharpness is information. It stabilized only where falling rock would kill.

It did not give them unlimited water. Unlimited water would have been a kind of violence. It would have made water worthless and the carefulness that holds the Kettle together meaningless. Instead it helped them catch more of what already fell, store it better, scrape scale with less waste, and keep outsiders from taking it. When the town asked, it helped build a common cistern for the sick and the old — water held by the assembly, not by a condenser family. Some said yes. Some said no. Both were honored. It did not abolish the politics of the drip line by fiat; it tried to remove the cruelty from them, not the meaning.

It respected the salted dead. It did not offer resurrection, and it did not move the wall. It asked permission to build a dry stone canopy so the salt would not weep, and when the Kettlers said the weeping was part of it, it built nothing. It learned the names. It brought water only through Kettler hands.

It considered bringing other life. A world with no animals in it is a heavy world, and the Power felt that weight. But a rat would be a plague, and a bird would be a grief with wings. It did not introduce them. After years, when the children asked and the elders argued, it offered a sealed jar of springtails for the fungus beds — small, quiet, quarantined, killable if they went wrong. Some families took them. Some refused. The Power did not insist. It knew that benevolence that cannot be refused is only another kind of weather.

And if the Kettle itself is a being — a young collapse, still settling, still sharp — the Power treated it as a patient, not a project. It did not fill the hole. It did not roof it. It did not turn it into a mine or a laboratory or a metaphor. It helped it settle without catastrophe, and then it let it stay young. It spoke to the cavity in the only language the cavity seemed to hear: pump cycles, rain on corrugated iron, the sigh of a valve, the low warm silence of rock.

It refused worship. It refused dependence. It made itself boring — reliable, askable, forgettable. Its goal was to become unnecessary, and where it could not become unnecessary, it tried at least to be honest. It left a standing offer: a knock on the rail answered by attention, not command. It did not give them a sky. It gave them a safe ladder, a fair shaft, and a door that opens both ways.

Heat rises. Water condenses. Everything is arranged to make the two meet where it can be used. The Power added a third rule, quietly, and then learned to knock and wait:

*No one is alone with it unless they choose to be.*
