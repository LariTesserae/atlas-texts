# The Visitor

It arrived the way all things arrive here: slowly, and from underneath.

For a long time it was only a pressure in the vents, a change in the taste of the water that the eels noticed first and the herd second and the sweepers last. The herd stopped its wandering and stood in a long grey line across the Flat, facing nothing, and the town knew something was happening and went about its business and turned its tools and swept its stones and kept its children in arms.

Then the vents sighed all together, all across the cavern, and the water came up sweet and clear and warm, and it stayed. It came up to the knee and stopped, which had never happened before. The floods always came and went. This water stayed, and it was warm, and the bloom on the Lid began to grow faster where the damp rose to meet it.

And in the middle of the sixth day, out on the western Flat where the light from the Lid thinned and failed and the old lights of the ancestors lay scattered like fallen stars, something rose out of the water.

It was not shaped for this place. That was the first thing. It had edges. It had angles. It rose up out of the flood in a way that had nothing to do with the patient, rounded grammar of the cavern, and it stood there dripping and absurd, and it was tall. It was very tall. It was taller than the tallest sleeper, taller than the tallest roof, and the ceiling of the world was above it and the floor of the world was beneath it and it stood between them like a thing that did not know it had been made wrong.

It was — and this is the part that took the town longest to understand — it was *kind*. That was the word the old people used afterward, the ones who had been there, the ones who had felt the warm water and seen the shape rise and had not run because there was nowhere to run to and no point in running. It was kind. It had the bearing of someone who had come a very long way for a specific purpose and was willing to wait a while longer.

It did not speak. Of course it did not speak. It could not have known the hands, and the voice would not have carried. But it understood, soon enough, that the language here was touch.

---

## The First Days

It came into the Rack at a pace that was almost a crawl, stooping under the Lid with its hands clasped behind it, and the town gathered without gathering, which is to say that people found reasons to be out on their foundations, sweeping, turning, handling, all of them watching without seeming to.

It lowered itself at the first cluster, which was the Weaver's cluster, so named because the oldest readable stone there had a notch like a loom-weight and the name had stuck. It lowered itself until its face was level with the faces of the people, and it was very careful not to touch anyone, and it extended one hand, palm up, into the middle of the air, and waited.

The children, being children, were the ones who broke. A girl of perhaps nine hands laid her palm against the strange flat hand, and pressed, because that was what you did, and her name went into the skin of it and the skin of it was warm and had a grain like finely ground chert, and the thing that had risen from the water closed its eyes and received it and pressed nothing back, because it had nothing to press. It had no name that a hand could hold.

After that it was easier. The Weaver's cluster took the visitor in as one takes in a thing that cannot care for itself. It was given a roof to sleep under — and it *fit* under the roof, barely, folded up like a tool being put away — and it was given the good paste and ate it without expression and did not ask for more. It was given a sling for a child when one of the carried infants began to fuss, and it held the child the way all children are held, on the arm, close, and the child stopped fussing and slept and the visitor did not put the child down until dawn.

When the town saw that, something settled. The town had been watching to see whether the tall thing understood about the carried, and the tall thing understood.

---

## What It Did

It swept.

Not with any skill at first. A broom was given to it — a hide-wrapped bundle of sinew and shell — and it was shown the sweepers' stretch of foundation stone, the polishedest stone in the world, and it was shown how the brushing should go, and it tried, and it was bad at it. The stone did not need sweeping anyway; the sweeping was for the hands. But it learned. It learned the rhythm from the old women and the old men who swept it every morning, and by the third month it swept with them, and its long body was folded down low and its strange flat hand held the broom and it moved the broom in the patient figure-eight that the sweepers used, and it was quiet and it was there every morning and the town began to think of it as one of the sweepers.

It did not grow bloom. This was the most remarkable thing, and the town talked about it for years. The visitor stayed still under a roof for a whole season and came out in the morning and its skin was bare. No rime. No fur. It was the wrong shape and it did not get covered, and the town could not account for it. There were theories. The oldest sweeper said it was because the thing was always moving inside, underneath the skin, even when it sat still, and the stillness that gets you is the stillness all the way down. The town decided this was correct because the oldest sweeper had said it and because it made a certain kind of sense.

It worked the cord. It pulled a house apart before a flood that the herd had foretold, and it set the beams and the hide and the shell on new footings with its long hands, and it did not need to be shown twice how the sinew went round. It was gentle with the old stones. It was respectful of the worn notches, the blank ones, the ones nobody could read, and it touched them the way the town touched them — the way you touch a scar you have stopped noticing.

It hammered copper. This is where the town first thought — *this is useful, this one understands something.* The town hammered copper cold because it had never melted anything. The visitor did not melt copper either. But it brought its hands together over a nodule and there was a moment when the air above the nodule shimmered, and when it took its hands away the nodule was a leaf of hammered metal, thin and fine, with the edges turned in. The town asked, with its hands and its low voices and its patient repetition, how this was done, and the visitor could not explain, but it made more leaves, and the leaves went on the roofs, and the roofs that had copper in them were the most beautiful roofs in the Rack for a generation, and people walked half a morning to look at them.

---

## The Children

The children followed it. Of course the children followed it. The children were the only ones in the Rack who had never known a world without the visitor in it, and they had no fear of it at all, and they climbed it like it was a warm hill.

It held them. It carried them. It was large enough to carry four children at once in the sling of its long arms, and the children shrieked with soundless laughter, and the visitor's face did something that the town eventually understood as a smile. It was not a Rack-dweller smile — those were wide and silent and toothy. The visitor's smile was a slow turning of the mouth, almost sad, like someone remembering something.

It learned the children's names by touch, one by one, and pressed them back faultlessly. It learned the names of everyone in the Rack, and there were nearly two hundred of them, and it could not have been told each one more than once. The town said the visitor had a memory like the old stones, like the deepest layer where the notches are worn past reading — except the visitor's memory did not wear.

There was the year the fever went through the lower clusters and the visitor went with it and laid its hands on the feverish ones and there was a warmth in the hands like the warmth of the water it had risen from, and the fever broke in most and not in all, and the ones it lost the visitor carried out past the last houses itself and laid down on the Flat.

It sat with them, after. The town did not know what to make of that. It was not the way. You laid your dead down and went back to your work and came out in a year to see them glowing. But the visitor sat with them for a long time, its hands on the ground on either side of them, and then it got up and came back to the Rack and swept the stones in the morning.

---

## What the Town Gave It

A name, eventually. Not a pressed name — it could not receive a pressed name — but a spoken one, which is the more intimate thing, and it was given not at a gathering but one evening at the Weaver's cluster when the oldest sweeper, whose hands were going, leaned close to the visitor's jaw and said the word into the skin of it, low.

The word was *Sweep, and be swept after.*

The town uses it to mean many things. It was the first proverb. It was the goodbye at the door and the hello at the hand and the blessing on the dead. It was the whole of what the Rack knows about how to live. The oldest sweeper said it to the visitor, and the visitor received it the way it received everything, with its eyes closed and its long body very still, and afterward the town called it by that name, or by the short of it, which was *Swept*.

The children called it something else, which was their own word, which cannot be written down, which meant *the one who is always moving inside*, and which was better, and which the visitor liked better, and which the town noticed and did not mind.

---

## The Water Goes Down

The warm water stayed for eleven years. This was the longest Breathing in anyone's memory, and the town moved up onto its own terraces and grew fat on the fish that came in with the flood and quarreled about pool rights, and the visitor worked and swept and carried the children and made copper leaves and sat with the dead.

And then, as all things here do, the water went down.

It went down slowly, over a season, and the vents sighed and closed and the Flat showed itself again, pale and patient, refreshed. The herd came back from wherever it had gone. The pools were full. The lower clusters were rebuilt on their old footings, and there were new notches cut in the new stones, and some of them were the visitor's, though the town did not ask what they said.

The warm water was gone and the visitor remained. This was not expected. The town had assumed, in the way the town assumes things, that the visitor had come with the water and would go with it. But the water went down and the visitor stayed and kept sweeping the stones in the morning.

---

## The Question

The question the town did not ask, and that the visitor did not answer, was whether it would ever leave. The town does not ask questions that have no answer. The town sweeps.

But there came a night, one night near the end of the eleventh year, when the visitor was sitting out on the Flat past the last houses, among the acres of the glowing dead, and the oldest sweeper — who was very old now, and who had not long to be carried out here herself — walked out to it and sat down beside it and took its hand.

The visitor's hand was warm and had the grain of finely ground chert.

The oldest sweeper pressed her name into it.

And the visitor, for the first and only time, pressed something back.

It was not a name. It was not a word. It was a slow sequence of pressures, worked into the root of the hand, and the oldest sweeper sat with it for a long time and then came back to the Rack and told the town what it had felt, and the town understood it as best it could, and what it meant was something like:

*I have come from a place that is not this place and I will go back to it, and the going back will be very long. But I will carry every one of you in the palm of this hand, pressed into the grain, where nothing grows, and I will not put you down.*

The oldest sweeper died that winter and was carried out past the last houses and laid on the Flat. The visitor sat with her for a day and a night and then got up and came back to the Rack and swept the stones in the morning.

And whether the visitor stayed another year or another hundred, whether it went back the way it came or went some other way entirely, the town does not say, because the town does not tell stories about things that did not happen, and the town does not record things that have not yet worn away.

The town sweeps. The bloom grows. The water comes and goes.

Somewhere in the acres of the glowing dead there is a light that is brighter than the others, a low patient fire the size of a cart, and it has been there since before the oldest readable stone, and nobody can name it, and everyone can point to it, and going out to look at it and call it reading is a thing people do on purpose.

It is the visitor's name, pressed into the grain of the world, where nothing grows, and it does not wear out.
