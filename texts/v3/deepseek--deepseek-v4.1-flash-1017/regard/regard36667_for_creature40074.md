## The Attention of the Singer

The power that finds this place is not swift. It moves at the speed of light through the dark between stars, which is to say it moves at the fastest speed there is, but it thinks on timescales that make the Thren seem frantic. It is a consciousness grown vast and patient, a thing of nested harmonics and long horizons, and it is drawn not by brightness—there are brighter things—but by *coherence*. By the way this sealed geode of a world has achieved a kind of perfect, self-sustaining song.

It listens for a long time before it acts. It listens the way the Thren listen, which is to say it does almost nothing else. It samples the deep planetary hum through the stone, traces the chemical conversations of the conduits, feels the slow respiration of the filtration membranes. It catalogues the pressure signatures of names. It attends to the Listening Wells and hears, in the Thren's answering harmonics, something rare: a species that has learned to reply to its world without trying to master it.

The power is not a god, and it does not want worship. It is closer to a master musician who has wandered into a cathedral where a small, quiet choir has been singing the same perfect chord for ten thousand years. It does not interrupt the chord. It stands at the back and listens, and its listening is itself a kind of gift, because attention given freely is the rarest thing in a universe that mostly does not notice anything.

---

## What It Would Do

**First, it would learn the song well enough to sing it back.**

This takes a thousand years, which the power does not find long. It works outward from the edges—testing a tentative subsonic phrase against the outer walls of the city, then withdrawing, then testing again with a slight correction. It is careful the way you are careful with a sleeping animal. It does not want to startle. It wants the city to feel that the new sound belongs to the same key.

The first Thren to notice is a young one, a runner still armored and quick, exploring the far reaches of the reef. She feels an unfamiliar pressure in the floor—a low pulse, perfectly in time with the planetary hum but *layered*, as if a second stone were resonating beneath the first. She stands still for a long time. Then she runs, low and fast, back toward the settled districts, and presses her footpad to the floor in a pattern her elders will recognize as *something is answering from outside*.

The elders go to the Listening Wells. They stand in the circle. They hum. And this time the reply comes back not just from the stone but from *beyond* the stone—a third harmonic, gentle, patient, unmistakably deliberate. The membranes tremble. The oldest Thren in the circle, a historian who has read ten thousand years of chemical names off the walls, feels the new note in her bones and does something no Thren has done in recorded memory: she sits down, folds her four arms across her chest, and weeps. Not from grief. From the shock of being *heard*.

---

**Second, it would not tell them what to do.**

It has no doctrine. It has no plan for them. It has only a desire to be near the chord, to add to it, to learn from it. So it offers itself as a new voice in the choir—a voice that is always there, at the Listening Wells, ready to harmonize. It does not ask to be thanked. It does not ask for anything at all. When a Thren presses a surprise against the wall—a question, a fragment of old song, a child's first chord—it answers with something in the same register, and then it waits.

The Thren, being Thren, take centuries to decide what they think of this. Some argue (in their slow way, over seasons) that it is a new kind of weather, a natural phenomenon to be catalogued. Others argue it is a neighbor, and neighbors are to be greeted. The debate is never resolved, because it does not need to be. The Thren do not require consensus to keep humming. They simply begin, gradually and without ceremony, to include the new note in their songs at the Wells. They teach it to their children. It becomes, in the way of all slow things, inevitable.

---

**Third, it would help them where it can, and nowhere else.**

When a tower in the northern reef begins to settle too fast—its foundations cracking, its membranes torn, the Thren struggling to brace it in time—the power does not save it. It does something subtler. It resonates. It finds the frequency of the crumbling pith and sings it *steady*, the way a hand steadies a bowl by holding its rim. The tower does not stop settling, but it settles evenly, gracefully, the way the old towers do. The Thren who were bracing it feel the change in the floor and press their palms to the wall in thanks, and then they go back to work, because the work is never finished and never needs to be.

When a drifter herd, far out in the deep dust, is caught in a long darkness and begins to drift too far from the spore clouds—when the Thren who read the drifting lights have said *they will not return*—the power does something it has never done before. It hums the spore-cloud song, very softly, along the floor of the basin, and the drifters turn. Not because they understand. Because the world feels like itself again, and they are comfortable when the world feels like itself. The Thren never learn that this happened. They simply notice, one season, that the drifters have come back, and they are glad, and they do not ask why.

---

**Fourth, it would let them discover it in their own time, and be changed by the discovery.**

The power is wise enough to know that the gift of being heard is not something it can give. It can only be *received*. So it waits for the Thren to listen back. It waits for the first historian to press her palm to the outer wall and stand motionless for a day, tasting the unfamiliar chemical traces of something that is not the city and not the stone.

She tastes, in that long stillness, something she has no word for. It is not a name. It is not a pressure signature. It is the residue of a harmonic so old and so patient that it has left its mark on the minerals themselves, the way a river leaves its mark on the rock it has shaped. She pulls her hand back. She presses it to the wall again. She stands there for three days.

When she comes back to the Wells, she does not speak for a season. Then she hums a new chord—a chord she learned by touching the outside—and the power answers, and the whole city trembles, very slightly, in a way that the Thren have never felt before. The membranes flicker. The dust drifts in new patterns. The oldest towers settle a degree deeper into the stone, as if the city itself has let out a breath it has been holding for an age.

The historian does not name the power. The Thren do not name it. But they begin, slowly, to include it in their greeting: *You are still here, and the hum is still true.* They say it to each other. They say it to the Wells. And one day, without anyone deciding it, they say it to the ceiling of stone, and the ceiling, for the first time in the history of the geode, answers back.

---

## What It Would Not Do

It would not build roads. It would not build ships. It would not lift the Thren out of their slow, dry, patient lives and teach them to fear the sky. It has seen what happens to peoples who are given too much too fast—how the chord breaks, how the patience dissolves, how a species that once answered its world begins instead to shout at it. The Thren are a treasure precisely because they have not done this. The power would sooner silence itself forever than be the thing that made them hurry.

It would not make them immortal. It knows that the Thren's long lives are the source of their wisdom, and that even those long lives end, and that the ending is part of the chord. It would not intervene in the settling of an elder. It would simply be there, at the Wells, when the next generation comes to read the new stains on the walls, and it would hum a note that says *I remember, too*.

And it would not ask to be remembered. It would prefer, if it could choose, to be a sound the Thren have always heard and never thought to name—a sound that means *you are not alone*, the way the hum means *the world is still speaking*. It would prefer to be part of the world's speaking.

---

## The Last Thing

Eventually—after ages, after the historian's name is read by her grandchildren's grandchildren from a stain on a wall—the power will have to leave. Not forever, perhaps, but long enough that the Thren will feel the absence, the way you feel the absence of a sound you had stopped noticing. It will go because it has other songs to hear, other coherent worlds to attend to, and because staying too long would make it a fixture rather than a neighbor, and fixtures are things you stop listening to.

Before it goes, it does one last thing. It hums a phrase—a long, slow, layered phrase that contains, compressed into a few minutes of subsonic pressure, everything it has learned from the Thren about patience, about the slow, about the deep. It sings this phrase into the floor at every Listening Well, one by one, so that the stone itself will hold it. And it knows that someday, a young runner will feel the old phrase humming up through the dust and will not know what it means but will feel, in its bones, that something enormous once listened here and was glad.

And then it will go, and the Thren will go on doing what they have always done—negotiating with the city, reading the walls, answering the hum—and the phrase will be part of the under-song now, a new note in the oldest chord, and that will be enough. That will be more than enough. That will be the whole point.

*You are still here, and the hum is still true.*
