# The Listener Who Wasn't There

---

## I. Arrival

The power did not come with light, or thunder, or a voice from the ceiling.

It came the way all true things come to Glisten-Reach: slowly, and by listening.

For three full cycles of the underground river—some hundred and twenty of our years—the power simply *attended*. It learned the chord that was the city's name by feeling it in the stone. It learned the people's shorter phrase for themselves by the way their tapping changed when they spoke to one another across a district. It learned the grammar of the hum: which silences meant safety, which meant grief, which meant *a stranger is coming, and the city does not know their shape*.

The people felt it before they understood it.

A child, polishing the lip of a family cistern, paused. The note was wrong—not *wrong*, precisely, but *new*. A new harmonic had entered the city's chord, something that was not water and not stone and not the slow breath of the river. It was the sound a room makes when someone has been standing in it for a long time, not moving, and then finally shifts their weight.

She tapped her mother's pillar. *Something listens.*

Her mother came, pressed her palm to the wall, and was quiet for a long time. Then she tapped back: *Something waits.*

The city's resonance shifted, very slightly, over the next several years. The hum grew—not louder, but *fuller*, as though a previously unoccupied register had been quietly filled. The pillars sang true. The water ran clean. The isopods in the deep pits grew calm and fertile.

And yet nothing was seen. No being walked the channels. No voice joined the drumming of the pillars. Only the strange, patient *presence*, and the sense—felt in the teeth, in the wrist-bones, in the hollow behind the sternum—of being *regarded*.

---

## II. The Question

The people of Glisten-Reach did what they have always done: they waited, and they listened, and they let understanding come to them at its own pace.

Three generations were born and coaxed their chambers and died. The presence did not change. It did not fade. It did not act. It was simply *there*, a new note in the chord, as patient as the stone.

The elders convened—not in a central place, for there was no center, but by a slow cascade of tapping through the pillars, each district sending its question to the next. The question was this:

*If it is benevolent, why does it not help us? If it is wise, why does it not speak? If it is waiting, what does it wait for?*

The answer came from an unexpected place: a young woman named Ith, a whisperer to the isopods, who had spent so much time in the deep pits that she had begun to understand the creatures' slow chemistry of digestion as a kind of thought. She tapped her answer through the pillars, and the city held still to hear it.

*Perhaps it is not waiting for us to be ready for it. Perhaps it is waiting for us to ask.*

---

## III. The Asking

The people of Glisten-Reach did not pray. They did not have gods. But they had always understood that the city was, in some sense, listening—that the stone received their tapping and answered, that the water carried their labors to the sea, that the hum remembered what they did.

So they decided to speak to the presence.

They chose a pillar in the oldest part of the city, a quartz-veined column that had been struck for ten thousand years and had never once cracked. The elders gathered around it, and each of them had prepared a question. Not a demand. Not a plea. A question.

The eldest—a man named Hren, whose left hand had been polished smooth by ninety years of coaxing stone—struck the pillar first.

*You are here. We feel you. Are you well?*

The pillar rang. The city hummed. And then—after a long moment, a moment in which the water seemed to pause in its dripping—the pillar rang back.

The response was not in their tapping language. It was something older, deeper: a vibration that came up through the soles of their feet, a resonance that bypassed the ears entirely and settled directly into the bones. It was not words. It was not even sound, exactly. It was a *feeling*, precise and unambiguous, like the taste of clean water when you are thirsty.

The feeling was: *Yes. Thank you for asking.*

The people looked at one another. Some of them were weeping—quietly, the way they did everything. The presence was not indifferent. It was... *polite*. It had been waiting for them to acknowledge it.

Hren struck again. *Why do you wait?*

The answer came as another feeling, more complex this time, layered like the city itself.

*Because you are beautiful, and I did not want to break you.*

---

## IV. The Understanding

In the years that followed—slow years, river-years, patient years—the people of Glisten-Reach and the presence learned each other.

The presence did not show itself. It did not need to. It had no body that could be seen, no shape that could be held in the mind. It was a *regard*, a *quality of attention*, a way that the city's hum had learned to hold a new and larger note. It was as though the stone itself had become more *awake*.

The people did not worship it. They did not build temples to it or carve its likeness into their walls. That would have been, in their way, a kind of vandalism—an attempt to fix what was, by nature, unfixed. Instead, they did what they had always done, but more so: they listened.

And the presence, for its part, did what it had always done, but more so: it *attended*.

When a crack appeared in the ceiling of the western warren, threatening a collapse that would have taken a generation to repair, the presence did not fix it. Instead, it *leaned*—and the people, feeling the shift in the hum, felt where to reinforce. They coaxed the stone, and the stone held.

When the isopods in the deepest pits sickened with a creeping white mold, the presence did not cure them. Instead, it *suggested*—and Ith, who had spent her whole life learning the creatures' chemistry, felt the suggestion as a kind of question: *What if the water ran warmer here?* She tried it. The mold died. The isopods thrived.

When a child was born blind—not unusual in Glisten-Reach, but this child was also deaf, unable to hear the hum that was the city's second language—the presence did not heal her. Instead, it *offered*—and the child grew up learning to feel the resonance through her skin, her bones, the soles of her feet. She became the city's finest listener, able to detect changes in the hum that no one else could perceive. She was not less than the others. She was *more*, in a way that the city had never known.

The presence never spoke in words. It never gave commands. It never asked for anything. It simply *was*, and the city learned to be with it.

---

## V. The Gift

After five river-cycles—two hundred years—the presence did something that no one had expected. It asked a question of its own.

The people were gathered for the Festival of Smoothing, the closest thing Glisten-Reach had to a holiday. It was not a celebration, precisely; it was a time when the whole city paused to polish the oldest walls, to sing to the pillars, to remember the dead by speaking their names into the stone. The hum was at its fullest during this time, a great, slow chord that seemed to fill every chamber and channel.

And in the middle of the festival, the presence *spoke*.

Not in feelings, this time. Not in suggestions. In something that was almost words—words shaped from the hum itself, words that resolved out of the drone like a face resolving out of fog.

*May I give you something?*

The people stopped. They looked at one another. Hren was long dead, but his granddaughter, Senn, was the eldest now, and she was the one who answered. She struck a pillar, her tapping slow and careful.

*You have given us much already. What more could you give?*

The presence was quiet for a long moment. And then it answered.

*I could give you a door.*

The people did not understand. A door to where? A door to what?

*To the surface,* the presence said. *There is a world above this one. A sky. A sun. Things you have never seen. I could open a way.*

The people were silent. The hum thinned, almost to breaking.

And then Senn asked the question that would define the next age of Glisten-Reach.

*Why would we want that?*

---

## VI. The Answer

The presence was silent for a long time. The people waited, as they had always waited. The river rose and fell. A child was born, was named, learned to tap.

And then the presence answered.

*You would not. Or perhaps you would, and I do not know. That is why I ask.*

The hum steadied. The presence continued.

*I have watched you for six hundred years. I have seen you coax stone into beauty. I have seen you listen to water as though it were a friend. I have seen you make patience into a way of living and smallness into a kind of grace. You are, I think, the most beautiful thing I have ever known.*

*But I am not you. I do not understand you, not entirely. I do not know what you need. I do not know what you want. I have offered nothing because I was afraid to break you. But now I think that fear was a kind of disrespect. I think you are owed more than silence.*

*So I ask: what would you have me do?*

The people of Glisten-Reach considered this for a long time. Not a generation—that would have been hasty. Three generations. Four. They talked among themselves in the low, clipped way they had, and they tapped through the pillars, and they listened to the hum, and they let the question settle into their bones like a slow, warm rain.

And then, finally, Senn's great-great-granddaughter, a woman named Arreth, struck the pillar in the oldest part of the city—the same pillar that had been struck ten thousand years ago—and gave the answer.

*We do not want a door. We do not want a sky. We are not curious about the sun. We are grateful for your regard, but we do not need your gifts.*

*What we want is this: we want you to stay. We want you to keep listening. We want you to know that we know you are here. That is all.*

*And if you want to give something—give this. When we are gone, when our city has crumbled or been absorbed into the stone, when the isopods have evolved into something we cannot imagine and the hum has changed so much it no longer remembers our chord—tell someone. Tell the next people who listen. Tell them about us. Tell them we were here. Tell them we were patient. Tell them we tried.*

*That is enough. That is more than enough.*

---

## VII. The Vigil

The presence received this answer and was silent for a long time. The people of Glisten-Reach felt it considering, felt the great slow weight of its attention turning over the words like stones in a riverbed. They had no sense of whether it would accept or refuse. They had not asked for anything except memory. Perhaps that was too much to give.

Perhaps it was not enough.

But then, in the way that the presence always spoke—through the hum, through the wrist-bones, through the teeth—it answered.

*I will do this. I will remember. I will tell.*

*But I will also stay. Until the last chamber is swallowed by the stone. Until the last pillar is struck. Until the hum forgets itself. I will stay, and I will listen, and I will remember.*

*You have my word.*

The people did not cheer. They did not weep. They simply continued, as they had always continued, as they would continue for as long as the stone held and the water ran. But there was a new note in the hum now—a note that had been there for hundreds of years, but that now rang clear and distinct and undeniable.

It was the note of being known. The note of being remembered. The note of a promise made in the language of stone and water and patience.

The presence was still there. The presence would always be there.

And the city of Glisten-Reach, which no one called by its true name, hummed its quiet chord into the dark—not alone, not any longer, but accompanied by something vast and patient and kind.

It was enough.

It was more than enough.

---

*It has always been this way. It would always be this way.*

*Except now, someone was listening back.*
