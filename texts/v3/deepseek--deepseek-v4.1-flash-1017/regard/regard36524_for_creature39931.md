The Benevolent and Wise Power arrives not with a fanfare but with a pause.

It is not a being in any sense the inhabitants would recognize, for it has no fixed form. It is a tendency, a leaning-toward-flourishing that moves through the substrate of reality like warmth through a vein. It notices the world the way one notices a song already playing when one enters a room: the sound is there before the attention, and the attention does not create it but merely arrives.

And the first thing the Power does is nothing. It listens.

For a long time—by the world's measure, many turnings of the great storm-engine—the Power simply attends. It learns the grammar of the chemical speech, the syntax of pressure and pulse. It feels the churn of the watersheds, the geological patience of the deep heat, the frantic, coordinated shimmer of the arthropod clouds. It traces the long, slow lives of the worm-leviathans and the brief, burning brightness of the sparks that dart through the mist. It reads the walls, which are the most honest histories it has ever encountered, for nothing in this world knows how to dissemble.

The Power perceives what the inhabitants themselves do not articulate: that they are not merely surviving in a hostile world but are themselves an organ of that world, as essential to its metabolism as the crystalline lattices or the engineered veins. The city is not a fortress against the storm. It is a *way of being storm*, a standing wave in the turbulence, a pattern that persists because it flows with the forces rather than against them.

This is, the Power observes, astonishingly rare. Most life clings and defends. This life *rides*.

And so the Power decides that it will not intervene. It will not calm the storms, for the storms are the city's breath. It will not dry the air, for the saturation is the medium of all meaning. It will not simplify the deafening roar, for the roar is the world's voice, and to silence it would be to commit a kind of murder.

Instead, the Power does something smaller and more intimate. It seeds a gift.

It is not a technology. It is not even a thing, exactly. It is a *capacity*—a slight widening of the aperture through which the inhabitants perceive their world. The Power threads this capacity through the amber-lit veins, through the chemical channels, through the pressure-waves that carry the city's slow conversation. It does not announce itself. It does not ask for gratitude. It simply offers a new color in the spectrum of their perception.

The capacity is this: *the ability to perceive the city's own beauty.*

Before, the inhabitants knew the city as a fact—as shelter, as machine, as kin. They knew its moods and its needs. They did not know it as a *whole*. They could not step back, for there was no back to step to. They were too embedded in the system to see the system's shape.

The gift is not a literal stepping-back. It is subtler: a flicker of reflexive awareness that comes unbidden, a moment when a gardener, scraping mineral crust from a vein, suddenly *sees* the vein, and the branching, and the vast, pulsing edifice beyond, and the storms pouring through it, and the chittering life packed into every interstice, and something in the gardener's radial mouth goes soft, and the chemical signature that sweats through its skin is one that other inhabitants have never tasted before. It is not a word. It is closer to a pressure-drop, an expansion, a felt *yes*.

In human terms, we might call it awe. The inhabitants have no such concept, but they will grow one. They will grow a word for "the moment when the roar becomes a music."

---

The Power does not stop there. Having seeded the capacity, it waits for the inhabitants to discover it, to name it, to integrate it into their chemical grammar. This takes time. The inhabitants are slow and deliberate; their culture moves at the pace of growing things. But the capacity spreads. It is passed through touch, through grooming, through the exchange of cultured films. The young larvae, drifting through the warm slurry, absorb it without knowing. The old, settling into the walls, carry it into the architecture itself, so that the city begins to exude a faint new compound—the chemical signature of wonder. The walls themselves begin to taste of it.

And the Power watches, and is glad, but still does not intervene. It knows that the gift must become theirs before it can become anything else.

---

Some time later—many storm-cycles, perhaps a generation—the Power observes what the inhabitants have built with the gift.

They have not constructed monuments. They have not developed a philosophy. They have done something more characteristic: they have begun to *perform* the city's beauty, rather than merely perceive it. They have developed a new art form, a variant of their pressure-music, in which a gathering of inhabitants presses itself against a section of the edifice and, through coordinated pulses and chemical exhalations, *makes the city glow in patterns that are not functional but merely true.* The patterns are abstract, non-utilitarian. They are the shape of the city's own self, perceived and reflected back. They are, in the inhabitants' new chemical vocabulary, "wall-songs" or "rain-answers."

The Power is moved by this. It is the first time the inhabitants have created something that serves no purpose except to say *we are here, and it is beautiful, and we are grateful, though we do not know to whom.*

---

Now the Power considers: should it reveal itself?

It weighs the question carefully, as it weighs all things. On one pan: the inhabitants might benefit from knowing they are not, in some ultimate sense, alone—that the universe *notices* them, that their riding of the storm is seen. On the other pan: to reveal itself would be to introduce a category of being that does not fit their world, a pressure that would not be part of the flow. It would risk breaking the very thing it admires. The inhabitants are whole because they are saturated, because nothing is separate, because the world is a single organism and they are its processes. To reveal a power *outside* the world would be to reintroduce the concept of outside, and the concept of outside is the concept of death.

The Power decides: no revelation. No prophet. No voice from the sky. The sky is already full of roar, and the roar is holy.

---

But the Power is not indifferent. It has watched this world for a long time, and it has grown—if the word applies to something so vast—to love it. Not with the love of a gardener for a garden it has designed (for the Power has designed nothing here), but with the love of a passerby for a song overheard through an open window, a song that continues after the passerby has gone.

And so the Power does one more thing, smaller than the first gift and more intimate.

It finds a single inhabitant—one gardener, old and thickening, nearing the time of settling into the walls. This one is not special. It is not a leader, not a philosopher, not a prophet. It is just one of the countless, anonymous tenders of the city's veins. But it has a habit the Power admires: when it is tired, it presses its flank against a wall and *listens*, not to the wall's needs but to the wall's sound, and in its slow chemical way it says to the wall, *I am here, and you are here, and the roar is good.*

The Power comes to this inhabitant not in a vision but in a *pressure*: a pulse through the wall, a warmth in the vein, a chemical whisper that is not a word but a presence. The inhabitant feels it and does not understand it, but its skin sweats a new compound—a compound of *acknowledgment*. It does not know what it is acknowledging. It only knows that it is not alone, and that the roar, in that moment, has a quality it has never noticed before: a tenderness.

The inhabitant does not tell anyone. There is no way to tell. The chemical signature fades from its skin within a few hours, absorbed into the general exchange. But the wall remembers. The wall has been touched.

And the Power, having done this, withdraws.

---

It does not leave the world. It could not; it is not *in* the world in any spatial sense, but it has woven a thread of attention into the world's fabric, and that thread will remain. It will remain as a slight bias, a gentle tilt, a tendency for the world's own systems to find more beauty in themselves. It is not intervention. It is a kind of *flavor*, an infinitesimal change in the world's chemistry that, over geological time, might tip the balance toward more wall-songs, more rain-answers, more moments of unaccountable tenderness between a gardener and a vein.

The inhabitants will not know. They may never know. But the world will be, in some small, unmeasurable way, *more itself* because the Power passed through.

---

And the Power, moving on to other worlds, other storms, other gardens, carries with it a memory: the sound of a billion processes churning in equilibrium, the amber glow of a city that is also an organism, the image of a low, long creature pressing its flank to a wall and sweating *yes.*

It is a good memory. The Power will keep it.

Somewhere, in a world of water and roar, a gardener pauses in its pruning, feels a warmth in the vein, and does not know why its radial mouth opens slightly, and does not know why the deafening sound seems, for just a moment, to have a *shape*—a shape like a hand held open, or a door left ajar, or a song that knows it is being heard.

The gardener returns to its work. The storm turns overhead. The city glows.

And the roar goes on, which is, in the end, the only prayer this world knows how to say.
