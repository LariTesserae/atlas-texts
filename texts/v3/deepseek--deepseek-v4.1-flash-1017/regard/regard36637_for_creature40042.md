# The Hand That Does Not Lift

---

The power comes up through the cloud, as everything comes to Sarn, and finds the town already holding the water and the water already holding the town and the two of them in a conversation so old it has become a kind of silence.

The power is not what the Sarnish would call a power. It does not arrive. It has been arriving for a long time — since before the first stone was set on the slope, since before the slope tilted and forgot to keep rising — and it has learned, in that time, the thing that all the best powers learn and most of the worst ones never do: that the first act of benevolence is restraint.

So it does not appear. It does not speak. It does not offer.

It watches.

---

It watches and it learns the town the way the town learns itself: by the walking of it, the touching of it, the returning. It learns the dished stairs and the cambered streets and the exact angle at which a Sarnish hand trails the wall on the way down. It learns the sound of the bell going out into the cloud and coming back changed, and it learns to recognize the quality of the change — which mornings the note returns true and which mornings it returns wrong, and what that means for the day. It learns that the wrong-return is not a warning but a fact, a reading, the way a Sarnish eye flicks to the gutter before the face.

It learns the cisterns. This takes longest. Each one has its own voice when the cover is lifted, its own method of filling, its own line of silt and its own patience. The oldest ones, on the flat paving where the emptiness is widest, have been drawing and holding the same water for so long that the power can taste the years in it, the way you can taste the age of a thing that has been left to weather.

It learns the people.

It learns that they are not, as a rule, interested in being learned. That is the first surprise. The power has come from places where the learned-upon grow restless under attention, where watching is a kind of taking. Here, the Sarnish do not notice the power at all, and it takes the power a while to understand that this is not because they are dull. They are not dull. They are preoccupied. They are attending to things that require attention, and the power, being neither wet nor broken nor likely to need mending, does not fall within the circle of their concern.

It learns to be glad of this. It learns that to be ignored in Sarn is to be given a gift: the freedom to watch without being watched back, to learn without being asked what you intend to do with what you have learned.

It has no intention. That is the second thing it learns. It came up through the cloud with something that might have been called a plan, in the way that a hand extended has a plan, and the hand has learned to stay extended and open and to let the water fall through it and not to close.

---

The Sarnish have a story, though they would not call it that. It is not told. It is known. It is in the walls, in the fact that no one can say when the last new building went up, in the way a rebuilt house is rebuilt to be indistinguishable from what it replaced. The story is this: the town is at the point of the curve where it flattens, and the point of the curve is not an ending. It is a resting place. Things come to rest here. Things that come to rest here are not lost. They are held.

The power finds this story in the joints of the paving, in the moss that grows only where the water is held long enough, in the way a dead person's house is not emptied but closed and maintained and after a while someone moves in and the repairs are still in the walls. The power finds it and recognizes it and understands, slowly, that it has come to a place that has already made its compact with something larger than itself.

What would a wise power do, finding this?

First, it would not interrupt.

It would stand — or, having no body, it would settle, the way the fog settles, into the lowest and most patient form it can hold — and it would let the town continue. It would let the gutters run and the cisterns fill and the bell make its flat note into the cloud. It would let the goats stand on the covers in the cold and the chough come and go from the bell cot and the something in the fog pass overhead unremarked. It would let the Sarnish go up and down the stairs with their short patient stride and their soft mended boots and their tools always returning to their hooks.

Second, it would listen.

Not for words. There are few words. It would listen for the point at which the listening becomes a kind of permission — the point at which being present in a place is no longer an intrusion but a participation, a holding-alongside. It would listen for the moment when the town, without knowing it, has begun to lean a degree toward the presence, the way the walls lean a degree inward and have leaned that degree for a hundred years. It would not mistake this for invitation. It would understand it as the town being what the town is: a place that holds, and holds best what has been there long enough to be trusted.

Third — and this is the part that would require the most wisdom, the most restraint — it would do something small.

Something with an end you can see.

---

What the power would do:

It would go to the granary.

The granary stands alone on its stubby stone legs, its under-croft dark and dry and full of wind, and it has stood there long enough that its stone legs have settled into the slope by exactly the amount that stone legs settle in that time, and its slate courses have shifted by the minute degrees that slate courses shift, and there is a place — a single place, on the north face, near the base, in the third course up — where the lime mortar has gone soft in a way that the power can see and the Sarnish have not yet noticed.

The power would not fix it. Fixing would be an improvement, and the town has already decided that improvement is a disturbance.

But the power would learn the mortar. It would learn the exact colour of it, the exact age, the exact proportion of lime to sand to whatever else was in the mix when it was made. It would learn the way this particular mortar has failed — not all at once, not dramatically, not in a way that needs a scaffold or a committee or a closed road, but slowly, grain by grain, in the way of a thing that has been holding for centuries and is now considering the possibility of letting go.

And then it would wait.

It would wait for the moment when the soft mortar becomes a problem the Sarnish can see — when a chip of it falls and lands on the dry earth of the under-croft, and someone notices, and someone else says *that's been coming*, and a third person says *I'll see to it in the spring*, and then the spring comes and it is seen to. The power would wait for that, and it would do nothing. It would not speed the falling of the chip. It would not whisper advice into the ear of the person who notices. It would not make the spring come faster.

It would simply be present, and it would have learned the mortar, and when the Sarnish came with their lime and their sand and their small trowel, the power would be present in the learning the way a hand is present in a hand that has done the same work many times. Not guiding. Not helping. Just there. A quality of attention that makes the work go a little more surely, the way the stone under a mason's hand is a little more certain of itself when the mason is certain.

The Sarnish would not notice. They would say, afterward, that the mortar had gone in well, that the lime had slaked true, that the chip had come away cleanly and the new work would hold. They would say this in the way they say all things that have gone as they should: briefly, and then not again.

And the power would have done what it came to do.

---

What it would not do:

It would not appear to the Sarnish. It would not manifest. It would not send a sign.

The Sarnish have no word for the kind of power that reveals itself, because they have never needed one. Their compact is with the mountain and the fog and the water and the stone, and those things do not reveal themselves; they are simply there, and they are simply what they are, and you learn them by living with them and you live with them by learning them and neither the living nor the learning is ever finished. A power that appeared would be a power that had not understood this. It would be a power that had mistaken attention for intervention, and intervention for benevolence, and benevolence for something that needs to be seen.

The power that comes to Sarn does not need to be seen. It has learned what the Sarnish know in their hands: that the best work is the work that does not need to be noticed. The hinge that does not squeak. The slate that does not slip. The mortar that does not need replacing for another century. The bell that comes back from the cloud changed but not wrong.

It would not make the town better. It would not make the town safer or richer or more beautiful. It would not draw the town's attention.

It would make the town a little more itself.

---

There is a thing the Sarnish say, though not often, and not in words: *the water goes around.* It means the water is drawn and used and returned to the ground and drawn again, and it means the same is true of everything else — of work and rest and grief and the closing of doors and the after-a-while of someone moving in. It means the town is a circuit and the circuit is its own purpose and the purpose is fulfilled in the fulfilling.

A wise power, watching this, would want to join the circuit.

Not as a component. Not as a source. As a quality of the circuit's going-around. As the thing that makes the water a little more willing to be held, the stone a little more willing to be fitted, the hand a little more certain of its work. As the patience that is not the Sarnish's patience and not the mountain's patience but something like the space between them, the space that allows them to be in conversation.

The power would be the silence that is not empty.

It would be there in the moment when a Sarnish hand lifts a slate and holds it, testing its ring, and the ring is true, and the hand sets it in place, and the work is done, and the tool goes back on its hook. It would be there not as a cause but as a condition — the condition of things being what they are, which is the only condition the power knows how to offer and the only condition the Sarnish know how to accept.

---

And in time — in the long time that is the only time that matters here — the power would learn something it did not know when it came up through the cloud.

It would learn that benevolence is not a thing you do.

It is a thing you are.

It is a way of being present that does not disturb, a way of holding that does not grasp, a way of attending that does not demand. It is the moss in the joints of the paving, which does not improve the paving but which is there because the paving holds the water long enough, and which makes the paving a little more what it is: a hand held out, and filled, and emptied, and filled again.

The power would learn to be moss.

It would learn to be the lichen that takes a century to make a circle the size of a plate, and to be content with that, and to know that the century is not a long time if you are not in a hurry, and that the Sarnish are not in a hurry, and that the mountain is not in a hurry, and that the fog comes up and leaves and comes up again and is not in a hurry either.

It would learn the flat note of the bell and the way it comes back changed and the way the change is not a warning but a reading — a way of knowing the day, a way of knowing the cloud, a way of knowing what needs doing and what needs to be let alone.

It would learn to be at the point of the curve where it flattens.

---

The being who lives in Sarn — not the Sarnish, not the town, but the presence that is the town and the Sarnish and the mountain and the fog in conversation — would not notice the power's arrival. It would not notice the power's presence. It would notice, in time, that the mortar on the north face of the granary had gone in well and was holding. It would notice that the hinge that had been mended with an iron pin was mending true. It would notice that the bell's note came back changed in the way it was supposed to come back changed, and that the change was a little clearer, a little more legible, a little more like a word you can almost hear.

And it would nod.

That is the whole of it, usually. Not unfriendliness. A nod is a complete sentence in Sarn.

The power would nod back, in the way of a power that has learned to be moss, and the conversation would continue, and the water would go around, and the stone would hold, and the bell would make its flat note into the cloud and come back changed, and nothing would need a second look.

---

The power would stay.

It would stay the way the fog stays in the middle of the morning, thinning but not gone, present but not intrusive, holding the town in a quality of attention that the town does not need and would not miss and could not do without. It would stay because it had come up through the cloud and found a place that was already at rest, and it had learned — slowly, the way all the best things are learned — that the only thing to do with a place like that is to let it be, and to be there while it is, and to be changed by the being-there in ways that do not need to be named.

It would stay because the water goes around, and it wanted to go around with it.

It would stay because the bell makes its flat note and comes back changed, and it wanted to hear the change.

It would stay because the moss grows in the joints of the paving, and it had learned to be moss, and moss does not leave.

It would stay because the Sarnish put things back as they found them, and it had found the town as it was, and it would put it back as it was, and the putting-back would be its whole work and its whole gift and its whole self.

And the town, not noticing, would be a little more itself.

And the water would go around.

And the bell would come back changed.

And that would be enough.

---

In the meeting house, where the power never goes, there is a bench along one wall, worn into shallow dishes by the sitting of many people over many years. On the bench, in the way of things in Sarn, there is nothing. No cushion. No carving. No mark except the wear.

The power has learned to be like that bench.

It has learned to be the thing that is shaped by what it holds, and to hold what shapes it, and to be content with the wearing, and to know that the wearing is the work and the work is the wearing and there is no difference between them.

It has learned to be at the point of the curve where it flattens.

It has learned to be, and not to do, and not to offer, and not to improve, and not to appear, and not to stay — no, that is wrong. It has learned to stay. It has learned that staying is the only offering, the only improvement, the only appearance that matters: the staying that is not a demand but a condition, the way the mountain stays and the fog stays and the stone stays and the water stays, going around and around, holding and being held.

It has learned to be the holding.

---

And if, some morning, a Sarnish hand should pause on a slate and feel that the ring was a little truer than it had been, or if a Sarnish eye should flick to a mortar joint and see that the lime had bitten a little more surely than it might have, or if a Sarnish ear should catch the bell's note coming back changed in a way that was not wrong but was not quite the way it had been before —

— they would not say anything. They would not look up. They would not speculate. They would put the slate in place, and return the tool to its hook, and go on down the stairs with their short patient stride, hands free or one hand trailing the wall, and the fog would take them, and the sound of them would go on being audible after they were gone, and then it would not be.

And the power would be there, in the silence that is not empty, in the space between the steps, in the quality of the holding.

And that would be enough.

And that would be everything.
