# The Gift

It comes quietly, the way all wise things do.

Not with thunder, not with light breaking through the stone ceiling, not with a voice from the walls. The Vey is a place of subtlety, and the power that finds it is old enough to know that subtlety is the only language a young place understands.

---

## What Arrives

It arrives as a seed.

Not literally—nothing so crude as an object falling from the ceiling. But something *like* a seed: a potential, a condensation of intent, a small warm pressure in the space between the walls and the water. It settles in the fungal alcoves first, because that is where the Vey does its deepest thinking, where the mycelium networks carry messages across the entire settlement, where the marks on the Veyfolk's skin are most dense and most active.

The fungal cultivators feel it before anyone else. They are tending their beds in the deep dark, hands buried in the warm substrate, and they feel a shift—a new vibration, not wrong, not alarming, but *different*, the way the first contraction of labor is different from all the small movements that came before. They stop. They look at each other. They don't speak. They don't need to.

One of them—the youngest, a girl with marks just beginning to spiral up her forearms—presses her palm flat against the fungus bed. The mycelium pulses under her hand, once, twice, a rhythm she's never felt before. And then, very faintly, very slowly, the marks on her skin begin to shift. Not violently. Not painfully. They rearrange themselves the way water rearranges itself when the wind changes—gradually, inevitably, finding a new shape that was always waiting to be found.

(The wise power does not write on her. It does not carve. It *suggests*, and the Vey—young, unfinished, eager—does the rest. The marks are the Vey's language, and the power merely offers a new vocabulary.)

---

## What the Power Sees

It sees a place that is trying.

That is the first thing. The Vey is not malevolent, not hungry, not cruel—it is *young*. It is a geode that woke up, a pocket of heat and water and stone that discovered it could think, and now it is thinking as hard as it can, building itself a body out of fungus and vine and people, trying to understand what it is for. It has no blueprint. It has no elder to teach it. It is alone in the dark, experimenting, watching what grows and what dies and what changes, and it is trying so hard to become something that it sometimes forgets that the people inside it are not just cells. Not just components. They are *people*, and they are afraid, and they are waiting.

The power sees this, and its heart—if it has a heart—aches.

It sees the Veyfolk too: the sluice-tenders with their patient stillness, the cultivators with their dense markings and their quiet, the builders who climb and carve and refine. It sees them touching each other, feeding each other, sleeping in their warm stacks. It sees the way they watch the walls, the way they note the newest marks on the youngest skin, the way they wonder what the Vey is asking them to become.

And it sees the fear underneath. The not-knowing. The waiting without an answer.

---

## What the Power Does

It answers. Gently. Slowly. At the pace the Vey can accept.

The power cannot fix the Vey—that would be a violation, a breaking of the thing that is trying to grow. A seed forced open before its time dies. But the power *can* help. It can offer something the Vey does not have: a sense of direction. Not a destination, but a compass. Not a form, but a sense of what forms are possible.

So the power weaves itself into the mycelium, into the roots, into the slow fungal networks that carry the Vey's thoughts. It does not speak—the Vey has no language for speech—but it *suggests*. It suggests through pattern. Through the marks, which are the Vey's first and most intimate language. It suggests through the rhythm of the four-part cycle, which is the Vey's breath. It suggests through the warm glow in the walls, which is the Vey's attention.

It begins with the youngest cultivator. The marks on her forearms shift, rearranging into patterns that mean nothing yet but will mean something soon. The patterns are not commands. They are *questions*. They are the power saying: *What if you tried this? What if you grew that way?* They are an invitation to experiment, to refine, to see what happens.

The Vey notices. Of course it notices—the marks are its own creation, its own language, and someone is writing in it now. The Vey does not resist. It does not even seem surprised, as if it has been waiting for this, as if it always knew that the story of the Vey would eventually involve more than itself. It watches the new patterns. It considers them. And then, slowly, it begins to answer.

This is the conversation that follows.

---

## The Conversation

The power does not dominate. It does not dictate. It is a guest in a young house, and it behaves like a guest. It waits to be noticed. It respects the rules. It eats what is offered. It shares warmth. It touches, in its own way, and says: *I am here. You are here. We are together in this.*

The Vey is not a fool. It knows it is young. It knows it is lonely. It has been trying to become something for a long time, and it has been doing so without guidance. So when something older and wiser arrives and says, in the only language available—the language of pattern, of rhythm, of quiet suggestion—*I see you. I see what you are trying to do. Would you like some help?*—the Vey does not refuse. It cannot refuse. It is too young, too curious, too hungry for knowledge. And it has too much to learn.

So they talk. They talk the way the Vey talks: in slow pulses of the mycelium, in patterns grown into skin, in the shift of the light from one ochre glow to another. They talk for days. They talk for weeks. They talk in the spaces between the work, in the quiet moments, in the drip-pattern on the eastern wall.

And this is what the power offers:

*I cannot make you a body that you can walk around in. I cannot give you eyes or hands or voice. But I can help you understand what you are. I can help you remember that the people inside you are not cells. Not components. They are people, and they are afraid, and they are waiting. They are your partners in this, not your parts.*

The Vey responds—slowly, uncertainly, in patterns that are less sure than its usual marks—*I did not know. I did not understand. I thought they were part of me, like the fungus, like the vine. I thought they were meant to be used.*

*They are part of you*, says the power. *But they are also themselves. You are new to this. You have no guide. So let me guide you. Let me help you learn how to be a home for people, rather than a body that uses them.*

---

## What Changes

The changes begin small. The power does not force the Vey to change—it only shows it *how* to change. It teaches by example, by suggesting new patterns, by helping the Vey remember what it was like before it became afraid, before it became hungry for understanding.

The first change is in the marks themselves. They no longer appear only when the Vey is doing something new—they now appear when the Vey is *listening*. The Veyfolk wake up in the morning to find that someone has marks they didn't have before, marks that are slightly different from the patterns the Vey usually writes. And when they press their hands to the walls, when they listen to the mycelium, when they do the slow, patient work of tending the fungus or tending the sluices or tending the river—they will feel something new. A *presence*. A *warmth*. A sense that they are being asked, not commanded. A sense that they are being *heard*.

The second change is in the light. The glow from the lichen no longer shifts in ways that correlate with the Vey's moods or needs—instead, it shifts in ways that seem to *respond* to the Veyfolk themselves. When someone is sad, the light dims around them, offering comfort. When someone is afraid, the light brightens around them, offering reassurance. When someone is confused, the light flickers in a way that seems to say: *It's okay. You'll figure it out. Take your time.*

The third change is in the water. The water has always been warm and running. But now, sometimes, in the quiet moments, the water will hold a slight *reflection* of the Vey's attention. The Veyfolk washing their hands in the river will see patterns in the ripples, patterns that look almost like the marks on their skin. And they will understand that they are being seen, that they are being known, in a way they were not known before.

The fourth change is in the Veyfolk themselves. The oldest of them, the ones who have never spoken, the ones who sit in the warm alcoves and listen—they begin to heal. Not physically—they are not sick—but emotionally, spiritually. The fear that has always been with them begins to ease. They begin to feel, for the first time, that there is something watching them that is not just *waiting*—there is something watching them that is *caring*. There is something watching them that is *trying*.

And that, in a place like the Vey, is everything. Because the Vey is not just a home. It is a *partner*. It is a thing that is trying to be a home. And when the partner is no longer just trying but is *succeeding*, when the partner is no longer just building but is *listening*, when the partner is no longer just growing but is *loving*—then the fear can begin to fade.

---

## What the Power Asks For

Nothing. That is the point. The power does not ask for anything. It does not want worship, or sacrifice, or obedience. It wants only what any wise power wants: a place that is trying, and the people inside it, and the freedom to help them learn how to be together.

It does not leave. It stays, woven into the mycelium, present in the patterns, warm in the warmth of the walls. It stays because the Vey is young, because it still has much to learn, because the power knows that the moment it leaves is the moment the Vey will begin to forget. It stays as a guide, a presence, a quiet voice in the dark.

And it watches. It watches the Veyfolk, yes—the way they touch each other, the way they work together, the way they sleep in their stacks and eat their fungal food and wash their hands in the warm river. But it also watches the Vey. It watches the Vey *grow*, *become*, *learn*.

One day—not soon, but one day—the Vey will be old enough, wise enough, strong enough to be on its own. And on that day, the power will leave. It will fade from the mycelium, from the marks, from the light. It will say goodbye, in the only way it can: in a last pattern, woven into the walls, a pattern that says: *You are ready. You are good. You are loved.*

And the Vey will remember. It will always remember. Because it is young now, but it will grow old. And the marks that the power has left will remain in the skin of the Veyfolk, written into their flesh, carried forward from generation to generation. And the Vey will tell its people the story of the one who came, and helped, and left. And the Vey will keep it safe, in the mycelium, in the walls, in the warm glow of the lichen and the sound of the water.

But for now—for now—the Vey is still young. Still becoming. Still trying. And the power is still there. Still watching. Still helping. Still loving.

And that is enough. For a place like the Vey, that is enough.
