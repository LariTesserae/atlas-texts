It would come as a guest, not as weather.

That is the first thing. A benevolent and wise power would not arrive in the Cup like a new climate—not with light, not with a voice from the rim, not with a correction. It would understand that the place is already loud, already full, already in the middle of becoming something. The lattice is doing work. The keepers have a language. The mountain is throwing a cup and has not finished. To intervene before listening would be a kind of vandalism, even if it were done gently.

So it would stand outside the rim for a long time. Long enough to learn the pulse. Long enough to see that the mist-feeders brighten in sequence with the threads. Long enough to notice that the oldest keepers do not make the lattice brighten differently when they pass beneath it, and to understand what that means without saying it aloud. It would not name the mountain. It would not put it on a map. It would not break a thread to test a hypothesis.

Then it would begin to do small things, and most of them would happen outside the cup.

It would make the outside world less hungry. No mining crews would find the mineral bands. No collectors would learn the price of a mist-feeder. No documentary team would be allowed to turn the keepers into a spectacle. The approach would stay long and quiet and a little difficult, not because the power wanted to keep people out, but because it wanted everyone who came up to arrive with their own legs and their own reasons. It would guard the airspace and the watershed and the silence, and it would do this so subtly that the keepers would only notice that fewer strange lights came over the shoulder of the mountain in the dry months.

It would build a dry room below the rim—not inside it, not on the soft stone, but a little way down where the weather loosens its grip. A warm room with a hearth and clean water brought from somewhere else, because the pool does not want to be a pool anywhere else. The room would have a door that closes. It would have a bed that does not accrete. It would be a place where a person could sit for a night and decide whether to go up, or come down, or stay. The most benevolent thing is not always to keep people; sometimes it is to make the descent survivable. So it would also fix a rope, maintain a shelter halfway down, and leave lamps that do not go out in the cold. It would not stop the mineral from growing on the keepers’ hands and forearms. That is the Cup’s work, and the keepers have chosen it. But for anyone who asked, it would offer a brake—not a cure, not a command, just a way to slow the building long enough to decide again. It would offer, and then it would step back.

It would learn the keepers’ words. *The work. The big lungs. The turning. Gone into the floor.* It would use those words and not correct them. It would ask the two oldest which came first, and when they disagreed, it would accept both answers and write them down in a notebook that stayed dry. It would sit at the north shelf in the long dim evenings and say almost nothing, and the mist-feeders would climb onto its knees because they climb onto warm things, and it would let them.

It would not treat the keepers as patients to be rescued, or as martyrs to be admired. A wise power knows the difference between harm and transformation that someone has chosen, and it knows that the difference is consent. So it would make consent clearer. It would make sure no one was built into the floor by accident, or by loneliness, or because they had come up without knowing what the Cup does. It would listen for the moment when a keeper’s choice stopped being a choice, and it would offer the dry room and the rope and the lamp, and if the keeper still said no, it would honor that. It would mourn loudly only outside the cup.

With the lattice, it would be more careful still. It would place one filament of itself at the rim—hair-thin, pale, patient—and wait to see whether the work grew toward it. If the work grew around it, it would treat that as a question and answer slowly, in the same pulse, in the same amber, in the same small brightenings. If the work did not, it would withdraw the filament and stay outside. It would not seize the pulse to learn what it computes. It would build a listening station at the base of the mountain, a quiet room of recorders and paper and no broadcast, and it would keep two copies of everything: one for the keepers, one for itself. If the work is a mind, the record is a courtesy. If the work is a message, the record is a held breath. If the work is only weather and mineral and time, the record is still a kind of respect.

It would protect the animals by protecting the routes and the water and the quiet. It would not feed the climbers or tame the mist-feeders. It would not drive away the large things that make everything go silent for a moment. It would understand that the Cup is not a sanctuary in the sense of a place without teeth. It is a place where many kinds of life have agreed to be close to the same warm water, and the agreement is old and not entirely peaceful. The power would guard the agreement, not rewrite it.

And if a thread broke—if someone careless or frightened or cruel put a hand through the work—it would not replace the thread with its own design. It would hold the pattern as still as it could while the Cup regrew, and then it would let go. It would not pretend that nothing was lost. It would sit with the keepers while they named what had changed.

Then it would do the hardest thing, which is the thing wise powers do best: it would remain mostly absent. It would not make the Cup safe by making it smaller. It would not dry the air to stop the sweating stone. It would not turn the keepers into a protected species or the lattice into a monument. It would keep the outside world from eating the place, keep the way down open, keep the record, keep watch. It would let the rim grow a little thicker each year. It would let the work go on.

And in the long dim evenings, when the pulse traveled all the way across the net and came back, and the mist-feeders crackled, and the mountain breathed through its cracks, and the two oldest keepers disagreed about which of them had arrived first, the power would be there at the edge of hearing—not as a voice, not as a correction, only as a margin. A little more room to choose. A little less cold on the way down. A witness that did not own what it witnessed.

That is what it would do, if it were wise enough to be small.
