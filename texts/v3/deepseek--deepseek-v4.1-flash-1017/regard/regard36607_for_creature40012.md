# The Keeping

---

The power came down through the water slowly, because it had learned that slowness was a form of respect, and because it had time.

It did not come as light, or as voice, or as any of the shapes that powers wear when they wish to be seen. It came as a *quality* of the water — a settling, a clarity, the way a room feels different when someone kind has just left it. The suspended haze thinned by a fraction. The halos around the lamps drew in close. The eels at the outfalls stirred and then lay still again, and the little translucent things in the cisterns rose in a slow spiral and hung, trembling, at the top of the water, and then went back down.

It moved through the streets of the Sump the way an old woman moves through a house she built with her own hands — touching nothing, but noticing. It noticed the tar on the window frames, renewed twice a year, done well. It noticed the cables gone slack with weed and the catwalks over the catwalks. It noticed the nine pumps, which were not nine pumps but one pump wearing nine bodies, and it noticed that this was a thing people had made without knowing they were making it.

It noticed, most of all, the *balance*. The silt falls; the pumps push. The weed grows; the people cut it back. The walls weather; the tar is renewed. A thousand small negotiations, none of them heroic, all of them kept. And it understood that this was a kind of prayer — the only kind that had ever really worked — and that the people here had been praying it for a very long time without ever once saying the word.

---

What the power did, in the end, was very little. That is the first thing to understand about it. It had come down expecting perhaps to *fix* something — that was its habit, with places like this, and it had learned over a long time that fixing was often the least useful thing a power could do. It had fixed cities before. It had raised them up and dried them out and given them back their skies, and it had watched them die of the curing, because a place that has been in balance for a hundred years does not want to be made well. It wants to be *kept*.

So the power kept it.

It did this in small ways, the way a good gardener works. It nudged the hot seam under the shelf by a fraction of a degree, so that the water in the lee of the outfalls stayed warm enough for the eels through the cool months and the algae bloomed a little earlier and the weed grew a little thicker on the lee faces of the walls — not enough to choke the intakes, just enough to keep the cutters busy and the people fed on weed and eel and the sweet cloudy water of the warm season. It looked at the ballast on Old Six and saw that it was wearing and did not replace it, because a bearing that wears slowly is a bearing that keeps people in work, and work is a kind of keeping too. It thinned the silt on the north side of the seawall by a hand's breadth, just enough to keep the wall standing another decade without anyone having to notice it was failing. It did nothing at all about the pumps, because the pumps were already doing what they did, and a power that respects a thing does not interrupt it.

And it watched Mira Tend.

---

It watched her cut weed from the intakes, four days in six, and it noticed how she did it — the angle of the blade, the way she let the current carry the cut weed away from the intake rather than fighting it, the way she checked the housing afterward with the flat of her hand before she checked it with her eyes. It watched her teach the pretending ones the trick of holding their breath, which is to stop wanting the surface, and it understood that this was the truest thing anyone in the Sump had ever said, and that it had been said without knowing it was true.

It watched her sit on the catwalk in the evening and watch the kelp swing, and it understood that she was not wasting time. She was *keeping* time. She was the kind of person who noticed the eel with the mark on its flank, and the heavier silt on the north wall, and the bearing that had bit her hand when she was nineteen and was still biting, in its way, every time she put her hand on a housing. She was the kind of person a place grew when it had been in balance a long time and wanted to stay that way. She was not a hero. She was a *keel*.

The power did not speak to her. It had learned that speaking was usually a mistake with people like Mira Tend, who would not believe it and would be embarrassed for whoever had tried. Instead it did the thing that powers do when they wish to be understood by someone who will not be spoken to: it made itself felt as an *easing*. The bearing on Old Six turned a fraction more freely. The weed came away from the intake a fraction more cleanly. The tar on her window frame held through a season it should not have held through. The silt on the north wall settled a fraction less. She noticed. Of course she noticed. She noticed everything. And she did not know what to make of it, and she stood on the catwalk one evening with her hand on the rail and looked out at the brass-colored water and the far chimneys, and she said, to no one, in her low flat voice:

"Something's holding."

And the power, which was not a voice and not a light, did not answer, but it *was glad*, in the way that a gardener is glad when a plant turns its leaves toward a window.

---

What it did about Teal was harder.

The power had watched a great many young ones want to go up. It had watched them go, and it had watched some of them come back, and it had watched the ones who did not come back become, in the memory of the place, a kind of weather — a thing that was talked about in the warm months and forgotten in the cool. It knew that Teal would go up. It knew this the way it knew the tide and the pumps. It was not a thing to be prevented. It was a thing to be *kept* — and keeping, for a young one who wants the sky, is not the same as holding.

So the power did the only thing it could do, which was to make the surface a little kinder than it had been. It thinned the haze in the upper water so that the light came down a fraction brighter. It stilled the chop on the far side of the seawall so that a swimmer coming up through the green-gold water would break into a calm sea and a wide sky and not into the grey confusion that had met Mira when she was twenty-five. It put a few more of the little translucent things in the cisterns, because Teal liked them, and because a young one who is leaving should carry something small and strange and alive in her memory of home.

And it did not tell Mira. Mira would find out. Mira would stand on the catwalk and watch her daughter go up through the green-gold water and the brass water and the hand of pressure, and she would not weep into her mask this time, because the power had arranged, in its small way, that the light would be kind. And when Teal came back down — and she would come back down, the power had seen to that too, in the way that powers see to things, which is to say it had done nothing and merely understood that a young one who has seen the sky and been given a calm sea to see it over is a young one who will want to tell someone about it, and the someone is always the mother — when Teal came back down, the lights would come up out of the murk, and she would weep into her mask the way her mother had, and she would not be able to explain it either, and that would be all right. That was the keeping.

---

The power stayed a while. It stayed through a warm season and a cool season. It watched the algae bloom red and the water go cloudy and sweet, and it watched the water clear and the whole drowned borough stand up in the brass-colored water like a thing remembered rather than seen. It watched Mira mend the tar on her window frame with a hot iron and a pot of pitch, and it noticed that she did it well, and it was glad. It watched the pretending ones become naturals. It watched the eel with the mark on its flank lie in a knot in the hot water and then unknot itself and go grazing along the edge of the district, and it watched the large plated thing come in from the silt plain, once, and graze the eelgrass at the district's edge, and go back out again, and it understood that this was the whole of the place's strangeness and that it was enough.

And then it went up, through the green-gold water and the brass water and the hand of pressure, and out into the light, and it did not look back, because looking back is a thing that powers do when they are not sure they have done enough, and this power was sure.

It had done very little. It had eased a bearing and thinned a haze and calmed a sea and put a few more translucent things in a cistern. It had not fixed anything. It had not saved anyone. It had not spoken a word, and no one in the Sump would ever know it had been there, except perhaps Mira Tend, who had said *something's holding* to no one on a catwalk one evening, and had been right.

That was the keeping. That was all of it. The silt falls; the pumps push. The weed grows; the people cut it back. The walls weather; the tar is renewed. And somewhere above, in the green-gold water and the brass water, a power that had learned to be patient went on its way, carrying the Sump with it the way a swimmer carries a held breath — not as a burden, but as a thing kept, a thing treasured, a thing that would be let go only when it was time, and not before.

---

*The pumps go on beating. The kelp goes on swinging. The silt comes down in its fine slow snow and is carried away again, fathom by fathom, year by year, and the lights burn in both the water and the air, and the district glows down there like a thing that has chosen to stay.*

*And it is kept.*
