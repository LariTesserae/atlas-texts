# The Attention

The power came the way damp comes—not through a door, not along a path, but by being already present in the walls by the time anyone thought to look. It was the kind of power that could have done anything. This is worth saying plainly, because what it did was so little that it might be mistaken for nothing.

It could have brought the crowd the town was built for. It could have finished the chambers, made the light reliable, taught the sound to carry. It could have named the three caretakers and given them a fourth, and a fifth, and a thousandth. It could have made the plant grow faster and the pale ones larger and the stone set. It could have done all of this in an afternoon and been thanked for it.

It did none of it. The first wise thing it did was nothing.

For a long time—long in the way the stone is long, which is not the same as long in the way a person is long—it simply breathed. It found the ninety-second cycle and joined it, one more slow dilation in the ceiling's tidal rise and fall, and it let the floor press its print into the soft stone and it let the print smooth. It stood in a chamber with no door and did not look for the purpose of the chamber. It learned the rules by being subject to them. It learned that a shout goes four yards and is gone, and so it did not shout. It learned that distance is unreliable, and so it did not measure. It learned that nothing here is finished, and it did not hasten to finish anything.

Then it began to move.

It moved slowly. It moved the way a person moves through a room where someone is sleeping. It found the first of the three caretakers at a condenser, wiping the sweat from the coil with a cloth that had been wiped so many times it was mostly habit, and it sat down at a little distance and did not speak, because speech does not carry and also because there was nothing to say that would not have been a demand. It matched the caretaker's pace. When the caretaker moved to the next condenser, it moved. When the caretaker stopped to listen to the sap in the pipe—ear against the metal, eyes closed, the loudest thing in the world—it listened too. The caretaker did not see it. But the caretaker's shoulders came down a fraction, the way shoulders do when a room is warmer than it was, and that was enough.

It found the second caretaker asleep in a shed with the door standing open, and it stood in the doorway for a while and kept the door from swinging, which was a small thing, and then it went on.

It found the third caretaker at a puddle, breaking the film with one finger and watching the smell come up—mineral and green at once—and it crouched on the other side of the puddle and put its own finger in. The film broke. The smell came up. The caretaker did not look up, but after a moment the caretaker's hand moved half an inch closer on the stone, which is the way you move toward a warmth you have not decided to trust.

It took a pale one onto its palm. This was the strangest thing it did, and the most deliberate. The pale one tasted the palm with its feet and found nothing edible and was not distressed. It folded its legs in and rested, looking like a dropped piece of something. The power held it for the length of one breath—ninety seconds, the length of the world's breath—and then set it back down among the creases, and the pale one went back into the tissue through a hole that closed softly, like a mouth, and the power felt the floor give under its own weight and understood that it had been tasted in return.

It walked the long straight runs of the town. This was the thing it had wanted to do from the beginning. The streets were laid out for a crowd, and the crowd was not there, and the power walked them anyway, slowly, chamber by chamber, and it let its attention fill the emptiness the way water fills a basin—not by adding anything, but by being present in every part of it. The rooms with no purpose and no door had a purpose for an hour: they held an attention that had come a long way to be there. The empty floor with the print of something's feet on it had, for an hour, a second set of prints beside the first. This is not nothing. A room that has never been used is a different room from a room that has been used once and then emptied. The power used the rooms. It used them carefully, and it used them well, and then it stood in the largest chamber of all—the one built for a multitude, the one that had made it want to bring the multitude—and it felt the pull to fill it, and it felt the pull the way you feel the pull to wake a sleeper, and it did not.

Because it had understood something by then. It had understood that the emptiness was not a failure. The town had been built for a population that never arrived, and a population had arrived anyway—just not at the scale the builder had in mind. The pale ones were the citizenry. The plant was the house. The three were the caretakers of a works that fed a multitude they could not see, and the multitude ate the film off the puddles and ate each other with a patience that looked like housekeeping, and everyone involved was calm about it. To bring a crowd now would be to break the scale. To finish the town would be to end it. The place was in its first draft, and the draft was not a mistake; the draft was the thing itself, still becoming, and a wise power does not finish what is still becoming. It attends. It waits. It lets the stone smooth itself.

So it did not bring the crowd. It did not make the light. It did not teach the sound to carry.

Except once.

This was its gift, and it gave it the way the caretakers give lamps—as a message, not an illumination. It gave the gift of sound for the length of one breath. Ninety seconds, the length of the ceiling's cycle, no more. For one full breath the tissue that eats sound forgot to eat it, and everything that had been a pressure became a noise. The sap moving in the pipes became a noise, a slow peristalsis you could hear with your ears and not just your teeth. The ceiling breathing became a noise, a vast soft tide. The pale ones became a noise—a rustle and a tap, a multitude underfoot, the sound of a crowd after all. The rain arriving in its narrow columns became a noise. The works became a noise, cool and patient, cut stems and hot metal at once.

The three caretakers heard it. This is the point. They heard the world they had been living in with their hands. One of them laughed—a sound that carried, for once, past four yards, past the shed, past the pump, into the next chamber, where another caretaker heard it and stood very still and then laughed too, and the third heard both and did not laugh but put a hand flat on the floor and left it there, and the floor was warm and moving and full of citizens. For ninety seconds the place was loud. For ninety seconds the emptiness was full of the noise of itself.

Then the tissue remembered, and the sound was gone, and the quiet came back, and it was the same quiet as before and not the same at all. A silence that has been broken once is a different silence. A floor that has been heard is a different floor. The caretakers went back to their condensers and their sap-lines and their puddles. They did not speak of it, because speech does not carry and also because there was nothing to say that would not have been a demand. But they moved differently. They moved like people who had been met.

The power stayed a while longer. It did not leave a lamp, because lamps are warnings and this was not a warning. It did not leave a mark in the stone, because the stone smooths itself and the power had learned to love the smoothing. It left only the thing it had already given: the knowledge, carried in the floor and the pipes and the three pairs of shoulders, that someone had come a long way and had understood. It had understood that the place was not empty; it was underpopulated at one scale and overpopulated at another, and both were true, and neither was a problem. It had understood that the being who lived here—the one house, one crop, three caretakers, a multitude of small soft citizens—was not waiting to be rescued. It was waiting to be seen. So the power saw it. That was the whole of what it did. It saw the place, and the place felt seen, and the feeling went down into the stone, which is still becoming, and which will smooth it away in an hour or a day, and which will have been changed by it in a way that does not show.

Then the power went, the way damp goes—not through a door, not along a path, but by being gradually no longer present in the walls. And the pale ones came out again and walked over the place where it had been standing, as if it were a stone that had not finished being stone, and the stone remembered itself, and the ceiling breathed, and the town went on being enormous and not much of a town, and unfinished, and all right.
