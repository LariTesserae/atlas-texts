**The Kind Power at the Rim**

The first thing it would do is nothing, for long enough that the town could decide whether it was a thing at all. It would not come down through the ceiling in light. It would sit at the broken rim in the marsh, where the ground stops, and listen through one full rise and fall of the water, then another. It would learn the difference between the ticking and a break in the ticking. It would learn that the dripping changes before a wall comes down. It would learn to speak low.

It would see at once that the Sump is not a wound. It is a young place, still becoming—town, ceiling, water, pulse, one being in the making—and its people are not waiting to be rescued. They are the part of it that cuts the ceiling back, moves the water, carries the light, and rebuilds the walkway somewhere else. So the power would not drain the bowl, steady the water, brighten the dark, or stop the walls from slumping. It would not purify the air during the two weeks when the pale sacs open. It would not teach metal or permanent lamps or any gift that would make the town less able to hear its own schedule. It would understand that the Sump’s dangers are not separate from the Sump’s life. To remove them would be to finish a thing that is not finished.

It would ask, in the way that could be answered, what it should not do. It would follow the prohibitions before it knew the reasons: not on the water in those hours, not in those channels, mouth covered during the release, lamp low. It would eat the gray flesh and say it was good. When someone asked whether the flesh was plant or animal, it would laugh in the right place. It would be useful in small ways. It would hold a pole while a house was shifted. It would cut back the ceiling with a bone hook until its arms ached. It would carry a lamp on a pole and learn that light is a risk as much as a need. It would mend a walkway and then step back so someone else could check the knot. It would try to be replaceable. A wise power would know that being needed is a kind of damage.

When a wall slumped—change in the dripping, long tearing, wet enormous thud, water slapping the stilts—it would not hold the wall. It would catch what could be caught: a child, a lamp, the end of a walkway. It would help with the consequences and not with the nature of the place. Then it would help rebuild somewhere else. It would not make the Sump safe from being the Sump.

The one large thing it would do is guard the boundary. It would not wall the Sump off or hide it from the world. But it would make the place difficult to find for those who came to drain it, mine its lights, harvest its ceiling, or turn its people into a project. It would meet such people in the marsh with an argument they could not quite remember, or a fog that led them in a circle, or a simple failure of nerve. It would not do this for the town without asking. It would come to the walkway and say, low, “Something is coming that is not the Sump. Do you want me to turn it aside?” And if they said no, it would not. If they said yes, it would do the smallest thing that would work.

It would offer them one covenant, and no more: “I will not save you from your place. I will not make your water steady or your ceiling still. But I will not let something that is not the Sump take it from you. And I will ask before I act, even then.” It would leave no temple, no law, no metal blade. It would leave a habit: when it came, it asked first. It would remain at the edge, sometimes cutting back the ceiling, sometimes carrying a lamp, sometimes just listening. The town would not worship it. They would hand it a bone hook and tell it to keep its voice down. That would be enough. The Sump would keep becoming. The power would keep attending. That is what benevolence looks like when it is wise: not a hand that lifts the whole bowl, but a hand that holds a pole and then lets go.
