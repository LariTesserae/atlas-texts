The power came the way water comes to the sump—without announcement, without hurry, and without any need to be noticed.

It did not arrive as light or voice. It arrived as attention. The grove felt itself being *regarded*, and the moss did not shrink from it, and the fungi did not dim, and the Sump-Keeper, curled in its hollow, opened one wide-set eye and then the other, and did not move.

The power saw. That was its first act, and for a long while it was its only act. It saw the drop gather on the leaf-tip and fall, and it saw the moss receive it. It saw the beetles on their worn path, and it saw that the path was a kind of memory, and that the memory was older than the beetles. It saw the arc of stones and understood, without counting, that each stone was a rain and a waiting and a hand—patient—nudging the world a finger's width. It saw the three-fingered print on the wall and knew it for what it was: not a claim, but a greeting left for whoever might come, in a language that did not require an answer.

And it saw the Sump-Keeper—saw the long low shape in the water, the smooth back, the breath that barely stirred the duckweed—and it understood that here was a being that had made peace with the ceiling of its world. The ceiling was made of light. The light changed, and the Keeper watched it change, and that was enough. The power, which was old and had seen many beings make war on their ceilings, found this worthy of its full attention.

It did not speak. Speaking is for those who are uncertain. But it *inclined*—the way a wise head inclines toward a small and complete thing—and in that inclination the air of the grove changed by perhaps a degree, or less, the way the sump rises a finger's width after rain and then falls again. Nothing that lived there would have named it. The beetles did not quicken. The frogs did not blink. But the moss, which is slow, seemed to settle a little deeper into the stone, as if it had been assured of something it had always suspected.

What the power did next, it did over seasons, in increments too small to measure, the way the Keeper nudged the stones.

It did not clear the root-ladder. It did not widen the coin of light. It did not drain the sump or teach the Keeper to croak. These would have been *improvements*, and the power had lived long enough to distrust improvements. An improvement is a ceiling, too, and every being deserves to choose its own.

Instead, it did what a good gardener does, and then stopped, and then did less.

It attended to the edges. It noticed that the shelf-fungus sometimes failed in the deepest weeks of the wet, and it sent—not food, but *spores*—a drift on the still air that found the pale shelf and took root there and fruited a season later, enough to carry the Keeper through the lean time without the Keeper ever knowing it had been lean. It noticed that the saplings, twisted by the search for light, sometimes cracked under their own furred weight, and it eased the weight—not with wind, which does not come here, but with a slow loosening in the wood itself, the way a hand opens. It noticed that the water's marks on the eastern wall were fading into the moss, and it did nothing at all, because the marks were the Keeper's book and the Keeper was reading it still, and a book that is read is not lost.

It noticed, too, the handprint. Three fingers, ochre, fading. The power regarded it for a long time, and then it did the one thing it had not yet done. It *touched* the print—not to restore it, not to seal it, but as one touches a letter from someone long gone, with the flat of its regard—and it left a little of itself in the ochre, the way warmth stays in a stone after the sun has moved on.

The Sump-Keeper felt this. It had no word for it. It came up from the sump in the hour when the waxy flowers open, and it climbed the eastern wall to the ledge, and it sat, and it pressed its three-fingered hand to the print on the north wall—the first time it had done so in many rains—and the ochre warmed under its palm. The Keeper's throat pulsed. It did not make a sound. But it pressed harder, and the print took a little of its stain again, and the two—the old mark and the hand that made it—were, for a moment, the same age.

The power watched this and was glad, and its gladness was not the gladness of having helped. It was the gladness of having *witnessed*—of having been present at the exact moment when a patient being discovered that its patience had been kept faith with, by something it had never known to expect.

Then the power did the hardest thing a benevolent power can do. It *withdrew*. Not entirely—it does not entirely withdraw from places it has loved—but it stepped back to the lip of the sinkhole, to the bright and dry and loud world above, and it left the grove to its own time.

It left the rain to fall in its long soft exhalations. It left the drops to land with their small wet *tock* on the broad leaves, and the moss to swallow the sound. It left the flowers to open in the strongest hour of the light and close into seed-pearl buds. It left the beetles their worn path, the frogs their starfish toes, the fungi their hundred small lights. It left the arc of stones, and the ledge, and the hollow in the moss, and it left the water to rise a finger's width after rain and fall again over days.

And it left the Sump-Keeper with its hand against the ochre print, warmed by a regard it would never name, in a grove that had been seen and found sufficient and left, gently, to continue.

The Keeper sat on the ledge until the light above went from gold to blue. Then it climbed down, unhurried, and folded itself into the moss hollow, and closed its eyes, and laid one broad soft-palmed hand flat against the stone, the way it had always done. Above it the coin of light turned over, and the insects hummed their low hum, and a drop gathered on a leaf-tip and fell.

And the grove continued, warm and wet and patient, secure in the knowledge—which is not knowledge, but something older, something the moss knows—that it had been found worthy of attention, and that the attention had not required it to become anything other than what it already was.

The handprint on the wall keeps its three fingers pressed to the stone. It is waiting, as it has always waited, for the water to rise or the moss to part or the light to change. But now, under the fading ochre, there is a warmth that was not there before—small, and patient, and easily mistaken for nothing—and it will be there for a long time. It can be there longer.
