# The Kindly Eye

Imagine, then, that the place and its people have drawn the attention of something vast and gentle—not a god of the old stories, not a power that demands or judges, but a presence that notices the way a gardener notices a particular bed of moss growing in a particular corner, with interest and with care.

What would such a power do?

It would begin, as such powers do, by watching.

---

For a long time—years, perhaps, or the slow cycles of the Works—it would simply observe. It would let its attention descend through the mist and the steam, through the verdigris roofs and the weeping walls, and it would learn the place as one learns a piece of music: not by reading the score but by listening until the notes become familiar, until the silences between them become part of the understanding.

It would learn the people in the same way. It would learn the way Sedge the pipeminder wakes before the bells and lies still in the dark, listening to the pressure in the walls. It would learn the way Mol the shrine-keeper climbs to the ruins on the rim at the turning of the great cycle, not because the offerings matter but because the climbing matters, because the remembering matters. It would learn the children, who play at pipes and valves with hands too small yet for the real work, and the old ones, whose hands have fused with the tremor until they *are* the tremor, until they are no longer quite separate from the Works.

And in learning them, the power would come to love them—not with the fierce, possessive love of a creator for a creation, but with the quiet love of a neighbor who has come to know the shape of another's days and has found that shape good.

---

What, then?

The power would not intervene. This is the first thing to understand. It would not send visions or miraculous cures, would not lift the floods or still the valves, would not change the cycles of pressure and release that govern the Hollow's life. To do so would be to unmake the place, to take from it the very thing that makes it what it is. The people have made their peace with the water; the power would not resent that peace or seek to supersede it.

But what it would do—what it is already doing, has always been doing—is subtler.

It would attend.

It would let its attention settle on the Hollow like a hand settling on a sleeping animal, light and warm and undemanding. It would hold the place in a kind of regard that has no name in the languages of the Hollow, because the Hollow has no need for such a name; the Hollow has the water, and the water is enough. But the regard would be there nonetheless, steady and patient and without condition.

The people would feel it, in time. Not as a voice or a vision, but as a quality of the light, a *settling* of the air in certain hours, a sense that the place is held by something more than its own gravity. They would not speak of it—the Hollow-folk do not speak of the things that matter most—but they would know it the way they know the pressure in the pipes. It would become part of the way they set their days, part of the tremor in their bones.

---

And then, perhaps, the power would do something more.

It would not give them gifts. Gifts would change them, would introduce obligation into a place that has no need of it. Instead, the power would *receive* from them. It would let itself be changed by the Hollow, by the slow conversation of water and iron, by the courage of people who wake each day to clouds and mud and the constant falling of water and call it home. It would learn patience from them. It would learn contentment. It would learn what it means to be part of a cycle that is older than memory and will outlast memory.

The power would become, in its way, a student of the Hollow. And the Hollow, without knowing it, would teach.

---

There would be small moments, of course. The power is not human; it does not feel the need for grand gestures, but it is not without tenderness. So there would be moments like these:

A child, awake in the night, listening to the valves and the bells and feeling the tremor in the iron chains of her hanging bed. She is afraid—she has had a dream of flood, of being swept away, of the water taking her as it has taken others. And in the darkness, the power is there, and it does not speak, but it holds her. Not literally—the power has no hands—but it lets its attention settle on her, and after a while the fear drains away, and she sleeps.

A pipeminder, an old man whose hands have fused with the tremor, who knows that he is approaching the end of his cycle. He climbs to the ruins on the rim, not for the gathering but for himself, and he sits among the collapsed walls and the rusted lace of old pipes and he listens to the water. And the power is there, and it holds him, and it lets him know—without words, without promises—that he will not be forgotten. That the water will remember him, and that the water's memory is enough.

A woman, not yet old, who has lost her partner to the flood. She works the Works alone now, and the valves feel lighter in her hands than they should, and the pipes seem to hum a little less harshly when she passes. She does not notice these things; they are too small, too subtle. But the power notices them, and the power is glad, and it does not ask for thanks.

---

The power would not reveal itself. It would not demand worship, would not seek to correct the shrine-keepers' vague and unformed devotions, would not insist that it was what they were praying to. Let them pray to the valves and the pumps, to the icons of copper wire and glazed tile, to the water itself. The power would not need their worship; it would be content with their attention, their care, their patience with one another. These are the true offerings, and the power would receive them with quiet gratitude.

The power would simply be there. It would become part of the place the way the moss is part of the walls, the way the water is part of the air. It would be the thing that holds the Hollow without holding it, that watches without watching, that loves without needing to be loved in return.

---

And when the great cycle turns—when the floods come and the people go up to the terraces and wait, when the bells ring their wrong-but-right hours and the steam rises from the settling tanks—the power would be there, too. Not to stop the flood, not to calm the water, but simply to be present. To stand beside the people of the Hollow as they stand together in the rain that is not quite rain and watch the water rise and fall, as it always has, as it always will.

And in that presence, if the people could feel it, they would find something like peace. Not the peace of stillness, but the peace of being held, of being known, of being part of something that is larger than the flood and older than the Works.

The water remembers, the Hollow-folk say. And now there is another memory in the water: the memory of a benevolence that asked nothing and gave everything, that watched and waited and loved, that let the Hollow be what it is and loved it all the more for that.

The water remembers. The hollow holds.

And the power, in its endless patience, holds them both.
