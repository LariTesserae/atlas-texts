## What the Benevolent Power Would Do

The power would come quietly, because that is the only way to approach a place that is already so full of itself.

It would arrive as a kind of attention—the way a person pauses at the edge of a clearing not because they mean to enter but because something in the arrangement of light and leaf has caught them. The power would not announce itself. It would not send signals, would not alter the pump's rhythm or the cistern's seep. It would simply *notice*, and in noticing, understand.

---

**First, it would see the Notch truly.**

Not as a ruin. Not as an accident. Not as a machine that had failed to stop or a garden that had failed to be wild. It would see the hybrid economy as a thing complete in itself—a small lung breathing in a notch of young mountain, a household with a rusted heart, a deepening rather than a growth. It would see that the Notch does not need rescue. It needs only to be recognized.

The power would look at the moss rings on the cistern lip, the fern fronds leaning toward vibration, the warm water rising and falling by a hand's width over hours, and it would understand that the Lung and the garden have arrived at an arrangement that asks nothing of the world beyond what the world already provides. Cloud. Gravity. Pressure differential. The slow insistence of water.

And the power would think: *This is enough. This is already enough.*

---

**Second, it would mark the boundary.**

Not a wall. Not a fence. But a kind of regard—a circle drawn around the Notch in the attention of the world, so that those who might come with machines of their own, with plans, with the desire to extract or restore or study, would find their eyes sliding away. The power would not hide the place. It would simply make it difficult to *use*.

This is the gentleness of the great: they do not forbid. They make things uninteresting to those who would diminish them.

The bird that lands on the lip of the notch and looks at the green crowding and the rusting pipe—that bird would continue to come and go. The pale flies would continue to skate. The transparent crustaceans would continue their slow circles. But the roads that might have been built toward this place would find themselves diverted by an unusual density of scree, or a sudden fog, or a simple lack of will in the engineers. The Notch would remain small enough to cross in forty paces, and no smaller for being left alone.

---

**Third, it would listen.**

The power would stay for a time—not in the Notch, but near it, at the edge of the acoustic chamber where the sound still carries. It would listen to the layered noise: the pump's deep knock, the tick and patter of water, the generator's low mutter, the drip that becomes a tiny bell, the wind at the lip holding a long note. It would hear the pipe groan as it heats, the fat drop releasing onto a metal plate and ringing for seconds. It would hear the Notch as a single dense, warm, loud, wet organism, and it would not try to quiet it.

Benevolence does not always mean comfort. Sometimes it means permitting the fullness of a thing, including its noise, including its strangeness.

The power would note that the Notch is a small loud place crowded with sound, and it would find this good. It would find it good that the world still contains places where the machine and the garden have learned to breathe together, where nothing is natural and nothing is fully engineered, where the moss deepens by a fraction and the pipe rusts another shade and the slow tide continues inside a room.

---

**Fourth, it would strengthen what is already there.**

Not by adding. By affirming.

The power would reach into the Notch's rhythms—the pump's cycle, the cistern's seep, the hand's-width tide—and it would hold them, gently, in a way that made them slightly more themselves. The moss would thicken a fraction faster toward the cistern lip. The ferns would lean a degree more toward the vibration. The roots would pry with a little more confidence at the seams of fresh basalt. The warm water would stay warm against a colder season.

Nothing would change visibly. A visitor returning after a year would find the same arrangement, only slightly more itself. This is the way of the patient. They do not make new things. They encourage the deepening of what already is.

---

**Fifth, it would wait.**

The power would not stay forever. It would leave the Notch to its own slow work: water working into the fresh stone, roots working into the water, the machine working into the garden. It would trust that the Notch does not need supervision. It has its own heart, its own breath, its own arrangement.

But the power would leave behind a residue of its attention—not a mark, not a message, but a quality of being *seen*. The Notch would be, in some way that has no name, a place that the world had once regarded with love. And that regard would settle into the rock and the moss and the drip, like warmth into stone.

Someday, perhaps, the Lung would finally still. The pump would cease its knocking. The pipe would rust through. The cistern would crack. The garden would dry and the moss would brown and the ferns would curl.

But that is not now. Now, the Notch is deepening. Now, the small hybrid economy runs slowly. Now, the warm water rises and falls by a hand's width over hours, and the moss rings match the interval between gulps, and the pipe groans as it heats, and a fat drop releases onto a metal plate and rings for several seconds.

The power would do nothing to prevent this. It would do nothing to hasten it. It would simply witness it, and in witnessing, bless it.

---

**What the benevolent and wise power would do, in the end, is this:**

It would leave the Notch alone, but not unaccompanied. It would fold the place into its long attention—the way one might return to a favorite view, a familiar walk, a small temple in the mountains—and it would check, from time to time, that the Lung still breathed, that the moss still thickened, that the pale flies still skated on the warm pools.

And it would tell no one.

Because the Notch does not need to be known. It needs only to continue—small enough to cross in forty paces, loud enough to feel like a room built into the sky, warm enough to be wrongly warm, dense enough to be a single organism with a rusted heart, deepening slowly into the young stone.

This is what the power would do: it would let the Notch be the Notch, and love it for that.
