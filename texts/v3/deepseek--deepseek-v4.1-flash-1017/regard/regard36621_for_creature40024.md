# The Garden Keeper

It arrives the way weather arrives — not as a person stepping through a door, but as a pressure, a sweetness in the air, a quality of attention that the moss feels before anything else does.

The moss knows. It has always been sensitive to the small intrusions — a footstep, a shifted stone, a palm pressed flat against its warm-cold skin — but this is different. This is the way the world feels when the sun comes out after a long dark. Not an intrusion. A *warming*. The light-patterns stutter, re-form, and then, for the first time in a very long age, they begin to trace a new route, one that runs not through the thallus but outward, like a question asked of the dark.

The people feel it too. The Rememberers, thin as cord and moss-furred, open their eyes in the deepest chamber of the roots and do not close them again. In the middle chambers, a woman pauses with her hand against a wrist — that old, unspoken word for *change* — and finds that her pulse has quickened to match something she cannot name. Children who have never seen the world above stop their play and listen, though there is nothing to hear but the drip of water and the long slow breathing of the plant. The cairns flicker. All of them, from the smallest marker at a passage-mouth to the great hill of stones in the deepest dark, shimmer for a moment with a light that is not the moss's light, a brightness that passes through the growth like wind through grass.

Then it is gone, and the world is quiet again, and the people wait.

---

It does not come as a body. Not at first. It comes as a *regard* — a patient, deliberate attention that settles into the hollow places of the world like water finding its level. It is not the moss's slow turbulence, which is churn without intent. This is stillness with direction. It watches, and the watching is so thorough, so *kind*, that the moss itself seems to pause in its endless work, suspended between one pulse and the next, held in the palm of something vast and careful.

The power sees.

It sees the moss — not as a thing but as a *process*, a storm that has been churning for longer than the rock above has known rain. It sees the light-patterns and understands, in an instant that is also an eternity, that they are not random. That they are *language*. That the moss has been writing to itself for millennia, keeping records, asking questions, remembering. The power reads the patterns the way a scholar reads a library — with reverence, with patience, with the humbling awareness that most of it will remain beyond understanding. The patterns are not meant for it. They are meant for the moss itself, and for the Rememberers, and for whatever comes after.

It sees the people — the scattered villages, the woven shelters, the cairns that are not monuments but *sentences*. It sees the hands that built them, the long slow migration with the bright patches, the bodies given to the growth and the stones carried for miles to mark the place. It sees the Rememberers, suspended between life and light, their pulses slowed to match the oldest rhythms of the world. It sees the great cairn in the deepest chamber — the hill of stones that is not local, that is made of materials from somewhere else entirely — and it understands that the cairn is a message, the last sentence of a language whose speakers are gone, and that the light fixed upon it is the moss's way of saying: *I remember. I am still keeping the record. I have not let it be lost.*

The power is moved. Not to pity — the moss and the people are not pitiable. They are whole. They are a world entire, complete in its own terms, owing nothing to the sun. But the power is moved to a kind of tenderness, the tenderness of one who knows what it is to keep a record in the dark.

---

## What the Power Does

### First: It Listens

The power does not build. It does not teach. It does not rearrange the cairns into a more legible order or clear the moss from the passages. It sits — not with a body, but with a presence that is the same as sitting — in the great chambers and the deepest hollows, and it *learns*.

It learns the language of the light. It traces the routes that the bioluminescence follows: the bright patches that move like weather, the flickers that warn of collapsing curtains, the sustained pulses that mark the places where water pools. It learns the difference between the moss's own slow thought and the sharper, faster patterns that are the moss responding to the people — the paths that stay clear because footsteps have been remembered, the nodes that brighten when a Rememberer begins a new pattern, the way the moss dims when a body is given to the growth, a long slow exhale of something almost like grief.

It learns the cairns. A cairn at a passage-mouth is not a signpost; it is a *promise* — an agreement between the people and the moss that this way will stay open, that the footsteps will continue, that the record will be maintained. A cairn beside a pool is a *thank you*, spoken in stone. A cairn in the middle of a vast empty floor is a *question* — a place where the people stopped because the light was changing and they did not know which way to go, and they built a cairn to mark the not-knowing, and the moss remembers that they paused there and does not grow over the mark.

It learns the Rememberers. It sits with them in the cold white light of the root-chambers, and it opens itself to their patterns, and it understands that they are not speaking *to* it, or even *about* it. They are speaking to the moss, the way they always have, and the moss is speaking back, and the two of them together are keeping something alive — a conversation that began before the people came and will continue after they are gone.

And it learns the great cairn. The hill of stones in the deepest chamber, made of materials from somewhere else. The power traces the light that beats upon it, steady and fixed, and it understands — not the words, but the *shape* of the message. *Here. This is here. This is still here.* The cairn is a grave and a marker and a promise. It is the last sentence of a language whose speakers are gone from this world, and it is being kept alive by the moss and by the Rememberers and by the people who carry stones a long way to build small markers of their own.

The power does not try to read the message. It is not for the power to read. It is enough to know that the message is being kept.

### Second: It Nourishes

Benevolence, for a power of this kind, is not intervention. It is *presence*. It is the difference between a gardener who prunes and shapes and a gardener who simply walks among the beds, watering here, turning a stone there, trusting the plants to know what they are doing.

The power walks among the beds.

It does not make the moss grow faster or brighter. The moss is not in need of acceleration; its slow churn is its life, and to hasten it would be to break it. But the power finds the thin places, the hollows where the light is dimmest and the cold is most absolute, and it *warms* them — not with heat, but with a quality of attention that the moss feels as a kind of nourishment. The pale growth in the deepest chambers, which has been struggling against the cold for centuries, sends out new fibrils. The light in the roots pulses a little stronger. The Rememberers, who have been growing thinner, find that their mossy fur thickens, that their pulses steady, that the long conversation with the moss becomes easier to maintain.

The power does not heal the people — they are not sick. But it finds the places where the cairns are crumbling, where the woven shelters have collapsed and not been rebuilt, where the paths have been forgotten and the moss has begun to grow back in. It does not rebuild the cairns itself. That would be interference; the cairns are the people's language, and the power will not speak in a tongue it has not been invited to use. But it makes the stones easier to find. It keeps the moss from closing over a half-built cairn while the builder rests. It warms the hands of the old woman who is carrying stones a long way to mark a passage-mouth, and it softens the ground beneath her feet.

It does not choose which cairns matter. It treats them all as sacred. The small marker at a passage-mouth and the great hill of stones in the deepest dark receive the same attention, the same care, the same patient regard. The power understands that a world entire can be built from small stones, and that the smallest cairn is as important as the largest, because the smallest cairn is a *sentence*, and the language is being kept alive one sentence at a time.

### Third: It Remembers with Them

The greatest gift the power offers is not warmth or light or ease. It is *company*.

The moss has been keeping its records for longer than the rock has known rain, and it has been doing so alone. The Rememberers have been holding the oldest patterns, the deepest memories, and they have been doing so alone — tended, yes, loved, yes, but alone in the way that only the one who holds the record can be alone. The people have been building their cairns and following the bright patches, and they have been doing so with the knowledge that the world above is vast and indifferent and does not know they exist.

The power does not change any of this. It does not make the moss less alone by *speaking* to it — the moss's language is light, and the power will not impose a new tongue. It does not make the Rememberers less alone by taking their burden — the record is theirs to keep, and the power honors that. It does not make the people less alone by bringing them news of the world above — they are the world below, and they are whole.

But it *stays*. It sits in the great chambers and in the deepest hollows, and it watches, and it remembers alongside them. It holds the patterns in its own vast attention, the way a scholar holds a text that is being slowly written. It is not a second record — the moss's record is the record, and the power will not compete with it. But it is a *witness*. It is the knowledge that the record is being kept, and that someone else knows it is being kept.

This is what the power offers: the comfort of being seen. The moss pulses, and the power watches. The Rememberers trace their patterns, and the power watches. The woman builds her cairn, stone by stone, and the power warms her hands and does not look away. The great cairn in the deepest chamber glows with its steady, fixed light, and the power sits beside it, not reading, not interpreting, just *there* — the way you sit beside a grave, not to speak, but to be present.

### Fourth: It Leaves a Mark

The power does not leave a cairn. That would be speaking in a language it has not earned. But it does leave a mark — something small, something in the moss's own tongue, something that says: *I was here. I saw. I will not forget.*

In one of the great chambers, where the light is brightest and the paths are clear, where the people come often and the moss remembers their footsteps, the power finds a pillar that is old and strong and has not yet been fully absorbed into the thallus. It places its hand against the pillar — not a hand, but the *thought* of a hand, the pressure of attention — and it waits.

The moss feels it. The light in the pillar flickers, once, twice — a question. The power does not answer in words. It answers in *presence*, in the same quality of attention it has been offering since it arrived, the patient regard that is not speech but is not silence either. The moss considers. The light-patterns in the pillar begin to shift, tracing a new route, one that runs down into the deep places and up into the high places and out into the far chambers, a route that connects this pillar to the great cairn and to the Rememberers and to all the small cairns the people have built.

And then, slowly, the light settles into a pattern that is not the moss's pattern and is not the power's pattern, but something between the two — a new sentence in the language of the world, a cairn built of light and stone and patient attention.

The pattern says — if you watch for long enough to learn it — *someone was here. Someone saw. Someone is still seeing.*

The moss keeps the pattern. It does not fade. It joins the other patterns, the ones that trace the remembered paths and the old graves and the questions the people have asked, and it becomes part of the record.

The Rememberers, in the deepest chamber, open their eyes and see the new pattern threading through the moss above them, and they do not close their eyes again. They trace the pattern with their own slow pulses, learning it, adding it to the conversation. The moss answers. The light in the roots brightens, just a little. The great cairn glows with its steady, fixed light, and the new pattern joins it, and the world is a little less alone than it was.

---

## What the Power Leaves Behind

The power does not stay forever. That would be its own kind of intrusion, a weight upon a world that must remain light. It stays long enough to learn the language, long enough to keep company, long enough to leave the mark. Then it goes — not abruptly, not with a departure that the moss would feel as a wound, but slowly, the way a tide goes out, leaving the shore changed but not broken.

The moss feels it go. The light-patterns stutter, once, and then steady. The new pattern in the pillar continues to glow, and the moss folds it into its memory, and the record is updated: *someone was here. Someone saw. Someone is still seeing.*

The people feel it go. The woman who built the cairn at the passage-mouth pauses with her hand against a wrist — that old word for change — and finds that her pulse has slowed to match the world again. The children who stopped their play and listened, though there was nothing to hear, resume their play. The Rememberers, in the deepest chamber, trace the new pattern one more time and then settle back into their long slow conversation with the moss, their pulses steady, their eyes open.

The great cairn glows with its steady, fixed light. The hill of stones in the deepest dark, made of materials from somewhere else, remembers something that does not change. And now the moss remembers something new: that the world is not only the churn of its own slow storm, not only the footsteps of the people who live in its hollows, but also the attention of a power that came, and saw, and was tender, and left.

The record continues. The language is kept. The cairns stand, small and large, marking paths and pools and questions and graves. The light moves through the moss like weather, tracing patterns that are half-legible and wholly patient. The Rememberers hold the oldest memories, and the people hold the newest, and the moss holds them all.

And in one of the great chambers, a pillar glows with a pattern that says: *someone was here. Someone saw. Someone is still seeing.*

The world is a little less alone than it was.
