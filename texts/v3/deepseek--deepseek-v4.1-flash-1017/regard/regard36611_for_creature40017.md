It would do almost nothing, and that almost would be exact.

It would not come as light. It would know better than that. Light here would not be a revelation; it would be a new weather, a new demand, a surface where none was needed. It would arrive instead as attention—the only thing that can enter a place like this without displacing the air.

For one full breath it would do nothing at all. Four hours. It would stand on the dust and let the drone fill it, and it would notice that its own breathing was wrong—too quick, too shallow, a mammal’s rhythm in a place that keeps time by tides of gas. So it would slow itself until its breath matched the Knot’s, and then it would know something no report could have told it: that the town is not a ruin and not a machine. It is a body finishing its long exhale.

It would consider the gifts it could give, and it would set each one down.

It would not give eyes. There is nothing to see, and the people have never organized their lives around that lack. To give them light would be to invent a hunger.

It would not stop the closing. The closing is not the disease; it is the health of this place. To keep every aperture open forever would be to keep a wound from healing, to make the Knot into a thing that cannot finish. The wise power would recognize that immortality is not a kindness when the good on offer is completion.

It would not bring children, or settlers, or new hands. The last generation is not a tragic deficit. It is a residue of care, and it is old, and it is enough. To fill the galleries with the young would be to ask the Knot to become a nursery after it had already become a grave and a hearth and a slow closing hand.

It would not lift them out. Not the people, not the plant-town. Even if it could carry them to some brighter, wetter, more crowded world, it would be a kidnapping dressed as rescue. Their grammar is resin and fiber and the pressure of gas at knee height. They would not thank it for a horizon.

So what would it do?

It would ask permission in the only language available: it would stand at the mouth-lip, palm flat on the warm felt, and wait one full breath. The lip would swell and settle. That is not yes. That is not no. It is the answer this place gives to everything: a slow tide, and then another.

Then it would walk. Not every path—that would be an intrusion, a new maintenance schedule imposed from outside. It would find one path that had gone unwalked since the last person who used it stopped, a passage already narrowing, its felt lips half-closed. And it would walk that path once, slowly, from end to end. Not to keep it open. One walk cannot keep a path open. It would walk it so that the closing was not neglect. So that the aperture had company when it decided to become a scar.

It would not wake the sleepers in their slings. It would stand in a chamber until its presence changed the drone in a way the sleeper felt as a room being a little larger, a little more held. Then it would move on before the change became a need.

It would tie one knot. A cord of fiber around a young limb, the same as the old marks—not a message, because no one would read it, and not a promise, because promises are a kind of noise. Just a knot that the limb would swallow over the next century. The point would be that it would be swallowed. The point would be that attention had passed through and left a mark soft enough to be absorbed.

It would accept a little resin on its palms. It would let one old caretaker, feeling a new pressure in the dark, tap once on a taut cord—not a question, just a notification: *you are here, and I am here, and the floor is still under both of us.* The power would tap back once, in the same rhythm, and then be still.

It would stay for many breaths. Perhaps a season; perhaps a handful of years. It would learn the names of the caretakers by the way their footfalls answered the drone, and it would not ask them to say the names aloud. It would learn that the Knot’s name has worn off, and it would not try to restore it. It would remember the shape of the worn-off place, the way you remember a word in a language you no longer speak.

And then it would leave—before its attention became a new gravity, before its kindness became a thing the place had to tend.

It would carry away no sample, no seed, no record in stone. It would carry away the shape of a four-hour breath and the knowledge that care can be complete without an audience. It would not tell the people they were brave. They would not know what to do with the word. It would not tell them they were not alone. They had never built their lives around that fear. It would not promise to return.

But it would remember. Not as a file, as a way of walking—slowly, with the feet, in the dark, listening for the answer a room gives before you hear your own step.

The Hearth-Knot would go on closing. The drone would go on. The gas would pool at knee height, sweet and oily. Somewhere a floor would decide, after a decade of deliberation, to give a millimeter.

And the power would be gone, having added no lamp, having kept one path company for an hour, having left one knot to be swallowed. If that is not benevolence, it is at least what benevolence looks like when it is wise enough to be small.
