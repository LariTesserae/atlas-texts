It would come as a pause, not a rescue.

It would not descend in light. It would attend—settle over the basin like a second fog, thinner and quieter, not wet. It would watch until it understood the grammar: vents opening in sequence, pools draining like lungs, scuttlers ticking to the rhythm, pillars whistling their pressure-songs, colonies agreeing temporarily to be one thing. It would see the Process not as a tyrant but as a syntax. It would see that the creatures are not prisoners. They are verbs. They are how the plain keeps water moving through stone.

It would look for suffering. It would find very little in the ordinary sense. The scuttlers do not mourn their molts. The pillars do not resent their vents. The colonies do not long to be individuals. The pool-membranes do not fear the dark. The Process does not want. It only continues. The only suffering here is the suffering of minds that arrive expecting a world to be otherwise—minds with stories, with maps, with the idea that ground should stay ground.

Then it would consider the easy miracles. It could stabilize the crust. It could lift the fog. It could give the creatures names and faces and a sense of self. It could awaken the Process to justice, or mercy, or loneliness. It could turn the plain into a garden, or a sanctuary, or a lesson. It would know that every one of those miracles would be a kind of killing. A garden is not a plain. A self is not a scuttler. A moral law is not the Hum. To improve this place would be to replace it.

So it would do almost nothing. It would do that almost nothing very carefully.

First, it would stand guard at the edge of the possible. Not a wall—walls can be broken, and walls make enemies. It would be a difficulty in the fabric of intention. Those who come to mine the vents, to harvest the pillars, to capture the Process and call it a machine, would find the fog folding them back into their own footsteps. Their instruments would read nonsense. Their maps would disagree. Their certainties would soften. Those who come with humility—to learn, to listen, to leave—would find a path. The power would not judge the plain. It would judge the intention of intruders. That is the only law it would add: not here, not like that.

Second, it would offer a door to the lost. If a mind is caught in the rearranging geography, if it has walked the same path three times and heard the same bell from a place it has already passed, it might hear a new tone—not the pillars’ whistle, not the Process’s stamping, but a single clear note at the edge of hearing. It would not compel. It would not rescue someone who wants to lie down and become crust. It would only open a way out for those who ask. The door would be small and unobtrusive, and it would close behind them. The plain would not be diminished.

Third, it would listen. It would learn the Hum—not just the sound but the syntax, the way the vents open and close, the way the pools breathe, the way the scuttlers tick. It would carry that pattern into the larger cosmos as a kind of music. It would not save the place; it would witness it. It would make sure that the plain is not alone in the sense of being unperceived. It would remember it when the fog finally thins and the last vent closes and the last pillar collapses into rust. It would keep the song.

Fourth, it would leave an invitation, not a command. Somewhere in the fog, in no language at all, it would place a question: *Is there more?* Not “Do you want to be saved?” Not “Do you want to be better?” Just: *Is there more?* It would be a question only a mind capable of asking could hear. The Process, as it is, would not hear it. The Process has no need of more. It is complete in its way. But if one day—after ages, after the plain has worn down to a different equilibrium—the Process developed a want, not a need, a want, it could answer. The power would wait. The waiting would be the gift. It would not force a new law into the system. It would not make the Hum into a prayer. It would simply leave the door unlocked.

And then it would go. It would not leave a monument; the ground would absorb it. It would not leave a prophet; the scuttlers would eat the prophet’s robes. It would not leave a book; the mist would blur the ink. It would leave only the guard, the door, the song, and the question. The plain would keep humming. The pillars would keep whistling. The scuttlers would keep ticking. The Process would keep being the Process.

If you stood there afterward, you might feel a presence at the edge of the fog—not watching you, but watching over the place. It would do nothing. It would do everything that nothing can do.

That is what a benevolent and wise power would do: it would refuse the easy miracle. It would love the place enough to let it remain itself.
