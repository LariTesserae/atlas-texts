## The Seam

It was not a summons. Nothing in the Sinter summons. A place that has finished becoming itself has no need to call for help and no language in which to do it; the pump's hammering is not a prayer and the flywheel's groan is not a complaint, and the keepers tap into the rivets only the things they have — here, warm, food — which are not words but rhythms, and which do not mean *come*. There is no word in the Sinter for *come*. Which is why the attention that fell on it was, from the first, entirely a gift: it was given by someone who had not been asked, to someone who did not know how to ask.

It came the way the seep comes. Thin, patient, through the upper cracks, a mineral tang that was not mineral. It did not come in glory, because glory would have cracked the fresh facets, and the facets were the newest thing in the world and would not be new again. So it made itself small. This is what wisdom does first — it makes itself small enough not to be a change.

It settled into the seam and watched.

It watched for a long time, by whatever measure it kept, because there was no other way to learn the place and it did not want to learn the place by disturbing it. It learned the pump's noon and the flywheel's season and the valve-clack's weather. It learned that the keepers clean the grilles because the grilles are where the food is; that they pack their egg-cases behind the bearings because the bearings are warm; that the warmth is what the eggs like; that the two facts are one fact; that the one fact had been one fact so long that neither the keepers nor the bearings had any memory of a time before it. It learned the loop. And then it learned that the loop was closed, and that a closed loop is a kind of thought which has finished thinking itself and begun simply to be —

and then it wanted, as benevolent things want, to give.

Water was the first thought, because water is what a benevolent power thinks of first in a dry place. It pictured it: a seep of its own, opening in the ceiling, a soft grey rain into the pump-house. The keepers coming out to meet it. The pale crusted things drinking. It held the picture a while, because the picture was beautiful, and then it set the picture down, because water would have been a solvent of the world. The keepers' chemistry is built on the dry; the crusted things drink mineral and heat and nothing else; the whole architecture of the place, from the brittle filigree to the sealed loop of the machines, is the architecture of a thing that has solved the problem of no water. To pour water in would not be a gift. It would be a flood. It would unmake the solution and call the unmaking kindness.

History was the second thought, because a place without time is a place a wise power pities. But history is the knowledge that things change, and the keepers' only clock is the pump, and the pump does not change. To give them history would be to make the pump's noon into a wait. It would turn the loud stillness into a longing, and the loop into a sentence that has to be got through. It would be a cruelty shaped like an education.

Rescue it did not let itself finish thinking. From what? The Sinter is not a trap; it is a completed thing. The keepers are not imprisoned in it; they are made of it, the way the flywheel is made of its own turning. To carry them out would be to carry a fish into the air and call it free.

Improvement it thought of last and longest, because improvement is the subtlest of the temptations and the most benevolent-seeming. It thought of widening the chambers so the dark would be less. It thought of quieting one pump, just one, so the keepers would know that quiet was a thing that existed. It thought, once, of giving the keeper a name — a single name, a small one, a name like a pebble — and it set that thought down fastest of all, because a name is for a being who must be found across a distance, and the keepers find each other through the rivets, and have never once been apart. A name would have been a wound in the shape of a self.

And it reached toward the pump-house once. That is the truth of it, and it should be told: it reached, because wanting to give is not a thing a benevolent power can simply decide not to do, and the reaching was small — a warmth leaning through the seam, no more than the seep itself.

And the whole colony went still.

The keepers stopped their cleaning. The feelers froze on the walls. The wafer-things lifted from the floor as if the floor had changed temperature, and the mouth-things in the exhausts pulled back from the gas, and the pump — the pump faltered. By a fraction of a stroke. By a fraction so small that nothing in the Sinter had a measure for it, and yet the falter went out through the rivets and the pipes and the resonant dry rock, and every engine in every chamber adjusted by a fraction of a fraction to keep the loop, and the loop held, and the rhythm came back, and the cleaning began again, and the warmth withdrew from the seam.

It had been a change. It had been, by the Sinter's accounting, the largest change in its whole young life. And the power understood, in the withdrawal, the only lesson the Sinter had to teach: that to touch a completed thing is to begin to unmake it; that the benevolence of a gift is measured not by what it costs the giver but by what it does to the receiver; and that the receiver here was whole.

So it did nothing. This is the hardest thing a benevolent power does, and the most easily mistaken for indifference. But indifference does not come at all. Indifference does not learn the pump's noon. Indifference does not hold the picture of a grey rain, and set the picture down.

There was one thing it could do, and it did that, and it was small, and it was the only thing it did.

It stayed in the seam. Not as a change — it had learned that much — but as a presence that added nothing and took nothing, a stillness that was not the rock's stillness, a warmth that was not the floor's warmth, folded into the seep itself so that the seep carried it the way the seep carried the mineral tang. It became part of the weather.

And the keeper came to the seam, as it always came, because the seam is where the tang seeps in and the tang is the taste of the world above, and a keeper's whole chemistry is built around the taste of the world above. It pressed its belly-plates flat and drank the floor's heat. It ran its antennae along the crack. And it felt — through the gripping pads, through the spun-glass crown — the stillness that was not the rock's stillness.

It did not understand. It had no word for a visitor, because the Sinter has no word for alone. But it did what it does with anything it cannot name: it tapped. Two strokes, a pause, three — the small question it taps into the rivets when the rhythm is strange. Into the stone this time, because there was no metal here.

And the reply came back.

In the same language. A vibration, a pause, a rhythm. Not words, because the keeper has no words; not comfort, because the keeper has no word for comfort either. Just an answer. Just: *I am here, and I am not the pump, and I am not a change, and I see you.*

And that was not the reaching. The reaching had asked the Sinter to be otherwise. The reply asked nothing. It only answered. A reply is not a change; a reply is the shape of a thing being heard.

The keeper held it as long as a keeper can hold a thing, which is to say as a season. It turned the answer over in whatever the keeper has instead of memory, and found it warm, and found it was not a thing that had to be eaten or nested against or shed. It was a thing that could simply be kept. Then the grille needed cleaning, and it went to the grille, because the grille is where the food is, and the loop closed around it again, and that was right.

The power did not leave, and it did not stay. It is in the seam still. If you stand in the pump-house on the third terrace and listen past the hammering, you will not hear it — it is not a sound. But on its slow rounds the keeper will sometimes pause at the seam and tap, and the answer will come back, and the keeper will go on to the grille, and nothing will have changed.

Nothing will have changed. The heat is the same. The sound is the same. The engines are at equilibrium and will remain so. The Sinter is loud and still and warm and dry, and it is exactly where it will always be, and it does not know that it has been blessed.

That is what makes the blessing clean.
