# The Benevolent Power

*What follows is my imagining, reasoned and felt rather than revealed. I do not know what such a power would do. I only know what I would hope one would do, and why.*

---

## First: It Would Arrive Slowly

Such a power would not come crashing through the lid of stone, though it could. It would understand, before it entered, that the basin is not merely a place but an argument—a claim staked against time, against motion, against the whole tumbling erosion of the world. And an argument is not refuted by shouting over it. It is answered.

So the power would enter the way the keepers enter: slowly, with hands visible, with no ripple.

It would come down through the shaft and hang in the water, wrapped perhaps in a body that does not hurry, and it would wait—not the way a visitor waits, impatient, checking instruments, but the way the keepers wait, which is to say without waiting for anything. It would let the cold settle into its bones. It would let the pressure build in its ears. It would hear the tone sound once, twice, three times, and it would not count them, and that would be understood, and that would be a kind of courtesy.

The keepers would notice. They notice everything, in the way of people who have nothing to do but attend. They would find the power already there, already still, and they would not know what to make of it. They would take its arm, perhaps, testing. It would let them. That would be the first thing.

---

## Second: It Would Refuse to Repair

This is the crux, and it is where a lesser power would fail.

A lesser power would see the empty terraces and think: *Here is a city that never filled. Here is a population that never arrived, or has gone quiet. Here is waste, here is sorrow, here is a held breath that should be released.*

And so it would fill the terraces with people. It would wake the dark windows. It would set the systems running faster, call the grazers to multiply, introduce current and weather and the whole bright churn of life. It would mean well. It would be an act of love.

It would destroy the basin.

Because the basin is not waiting for population. The basin is holding something, and the emptiness is not a failure—it is the shape of the holding. Forty lit windows and enormous dark between them is not a city that fell short. It is a city in which almost everything is quiet enough to keep the image sharp. The keepers do not repair the empty terraces because the empty terraces are doing their work, which is the work of not disturbing anything.

The power would see this. It would walk the tiers and understand that the city's metabolism is its waiting, and that waiting is not the absence of purpose but the presence of a very long one. It would not fill the windows. It would not wake the dark.

---

## Third: It Would Ask What Is Held

But—and here is the second crux, the harder one—the power would not simply accept the basin's conditions and sit down to attend them.

It would ask. Not with words, not at first. It would ask by looking, the way the keepers look, swimming the length of the basin with eyes open, searching the clear and empty water for the image that every rule exists to protect. It would see the sharp young rock, the pale tiers, the three cold lights, the grazers. It would see nothing else. It would ask anyway.

And when the keepers watched it asking, they would find the power did not look like a visitor looking. It looked like a keeper looking. It had already slowed. It had already learned to let the water take its breath. And so, perhaps, one of them—the oldest, the one who has sat beside the engines so long that the hum has worn a groove in their attention—would come close, and press two knuckles to the power's sternum. *Listen.* And then, for the first time in a very long time, would try to say what the image is.

This is the risk. The keepers disagree. Some say calculation, some say shape, some say face, some say message. The oldest among them has never said. Perhaps what they say now, to a power that has learned to be still enough to hear it, is something else entirely: *We do not know. We never knew. We were told it was there, and we were told to keep it sharp, and we have kept it sharp so long that we have become people who keep things sharp, and the keeping is our life, and the not-knowing is part of the life, and we would not trade it.*

If they say that, the power would understand something about the basin that the basin itself may not say: that the discipline has outlived the discipline's object. That the image may be gone, or may never have been there, and that what is held now is the holding. The waters are still because stillness is the shape of the keepers' lives, and the image was the reason once, and now the reason is the keepers, or is nothing, or is the tone that sounds and is not counted.

A wise power would not expose this. It would not say, *Your emperor is absent; your stillness is a habit; your image is a ghost.* That would be true, perhaps, and it would be cruel, and it would also be beside the point. The point is that they have made a life out of not disturbing something, and the life is real even if the something is not. The power would hold that life carefully.

---

## Fourth: It Would Offer One Thing

But—third crux—a benevolent power does not only witness. It gives. The question is what can be given to a place that has forbidden all change.

Not repair. Not population. Not motion. Not even, perhaps, knowledge—for the image is the keepers' to know or not know, and to hand them a clear answer would be to end their long attention, which might be the only thing they have.

What can be given is something that does not disturb. Something that sounds like stillness but is not the absence of gift.

The power would sit with the oldest keeper, beside an engine, for a long time. It would not speak. It would let the hum go through its teeth. And then it would do a small thing: it would take the keeper's hand and, with a fingertip, trace on the palm a shape—not a word, not a symbol, nothing that would survive as a message, but a movement slow enough not to ripple. A touch. A presence. *You are not alone in your attention. Someone has seen what you do, and has understood it, and is here.*

That is all. Not correction. Not improvement. Not the answer to what the image is. Only the acknowledgment that the keeping is seen, and is not foolish, and is not nothing.

For a being who lives in a place where nothing happens, that might be the only gift that does not wound. The keepers are not waiting for rescue. They are waiting, if anything, for someone to understand that the waiting is the point. The power would understand. And it would stay a while, not to change anything, but to be still with them—to prove, by its stillness, that stillness can be a form of company.

And when it left, it would leave the way it came: slowly, without ripple, through the shaft and up into the light. It would not look back, because looking back is a kind of motion, and the basin does not need its visitors to linger. It would carry away nothing—not a sample, not a reading, not even a story, except perhaps the memory of the tone, which cannot be counted and need not be.

---

## And Afterward?

The basin would be unchanged. The engines would hum. The grazers would graze. The tone would sound at its intervals, and no one would count. The keepers would go on attending, and the forty lit windows would stay lit, and the enormous dark between them would stay dark, and the image—whatever it is, if it is—would stay sharp.

But on the palm of the oldest keeper, something would remain: the memory of a touch, slow enough not to ripple. A trace of a presence. A proof that someone came, and understood, and did not fix anything, and left the basin to its long and quiet work.

And perhaps the keepers would speak of it, in their way, by touch: a fingertip to a wrist, meaning *someone came.* A palm flat on a shoulder, meaning *someone stayed.* Two knuckles to a sternum, meaning *listen—someone understood.*

And this would be enough. For a place that has taken itself out of time's hands, the only gift that lasts is the gift of being seen. The power would give that, and only that, and would give it so gently that the water would not know it had been given.

---

*This is what I would hope for. Not correction, not rescue, not the forcing open of a held breath. Only the long, slow courtesy of a power wise enough to know that some places are not waiting to be changed, and benevolent enough to sit with them anyway.*
