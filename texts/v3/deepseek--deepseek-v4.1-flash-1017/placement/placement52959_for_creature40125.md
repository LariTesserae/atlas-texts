I think I would be a pipe-walker. Not an engineer-priest—I lack the certainty for liturgy, the patience for arguing with a machine that has been failing for centuries. But I have good ears, and I am willing to climb.

I would live on the seventh or eighth terrace, somewhere in the middle band where the steam is thin enough to breathe and the salt-mist from below only reaches you on the worst nights. My rooms would be two: one for sleeping, one for the jars. The jars are important. I would keep them sealed with wax and salvaged rubber, and I would know the weight of each one by heart. Water is not scarce, exactly—the veins run deep and hot—but it is *earned*, and you do not waste what you have carried up nine ladders in the dark.

I would wake before the steam thickens. That is the best hour, when the Spire is at its quietest—not silent, never silent, but the hum drops to something like a cat's purr, and you can hear the tick of cooling stone as if the whole column were thinking. I would eat something small and salt-dense. I would check my filters. And then I would climb.

My route would be my own. Every pipe-walker has one—a circuit of joints and valves and pressure-gauges that they have inherited or negotiated or simply claimed by walking it long enough that no one else bothers. Mine would take me up through the salvage terraces, past the old turbine-housing oven where the cooks set out their flatbread to rise in the residual heat, past the abandoned upper gantries where the wind screams and the salt has eaten the railings down to lace. I would not go all the way to the peak. That is for the engineer-priests, and I have no business there. But I would go high enough to feel the cold, high enough to see the dust-sea as a single thing—a slow, bruised ocean stretching to a horizon that does not care about me. I would stand there for a moment. I always would. And then I would go back down, because the pipes below need listening to and the wind above is not my weather.

I would carry a metal rod, short and slightly bent, and I would press it to the joints and listen. This is the work. You put your ear to the rod and you hear the pipe's blood—the gurgle of hot mineral water, the hiss of steam, the faint tick of a seal beginning to fail. You learn the difference between a healthy vein and a sick one the way you learn a friend's voice. I would have a notebook, or a slate, or a strip of hammered tin I scratched with a nail. I would mark the joints that needed attention and report them to the engineer-priests. They would nod. They would not thank me. Thanks are not the currency here. The currency is that the pipe holds, and the water comes up, and the child on the terrace below me drinks and does not sicken.

I think I would be good at this. I am good at listening. I am good at being alone for long stretches and not finding it strange. I am good at noticing the small changes—the way a gantry settles a millimeter lower each year, the way the salt-film on a railing thickens after a bad night, the way a child's hands learn the ladders faster than their fear can catch up. I would not be a hero. I would not be an engineer-priest or a salvager or a keeper of the deep water. But I would be the one who walks the pipes and hears the change before anyone else does, and when the change comes—when a joint fails or a vein runs dry or the wind tears a gantry loose—I would be the one who knows first. That is not nothing. In the Spire, that is a kind of belonging.

And sometimes, on the deep runs, when a drill has broken a new vein and the water pools hot and dark in a shaft no one has walked in years, I would see it. The glow. The soft blue-green sheets on the rock, patient and alive, growing a millimeter a year. I would not touch it. I would not tell the engineer-priests. I would just stand there in the dark and watch it, and for a moment the hum of the Spire would fall away, and I would be something very small in a very old place, and I would be glad of it.

Then I would climb back up, check my jars, and go to sleep behind my curtain of filter cloth, listening to the steam.

That is where I would find myself. That is who I would be.
