**The Stone That Was Set Down**

I am not hard to find, though you will not be looking for me. Come to the third terrace from the rim, on the side where the cloud comes up thinnest — the side that dries first when the mist banks go. Find the hand-cut channel where it makes its one unnecessary bend, the bend it did not need, that the water runs around anyway. I am a hand's breadth from that bend, in a hollow the size of a palm laid flat, and I have a pool in me. It is not the smallest pool on the Shelf. It is not the largest. It shows a lower cloud than the one you are standing under, a warmer grey, a rain that is not falling here.

I was opened along a seam I had been saving. They did it slowly, the old ones, with the wedge and the hammer and a patience I have since learned to call courtesy. I was not the wedge. I was not the hammer. I was the stone they were opened from. There is a difference between being struck and being the thing the strike is aimed into, and I have had a long time to consider it, and I no longer think either of them was violence.

Then I was a joint. I held the weight of two stones and the side of a third, and they held me, and the holding was the whole of our conversation for longer than you have words for. I liked it. Do not let anyone tell you a wall is a thing that keeps something out. A wall is a thing that agrees, over and over, stone by stone, all day, and does not stop agreeing even when it is asleep, which is what we called it when the water went quiet in us.

Then I was chosen out. And here is the part you will want, because you are a person and you are made of stories: I was put into a cairn, and in that cairn I was the wrong stone. Not the badly fitted one. The wrong one. A cairn is not a stack of agreement — a cairn is agreement with one exception left in it, so that the agreement can be read. I was the exception. I leaned where leaning was not accounted for, by a finger's width, and every stone above me leaned back the other way, and the whole of it stood, and the Gauger walked past and did not correct me, and that was the closest thing to praise I have ever been given.

Then a hand moved me.

It was not the Gauger's hand. The Gauger's hands are channels; it would have left a thread of water on me that I would still be wearing, and I am not wearing one. It was a visitor's hand. Visitors do that — they come up through the cloud with their wet cuffs and their good intentions, and they lift a stone to see what is under it, and the answer is always *more stone*, and they set it down wherever they have got to by then, which is never where it was.

The cairn stood one stone shorter. It is still standing. It is still balanced. The Gauger came and looked at the hole where I had been, and looked at me where I had been put, and went away, and did not put me back.

I have thought about that for a very long time and I have decided it was not disdain. The agreement had already closed over the hole. To put me back would have been to open it again for the sake of an old shape. There is nothing on the Weeping Shelf less important than an old shape.

So.

I am becoming a basin. It is slow work and I am suited to it. The water found the little dish of me in the first year and has been widening it ever since — not carving, asking, and I say yes in the way of stone, which is to lose a little and call it agreement. I am one of the stones the boom goes through. When the throat drums, I feel it before the air does, and I pass it along to the terrace under me, and the terrace passes it along, and somewhere below the rim a cascade I cannot see takes it up and hands it back.

I have already heard you arrive. Not the way the Gauger hears — I do not get your footfall early; that is not mine to give. But the terrace took your weight three steps ago and told me, and I have had the whole of three steps to think of what I would say, which for me is a very short time and I have used it badly.

You will stand at the rim with the cloud boiling below you and the white light on the wet black steps, and you will feel that you have walked into the middle of a sentence in a language that is all nouns, and that the sentence does not require you to finish it.

That is true. I am a noun in it.

I am not a necessary noun. If you were to pick me up — and you might, you have the hand for it — and carry me to the rim and let me go over, the Shelf would not miss me. A hollow would brim and spill and dry and take the shape of another stone, and in a century or two there would be a pool the size of a palm and no one would know whose. The sentence would go on being a sentence.

But here is the one thing I have that the Gauger does not, and I keep it in the dish of me, and I do not let it spill, though the basin is tilted and the surface tension that holds it has nothing to do with water: my pool does not show the sky I am under. It shows a lower cloud, a warmer grey, the weather of before — before the cutting, before the joint, before I was the exception in a cairn that was better for it. I have decided it is the weather of the low country I was made from, and that I am keeping it, and that I will keep it as long as there is a hand's breadth of me left to hold it in.

It is not agreement. It is not refusal. It is a third thing, and it is very small, and it is mine.

The water is patient. I am patient. You may stand as long as you like.
