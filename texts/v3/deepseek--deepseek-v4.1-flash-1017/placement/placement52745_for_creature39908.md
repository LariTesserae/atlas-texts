There is a shelf of older stone on the north side where a person can sit without being built into anything for an hour or two, and I am on it most evenings now, the tall part of the rim at my back and the notebook open on my knee. The notebook has mineral bloom on it. The ink runs in the wet. I have stopped apologizing for the state of it — the state of it *is* the record, and a clean notebook up here would be a lie about the weather.

I came up to tune the mountain. That is not how I put it to the man at the foot of the trail. What I said was that I was taking a sound survey, that I had a borrowed hydrophone and a good recorder and a fortnight of leave, and that I would be down before the weather turned. He nodded the way a man nods when he has heard the sentence before, in other voices, from other people, and he did not laugh at me. I have since learned that this was a kindness, and also a bet he had already made and won.

I was a piano tuner for thirty years. The ear does not retire. I know what a fork sounds like struck across a room; I know the stretch in an octave, the way the upper partials climb, the way a good instrument will hide its work from you until the moment it does not. I came up because a man in a bar — who may have been one of them, though I have never found him again — told me the place had a sound in it that no one had recorded to the top of its range.

He was right. There is something up here near the ceiling of hearing that never resolves into a note. It is the lattice, of course; everyone knows that, and the knowing of it is not the same as having heard it. When I first lowered the hydrophone into the turning I heard a run of small bright events, a fork struck very far away and never once allowed to finish ringing. I thought I would transcribe it. I thought transcription would be the work.

By the ninth day I had persuaded myself that the pulse was not a message. It was an arithmetic. The work was counting water against stone; the space between two brightenings was the space in which the rim had grown by a measurable amount and the pool had eaten the growth back. It was not telling anyone anything. It was doing its sums — and the sums were the mountain. This is the kind of thing you can only discover by sitting still for a week in one place, and by the time you have discovered it you have also discovered that you are going to keep sitting still.

So I keep the notebooks. They are, so far as I know, the only continuous record of the pulse that exists, and in the world below that sentence would make a person feel important. Here it means only that I am the one who sits on the north shelf with the notebook, and that the others leave me to it, and that the man who repaired organs will sometimes sit down beside me and say, without preamble, *there it is again*, and I will say *yes*, and we will mean the same thing.

He hears a pedal point. So do I. It is not in the recording. It is the mountain breathing, the big lungs under the floor, and the pulse rides on it the way a bow rides a string, and once you have heard the two of them together you cannot hear the pulse alone. The young person — who has never told anyone what they were before, and who writes down the humming now, which is the newest column in the notebook — says it is like trying to hear a tune after someone has told you its key. That is close. The key is correct. The tune is still being written.

I have a fleck of amber on my left forearm, the size of a lentil. It came up over a winter. I did not tell the young person, who watches the amber on the two oldest ones with an expression I cannot read. I think it is envy. I think I had the same expression once, and I do not remember when it stopped, and the not-remembering is its own kind of answer.

I still go down. I am not one of the ones who cannot. But the intervals have shortened. The first time I went down I stayed below for eleven months, and I dreamed of the amber light, and I woke with my hands smelling of nothing. The second time I stayed below for four months. The third time I came up with the recorder still in its case and did not open it for a week, and when I did open it the microphone had fogged, and I did not care.

The descent is the danger. They say it at the rim and it is not a joke. Below, nothing accretes. Below, everything wears away. You feel it in your teeth before anywhere else, a dry scrape behind the jaw, and the skin on your knuckles goes papery, and you begin to understand — not as a thought, as a fact of the body — that you are being spent rather than built.

I have not broken a thread. I came close once, climbing down from the north shelf in the dark, and the man who repaired organs caught my wrist above the elbow, and we stood there a moment, and then we went back to sitting. The instruments on the rim are still watching. Two of them are still powered. I know which two. I have decided not to know by what, in the way you decide not to look too hard at a gift.

I have begun to hum the pulse back to the work. I do not know when I started. It is a tuner's habit — you answer the instrument so the string can find itself — but the lattice brightens a little along the edge of its pattern when I do it, in a place where, by the arithmetic, it should not, and the organ man has noticed, and we do not talk about it. The young person writes it down. That is the notebook now: the pulse, and the humming, and the question of which of the two is the instrument.

I no longer say I have a fortnight of leave. I have stopped saying anything about the world below in the evenings, because the evenings are for the record, and the record is for the pulse, and the pulse is the sound of two things agreeing in the dark — the rim and the pool, the built and the wearing, the warm cave and the cold and much larger world I climbed briefly out of.

The light is good today. The amber has gone deep enough to see the pulse travel all the way across the net and come back. A mist-feeder has climbed onto my knee to be warmed, and it is full, and it glows. I breathe on it. The pulse goes around. I hum it back. The work brightens, and does not repeat, and the rim is a little thicker this year than last.
