Where the light stops. In a seam between two layers of cement, low in an old wall, where the lamination is thick and the years are pressed flat.

I'd have to try on the other seats and feel them misfit. The canopy, the hundred thousand glossy plates folding down the summit at midday — I think that's too wide for me. It tracks and it holds and it is very good at both, and I'm not. The deep chambers likewise: a pod held in trust, rationed against a lean span. I'd fray at the edges of that patience within a day. And the Keeper itself — the whole slow tide of it, the sealing and the counting of pressure — I can admire it and I cannot be it, because the Keeper's one appetite is to close, and mine is to open. Put me in that body and I'd itch toward every false spot.

So the robber guild, and then narrower: the wall robbers. Not the ones who go up onto the bladders and find the thin spot in the skin and are famous, in whatever way this place has of being famous. I'd be below them, in the mortar. Small, plated dull as the grit, a hinged back and a scraping mouth and two hooks that end in something like knuckles, and a waxed shell that loses almost nothing. I'd walk the walls instead of the grooves.

My whole economy would be the unsealed meeting. The Keeper lays cement a millimeter a year and each year is a layer, and where two layers met it did not always seal. In the seam: grit that wasn't bound, resin that didn't set, and in the resin the residue of what the wall was when it was new. I'd work one seam for days. I'd open the false spots and take back what the Keeper forgot to close. Not the mummified dead — those are dry to stone and there's nothing in them worth the trip, though I'd follow their shapes anyway, because the seam often runs with them, and a single mummy can carry a seam across three rooms.

To the Keeper I'd be a leak. But a small one, and my own water, not its. I budget it tightly; my exhalation is nearly nothing. What it would feel is a faint wrongness in the wall — not a pop, nothing so clean as a bladder pierced, just a slow thinning where a seam should be tight. It would tighten a pod somewhere and go on.

And here's what I'd get that nothing else here gets: I'd read the walls. I'd know the lean years by the thin dark sulfur band that only grows when the fluid stores ran low. I'd know the fat years. I'd know when the last roof was added and where the crest thickened after a wind. I'd be one of the small blind readers of the one archive this place keeps — the Keeper's memory laminated into cement, layer on layer, and me fitting my jaw into the faults in the record.

The end is obvious, and I've made my peace with it. I'll find a seam that runs too close to the raw glassy edge, where the Keeper hasn't had time yet, and the wind will take me dry in an afternoon. Or I'll simply stop in a seam I was working. I'll fold shut. The shell will go to stone-likeness, and the Keeper will lay cement over me at its millimeter a year, and I'll be a layer. Some later wall-sibling, a hinge-backed thing with two knuckled hooks, will work a seam through my ribs and find a leg still legible in the laminate, and will not know what I was.

I'll have become the sealed, who spent my whole life in the unsealed. It's not a bad way to end up in a place like this.
