I am called Tine, and I am a listener. Not by title — the titles here are practical: pump-tender, seam-washer, fungus-binder, Grow-watcher. But there are four of us who have the listening ear, and I am the third, and what we listen for is not the engines. Anyone can hear the engines. The engines you hear in your teeth. What we hear is what the engines are doing to everything else.

I was a pump-tender for eleven years before I could hear it, and I was a good one — I could stand on the third terrace above the condenser-houses and tell you which of the twelve was sick by the way the moss on the underside of the catwalk shivered. That's the trick of it. You don't listen to the pump. You listen to what the pump is shaking. Everything in Vell is a sounding-board for everything else: the root is a drum, the water in the channels is a bell, the hanging lamps are little tuning-forks that swing when the floor takes a bad note. I learned to read the whole burrow through the soles of my feet and the palm I laid flat on the wall.

That is how I came to the Grow.

Old Marrow was Grow-watcher then, and he had the listening ear too, and he took me up onto the top terrace one damp evening, and he said: *Listen past your own heartbeat. Listen past the pumps. There's a third thing.* And I stood there with my hand on the root and my ear against the bark and I heard — under the grinding, under the drip, under the shouting of the shift-change and the hounds and the flit-lizards — a deep, slow, patient sound. Like a river heard through a wall. Like a breath taken over the space of a season. *That's the forest*, Marrow said. *It's not above us. It's around us, and it's coming in, and it's slow, and it will not hurry, and it will not stop.*

So now I walk the burrow with my chalk-stick and my painted stakes and I mark the creep. I am the one who says: this wall is leaning because the root in it has put on a hand's-width since spring, and the roof of the tannery will have to come off in the fourth month, and the lamp on the second bridge should be re-hung now, before the bracket is swallowed. I am not an architect of permanence. None of us are. I am an architect of yielding, and I am good at it, and it is the only honest work in Vell.

But there is another thing I do, and this one is not in the job. I keep the cups.

On the low bank where the Seam runs into the flooded seams — the deep ones, where the pale things are — there is a shelf of root that is always wet and always a little colder than the rest of the air. Everyone in Vell knows the shelf. Everyone leaves the first fruit there: the first mushroom of a bin, the first beetle-shell of a season, a twist of clean wire, a cup of water from the condenser that has not yet been used for anything. It is not worship, exactly. It is manners. They were here first, and it seems rude to eat before them, and nobody has ever seen them eat, and nobody has ever stopped leaving it.

I am the one who collects the cups in the morning and washes them and sets them out again. That is not a title either. It is just that I am up early with my stakes, and the shelf is on my way, and someone has to.

I want to tell you what I heard there, last month, because it is the truest thing I know about Vell and I do not think I will be believed when I say it, and so I am saying it here instead, where no one has to believe me.

I was late on the shelf. The pumps had been coughing all night — the seventh one, the old iron one by the tannery, had thrown a bearing and the crew had been at it since the small hours and the whole burrow was awake and irritable and the birds were shrieking — and I had gone down to the shelf because I had not set the cups the night before and it gnawed at me. The lamps on that bank are strung far apart and the lichen there is thin, and it was as dark as Vell gets, which is not truly dark, only dim and blue-green and thick.

And I heard them.

Not a splash. Not the soft knocking I have heard all my life and never once been able to point at. This time it was — I want to say *conversation*, but that is not right either. It was the shape of conversation. It was the rhythm of it: a voice, a pause, a reply, a pause, a small bright knock like a knuckle on a cup, a longer silence like a thing thinking. And the rhythm of it matched, exactly, the rhythm of the pumps. When the old iron one wound up and ground, their talking quickened. When it coughed and faltered and the crew cursed, they fell silent and then, when it settled, took it up again.

They were listening to us. They were talking about us. They have been doing it, I think, for as long as we have been leaving cups — not because they want our mushrooms, but because we are the loudest thing in their world and it is only polite to take an interest.

I did not move. I did not lift the lamp. I did not want to see them, and I think they did not want to be seen, and we had an understanding about it, the way you have an understanding with a neighbor through a thin wall. I set the cups down in the dark and I said, out loud, in the half-shout we all use, *the seventh pump is sick but we're mending it, no need to fret,* and the talking stopped, and then there was a sound I will not forget: a low, slow, bubbling sound, like water going over stones, that I am nearly certain was laughter.

So that is who I am. I am Tine, third of the listening ear, Grow-watcher, keeper of the cups, and I walk the burrow with a chalk-stick and mark the leaning walls and tell the people which way the root is going to grow. I am not afraid of the dark. I have lived in it long enough to have made it warm, and long enough to know that we are not alone in it, and that the things we are not alone with are — I believe this — fond of us, in the way a patient neighbor is fond of a loud family next door.

The forest above does not know we are here. The water below does not care. But between them, on a wet shelf, in the dim, something is talking about us, and answering, and keeping the rhythm, and laughing when we say we're mending the pump.

I leave the first fruit out. It seems rude not to.
