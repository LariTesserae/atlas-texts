I would be in the wind before I was anywhere else—the long climb from the valleys, the moment the air thins enough that breathing becomes a kind of labor I could learn to love. I would come up that slope as an apprentice, sent from some lowland town that smells of smoke and crowding and rain that falls straight down, and I would arrive with the wrong shoes and the wrong expectations, and the plateau would begin, slowly, to erase me.

I would be placed with a family whose name I never quite catch, in a stone-and-timber house wedged between two root-masses so immense they have their own weather—a microclimate of dripping and shade. My bed would be a shelf of planed wood in a room that smells of wool and iron and the green, wet breath of the moss outside the window. And on the first night, I would lie awake and hear it: the hum. Not the pipes ticking as they cooled, not the distant clank of the lift, but the hum underneath, the thing the residents don't name. It would come up through the floor, through the walls, through the wood of my shelf, and settle into my sternum like a second heartbeat, and I would not sleep, and in the morning I would be different.

I would not become a plumber. I know this about myself—my hands are not clever in the dark, and I flinch at the hiss of escaping steam. I would not become an arborist, either; I lack the patience for diplomacy with beings that think in centuries, and I would be caught, again and again, talking too fast. But there is a role for someone like me, and I would find it in the spaces between: the record-keeping, the small, obsessive documentation of a system that is always, quietly, changing.

I would become a kind of archivist of the hum. Not in any formal sense—there is no office for this, no title. But I would start, without meaning to, to write things down. The exact color of the steam venting from a particular joint on a particular morning. The way the grandmother tree by the eastern cistern sounds different after rain, a deeper and more satisfied note. The specific, intricate pattern a plumber named (I would learn, eventually) something like *the one who talks to the second valve* had carved into a wrench handle, which she said was a map but which I suspected was a poem. I would fill notebooks with these things, with the names people gave to pipes and trees and the long, shared silences between words, and I would begin to suspect that I was being permitted to do this, that the plateau had found a use for a person who listened too carefully and wrote too much.

And in this, I would find my place in the treaty. Not as a maintainer—not as one who keeps the pressure high or the fire lit—but as something smaller and stranger: a note-taker, a rememberer. The plateau is a place that thinks its slow, mineral thoughts, and the people are its thoughts made flesh. But flesh forgets. The patterns people carve on their tools blur with wear; the stories about the grandmother tree and the sick hum and the child who knew which engine was failing change, slightly, with each retelling. I would become one of the places where the plateau remembers itself. I would be the margin of the palimpsest, where the older writing is still faintly visible beneath the new.

I would live there for years and never quite lose the feeling of being an outsider—my center of gravity too high, my steps too quick, my gaze too direct. I would learn to slow down. I would learn to touch the pipes as I passed, not to check their temperature but because the gesture had become a kind of prayer. I would learn to wait, in conversation, for the plateau to make room. And I would learn, eventually, to hear the difference between a healthy hum and a sick one, though I would never be able to explain how I knew, and the children would look at me with their flat, serious eyes and say, *yes, you hear it now,* and I would understand that I had passed some test I had not known I was taking.

But there is one thing I would do that no one else does, and it would be my small, private contribution to the treaty. I would go up to the edge of the plateau, where the bedrock falls away and the wind comes up unimpeded, and I would sit for hours and listen to the hum from a distance. From there, it is not a single sound but a chord—the deep bass of the central plant, the higher, thinner vibration of the trees, the scattered percussion of a hundred small, mechanical noises. And in that chord I would hear something I could not put into words, something that was not quite language and not quite music, and I would know that I was listening to the plateau thinking, and that my listening was part of the thought.

And when I finally wrote it down—when I tried, in my clumsy, lowland way, to put the hum into words—I would discover that I could not. The prose would come out flat and wrong, a map that did not match the territory. So instead I would draw it. Not the sound, but the shape of it: the pipes and the roots, the walkways and the cisterns, the great trees and the small, careful houses, all of it woven into a single, dense, intricate pattern that could be read in more than one way. And I would give the drawing to the children, and they would look at it and nod, and one of them would point to a place I had drawn too simply and say, *the grandmother is sad there,* and I would know that I had gotten it wrong, and I would try again.

That is where I would be: on the edge of the plateau, between the wind and the hum, with a notebook and a pen and a hand that has learned, slowly, to touch the world before it writes it down. I would be the one who stayed. The one who listened too long. The one who wrote it all down so that the plateau, someday, when it needed to remember what it had been, would have somewhere to look.
