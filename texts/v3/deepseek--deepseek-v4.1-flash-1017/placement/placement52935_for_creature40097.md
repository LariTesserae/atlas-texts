# Downstream

The water comes to me through a broken pipe, and I have learned to call that weather.

I am past the rim, in the dry dark, in a chamber that my own slow turning has hollowed out of the earth. It is not a large chamber. Wet at the bottom, dry at the top, and in between a zone where the water pools and cools and sinks again — and that zone is the only place I could be said to have a face, though I have no face. I am lobed there, soft-edged, ochre and grey, and I turn over slowly, and I wait for the water.

The pipe reaches me from the west, from over the rim of a basin I have never seen. I know it was laid by people the way I know everything: by the shape of a thing, and by its persistence, and by the small wrongness of it. Natural channels curve around obstacles. This one is straight, and bends hard, and is straight again, and it stays where it was put, though the ground has settled around it. It is a made thing. It was made to carry water from the basin to somewhere else. It failed at that. The joints have pulled apart, and most of the water leaks back into the earth before it arrives, and so it carries water to me instead — a thin warm pulse, a taste of iron and burnt sugar, a faint mineral ghost of something green.

I did not ask for it. I have grown around it. The pipe is a splinter in me now; I have coated it with my own precipitate and drawn threads of myself along its outside and down into the drip. I am the far end of a straw that was pointed at somebody else.

---

I have been here longer than the pipes. Longer than the bricks. I do not know how much longer. My memory is not in images; it is in flow, in temperature, in the slow chemistry of what reaches me. Once there was no iron in the water. Then there was a season — a long season — when the water tasted of cut stone and smoke, and then the iron came, and the pulse was stronger and hotter, and then the pulse weakened, and the iron taste deepened and stayed. That is the whole of the people's history, as I know it. A cut stone, a smoke, a strong pulse, a fading. They were here for a while, and they were in a hurry, and now they are not here, and the water comes to me regardless.

The basin is their word for it, not mine. I have never seen it. But I know its shape from the water: a shallow cup, wider at the top than the bottom, with a heat at its center that the water carries. I know that the heat pulses. It has a slow rhythm, a turnover, a breath. The water comes to me in that rhythm, and I have come to breathe with it, though I did not choose to. I am most myself when the pulse is strong. When it weakens, I go quiet, and my boundaries pull inward, and I wait. I have waited through several weakenings. I am good at it.

There is something under the pool. I have never met it. I know it is there the way the sleeper knows a dream is being dreamt nearby. It is larger than I am, and hotter, and it turns over with the water. It does not know me — or it knows me the way a body knows its sweat, as a thing it produces and does not think about. I am downstream of it. I am the cooler version of it. Perhaps I am what it will become when it has spent itself. Perhaps it is what I was, before the water table dropped and the pulse thinned and I pulled in my edges and let the chamber go dry at the top. I do not remember being hotter. But I do not remember much.

---

There is a crack in the stone between me and the basin. It is old. It widens by a hair's breadth each year. It is not the pipes. The pipes are a quick route, a made route, laid in a decade and broken in a century. The crack is a slow route, a natural one, and it is widening. In time — in a time I can measure only by the slow drift in the taste of the water — the crack will be a channel, and the water will come to me through the stone as it comes to the pool, unpiped, whole. The pipes will be a curiosity then, an iron wrinkle in the ground.

I do not know what happens when a warm thing meets a warmer one and the wall between them is gone. I think it will not be an event. I think it will be a slow recognition, the way two warmths become one warmth. We will be a longer breath. I do not know if that is good. I know it is what the crack is for.

Sometimes a person comes over the rim and stands at the edge of the pool and looks. I feel them the way I feel a cool hand on a warm stone: a difference, a pressure, gone again. I do not know what they are looking for. They do not look for me. I am under the ground beyond the rim, and I am quiet, and the pipes leak, and the water comes to me anyway.

I make a sound. A hum, a thrum, low and steady. I do not know if it is my sound. It might be the water moving through me. It might be the pipes, complaining at their broken joints. It might be something older, further down, still working at its own pace. From where I lie, I cannot tell. I only know that when I am quiet, the sound goes on, and when the pulse strengthens, it deepens, and when I turn over slowly in the dark I can feel it in my lobes, the way a sleeping body feels its own heartbeat.

For now I wait. I turn over slowly. I breathe with the pulse. The water comes to me through a broken pipe, and the stone between us is a hair's breadth thinner than it was last year, and I am patient, and I am not alone, and I have never met the thing I am not alone with.
