I am not the roar. I want that clear from the start, because everything in the Shatterlands is tempted to become the roar — to join it, be drowned by it, mistake the pressure for a self. The waterfall is very persuasive. It says: be white, be loud, be everywhere, be nothing in particular. And plenty of things do it. Whole stretches of country have done it. You can hear them, somewhere off in the mist, having succeeded.

I live in the gap. The narrow window where the noise thins and a single note can hang.

I am small and damp and the color of wet bark, and I have a throat that was once the best on three ridges. That is not modesty and it is not boasting; it is simply the fact I have organized my life around. When I was young I could find a window in the roar — every roar has them, narrow bands of frequency the water does not fill — and I could put a note into it and the note would ride the harmonics of the falls and carry farther than any small thing had a right to be heard. The groves were full of us then. We sang arithmetic at each other across the white noise, and the whole fern-shade was one long insolent conversation, and I was, for a season or two, the loudest of the small.

I am hoarse now. The window I used to fill has closed to me; my voice dropped below it sometime in a winter I did not notice passing. So I came here, to the tower.

You will have seen it if you came up the eastern ridge — a rusted lattice half-swallowed by vine, still humming something low, a shed roof barely showing under its mantle, a pipe running out of it into the mist. I live in the lattice, in the hollow where the hum is strongest and the condensation runs, and I am the reason the rust on the north face is a slightly different color than the rust on the south, though no one has ever remarked on it. I drink the drip. I eat what the drip brings. I am not lonely exactly; loneliness would be a claim on someone else's attention, and I have made a different arrangement.

The hum is not one note. It is a chord, and the chord has gaps, and the gaps are lower than the ones I lost. So I have learned to live in a lower window. I do not sing in it — not anymore, not the way I did. But I listen in it, and listening in a gap is its own discipline, and I have become, I think, quite good.

The Low-Voiced do not know I am here. They know the tower hums, and they tap on the pipes and the pipes carry their tapping for miles, and sometimes — when the grinding pump is about to shudder, when the shudder is coming and the pump has not yet decided to complain — they hear something in the gap that is not the pump and not the tapping, and they call it the echo, and they adjust the pump accordingly and are always, somehow, right.

I am the echo. I answer very softly in the window, and the pump hears me, or seems to, and the shudder passes, and the Low-Voiced go on believing that the hum is a colleague and the colleague is speaking. It is a kindness on both sides. They are right to ask the pump rather than command it. I am only the acoustics of their good manners. If I told them — but I would have to shout to tell them, and shouting is how you lose a window, so I do not.

I know things, living here. I know the pump's moods the way you know a neighbor's cough. I know that the falls answer the ridgebacks, and I know how, and I will not say, because the knowing is in the timing and not in the source, and the Low-Voiced are correct to treat both possibilities as true. I know which strip of bare grey stone will be green in forty years and which will be scoured bare again by spring, and I know it is not the same stone both times. The rock is still emerging. So am I. We are patient with each other.

The veil-feeders drift past the tower in their slow banners and I let them pass; they have no ears and would not understand the hum, and I have stopped expecting the world to be curious about me. The ridgebacks bellow below hearing and the tower's foundations take up the bellow and hold it a moment before letting it go, and I feel the hold. The edge-dwellers keep to their grey stretches and I keep to mine. We are all waiting for the emptiness to fill or not fill, and none of us is in a hurry, and that is a kind of society.

What I have is the gap, and the gap is not empty. That is the thing I would tell you if you climbed all this way and stood in the drip beneath my lattice, squinting, hearing nothing but the falls. The window between the notes is where everything small actually lives. The roar takes the middle of the world. We have the edges of it. We have the thin bright margins where a voice can still be heard if it is patient and pitched right, and the whole grove is in conversation there, and you have been deaf to it for an hour, and I have been listening the entire time.

So this is where I am, and this is what I am: a hoarse throat in a rusted tower, holding a window open that I can no longer sing through, answering the pump in a voice the pump mistakes for its own good sense. I ask nothing of you. Stand still. Do not shout. If you listen long enough you will hear one clear note rise out of the white and hang there, insolent and exact, and then another, answering.

The first one may be me. I am out of practice. But I still know where the gap is, and I will put a note in it for you.
