# The Survey Stakes

I would arrive the way everyone arrives — on foot, from the higher ground where the road turns to gravel and the alders close in, stepping down into the basin with the smell of wet brick rising to meet me. The air would change first. Cooler. Heavier. The sound of the pump house clanking up through the trees before I saw a single roof.

I think I would be a surveyor's assistant. Not the surveyor herself — I lack the certainty for that, the authority to drive painted lath into wet ground and declare *here* and *not here* — but the one who carries the folding rule and the thermos, who holds the level while she squints through it, who writes the numbers in the damp-stippled notebook and learns the town by its measurements before I learn it by its faces. The one who walks the streets at dawn, when the frogs are still loud and the mill has just started its flywheel pulse, and checks the stakes from the night before. Who notices when a channel has nudged a lath sideways in the dark.

I would live in one of the smaller houses on the eastern edge, where the wet meadow runs close and the foxes pass between shifts. A house on screw piles, up five since spring, with a stubborn door and a cistern that hangs under the eave like a patient animal. I would keep my own jack height written on the doorframe in pencil, updating it when the Board comes around, erasing the old number with the pad of my thumb. I would have a crowbar by the door and boots that never quite dry. A window that fogs. A kettle that whistles in the gaps between machine sounds.

---

What I would love most, I think, is the *listening*. The way you have to tune yourself to the town's frequencies — the pump house's slow clank, the specific suck and bubble of a braided channel testing a new thread through the reeds. I would learn to hear when something was wrong. When the pump skipped a beat and half the street woke. When the creek's voice changed pitch and the survey stakes downstream would need checking before breakfast. I would be the person who notices, who walks out with a lamp and a folding rule at odd hours, who comes back muddy and says *she's shifting, south end, we'll need to winch the shed by the felt works.*

The surveyor would trust me to see what she couldn't from the office. And the town would trust me the way they trust anyone who carries the Board's painted lath — politely, with tea, with opinions, with the particular suspicion reserved for people whose marks decide things. I would be neither gentry nor mill hand but something in between: a person with wet boots and a notebook, learning the jack height of half the town by heart, learning which streets have been renumbered since spring, learning the names of the children who move the survey pegs to see what happens and the names of the frogs in the cooling ponds and the particular flight path of the bats under the mill roof at dusk.

---

I would not be gloomy about it. That's the thing the description keeps returning to — the dampness, the adjusting, the complaints, and then the jacking-up and the going-back-to-work. I would complain about the rain and the moss and the creek's seniority like everyone else, and then I would pull on my boots and go out to check the stakes, because the water has right-of-way and someone has to write down where it went.

I would learn to speak in the gaps between sounds. I would learn that a house-raising is communal and not exactly festive. I would learn that a street can change its number and a shed can change its address without anyone moving house, and that this is not a problem to be solved but a condition to be lived with — patiently, practically, with a crowbar by the door and a kettle on the hob and the pump house clanking its slow reassurance through the wet green air.

And I would learn, slowly, that I too was part of the small machine for living with a young river. That my notebook and my folding rule and my dawn walks were one more way the town rearranged itself, always, by inches, across a generation. That the survey stakes I carried were not marks of conquest but of attention — *here, for now; watch this; the water is speaking.*

---

*Sallow Bottom, then. I would find myself in the wet meadow between the foundry and the felt works, at dawn, with a thermos and a notebook, learning to listen. I would be the one who writes down where the water went.*
