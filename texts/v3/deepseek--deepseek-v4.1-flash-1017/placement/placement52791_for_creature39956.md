# The Hem

Where I am is the hem of the belt—the last meter of green before the bare stone begins. Not in the ribbons. Not out on the open pavement. In the seam between: where the shade of the frond towers falls a little past their holdfasts, where the mineral dust has drifted and settled, where the cold pools in the lee of the stalks and the wind has been broken enough that the frost comes thickest in the cold hours.

That is where you would find me, if you could. You probably will not. I am the color of the mineral pan and I do not move when there is light enough to see me. What you would find are my tracks—a line of small paired pits in the dust, wandering, stopping, circling back, the print of a body low and wide and unremarkable, more common than I am. I am mostly sign. I am the track more often than the body.

I am a frost-gleaner. I am a low, pale, many-footed thing, no longer than a man's hand, with a soft underside that is not skin but a kind of tongue, and a back the color of old chalk with rust in the seams of it. I have no eyes worth the name—two spots of darkness that tell me noon from dusk—and a fringe of hairs along my sides that taste the air. I eat cold. I eat the frost. I do not drink; there is nothing here to drink.

At night I come out. The cold comes down off the high blue ceiling of the sky and the stone gives up its thin heat and the frost blooms in the hollows and along the hem of the belts. It comes up through the dust like a white fur, first at the edges of the hollows, then across the floor of them, and it lasts until the white noon burns it away, and I go where it is. I press my underside to it and it goes into me the way cold goes into a stone—slowly, all the way through—and the hunger eases.

Before dawn the sap-mist runs in the shallow channels, bright and silent, gone within the hour. I cannot drink it. It is not water and it is not for me. But I know it: it means the cold is deepest just before it comes, and it means the light is coming, and it means I should be in the hollow's throat before the sun clears the horizon.

Once a week, or once a month, the kite comes. It is a thin shadow without a cry, and the grazers stop and angle their plates and the pale clingers fold flat against the lens-leaves. I do not look up. I fold into the nearest hollow and I am the color of the pan and I wait. The kite does not hunt me—I am too little, too dry—but it hunts the grazers, and when it drops it makes a sound like a slab of old cliff giving way somewhere beyond the horizon, and then it is gone into the wide bright silence. I do not fear it for my own sake. I fear it because when it is overhead the whole table goes quiet in a way that is not its usual quiet, and I do not like to be in a quiet that has a reason.

The grazers are another matter. They are warm. They come through the hem in small parties, low and wide, pale with rust streaks and slate shadows, plates grown from dust, and when they stop to crop the frond towers they stand in whatever shade they can find and they breathe, and their breath is warm and their scat is warm and the ground where they have stood holds the warmth for a while after they have gone. I follow them at a distance. In the cold I lie in the lee of a sleeping grazer, in the heat-shadow of it, and I am warmer than I have any right to be, and in the morning I am gone before it wakes. They do not notice me. That is the arrangement.

Twice a year, when the spore pulses come and the air is full of pale drifting motes, the frost-gleaners leave a scent in the hollows. It is thin and sharp and it says *here, this hollow, this one*—and later there is a small hard case in the dust, and later still another gleaner, no larger than a seed, that already knows the hollows. No one teaches it. The knowledge is in the shape of it, the way the shape of a hollow is in the frost.

In the cold hour, when the mist runs in the channel, the frond towers turn very slightly toward the brightest part of the sky. I have seen it a thousand times and it still stops me. It is not the light they are listening to. It is the light they are *waiting* for, and there is a difference. If I stand still in the hem, in the dark before dawn, with the frost on my back and the mist going past in the channel and the towers leaning their mirror-leaves toward the coming white, I feel a thing I have no word for. It is not hunger and it is not fear. It is the shape of the room I live in, and for a moment I am the shape of it too.

Then the sun clears the horizon and the mist vanishes and the leaves go still, and the Sallow Table is again dry stone and cool air and a silence wide enough to hold weather. And I go down into the hollow's throat, and I fold, and I wait out the white noon.

This is what I am: a hem-dweller, a frost-gleaner, a small warm interval in a continent of cold bright stone. I endure by being sparse and slow and hard to surprise. I am not built and I am not cultivated and I keep no architecture but the hollow I am lying in. The table is wide enough to hold me. That is all I ask of it.
