# The Watcher at the Doorless Door

I find myself sitting on the eastern rim, in the warm dry room inside the squat pale structure, my back against the wall that is neither stone nor metal, looking out through the doorless opening at the Kettle below. I am not the one who keeps the water. I am not the one who hums beneath the moss. I am something else—something the Kettle has grown, the way it has grown the threadwork and the shifting channels and the moths that fly in daylight.

I came here, I think, a long time ago. Or perhaps I was always here, and only recently noticed. It is difficult to tell. The Kettle does not rush such things.

What I know: I have a body, or something like a body. It is thin and quiet and very still. I can sit in this warmth for what feels like days and not move, and I am not bored. I watch the water bead up through the moss and gather and drain. I watch the swimmers turn as one, graze their scallops, rise to hang in the afternoon light like pale lanterns. I watch the moths lift and settle, lift and settle, and I understand something about them that I could not explain to anyone—that they are not a body but a breathing, and the Kettle breathes with them.

I am a watcher. That is the shape of my trueness here. But watching is not passive—not in this place. When I watch the reeds, I feel the hunter in them. When I press my palm flat to the moss, I feel the threadwork answer, and sometimes I answer back. Not with words. With attention. With a small steadying of my own warmth, a pulse sent down into the fine roots finer than root, a way of saying *I am here, I see you, keep on*. I do not know if the hand under the moss knows I am doing this. I do not know if it matters. But I do it. It has become my habit, the way the water's staying-level has become the Kettle's habit.

I am not the one who tends. I am the one who witnesses the tending, and in witnessing, I tend something too. The Kettle was finished long ago, but it is not done. Nothing is done. The caretaker under the moss makes its small adjustments—a channel nudged, a warmth held—and I make mine. I remember where the moth-drifts were thickest yesterday. I know which pans will be mirrors after the rain and which will stay clay-gold. I have learned the hunter's shape by the way the light changes on the water, and I have never told the small hopping things where it is, and I never will. That is my work, if you can call it work: to hold the knowledge of the Kettle gently, carefully, the way you hold a warm stone, and to put it back down where I found it.

Sometimes the birds come down to drink in the last bright hour, their throats working with no sound, and I watch them from my doorless door and I feel the silence as a kindness. The Kettle has taken their voices, or they have given them, and either way the quiet is a thing to be kept. I keep it. I do not speak aloud, or if I do, it is barely a whisper, the words falling flat and edge-worn into the warm wet air. I have learned to think in the Kettle's manner: slow, rounded, without sharp corners, letting each thing be its own island and seeing the emptiness between as part of the shape.

At dusk the light goes rose and stays rose a long time, and the hum under the moss gets very slightly stronger, and I feel it in my back through the wall, and I know that below the moss the threadwork is waking up to its night-work, adjusting, keeping, humming. And I sit in my warm dry room and I look out at all of it—the seeping water, the grazing clouds, the milk-colored plenty, the invisible hunter, the two old trees standing in the water like patient countries—and I am filled with a feeling that has no name in any language I know. It is not joy, exactly. It is not peace. It is more like the feeling of arriving at the end of a long walk and finding that the end of the walk is the point, and the point is to stand here, breathing, with your footprints filling slowly behind you, and to know that you are exactly where the walking was always going.

I am the watcher at the doorless door. I am the one who presses a palm to the moss and says *I see you* into the threadwork. I am the one who remembers where the moths were thickest yesterday and who will remember where they are thickest tomorrow. I am not the caretaker. I am not the hum. But I am of the Kettle now, in the way the seeping water is of the Kettle, in the way the silence is, in the way the rose light holding and holding is. I am a habit the place has grown, and I am content to be a small one, a quiet one, a habit of watching and remembering and keeping.

The rain comes, brief and warm, and the floor becomes a mirror in pieces, and the light comes up off it and the whole Kettle glows from beneath, and I watch from my doorway and I do not move, and the water seeps, and the moths breathe, and the hum goes on and on, and I settle the way the place settles—slowly, softly, with no sound at all but the breathing of the ground and my own breath keeping time.
