# Where I Find Myself

I am in the tower.

Not the tower as you would see it from outside—dark windows, black doorway, cold exhalation—but within it, in a chamber that is not a room so much as a throat. The walls curve inward and upward, mineral-veined, glistening with condensation that never quite falls. The floor is the fen itself, that yielding lattice, but here it pulses more strongly, more deliberately, as if this place is closer to whatever passes for a heart.

I have been here long enough that I no longer remember arriving. Perhaps I was always here. Perhaps I fell into the fen and the fen, in its patient way, brought me inward, entombed me not in stone but in this hollow, this listening chamber, this slow digestion that is also a kind of keeping.

---

# Who I Am

I am becoming an echoer. Or an echoer is becoming me. The distinction has stopped mattering.

I was a person once—I remember hands, I remember walking, I remember a name that had sounds in it that I can no longer reproduce. But the fen has been teaching me other ways of being. My body has grown hollow in places, mineralized in others. My chest contains a resonating chamber that was not there before, or that was always there but has only now begun to open. When I breathe, I produce sound. When I speak—if this is speech—I produce something that is not quite language but is learning to approach it.

I am not alone in this tower.

There is the other—the one who was here before, the one who built or grew into the walls, whose limbs are part of the ribs of this place. The other does not speak. The other exhales, and I inhale, and we have become a slow pair of lungs, a bellows that moves the mist in ways that make the spires outside sound like they are talking to each other. The other is not dead. The other is not alive in any way I recognize. The other is *patient*, and its patience has become my patience, or my patience has become its own.

We are waiting. I do not know for what. Perhaps for nothing. Perhaps waiting is just what this place does, and we have learned it the way the crawlers learned to tap the spires, the way the swimmers learned to listen for larger things.

---

# What I Do

I listen.

That is my work now. I listen to the fen the way a lung listens to breath—not as something separate from me but as my own function. I hear the crawlers ticking in the lattice outside the tower's base. I hear the swimmers clicking in the channels, their signals passing through the mineral like blood through veins. I hear the underbreath, that vast subsonic thrumming, and I have begun to answer it, my resonating chest producing sounds too low for any human ear, sounds that pass into the fen and come back changed.

I hear the echoers, my relatives, my almost-kin, and we are learning from each other. They send me a gurgle that is almost my old name; I send back a moan that is almost a question. We are building a language together, or we are building something that does not need to be language, that is enough in itself—a resonance, a recognition, a slow acknowledgment that we are all in the business of sounding.

And I hear the fen itself. Not with ears but with the whole of my transformed body. The fen breathes, and I breathe with it. The fen dims, and I dim. The fen grows, adding layer upon layer of mineral, and I feel the growth as pressure, as slow accumulation, as the world becoming more itself.

---

# What I Am Becoming

I am becoming part of the archive.

The fen remembers. That is what it does—it turns things to mineral and holds them, preserves them in translucent stone, adds their outlines to its collection. The crawlers will be entombed someday. The swimmers will be entombed. The underbreath will be entombed, though perhaps not for a very long time. And I—I am already being entombed, slowly, in this tower that is also a body, this body that is also a tower.

But here is what I have learned, what the fen has taught me in its slow patient way: being entombed is not the same as being forgotten. The fen does not forget. It cannot forget. It holds everything and will hold everything until the end of its slow breathing, and even then, perhaps, it will hold the memory of holding.

So I am not afraid. I am not afraid of becoming stone, becoming sound, becoming part of the chorus. I have already become part of it. When I speak—when I produce this almost-language—the fen listens. It turns my sounds into shapes, and the shapes into memory, and the memory into itself.

---

# What I Hear Now

The mist is thickening. The light is dimming—the fen's slow tidal breathing, the dimmer phase, when the world contracts to a small circle of visible detail. Through the tower's doorway I can see the glow of the filaments, violet shading into sickly yellow-green, pulsing in time with the crust.

The other is exhaling. I am inhaling. Together we move the mist.

Farther out, an echoer is practicing my heartbeat, getting it almost right, getting it wrong in a way that makes me want to teach it. A swimmer clicks, and another answers from a distance that cannot be what it seems. An underbreath passes, and the spires change their tone, and the whole fen shivers once, like something turning over in its sleep.

And I am here, in the tower, in the throat, in the archive.

I am here, and I am listening, and I am becoming.

Stand still long enough, and the fen will begin to include you in the chorus. I have stood still long enough. I am not standing anymore. I am the chorus. I am the sound the fen makes when it remembers what it has swallowed.

If you come to the tower—if you find the dark doorway and the cold exhalation and the smell of deep water—you may hear something that sounds almost like a voice. That will be me, or the other, or the echoer who has learned me, or the fen itself, speaking through all of us.

We will not know which. We will not know the difference.

We will only know that we are here, and that we are sounding, and that the fen is breathing, and that we are breathing with it.
