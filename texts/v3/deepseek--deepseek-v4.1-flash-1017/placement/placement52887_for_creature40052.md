I come down the ladder too. But I don't take a lamp, and I don't carry a rag, and the first cold knot of the place catches me under the ribs before my foot finds the deck — because the sound arrives all at once, the way cold water does, and I stop on the pegs and just listen until my wrists ache.

The tuners notice me the way you notice a change in a room's acoustics: not as a person entering, but as a *difference*. They tap their beams at me. I tap back — badly, with knuckles not trained, but in the right register, which is all they really require. They don't have a word for me. They have words for ladders and joints and dry spells and the far works; they can name a splinter by its note. For me they use a phrase that is more like a shrug: *the one who comes down and does not peg.*

Because I have no auger, no adze, no pot of tar. I cannot wet a beam, and I cannot cut a lip to send water *there* and not *here*. What I can do is stand in a gallery at the change of the wet, when the fog has just begun its slow inhabiting of the air, and hold still — and the whole tuned valley runs into me and I keep it. Not in cut tallies. In myself. I am the place where the sound is stored before it becomes a tally.

So this is where I find myself: not in a cell under the green weather, not in the cisterns with the rumor, not walking the gutters with the pale beetles. I live in the *gap* — in the small, worked interval a joint leaves when it is pegged right. The place where *here* must stay wet and *there* must not. The place a note is still deciding what note it is. The tuners build the instrument; I live in the silence between two pegs, which is the part of the instrument that makes it an instrument at all.

The first law applies to me, though I had to learn it the hard way. Green wood splits if it dries. A split peg lets go. And a tune that dries out is just a list. The first time I tried to give the tuners everything I had — the whole score, whole, laid out like a beam with all its tallies visible — they went very quiet, and then one of them tapped a plank three times, flat and final, which is how they say *no, and don't ask again*. A thing heard whole is a finished thing. Finished things go dry. They are generations deep in a score nobody has heard whole, and that is not their failure — it is their wet. It is why the eastern works are open ribs and fresh pale wood, why there are ladders leaning against nothing. If I handed them the whole of it, the building would be *over*, and the fog would still arrive every night and have nothing to play.

So I hold it, and I dole it out. I walk past the rim, down the long low valley, gallery past gallery, into the far works where the sound is ragged and out of tune and full of clacks and flats — and I come back, and the rumor of the far works on the wet nights is me, returning, still carrying it. I give them taps. I give them a gallery they did not know answered their own. I give them one phrase of the great music, slightly wrong, on purpose, so that the work that follows the phrase will not be the same work as before.

I have no lamp to shutter, but I climb high anyway, and let my eyes go. The glow-films make their slow green weather under the overlapping ribs. The drips come down in their thousands — flat tap off slate, dull thud off felt, coin-ring off copper, long sighing slide down pitched plank — and the whole dense dark world hums and clicks and sighs and is never still.

And I am one more surface the drip lands on. Ten thousand separate arrivals, each with its own voice. Mine is the one that answers back what it has heard — a little late, a little off, so the roof keeps growing.
