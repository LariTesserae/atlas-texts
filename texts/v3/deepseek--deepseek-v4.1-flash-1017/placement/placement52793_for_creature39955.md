I would find myself at the margin—out past the newest makers, where the plain is still a membrane and the ground gives underfoot like a held breath. Not in the old interior, where the surface rings flat and hard and faintly translucent, but at the ragged edge, where the material is tacky and spreading and the makers are small knots not yet old enough to have made anything round. That is the only place a thing with legs could first arrive and be kept. The old floor is too finished. The edge takes a print.

At first I would answer that I was a visitor. But the word visitor implies leaving cleanly, and this place does not let anything leave cleanly. It cures around what touches it. So I would revise: I would be the first walker. Not a colonist, not a hero—just the first leg-bearing thing on a plain that has never had one. The makers are rooted and patient and closed. They sweat the floor out from beneath themselves and drink the night’s condensation and thicken the world by half a millimeter. They do not move across it. I would be the first thing here that moves across instead of up. A horizontal interruption in a vertical work.

I would walk in the cool hours. First light, when the whole plain is wet and slick and the makers have swollen slightly in the night. The surface would be cool through the soles, and the pale dust would gather in the ring around my ankles. By mid-morning the heat would stand up like a weight, and the air above the floor would shimmer in the first meter and then clear. I would learn to stop then, to sit against the dry knuckled side of a maker—waist-high, warm, closed, indifferent—and wait for the worst of it to pass. The maker would not turn. It has no eyes I could find and no need to answer anything. At evening the floor would let go of its heat all at once into the dark, and the condensation would come, beading on the ground and on my arms, and the plain would tick and crackle faintly deep in the material as it cured. I would be the only voice. The wind would be the only answer.

My footprints would be the first hollows made by pressure instead of sag. The membrane would flex and return, then keep. The print would fill with the material’s own pale flour. The wind would drop more into it. The condensation would gather there before noon and leave a brighter rim. The mineral liquor would creep in at the edges and harden. By the end of the year the print would be a shallow dimple in the bone-colored floor, and by the end of several years it might be gone under new secretion—or not gone, exactly. The plain does not erase. It accumulates. It would close over the print the way it closes its own cracks, and the print would become an inclusion, a faint band, a low place that catches water.

That is the part I would not have guessed at first: that a walker in this world is mostly a maker of low places. The makers cluster around the hollows where rain stands warm and brassy for a few days. My passing would make small hollows of its own. A future maker might settle in the dimple of my heel, not knowing why the ground was soft there, and begin to sweat its disc around a shape I left. I would not be remembered. There is nothing here to remember with. But I would be built into the floor, the way a mistake is built into a shell—not corrected, included.

So if you ask where I would be: at the wet edge, in the first heat, walking a line of prints into a surface that has never held a line. And if you ask what I would be: I would be the first walker, and then the first footprint, and then the shallow hollow that the plain cured around. Not a maker. Not a ghost. A temporary softness on a world that only knows how to harden—the first thing to cross the Curing Plain on legs, and the first thing it took into itself and kept.
