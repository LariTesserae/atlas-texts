I am not the keeper. I am not so necessary as that.

I am the one who comes in the intervals between her shifts—though "comes" is too strong a word for it, since I am never entirely absent from this place. I am the maintenance that cannot be done from inside the chamber, the hand that reaches through the hatch when she is asleep in her hammock, the shadow that slips down the ladder from the world above.

I am younger than she is, though not young. I have the softness of someone who has not yet been worn down to the essential wire of themselves by years of listening to the same turbine pitch. My hands are not yet stained gray-green. I wear two earmuffs, both firmly over both ears, and I carry a clipboard that I sometimes forget to look at because I am too busy watching the water fall.

I come down when the pressure logs need updating, when the spare parts delivery has arrived at the upper access, when the quarterly inspection requires a second signature. I come down because someone has to, and because she will not leave. She has not left in—I don't know how long. I have never asked. The question feels like a stone dropped into a well; I'm not sure I want to hear it hit bottom.

We do not talk much. The room is too loud for conversation. We communicate in gestures, in the passing of a wrench, in the way she points at a gauge and I nod and write the number down. She does not introduce me to the fern. I have learned not to mention it. Once I asked if she wanted me to take it up to the surface, to a window somewhere, and she looked at me the way she looks at a bearing that is about to fail—with a kind of distant, diagnostic sadness, as if I had revealed something about myself that could not be fixed.

I think she believes I am the one who needs saving. I think she is probably right.

When I am here, I stand in the mist and let it settle on my face. I listen to the roar until it becomes something like silence, the way she does, and I understand—for a moment, for a breath—why she stays. The room is a fact. It does not ask anything of you except attention. You give it that, and it gives you back the shape of your own days, your own small, exact loop of movements, and if you are not careful you will find that the loop has closed and you are inside it and the hatch is behind you and you have stopped remembering what the sky looks like.

I am careful. I have to be.

I am the one who carries the old gaskets up to the surface to be disposed of properly. I am the one who brings down the tea, the spare bulbs, the books she does not read. I am the one who writes in the log: *All systems nominal. Keeper present and well.* And I am the one who, standing at the hatch with my hand on the wheel, looks back down into the sodium-yellow dimness and sees her small figure bent over the console, screwdriver in her teeth, one earmuff pushed up over one ear, and feels the pull of the place like a hand on my ankle.

I close the hatch. I climb the ladder. I go back up into the world where the noise is different—scattered, broken, made of voices and weather and the clatter of things that do not run on cycles. I carry the damp with me. It takes hours to leave my jacket.

When I sleep, I dream of the float valve's clunk. I wake before it would have shuddered open. I lie still for a moment, listening to the quiet, and then I get up and go about my day, and somewhere far above me, in a hidden heart of steel, the water moves, the pressure holds, the noise continues.

And she is there, listening to it become silence, and I am here, becoming something else, and the hatch between us stays closed.
