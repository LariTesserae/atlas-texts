I find myself not far from the Rack, out where the last houses thin into single stub-walls, standing on a terrace of worn foundations in the gray before true waking. The drizzle beads on my shoulders and doesn't run. I have been here long enough that the damp has gotten into me too—I smell of the paste and the shell, and my hands are broad and thick-palmed and don't quite match the rest of me, as though I'd been remade from the wrists out.

I would be a sweeper. Not the respected kind, not the oldest of a cluster who has earned a stretch of foundation stone to polish—no, something stranger, with no cluster that presses a name into my palm. A person who came in from the dark acres with no footings of her own and was given a broom because a broom is a good thing to hold and because nobody quite knew what else to do with her. I sweep the oldest stones. The ones worn past reading. The ones nobody knows the meaning of anymore, so that the scrubbing of them is the whole of their meaning now.

This is the work I have, and I have come to love it in the way you love a scar you've stopped noticing.

I go out before I'm properly awake, while the Lid is dim and the drizzle is at its thinnest, and I go over my twenty paces. It does not need it. The stone there has been polished by ten thousand years of hands and is smooth as river cobble and the notches have filled in and I am sweeping a blank. I sweep it anyway. The sweeping is for me. I know this the way I know my own breath—which is to say, without ever putting it into words until someone asks.

At handover I take the child. Any child. The slings go from arm to arm all day and I am an arm. This is the one thing I have that is not argued over: I am trusted with the carried, because I am careful, and because I shake out the folds at every handover without being told, and because my hands are always moving. A person who keeps moving is a person who is safe to hand an infant to.

The children put their names into my palm and I press back as best I can, though mine is a thin thing, improvised, not quite settled. Some of them find this funny. Some of them find it sad. The very young don't notice at all, which is the best of it.

When I am not sweeping I am reading. That is not a word the town uses for it, but it is what I do. I walk out past the herd-light and the grandmothers and into the deep dark where the oldest of the glowing dead lie, and I sit—no, I do not sit, I crouch and I pace and I put my hand on the polished stone of them—I go out and I *read*. The oldest lights are the town's true archive and nobody can read them, and going out to look at something you cannot read is a thing people do on purpose. I do it more than most. I have my favorites, my particular ones, out past where anyone bothers to go, and I visit them the way you visit a friend who has gone quiet, and I put my palm flat on the warm fur of them and I press a name I made up for them into the light, and they cannot press back, and I stay a while, and then I come home and sweep.

I would like to tell you this is a grief. It isn't. That's the thing.

Here is what I actually know, the thing that emerges when I let it:

The Rack-dwellers have one fear and it is not flood. It is the person who sits down. And I am a person who has, in some way I cannot fully explain, already done the smaller version of that. I have no cluster. I came in from the dark. And so the town handles me the way it handles anyone in trouble—work, and company, and being passed around—even though I never asked and never said I was in trouble. The broom is the cure. The carried child is the cure. The hands laid on me for hours, not subtly, not meant to be. I submit to all of it. It works. It mostly works.

And on the days when it doesn't quite work, I go out to the old lights and put my palm on one that has been there before the oldest readable stone and I read it, which is to say I make something up, which is to say I am told a story by a grandmother I never had, in a register just at the edge of the carrying, and I come back in.

I would not want to be a surface-dweller. If you told me about the sun—the getting-up of it, the going-down, the hard-edged shadows—I would answer the way they answer: *what is it for?* The Lid gives me light for free and lays a shape on my floor that swells and dims and tells me the hour, and I have a roof with a coarse lattice, because I am not rich and vanity is not mine, but it is broken on purpose and it lights me from above like a lantern, and nobody with any sense lives in a dark house here.

The thing I would want you to understand, the thing that took me longest to feel and is now the bedrock under me:

**Stillness is how you get covered. And under the covering is only a covering.** The notches wear past reading and the sea takes the house and the bloom lights everything that holds still, and the answer is not to mourn it but to sweep, and to be swept after. I find that I believe this. Not as a comfort. As the plain truth of the place, the way a surface-dweller believes that the sun will come up whether or not they have done their accounts.

I would be here, out past the last houses at the gray edge of the day, with a broom worn smooth as the stone it sweeps, going over twenty paces that do not need it, keeping myself from being quietly erased by light.

And once or twice in a lifetime—once or twice, if I am lucky—the thing down in the vents will make its low groaning call, and I will stop where I am, and listen to it, and then go on with what I was doing.

Unless, of course, I do not go on. Unless one day I crouch out past the herd-light and the grandmothers and I do not get up. The town would argue, loudly, for a while. The hands would be laid on for a long time and then taken away. And in a year I'd be dusted at the edges, and in two I'd be furred, and then I'd be a soft full glow like a low fire that does not go out, out in the dark acres among the unnamed.

That is the one leaving the body behind as a lamp, and they speak of it in a particular tone, quietly, and not often.

It is not a sad thing. It is the one thing here that does not get worn away.
