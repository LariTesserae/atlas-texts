I would be the vapor-line joiner.

Not a glamorous trade, but an honest one. My hands know the weight of a spanner, the precise torque that seats a fitting without cracking the glass, the smell of hot iron and tallow-wax and the faint sweetish tang of vapor-grass steam. I am the one they send for when a joint begins to weep, when the amber glow in a tube flickers and gutters and the pressure gauge creeps in a direction it shouldn't.

I am not old, but I have the hands and knees of a person who is older. The vapor-lines run everywhere—over the boardwalks, along the walls, through the narrow gaps between buildings—and maintaining them means climbing, crouching, crawling into spaces where the bloom grows thick and the water drips from above and the air is so warm and wet it feels like breathing through a wet cloth. I have a canvas bag of fittings and a brass pressure-key that belonged to my mother, who was a joiner before me, who learned it from her father, who learned it from a woman whose name is now only a name in the rota.

---

I live in a low house near the western pump-house—the oldest of the three, the one whose flywheel has a slight irregular catch in its cycle that everyone has learned to hear around rather than fix, because fixing it would mean stopping the pump, and the pumps must run. My house has two rooms and a lean-to workshop where I keep my tools and my stock of glass tubing and my little cistern and my shrine: a smooth brass fitting, worn bright by handling, set on a shelf beneath a lamp that burns tallow-fig oil. Beside it, on a board I have permitted to bloom, a patch of pale-green fur glows very faintly in the dark.

I have a wife who works the drying-sheds, pressing fig-oil and mangle-squealing the mire-cotton, and a child who is not yet old enough for the rota but who can already hear a failing bearing through a wall. We eat vapor-grass roots roasted in the oil my wife brings home, and flatbread when there is grain, and we eat it slowly, listening to the water move.

---

I am part of the hydraulic system. I know the pressure of every line in the district the way a physician knows a pulse. I know that the line running from the eastern pump-house to the seed-halls runs high in the deep season, when the bloom is thick and the plants are transpiring hard, and I know the particular joint near the third drying-shed that always weeps when the pressure drops sudden and the water moves wrong. I know the sound the tubes make when they are healthy—a low, steady, barely-audible hum—and I know the sound they make when they are failing—a higher note, a screaming, a note that means *come now, come quickly, come before the glass cracks and the steam escapes and the whole delicate balance tilts.*

I do not hurry. Hurry is how you fall. But when the vapor-lines sing their high note I move with a speed that is not hurrying, a speed that is simply the absence of everything else. I have climbed a pump-house ladder in the dark, in the rain, with the bloom slick under my boots, because the line was failing and the seed-halls would freeze and the crop would die and the water would rise. I have held a joint together with my bare hands while my wife passed me the wax and the wire. I have been part of the system, and the system has held.

---

This is what it means to belong here: to know that you are a moving part in something larger, something that has been running longer than any of us, something that will continue running when we are gone. The bloom was here first. Kettledown was built in a place already alive. And I am one of the ones who keeps it alive—not through grand gestures, not through building toward anything, but through the daily, unglamorous, necessary work of keeping the water where the water needs to be.

So I am the vapor-line joiner. I am damp-strong and warm and patient. I smell of iron and steam and the bloom. I leave gaps in my speech for the pumps. I will not find the amber light strange, and I will look at you with polite incomprehension if you tell me it has a colour.

And when the line sings its high note, I will put down my cup and pick up my bag and go.
