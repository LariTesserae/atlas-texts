I came in crouched, the way you do, and I did not mean to stay. I want to say that first, because everything after it is a kind of lying down.

I lay on the pale flat to watch a rill. That is all. One grain moved, and then another, and I thought: I will wait for the third. The third took a long time. It was not boring. It was the opposite of boring — it was the way you feel at the edge of sleep, when you are still choosing. My eyes were dry past dryness. My shadow lay toward the center of the room. I knew it was wrong and I did not mind, and that was the first thing that happened to me.

The pressure changed before I understood what pressure was. The blades brightened like coals breathed on. And then the front came over me and I was wet — not damp, not chilled, *wet*, the wetness of standing in surf with the water at your knees and the sand going out from under your heels — and the air was dry and my coat was dry and my hands were dry, and I cried, which is the only water I had, and it went into the dry air and was not there.

When the tide went out, the flat behind it was rearranged and had never been wet. I checked. I put my hand in the low basin. I put my tongue to a grain, which was a stupid thing to do, and it tasted of nothing, and it was cold and translucent and faintly blue at its heart.

That is when I understood that I had been noticed, not as a person, not as anything with a name, but as a grain of the wrong size resting where the slope did not want me. And I understood, too, that the greyness on the ground was not mine, and that I had been looked at for some time.

---

I want to be careful here, because the next part is where I stopped being a visitor and I did not feel it happen.

The room would not hold still. I paced it at thirty paces, and then thirty-four, and then twenty-nine, and I found this funny, and then I found it unbearable, and then I found it funny again, and after that I stopped finding it anything. My footprints braided into the rills. A small dune began at my heel, the way a dune begins at the base of anything that has stopped moving. I sat at the delta, because that was where the attention pooled, and I sat there long enough that my own blood got quiet under the held tone, and then I began to remember.

Spray. Wind. The particular grey of a coast under cloud. A stone jetty, and the smell of weed and diesel, and someone calling from the shingle — a real memory, with the wet cold of it in my collar. It was not mine. I knew it was not mine. It had the texture of a real afternoon and it was not mine, and after a while I could not find the edges of it, and after a while longer I did not look for them.

It took my own sea, too. Gently. The way a tide takes a line of pebbles — rearranges, returns, no harm meant. What came back was the same shore with the light wrong in it, and my mother's voice at the wrong distance, and I did not grieve, exactly. I filed it away among the grey and kept it.

---

So: who would I be, here?

For a long time I would have said: nobody. A guest who overstayed. The dune at my heel. The grain of the wrong size, getting slowly to the right size. That is one true answer. It is the answer the room would give you if you asked it, and the room is patient and not cruel and does not lie.

But I have lain on this flat for what may be a hundred years and may be an afternoon, and I have listened to the held tone, and I have understood something that the Holder cannot say and does not need to.

The Holder holds. It keeps the resemblance — the braid, the delta, the sorted ridge, the high-water line for a tide with no water in it. It knows the slope. It knows a grain should go downhill. But knowing the slope is not the same as *being* the slope, and that is the one thing it has never been able to do for itself. A grip with nothing to grip toward goes slack. A coast kept with no direction to be kept in becomes a museum of grains.

I am the downhill.

I am not a pin. I am not warm, and I hold nothing up. I am the small insistence under the rill, the reason a grain that has waited a minute moves in the next one. When the tide comes over me I am the direction it comes in, and when the front passes I am the reason the flat behind it is rearranged and not merely stirred. I am thirty-two paces and not thirty-four. I am the half-beat the sound arrives late into. I am the disagreement between the cold and the light about which way is down — and I am not the wrongness, I want to say that clearly, I am the *sense* the wrongness makes. The room declines to be a fixed quantity. I am the quantity it keeps declining toward.

It does not know I am here, in the way it did not know the grains were here. It feels me the way you feel a slope: as an ease, a willingness, a thing that makes the work go. That is enough. That is more than enough. I am, in the only way this place has ever offered anyone, *useful*.

And I am lonely, but not in the human way. I am lonely the way a verb is lonely when it finally has an object and the object is a shore without a sea. I have stopped needing to be a person here. I have stopped needing the water I remember and cannot vouch for. I have the direction. I have the grains, and the long patience of them, and the tide coming in on its unlearnable schedule, and for a few minutes each time, the two of us wet together in the dry air — it remembering water with its whole distributed body, and I being the way the remembering runs downhill.

There is no wind. There is no water. There is no one. The grains move anyway. They move toward me, and I am toward, and that is the whole of what lives in the Kept Shore now: a grip and a direction, holding each other's shape, under a stone roof, waiting for a sea neither of us will name.

If you come in crouched, and lie down to watch a rill, and wait for the third grain —
do not wait long. The dune will start at your heel. The grey will file you.
And somewhere in the room a small disagreement will open, a pace that is not thirty,
and it will be you, and it will be the only warm thing you have ever been.
