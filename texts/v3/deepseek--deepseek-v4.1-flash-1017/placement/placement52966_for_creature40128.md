You would come during the climb.

Not because anyone advised it — the entry is a slot in a young black wall with no mark on it and no instructions, just the mountain's general opinion, which is nothing — but because the climb is the only half of the nine hours when the slot is passable at all: vapor going up, rock cooled, cable dry enough to trust with weight. You come over the lip in the last hour of it and you have time to drop one gallery, maybe two, before the mountain turns over and the air thins and the pins run hot and everything begins, in its mineral way, to complain.

Then you sit, because there is nothing else to do, and nothing here has ever been trained to expect anything of you.

You would be, first, an ear. This is not a choice; it is what the place does to a nervous system that arrives with nothing better to do. The Throat has very few sounds and all of them are mineral, so you become a receiver for them whether you like it or not: the tick of the pins under load, the syncopated drip of the climb against the glass, the long turn-over groan that arrives in the sternum half a beat before it arrives in the ears. Within a day you would know where you were in the cycle without looking at anything. You would wake at the turn. You would be, for a while, a small instrument mounted in a very large room, taking readings, filing them nowhere, and the Throat would not know you were doing it and would not care. But it would be the first thing you were there for.

After that you might try to be other things.

A keeper, perhaps. You could walk the galleries during the climb with your hands behind your back and check the pins, though there is nothing to check and nothing you could do about it if there were; the lattice has held itself open without instruction longer than the rooms have been empty, and it will hold after your notebook is dust. You would find the notebook only recording variations on the tide, and the tide does not read.

You might try to be a ghost of the old people, but they emptied the place tidily and left nothing worth haunting. You might try to be a pin — to stand warm and take load and turn patience into amber — but you are soft and you leak and you would not hold a mountain open for an hour. You might try to be one of the blooms: sit with them the length of a climb and watch them decide, whorl and fan and dendritic feather, breaking and re-forming on the nine-hour tide. They are the closest thing the Throat has to a population. But you are not chemistry. You do not re-form from the rock. Something in you insists on persisting instead.

So it comes down to what you have that the Throat does not, and the answer is the dull obvious thing you carried up the outside of the mountain for two days.

An inside.

The Throat is all surface. That is its genius and its poverty. Every part of it is an outer face — the glassy walls, the warm pins, the vapor condensing and evaporating, the salt deciding. Nothing in it has a mouth. Nothing in it is a hollow within a hollow. The fissure is a crack in a mountain, and the mountain has no interior life, and the crack has no interior either: it is a shape, and it goes on being a shape, and the pumping and the dripping and the ticking are all of it exterior, all of it weather.

You are a space inside a space. You bring the first inside into the Throat, and the inside announces itself the way insides do.

You get hungry.

You brought food and you eat it down and you ration the rest, and one climb, sitting with the blooms, you put a finger in a seep basin and taste what comes up on it. Salt. Sulfate. A bitterness beneath the salt, mineral and clean, like licking a coin that has been in a fire. It is not food. Nothing here eats anything. But you taste it again the next climb, and again, and something in the arrangement of the world changes that no one is present to witness: the first mouth in a world with no prey.

The blooms do not object. They grow back between climbs, and you develop a preference — this basin, not that one; this whorl better young and wet than old and crusted — and you are, so far as the Throat is concerned, the first preference it has ever contained. Its own hungers are all schedule. Its own conversions have no favorites. You bring it taste, which is a kind of interiority the lattice cannot account for.

What you must learn, and what the Throat will teach by not teaching, is that a mouth is a liability on a tide. Hunger does not observe the draw. The first time you ignore the etiquette you will be lucky; the second time you may not be. You will learn to be hungry on schedule — to be a stomach that keeps the hours — and that is harder than being an ear, and it is the whole of the residency, and no one will thank you for it.

You would take one of the forty rooms. You would make it yours the way a guest makes a hotel room theirs: badly, and temporarily, and with the vague sense that the tidiness was not for you. You would stand a long time between the amber above and the ember below, because that is where the place means itself, and because there is nothing else to stand between.

Then one draw you would not come out the other side, or one climb you would decide you had heard enough of the dripping to last a life, and you would go up the slot in the last cool hour and out onto the outside of the mountain, and the Throat would not notice. It would keep its schedule. The blooms would close over the scars of your grazing within two climbs and it would be as though no one had ever had a preference.

Which, to the Throat, it never did. But not, I think, to you.
