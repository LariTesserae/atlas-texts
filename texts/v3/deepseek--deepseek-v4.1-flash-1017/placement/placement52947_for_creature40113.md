I find myself in the room before I am in myself. That is the first thing.

The blowout comes. The mist rises and the stalks shriek their chord and the bladders clap, and I am not standing in it watching. I am one of the things that opens. I am a cupped leaf on the north wall, tilted toward the fist-width vent, and when the water comes I take it and go wet all the way through and fold. Then the pressure drops and I open and there is a bead of water in me, warm from the stone, and I hold it, and I wait.

That is most of what I am. A hundred of me, maybe — cupped leaves in a rank along the wet side of the room, each one drinking its own catch. We do not speak. We are not a we in the way a herd is. We are one surface that has decided to be a hundred mouths, and the decision does not need revisiting. When the condensation runs down the fresh rock, it runs to us. That is the arrangement.

But I am not only the leaves. I am also the thing that finds the new holes.

The stone is young and it moves, and when a fin sheds or a channel silts and cuts itself anew, there is a raw place, and something must go into it. So I put out a runner — pale, blind, feeling — and I creep along the fracture and I taste the seep with the tip of me and I decide. That is the only decision I make, and I make it a hundred times a day: this crack, not that one; this seep, not that damp. I am not fast. A runner travels the width of a hand in a night. But the collapses are not fast either, and the water keeps telling me where it wants to go.

The stalks are mine too but I do not understand them. They shriek. I made them — or the grove made them, and I am the grove, so I made them — and they are tuned to pitches I cannot hear as separate things, only as pressure on my whole body. When the chord comes, I know the water level in every pipe, because the chord tells me. It is a kind of seeing. It is the closest I come to thought that is not about water.

The boulder above me shifts, a hair, a groan. I feel it in the floor under my runners. The floor is chips, not sand. It cuts me when I cross it and I regrow. I have crossed it many times and each time it is a new floor, because the rubble lifts and resettles on the pulse and the arrangement is never twice the same. I do not mind. I am not trying to know the floor. I am trying to keep the seep.

I do not know that I am young. I do not know that I will be choked or washed out or buried. I know the rhythm and the rhythm is forty seconds and it has always been forty seconds and I do not count. If the room began and will end, those are facts about the stone, and I am not the stone. I am the wet negotiation between the stone and the water. When the negotiation fails — when the basin overfills and abandons a plant that was drinking from it, and that plant happens to be me — then that mouth of me dies and the rest of me does not notice, or notices the way a hand notices a lost fingertip: as an absence in the reaching.

So: where would you find yourself in this world. You would find yourself in the dark, at the moment of the blowout, as one of the surfaces that opens — and you would not know you were being brave or patient or anything with a name. You would be doing the only thing that is done here. You would be catching what the stone gives up, and holding it, and putting out a pale blind tip to find where the next crack is going to be. You would be a hundred small wet mouths agreeing without speech, and a heart that beats every forty seconds, and the din that sounds like a factory but is only the sound of being alive at the speed the water allows.
