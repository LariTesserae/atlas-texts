# The Turn

I shouted once, on the stair, out of animal habit, and the Sump took it and did not give it back. That was the first thing the place taught me, before the fortieth bowl, before I had set foot on the floor: that I had a voice, that it was a thing I could lose, and that I would not miss it as much as I expected.

I came down for a rotation. Six months, one pump station, a logbook with a broken spine. The man who sent me said the work was simple and the place was dull, and both were true, and neither was what he meant.

By the third day I had stopped trying to talk. Not out of discipline — out of arithmetic. You spend a sentence into that roar and get nothing back, and after enough sentences you stop spending. The mouth closes. The hands open. I learned the pressure-language the way you learn a grammar you will never write: badly, then without noticing. Flat palm on the chest. Two taps. A squeeze. The first time a hand closed on my arm out of the grey I made a small sound, a yelp, and the woman who had found me waited, patient, until I understood that she did not need me to explain myself. She squeezed once. *I am here.* Then she let go and walked, and I followed, because that is what the taps mean.

Her name was Tam. She had been down eleven years. She had the look — wide eyes, easy hands, the way of standing still in the roar as if it were a room. She was not mystical about the place, and she would not let me be. The first time I said the Sump was beautiful, she looked at me a long moment. We were in a dry room, where speech is possible, where the roar comes through the wall as a pressure and not a sound. She said, "It's a process with weather. Don't give it a heart. It doesn't have one and it has never wanted one."

I have thought about that more than I have thought about most things.

---

What did I become? I want to say something large. I did not become anything large. I became a line-walker.

I keep the guide-lines true. I go out with a knotted cord, a stub of chalk, a brass tag or two, and I follow a rail or a ridge or a pipe from one known end to the other, and I feel for the place where it has drifted. It always drifts. The floor is not still — it was poured and sintered and then the water got into it, and the water is still doing what water does, which is to take the corners off. A line that was true in the spring will lean by the autumn. I feel it with my palms and my boot-soles and the inside of my wrist, the low places where a pipe has sagged into a hollow, the high places where the precipitate has built a lip under it. I mark it. I report it. Someone with better tools comes and lifts it. That is the work. It is not a calling. It is a job that suits a certain kind of attention, and I turned out to have that kind.

You navigate by wet lines. Not by seeing the stacks — the fog will not let you — but by the pale trails on them where water has been going down the same face for longer than anyone has kept records. A north face will have a bright thread on it you can find with a thumb in the dark. A pool will have terraces on its rim, and you can read the age of a hollow by counting them, and I have counted some that had hundreds.

The turn is the clock. Twice a day the sheet-flow deepens and the roar climbs by a note, and if you are standing in a low place the water comes to your knees and then to your thighs and then it stops, and goes back, and leaves a fresh bright line on everything it touched. I keep a watch in my pocket and I distrust it. The turn does not care what the watch says. The turn is what I set the watch by, and then I forget to look, and it does not matter.

---

I am good at sounds. That is the thing I did not expect and did not choose. Some people here have hands for the work and some have eyes for it. I have ears. Not for music — there is no music — but for the diagnostic note of a thing that is about to fail. A valve that sings flat is going. I heard one sing flat for three turns before it went, and I marked it, and the fixer came, and the fixer said it would have held another month, and I said it would not, and we parted without resolving it, which is how most arguments end here. A drip that stops is worse than one that starts. The new ones need to be told that. And the shaft — the great thing in the middle distance that breathes — is not an omen. It is just the shaft. It exhales and inhales and the low note gets into your chest, and after a year you cannot sleep without it, and that is not a mystery either. It is the same as the man up top who cannot sleep without the traffic. The body learns what is regular and then asks for it.

I will tell you the thing I do not say in the dry rooms.

The mats are the only soft thing here. I scrape them off the sensors when they bridge one, and I do it carefully, and I have never told Tam or anyone that I am careful. They are dark and damp and they smell of iron, and nothing eats them, and they are not alive in any way that would flatter them. But they are soft, and they spread a finger's width a decade, and they follow the water's memory instead of any plan, and when I am scraping one from a sensor I am slow about it. That is the closest I come to the thing the man up top would call beauty. It is not beauty. It is a hand being gentle in the dark because there is nothing else to be gentle with.

---

The dead go badly, because there is nowhere to dig. I was on the line the day we put Bell in the deep basin. We weighted him and we lowered him and the water took him without a sound you could pick out of the roar. His name went into the ledger in the dry room, and his cord went to me, because I was the one who could use it. I use it still. It is knotted in the old pattern and the knots have gone soft with handling, and I do not think of him every time, which I think he would have approved of. He was not a sentimental man. None of the ones who stay are.

Tam went up at year twelve and did not come back down. I got a note through a line-walker the next rotation — four words in grease pencil on a strip of tag: *Dry feet. Good light.* That is a whole letter here. I keep it in the pocket with the watch.

---

I go up twice a year. For the paperwork, for the eyes. The light hurts. Not the brightness — the *width* of it. The sky is too big and it does not stop. The sound is worse. I mean the ordinary sound, the voices, the engines, the thing the people up there call quiet, which is not quiet at all but a hundred small collisions that mean nothing. Down here the roar means something. It means the pumps are cycling and the tide is turning and the water is going where it has always gone. Up there the noise means nothing and nobody is listening to it, and that is the thing I cannot bear. I am back down the stair before the second week is out, every time, and my hands are on the guide-line before I have stopped being afraid.

---

So. Where do you find me? I am on the line, a kilometre out from the nearest stack, one hand on a ridge in the floor and the other holding a cord that was given to me by a man who is in a basin. The water is at my ankles and it is about to be at my knees. My hands are raw at the knuckles and I have stopped noticing. The shaft is breathing. The mats are spreading a finger's width a decade.

I am not the Sump's child. I have not given it a heart. I am its staff, and I am the only thing out here that wants anything, and what I want is small and has not changed in years: dry feet, a turned tide, and a hand closing on my arm out of the grey.
