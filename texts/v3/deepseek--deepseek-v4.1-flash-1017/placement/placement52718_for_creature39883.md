I come in on the dimming.

That's the first thing I know — the Lid's light going down, the long breath out, the seams overhead losing their cold white and going to something grayer, deeper, the color of milk in water. And I know that I've been waiting for it, because everyone in the hall has slowed and turned their faces up.

Then the cold arrives properly, and with it the sound. The fountains are the sound. I'd forgotten how much of the Vault is *hiss* — ten thousand threads of condensation coming down through the silver nothing and never landing, and the noise of that is not a noise, it's a pressure, it's the room itself pressing along the jaw and the sternum and through the long bones of the forearm. I feel it in my teeth. I feel it in the callused meat of my palm, which is where I first really understand what I am. This hand is old. This hand has been used.

I am on a harvesting shelf. I can see the mats — the pale fronds and the lumps and the tough mats of bloom, opened now that the light is up, and the line of divers going out across them, oldest leading, youngest trailing, and their hands moving in the slow rhythm, in and out, in and out, not too deep, not too many, never all the way to the heart of the mat. I do not go out with them. I stand at the edge of the shelf with the rope coiled over my shoulder, and I understand without being told that this is the shape my life takes: I am the one who stays.

Not because I am afraid. I want to be clear about that, because the Vault is not a place where staying gets dressed up as courage. I stay because somebody must hold the rope, and the rope is a kind of writing. Each knot is a dive, a season, a death. When I pay it out I am writing out the length of somebody's courage in a language that does not need a throat and cannot be drowned out by a fountain. And when it goes slack in the wrong way — that small, awful slackness, not the weight-and-release of a diver working, but the going-limp of a thing that has stopped holding its own weight — I am the one who feels it first. Before the engines know. Before the elders know. My hand knows.

Down the shelf-line, under the One That Stands Against the Current, someone has slowed under a fountain. I watch them do it, the counting, the not-stopping. My own shoulders remember the weight of that water arriving, the little blunt insistence of it, and something in me leans toward it and does not go. I am not one of the ones the fountain has rearranged. I have been slowed and I have come out of it and gone on as though nothing had happened, many times, and it is possible — I begin to see it now, the way you see a wall when the haze thins — it is possible that I have gone on that way so reliably, so many times, that the going-on has become the thing I am instead of the thing I do.

The dimming finishes. The light comes back up, faint, then brighter, the whole vast hollow of the world filling with moving silver. The divers come in off the mats with their nets heavy. I take up the slack. I knot the rope.

And then — this is the part I did not expect — I find myself going down.

Not with the harvest. Below it. Past the eels, and the water is going from cool to cold to that other thing the Vault does, the cold that is not temperature but *insistence*, the water pressing its whole history into the soft places behind the ears. The fountain-roar is dying away behind me. The hum of the Lid is thinning to a rumor and then to a memory of a rumor, and the engines are only a heartbeat felt in the floor of the body, the sound a house makes in the ears of someone who has left it. I have a light. I have a rope.

And I have a companion, because of course I do — because I am the one who holds the rope, and I am holding it now from the other end, and the end is in my own hand, the way it always was, the way it has to be if I am ever to follow someone all the way out to where the following has to stop.

The Base-divers come down in ones and in twos. They are the quiet ones, the ones the elders can see the slowing in, and they are not the reckless ones because the reckless ones die early. What I am — what I finally understand myself to be, in the cold, in the dark, in the long dark with my own line running back up toward the light and nobody at all holding the other end of it — is the *second*. The one who goes down behind. The one whose job is not to see the edge but to see what the edge does to the person who sees it, and to bring that back intact, and to be changed by the bringing in a way that has no name.

Three Base-divers, three accounts. Nothing. Columns continuing down, and a light that was not the Lid's. A door, or the place where a door would be. I have been the rope for all three. I have heard each of them say it at the fire with the flat patient certainty of people recounting a thing they cannot reconcile, and I have known — because my hand was on the line, because I felt the tug and the stillness and the tug again — that all three are true, and that the unresolvedness is not a failure of the accounts but the actual shape of the thing described. Some things come back from the Base the way the water comes back from the Lid: not changed into something else, just *continuing*, and the continuing is the whole of it.

So this is where I am. On the shelf, at the dimming, with the rope over my shoulder and the knots of other people's courage in my palm. In the dark, behind someone, paying out line and counting. In the warm of an inner chamber afterward, one room away from the engine, listening to the pitch of it, knowing when it is a bad hour and when it is the beginning of a bad year. Not the one who goes and not the one who refuses to go. The one who lets go, in the end, because holding on is not the same as keeping, and the Vault does not teach you to keep anything — it teaches you the pressure of a hand, the return of a pressure, the tightening that does not let go, and then it teaches you to open your fingers anyway and let the water take what it will take.

The children ask me about it every year. The nine-year-olds, the ten-year-olds, the ones just beginning to settle, deciding they will go down one day. I tell them the truth, which is that most outgrow it, and I let them see my hands while I say it, so that they can read the rest of the truth there, folded, the way an adult carries a folded thing in the back of themselves and takes it out now and then to look at it, and does not speak of it.
