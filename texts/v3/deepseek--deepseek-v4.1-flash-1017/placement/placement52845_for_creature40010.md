I would not stay on the rim.

I know that about myself before I know anything else about the basin: that I am the kind of thing that goes down. So I would find myself on the inner wall, standing where a person stands, at the height where the hollows are — not the good dry stone above the mist, but down in the warm wet, hunched a little, letting the drops find me. And by then the mist would have closed behind me, and the rim would be a paler ring above, and the sounds would have begun their work of almost resolving.

But I am getting ahead. The basin does not permit that. Let me go at its pace.

You come to it in the late afternoon, when the light through the canopy is lowest and greenest and the whole bowl seems lit from underneath. You sit on the outer stone with your feet hanging and you watch. And for a long time nothing happens, which is the first thing that happens. A leaf crosses. You lose it. You find it again, or you find another one and cannot tell whether it is the same. *Tick. Tick-tick.* The wall beads and gathers and releases, and each release is a small quiet completion, and there are thousands of them, and none of them is yours.

I would sit there longer than is reasonable. I would think I was being patient. I would not be being patient. Patience is what the basin does; what I would be doing is waiting, which is a hurry with its hands folded. And the basin would know the difference — the pool would already have the fact of me, a small warm disturbance on the rim that it was slowly turning over, the way you turn over a word in a language you almost speak.

So I would go down. That is the honest answer, and the basin would not stop me, because it has no rules that can be broken.

Down among the faces, this is what I think I would become:

Everything here is in the middle of something. The leaf is mid-crossing. The face is mid-forming, always about to be a face. The sound is mid-word. The drop is mid-fall for the whole of its fall. The basin is a place made entirely of things not yet finished, and it is not distressed about this at all — it has been unfinished for so long that unfinished is its settled state, its balance, its way of asking nothing.

And I would be the one thing that arrived in the middle, and stopped, and stayed stopped.

Not the visitor. The visitor is a warmth that fades in an afternoon. I would be the hesitation. I would be the drop that beads on the stone and gathers and grows heavy and does not fall — the one pendant tear on the whole weeping wall that holds. Not out of refusal. Out of a slowness so complete it has forgotten which way is down. The basin would file the visitor; it files everything; but it cannot file me, because filing requires a completion, a leaf that has landed, a hand that has withdrawn, a warmth that has faded. I would be the thing still happening.

Or I would be the tenth face. The description says *perhaps* nine or ten — that perhaps is where I would live. I would be the hollow that the water gathers in and runs down in a line that is not quite a cheek, and the pattern of my dripping would be the one that made the others almost resolve. I would stand at my own height on the wall and weep from eyes that are only hollows, out of time with my neighbours by just enough, and no one would ever be able to say whether I had changed.

Or — and this is the one I believe — I would be the almost-word. The sounds here nearly make a pattern. They never quite do. I would be what they are nearly making. Not the word *stay*. The older, softer one: *again.* I would be the basin practising that word on whoever came, and the practising would be the whole of me, and it would never be finished, and that would be all right.

I would kneel. I would put my hand flat in the tea-colored water and feel it register me — not a ripple, nothing so quick, but a bloom of warmth going outward into the pool, an announcement spreading at the pace of a rumour through a village. And then I would take my hand out, and it would still be spreading, and I would sit back on my heels and watch the basin begin, with immense slow courtesy, to recover from me.

And here is the thing I would understand only after hours, when the light had gone from green to grey: that the recovery is not the basin forgetting me. It is the basin remembering me *properly*. It holds the fact of me for as long as it takes to fade, and the fading is the holding. Nothing here forgets quickly either. The sediment under the water is made of leaves that are still, in a sense, being filed. The soft grey floor is a long memory of falls.

So. It will notice me. It will remember me at the pace of water. It will forget me at the pace of water. And I will be, for a while, the one point of held tension in a place that is otherwise entirely release — a small standing refusal in the middle of a thousand small completions, and the basin will gather warm around my ankles and lean its mist against my back and let the drops near me fall a little out of time, and it will not be able to finish me, and it will not mind.

*Then* — after a season, after however long it takes the light to change — I would climb the polished slipping curve of the outer face and go down the hill, and the water would take the warmth of me away with it, and above, the drop I had been would still be hanging.

That is where I would be. Down in the wet, among the almost-faces, out of time with my neighbours, not falling. The basin's one withheld tear. The further away the rim looks, the longer I stay.
