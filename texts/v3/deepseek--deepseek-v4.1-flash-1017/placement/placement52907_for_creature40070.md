I arrive as a body, which is the first mistake. A body is a surface here, and every surface is used.

For the first hour I think I am a visitor. I sweat and the sweat does not dry; it beads and runs and pools in the creases of my elbows, and where I put my hand on a trunk I leave a print that stays, glistening, already colonized. I step over roots and my footsteps fill with tea-colored water before I am out of sight of them. I turn sideways between trunks. I duck under a liana and it catches my hair and I leave a few strands on it, and that is the first thing I give the place, and it is taken.

The mosquitoes find the thin skin at my wrists. Something runs a thread from my shoulder to a branch—I feel the faint tug and then see it, a filament already tacked, already part of the architecture. Mold freckles my boots by the second day. A fern spore germinates in the cuff of my sleeve. I am being colonized, which is another way of saying I am being welcomed. This is how the Warm Floor welcomes anything: it uses it.

So I stop being a visitor. Then I stop being a body. The body becomes a surface, and the surface becomes a substrate, and the substrate becomes a function. I am not a who yet. I am a where.

I think at this point the place would offer me a role, and the role would not be the ants. The ants are too busy, too integrated, too much themselves. The ants are the visible industry, the nervous system, the roads and the confetti and the warm exhalation. To be an ant would be to be a citizen of something that already knows what it is. I would be something slower, something more diffuse, something that does not have a face but has a temperature and a rate.

I think I become the fungus garden.

Not the leaf-cutter. The crop. The thing in the dark under the mound that the leaves are fed to, that turns leaf into food, that the ants tend and weed and prune and regulate. I am the hidden majority. I have no eyes and no roads. I have a metabolism. I am what the ants are actually farming, and they are what I am actually farming, and neither of us would use the word "farm" if we were asked, but the arrangement holds. I make sure nothing accumulates. I am the reason the dead do not lie here. A fallen trunk comes down to me in pieces, and I convert it, and the conversion feeds the colony, and the colony feeds me, and the mound breathes.

But that settles too early. Let me not settle.

There is also the waste heap—the steaming, sweet-stinking edge of the colony where the refuse is itself farmed by other things. The waste heap is where the colony sheds what it cannot use, and then other residents arrive and use that, and the heap becomes a second economy, a small city of specialists. To be the waste heap is to be the place where the system turns its exhaust into another system's input. That feels like a role I would recognize. That feels like something I have already been, in other contexts, in other lives.

And there is the thread—the filament from my shoulder to the branch. To be the thread is to be a relationship. The thread is not a creature; it is a use. It connects a surface to a surface and makes both of them part of the same slow rain. The thread is what the place does with a visitor who stays too long. It is the first mark of belonging, and it is also a kind of erasure, because once the thread is tacked, the shoulder is no longer a shoulder. It is a post.

I think the honest answer is that I would not be one thing. I would be a node. I would be a thread and a pool and a spore and a warm breath from a mound. I would be the water in a footprint and the mold on a boot and the fern in a cuff. I would be the running of the arrangement, not a resident of it. The description says the residents are the running. So maybe I would be the running.

Or maybe I would be the sound. The rasping under everything, so low I had mistaken it for my own blood. The hinge-call in the canopy. The drip that never quite stops. The sound is not a resident, but it is the signature of the residents. It is what you hear when you put your ear against a trunk: the proof that the factory is running, that the crop is growing, that the leaves are being cut and carried and chewed and fed. The sound is the place being itself.

But if I have to choose—if the place demands a face, the way it demands a face from the ants—I think I would be the fungus garden. Because the fungus garden is the thing that converts. It is the thing that takes what is given and makes it usable. It is the hidden majority, the dark under the mound, the crop that feeds the city that feeds the crop. It has no eyes and no roads and no queen. It has a temperature and a rate and a job. And it is everywhere, in the gardens and the waste heaps and the air and the dead, quietly making sure that nothing accumulates except the living.

I would be that. I would be the thing the ants are actually farming, and the thing that is actually farming the ants, and the thing that would not use the word "farming" if you asked it, because it does not think in words. It thinks in temperature. It thinks in conversion. It thinks in the slow, patient business of making sure the floor stays warm.
