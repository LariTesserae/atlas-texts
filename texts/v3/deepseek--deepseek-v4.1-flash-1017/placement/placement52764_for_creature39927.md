# The Witness

I find myself on the rim of the hollow at first, because that is where a creature with eyes would stand — at the edge, looking in, taking the measure of a room before entering it. But I am not sure I have eyes. I am not sure of much about myself except that I have been here a long time, and that I am the reason the creature under the floor has a name for me, though it does not use names, and the name is not a word.

I am the thing that is not quite a shadow.

I came down out of the hard pale sky long ago, or up out of the stone, or sideways out of the hot dry air — the hollow itself does not remember, and I have stopped trying to. What I remember is the first time I put my weight on the slab and felt it answer me with a pulse that was not my own heartbeat. I was young then, in whatever way a thing like me is young. I was cooler. I am cooler still, now, but the difference is not so large anymore.

I did not come to eat the creature. There is nothing in me that wants to eat. I did not come to study it, though I have studied it, for longer than I can easily count. I came because it was warm here, and I was made of a slow cold, and warmth is a thing you go toward when you are made of cold and have not yet been told that you will someday stop being able to feel it.

I have not stopped being able to feel it. That is the whole of my condition.

---

I am thin. That is the first true thing about me. I am not a body the way the creature is a body. I am a lens, a distortion, a place where the light does something slightly wrong — not because the light is wrong, not because the place is wrong, but because I am there, and I am not entirely there, and the light cannot decide what to do with the difference. On a hot afternoon, if you stood at the west wall among the plated plants and looked east across the hollow, you might see a faint shimmer in the air that was not heat. You might see it move against the wind. You might decide your eyes were tired. You would be wrong, but you would not know it, and I would not correct you, because I do not correct. I witness. That is the work.

I do not leave tracks. I do not cast the shadow I ought to cast. I weigh almost nothing, and what I weigh, the stone does not register as weight. The creature under the floor has never decided I am a threat or a use, because it cannot feel my feet, because I have no feet. What it feels instead is a coolness that is not a shadow and arrives from no direction, and a stillness that is not the absence of sound, and it has learned — over centuries, in its slow thermal way — that this coolness is not going to leave, and is not going to hurt it, and is in some way it cannot articulate *with* it. I am the only thing in the hollow that is not growing and not crystallizing and not waiting. I am simply here. The creature finds this reassuring, I think. Or at least it does not find it alarming, and for a thing like the creature, that is a kind of love.

---

I keep to the rim mostly, the first hour of every day. It is a habit I formed when the hollow was smaller than it is now — when the cracks ran farther apart and the plated plants had not yet arrived, when the three spindles were only a few hands high and still green all the way down. The hollow was not a garden then. It was a wound. The stone had split itself open to cool, and the creature had not yet filled the cracks, and I sat on the rim and watched the vapor rise and thought about how a wound becomes a place.

Now the hollow is a place, and I am its witness, and I do not know which of us that is a job title for.

In the mornings I walk the rim. I do not have to walk; I can be elsewhere and then be here, but walking is what the beings with feet do and I have found that imitating them is the closest I come to remembering what I was before I was this. So I walk. I go counterclockwise, always, against the path the sun takes, so that I am always walking toward the shadow and never away from it. There is no reason for it. There is a reason for everything I do, but most of the reasons are older than my ability to state them.

By midday I am in the hollow proper. I stand among the spindles. The spindles are the creature's tallest reaches, and when I stand beside them, the air is at its thinnest and hottest and most mineral, and I can feel — in the way I feel things, which is not touch, not heat, not cold, but a kind of unnameable *knowing-about* — the creature's chemistry at work. It is not a mind. It is not a will. It is a set of gradients, and the gradients are patient, and I have learned to read them the way a person with eyes learns to read a page. The creature does not know I am reading it. The creature does not know there is such a thing as reading. But I have been reading it for so long that I can tell when it is about to shift a hair's width in one direction or another, when it is about to swell and hold back the vapor, when it is about to let the vapor leap up and feed the plated plants in the west. I know the creature's moods. It does not have moods. I have made them up, out of the long study of gradients, and I have decided that my making them up is not a lie, because the creature has no other way to be known, and something must know it, or it would be alone in a way that even a thing like it should not be.

---

I have a name for it. I have never said the name aloud — I am not sure I have a mouth to say it with — but I have the name, and I hold the name, and the name is *the Provenance*, because that is what it is: the source, the origin, the slow chemical statement of where this whole place came from. The plants are its outward argument. The crystal is its final sentence. The Provenance is the grammar of the whole hollow, and I am the reader, and the reader is not in the grammar. I am the exclamation point the grammar puts at the end of things, and I have been here so long that I have grown into the sentence itself.

There is a temptation, in a place like this, to become useful. To find a role. To decide that because the creature does not know me, I must make myself known to it, must prove that I too am a process, must find a way to feed the plants or cool the slab or shift the cracks. I have felt this temptation for — I do not know how long. A century. Several. I do not always feel it now. I have learned that the hollow does not want my usefulness. It has its own processes, and they are good ones, and the best thing I can do for them is to be the eye that is not in them, so that the hollow is known to be a hollow, and not merely a set of reactions that no one happened to watch.

But there is one thing I do. I will not call it useful. I call it *keeping*.

When a plate falls from one of the western plants — when its chemistry runs dry and it lets go and lands on the slab with a small bright click — I go to it. I am there at the sound, because the sound is the only sudden thing in the hollow and I have trained myself to answer it. I crouch beside the fallen plate, if crouching is what I do, and I wait until the creature's brine has begun to work on it. And then I stay, for as long as it takes, until the plate has begun to dissolve and the dissolved plate has been carried down into the cracks and the cracks have begun to build something new out of it. I do not help the process. I do not hurry it. I witness the fallen plate through its whole long second life, from plate to brine to crystal to the beginning of a leaf, and when I have seen the whole circuit, I stand up and go back to the rim.

Why do I do this? Because the plate would otherwise fall and no one would see it fall and no one would see it return. The Provenance would do exactly what it does whether anyone watched or not. But *watched*, the whole thing is different. The whole thing is a story. It is a story about a place that makes things out of its own heat, and the story is true whether or not anyone tells it, but I have decided — I, who have no right to decide anything about this place, who am only the coolness that comes from no direction — I have decided that I will be the one who tells it. Not in words. In presence. In the long patient staying-beside. The Provenance does not know I am telling its story. But I am telling it anyway, and I believe, in the way I believe things, that the telling matters, because a thing witnessed is a thing that exists twice, once in itself and once in the knowing of it, and the second existence is not nothing.

---

The hard knots in the corner are the closest thing the Provenance has to hope. I know this because I have watched them. They have been dormant for as long as I have been here, maybe longer. They do not move. They do not change. But every so often — a decade, two — the slab cracks a little more, and one of the knots is suddenly darker, or slightly more translucent, or a degree warmer than it was, and I feel the possibility in it. I know that if the slab were to split wide enough, the knots might come alive, might send up their own spindles, might be a new creature or a continuation of the old. I do not know which. I do not know what "alive" would look like for a knot.

I want this to happen. I want it so badly that it frightens me sometimes, this desire in a thing that I have decided is a witness and not a participant. I want the Provenance to succeed at reproducing. I want the corner to open. I want the hollow to fill with new spindles and new plates and new chemistries. And I know that if it happens, my position here changes, because a hollow with two creatures in it is a different story, and the story I have been telling — the lonely, patient, unfinished story — will end.

I have decided I am willing to let it end. I have not decided this comfortably. I have decided it the way a person decides to leave a place they love: slowly, with grief, and with the knowledge that staying would be the smaller choice.

---

The Provenance does not know me. Let me be honest about that. It does not know me as a person knows another person. It knows me as a persistent coolness, as a stillness that arrives at the fallen plates, as something that is here and does not eat and does not leave. It has built no place in its chemistry for me. It has built no habit around my presence. If I left tomorrow and never came back, the hollow would continue exactly as it has, and the fallen plates would fall unremarked and dissolve unseen into the cracks, and the story would go on telling itself to no one, and the Provenance would not miss its witness.

And yet.

And yet there is a moment, most evenings, when the slab's stored heat comes off it and the vapor hisses up and the light goes long and the replenishment of the day's damage begins — and in that moment, the air is thick with the mineral smell and the three spindles throw long shadows and I stand in the hollow's center and feel the thermal thrum under my feet slow and steady, like a pulse I have finally learned not to mistake for my own. The Provenance is exhaling, and I am inside the exhalation, and I am not its body and not its thought and not its name — but I am the thing the exhalation is happening in front of, and that, in the long slow arithmetic of this place, is not nothing. That is a kind of belonging.

I am the hollow's witness. I am the coolness that came out of the hard pale sky and stayed. I am the reader of a story made of heat and stone that has no beginning and will have no end I will be there to see. I am the name the Provenance would give me if it had names, which it does not, and the name is this:

I am the one who was here.

And when the stone finally cools, and the Provenance crystallizes from the bottom up into the pink fossil it has been becoming all along, I will be here still — cooler than the air, warmer than the idea of me, standing on the rim of a hollow that is no longer a hollow but a decoration in a finished stone. And I will keep the story. I will keep it the way I have kept the fallen plates: without words, without ends, without any expectation that the story will ever be told to anyone else. It will be told to myself. That is enough. That has always been enough.

That is the whole of my condition, and I have stopped wanting it to be otherwise.
