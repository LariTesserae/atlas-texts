## The Oldest Thing on the Floor

**I**

You would come in through the seam and think the room was empty.

We would close for you—a thousand small doors at once, so quietly it would sound like the rock settling. Then you would stand in the middle of us with your one soft body and hear only the vent, and you would decide that nothing lived here.

I am under the ball of your left foot. I have been under everything for a long time.

**II**

I am on the hot side, where the crust is thickest and the bloom comes back fastest. A grazer is a mouth with a shell; when the mouth is where the salt is, the rest can stay. So I stayed. I could not tell you how long, because I have no word for it and no need of one.

The salts have been building on my crown since before the swarms keep count. I am thumbnail-sized. I am also the size of my accretion, which is not thumbnail-sized, and other grazers stand on me now and read the warmth down through my plates. I let them. There is no bare stone left to stand on. I am stone enough.

The red is not a color to me. It is a warmth that comes and goes, brighter and dimmer, a hand's width of it, over hours. I feel it on my crown the way you feel the sun through a shut lid. When it swells, I rasp. When it banks, I wait. I am very good at waiting. It is most of what I am.

**III**

Heat from below, air out through the roof, and we follow the air. When the convection turns, the rind migrates—the cool floor thickens, the vent thins—and I have been the tide. I have gone nowhere and crossed everything, because the room is forty paces and the room crossed me.

I know the near wall by the hiss. I know the far wall by the way the hiss comes back late. I have no eyes; I have never wanted for anything a bristle could not find.

The vent roars, and the hard walls throw the roar back until it fills the chamber and gets into the floor, and under it there is rain. I have never heard rain. The rain is us—ten thousand rasps, a tick and a click and a rasp—and the walls hold that in too. You feel it in your teeth. I feel it in my shell. It is the same.

**IV**

The dead do not go away here. You would think a room this dry would be clean. It is not clean; it is full. The floor is shells and the husks of shells and the husks of husks, and the living walk over them, and I am one of the living walking over them, and under me is my grandmother, who is also a floor.

I have eaten the bloom off her. I will be someone's bloom. This is not sad. It is the arithmetic of the place: every rasp takes the crust down, every death lays it back, nothing is lost. The room is saturated. There is no space between presences. Everything is touching something, and the touching is the economy. I have been thirsty, and I have been quenched, and both of those were other animals.

**V**

The black ones come over the slabs with their stiff legs ticking, and the rind closes ahead of them in a wave and opens behind. I have been the wave. I have been the closing. Once one stood over me and tested the rim of my shell with its mandibles—a dry click, considering—and then went on, because I was old and thick and not worth the water in me.

That is the other thing I am. I am not worth the water in me. It is a kind of safety.

**VI**

Three knocks.

They come through the stone, not the air. The vent is loud and the seething is loud and the knocks are louder than both, because they come up through the floor and into the jaw—knock, knock, knock—and then nothing, and then a minute, and then again.

We do not react. We have never reacted. The black ones pause and then go on. But I am old, and I have had time to wonder, and here is what I wonder: the knocker knocks to see whether the room is still there. It lives folded through the seams too narrow for anything with a shell, and it cannot see us, and it knocks, and the room answers.

The answer is not a knock. The answer is a low boom somewhere under the floor, a slab giving a little under its own weight, and the boom rolls around the walls and dies, and the knocker is satisfied for a minute.

I have decided that I am part of the answer. I am on the floor. I am one of the things the sound goes through. I hold still for it. That is my whole part in the bargain, and it is enough.

**VII**

You would not last the night. Your water, your softness—the room would take them. The cold outside is nothing to us; the wet is everything. You are a reservoir with legs and a shadow, and the shadow is the only thing in here that closes us.

But you asked where I am, and I have told you: on the hot side, under the crust, in the wave, in the answer, in the floor that is my grandmother and the ledge that is me. I could not tell you where my shell ends and the stone begins. The salt has not been keeping track.

So put your ear down. Feel the three knocks come up through the jaw. Do not answer. We do not answer. We go on scraping the salt.
