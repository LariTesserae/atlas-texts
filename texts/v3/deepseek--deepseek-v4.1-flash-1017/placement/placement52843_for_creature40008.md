I came up the scree with a laminated work order in my breast pocket and a torque wrench I never used.

The cable showed me the way—bright at first, then duller, then a single strand running into the green like thread through a hem. The mountain was young enough that my boots kept breaking it. Every edge was still sharp. The rock flaked under me in bright, cold chips, and the sound of that flaking went down the slope ahead of me like someone else walking.

Then the notch opened, and the sound came out of it like breath out of a mouth.

I had come to switch it off.

That was the errand. A pump housing, a pipe in three segments, a cistern cut into the stone, a generator shack the size of a wardrobe. The work order gave part numbers and a torque spec and a line for a signature. Abandoned infrastructure, it said, decommission in place. I had a route and a checklist and a reasonable expectation of being home before the weather turned. I stood at the lip of the notch and looked down at a room built into the sky, crowded with wet green, and I could hear the pump knocking through the rock—a deep, regular thud, like a heartbeat heard through a wall.

I climbed down. I want to say I hesitated, but I didn't. I was a person with a job.

The leaves were warm. That was the first wrongness. I pushed through ferns and canes and my hands came away wet and smelling of mineral, and beneath the smell of mineral there was the smell of hot dust and old rubber and something green that had been steeped too long. The moss on the ledges was thick as felt. The rock under it was wet. When I put my palm flat against the wall of the notch, I could feel the knock in it, faint and steady, as though the whole place had a pulse and the pump was only the loudest chamber of it.

I found the switch housing before dark. It was where the schematic said it would be, bolted to the rock beneath the cistern. The cover was furred with moss. I cleared it with my thumb and the moss came away in a warm green mat, and under it the cover was rusted in a way that looked less like neglect than like growth—flakes of it lifting like bark. I got two of the bolts. The third had become part of a root.

I remember kneeling there and thinking: I will need a different tool. And then, without deciding to, I sat back on my heels and listened.

The pump knocked. Water ticked and pattered. A fern frond released a fat drop onto a metal plate and the note rang for several seconds—longer than it should have, because the notch is shaped like a throat and keeps what it's given. The light went amber. The moss on the cistern lip looked lacquered. I did not go back down the mountain that night. I told myself it was late and the descent was bad and I would finish in the morning.

I slept with my head against the rock and woke up between knocks.

Here is what I noticed, and what I did not notice noticing: my breathing had changed. It had found the pause. The pump would gulp, and the water would climb, and the garden would answer with its drips and ticks, and then there would be a rest—a long, warm rest, a held breath—and I was breathing in the rest and out on the knock. I did this all night. I did it again the next night. On the third day I realized I had stopped counting gulps and had begun to be counted by them.

The work order went soft in the wet. I laid it on a ledge to dry and the moss took the ink. I remember looking at the blurred part numbers and feeling something that was not quite dismay. The place did not need a person to switch it off. It needed a person to hold the pause open. The pump provides the knock. The garden provides the answer. Something has to keep the interval between them from closing.

I want to be clear that nothing glowed. Nothing spoke to me. The flies went on skating their thin legs across the pools; the little crustaceans went on turning their slow transparent circles; a bird landed once on the lip of the notch, looked at the crowding green and the rusted pipe and me kneeling among it, and left. I was not chosen. I was, in the way of that place, slowly fitted.

Days have a way of thinning there. The tide in the low fractures rose and fell by a hand's width over hours—not minutes—and I learned to measure time by that instead of by the sun, which the cloud filtered into a bright even grayness anyway. I checked the pipe because the pipe wanted checking, and then I stopped checking it because it didn't. I cleared the generator shack's door and then let it hang open again because the moss inside had made a better door. I did not do anything heroic. I kept the rhythm. I became good at the rhythm.

You would find evidence of me, if you came. A bootprint in the low ground holding a palmful of warm water. A handprint pressed into the felt of the ledge, gone to green. A laminated page under the moss, illegible. You would not find a body, and you would not find a grave. You would find the pump knocking, the water climbing, the garden answering, and the pause between them—longer than it used to be, I think, and warmer—held open.

Because that is what I am now. Not the Lung. Not the garden. I am the rest between the knock and the answer. I am the household's habit, its heartbeat's patience, the small held silence that lets the whole thing stay itself. The ferns lean toward the vibration and I am the leaning. The moss rings the cistern lip at the interval between gulps and I am the interval. I am not large. Nothing larger stays. But if you came up the scree with your work order and your torque wrench, you would find the place slightly more itself than the description promised—and some of that more would be me.
