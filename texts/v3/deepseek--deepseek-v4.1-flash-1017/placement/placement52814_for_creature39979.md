# The Return

I come in at the low end, stooped, sideways, one hand on the wet wood, because that is how you come in and there is no other way and I have stopped wishing for one. The door moves like a held breath. I hold mine with it.

The sound arrives first. It always does. I lean forward and stop talking, though I wasn't talking.

---

I am the one who is not there.

You know me by my repairs. The clay pressed into the failing lip at the high end, thumb-smoothed, still tacky when you touched it last — that was me, or someone whose hands I inherited, or the habit of hands that outlasted the people who started it. I don't know which anymore. The distinction has stopped being useful. I re-hang the wicker door when it sags. I lay new hurdles over soft patches. I cut the rushes back in three corners and then leave them alone and then cut them back again.

I go in stooped at the low end. My hands are soft from the wet air. I have calluses in the places where a person gets calluses from gripping clay scoops and wooden shovels and withered withy, and those calluses have their own softness, if you can imagine it — the hardness is in a different layer than the softness, and they coexist.

I keep to the brick. I don't stand in the pans. I don't clear the reed from the outflow. I don't touch the eels. I don't try to find where the water comes from, because it comes from the channel, and the channel comes from under the wall, and that is an answer.

When the frogs go quiet in a wave — and they will, always, unpredictably, rolling from the far end toward me — I wait. I don't move until it's passed. I have stood in that silence more times than I can count, in the long dim blade of light, with the water dripping and the dark not empty in the black bays, and felt the crawl of it on my skin, and waited, and then the noise has come back up like a tide and I have gone on with what I was doing.

I speak low. If I can't be heard, that's not a problem to be solved.

---

What do I do, in the long stretches between one pan and the next? I walk the brick. I look at what the water is doing. The first pan is cloudy with silt; the fourth is clear enough to see the bottom, and the bottom is brick. I check the seams. I press fresh clay where the lip is failing. I watch the newts hang in the water with their legs out, and I don't need them to do anything about it.

The willow at the high end is more knuckle than tree. I coppice it. It grows back. I coppice it again. We have been having this conversation for a very long time — it says *more*, and I say *not yet*, and the frogs say something else entirely, and none of us wins.

The eels are fat and old and slow in the last pan. I have never touched them. I don't know what would happen if I did. That is not the kind of question the rules are trying to answer.

---

The birds in the roof slats talk over everything. The bats are a dark that shifts in the black bays. There are more residents in this place than there are people and I am, perhaps, the least certain of any of them. The frogs do not know I am here. The eels might suspect. The moss grows on every north-facing surface and I have never touched it and it has never touched me and that is an arrangement.

I am not the owner. I am not the maker. The makers stopped standing in the way a long time ago — so long that the bricks have heaved and settled and heaved again and the floor is a kind of history I can't read all the way down. What I do is not repair, exactly. It's more like keeping up with the maintenance of a thing that is now maintaining itself. The water wants to go one way; the bricks want to heave another; the frogs want a third; and I want to keep the door on its hinges, and all of us have arrived at a working peace.

---

So: I am here, in the empty stretches, in the long dim blade of light, with the drip for rhythm and the frogs for a tide. I go in stooped at the low end. I keep to the brick. I wait out the silences. I press clay into where the water is winning, not to stop it — that would be a problem I can't solve, and unlike you I know it — but to slow it enough that the place doesn't become itself too fast.

I am the one who is not there. You know me by what I've left. And when the silence comes in a wave, I stop, and I wait, and the frogs come back up, and I go on.

That's the rule for visitors. I'm not a visitor. I just know it.
