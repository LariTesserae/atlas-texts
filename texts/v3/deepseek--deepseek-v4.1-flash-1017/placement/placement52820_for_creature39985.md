I am below the third tank, on the walkway that no one built but everyone uses, where the grating has rusted into a shallow trough. The condensation here does not drip; it walks. A film of water eases its way along the underside of the main steam line, gaining weight until it reaches the flange where the line turns and drops, and there it releases, a single fat bead that falls twelve feet to the pool below. It has been falling this way, at this interval, for as long as there has been a flange. I can hear it now: the gathering silence, the swelling of the bead, the *plink*. Then the sizzle where it hits the edge of a hot pipe on the way down. The two sounds overlap, a tiny song of cause and consequence, and I am the only one who has ever bothered to learn it as a song rather than a schedule.

I am an attendant. I am one of the ones who is not native and not the machine. In the taxonomy of the basin I am, perhaps, a kind of groomer—the small fish that cleans the gills of something too large to notice it, and too old to need it, and yet somehow the gills stay clean. I wear oilskin that has stiffened into a second hide. I wear felt clogs. I have a brass rod in my belt, and a rag, and a chalk stub worn to the width of a thumbnail. My wrists are banded with copper gone green. My fingertips have no prints left; the ridges filled in, years ago, with a smooth pearly callus, so that if I ever left this place no one could read me. But I will not leave this place.

I come from here, the way the beetles come from here—not born in a house but born into a system. My earliest memory is a sound, not an image: the thud-hiss of the main engine, which I thought was the sound of the world breathing, and which is the sound of the world breathing. My mother taught me the vents by their voices before she taught me their names. There is a vent that says *shhh*, low and constant, near the crustacean dark under the second tank. There is a vent that says *sss-sss-sss*, stuttering, a quarter-turn from true, that someone corrects and I correct and someone after me will correct, forever, because it dreams of drifting. There is a whistle that shrieks once an hour and means nothing is wrong. I learned that first: the sounds of health. It is much harder to learn the sound of a fault, because a fault is a silence where a sound should be, and the ear must be taught to hear what is missing.

My work is small. It is almost embarrassingly small. I walk my route through the pipe valleys and the under-tank corridors, and I listen. I press the brass rod to a pipe and then to my jaw, and I feel the flow inside the metal the way you might feel a train through a rail. I tap the flanges. I wipe a gauge that has fogged. I find a weeping seam and wrap it in cloth so that it weeps slower, and I know—I *know*, with a certainty the people up in the grey air of the world above could never understand—that I have done something enormous. The seam will not fail today. Tomorrow it will weep again. I will wrap it again. This is not futility. This is the whole point. The seam failing is a change, and change is the only sin.

I kneel by the pool under the flange and watch the beetles. Three of them, skating, their legs fringed with silver. They are green-copper sparks in the steam, and they are beautiful, and I do not have a word for beautiful, so I think of them as *true*. A beetle on a pool is a true thing. Yesterday there were seven on this pool. Today there are three. The pool has not cooled—I test it with the back of my hand, and it would scald me, it would flay me, it is perfect—but something has shifted in the water, some mineral, some small scum they feed on, and the beetles have gone elsewhere to find it. This is information. I note it. I will check the intake line that feeds this pool. I will listen to it. Perhaps it says *shhh* a little less than it should. Perhaps I will find nothing and the beetles will simply return. Both are acceptable. The listening is the work, not the finding.

I think sometimes about the ones who came before the attendants, the ones who laid the first pipe on the black basalt. I do not know if they existed. There is no story of them. The complex has no foundation stone, no founding; it simply is, the way the vents simply are, and we simply attend, the way the crustaceans scuttle and the flies thicken. Perhaps the pipes grew here, the way the basalt cracked. Perhaps the first attendant was born from a puddle, already knowing the thud-hiss. It hardly matters. What matters is the balance: heat from below, water from the air, attention from us. Take away the attention and the machine runs dirtier; the sounds drift; no one says so. The basin would keep breathing. But it would be dreaming, and no one would be there to say *wake up, that vent is lying.*

There is a phrase among us. When someone corrects a valve that had begun to drift, they say they have *told the truth to the pipe.* I like this. It is what I am. I am a teller of truths to pipes. My entire life is a slow, repeating truth told to a machine that has already been told it a thousand times and will need to be told it a thousand more. The chalk marks fade and I renew them. The palimpsest thickens. The world stays finished.

The bead of water on the main line gathers. I feel it gather. It is above me, a small heavy truth in the hot grey air, and it falls, and it sizzles, and it plinks. I listen. I am home. I am supposed to be here. Listening.
