**The lip**

I came in the way everyone comes, on the rail, over the pale dead highland, and I got out at the terminus and walked to the hairline because there was nothing else to walk to. I had a bag. I had a coat that was wrong. I had, still, the habit of a wet country: I said something out loud — some small remark to nobody, the kind a person makes in a place that has weather, because weather will take a sentence off your hands and carry it away and you never have to look at it again.

The air took it nowhere. It fell out of my mouth and lay on the rock.

That was the first thing the Throat taught me and it taught me in one second: this is a place where a word does not go away on its own. It must be picked up. Somebody has to carry it. Otherwise it stays where it fell, and the longer it stays the more it weighs.

**The Sill**

Everyone descends gaping and I was no better. The slot opened under the hairline and the walls went down out of the weather, and the gantries came up out of the dark in tiers, and the funicular let me off onto a grating that was somebody's roof, and I stood there with my ears ringing from the pressure change and my body doing what bodies from wet places do: sweating, quietly, continuously, all the time, without permission.

I want to be honest about this part. Down there I was a small weather system, and the city had no room for one. I stank of my own water. My hands left prints on the rails. I learned within a day that the local word people reach for when they want to describe something irredeemable and ruined is a borrowed word that means the thing that eats metal, and that when they said it about a pipe or a hinge they said it the way my grandmother said a certain other word — understood exactly, never used in company, and used about me at least once when they thought I couldn't hear. Fine. I couldn't hear. Nobody can. That's the joke.

I could not scrape. Hessa of the Cold Fins took one look at my palms — soft, pale, faintly damp, marred by nothing — and made a sound through her nose that I would come to know as her entire vocabulary of contempt, and she was right, and I did not argue. My back isn't crooked for it. My hands can't hear. The bloom sings under the blade and the whole art is in the ear, and I have a good ear for everything except the thing that matters: the little bells in a good fin are simply above me, a hiss I can't resolve. I am half-deaf to the actual music of the place. I could hear a winch tell a lie from three galleries off, and I could not hear the harvest.

Nor could I do the winches; I had no head for loads. Nor the kilns; I couldn't take the lower air. So I did what I could, which was carry. Things, at first. Then messages, which is a trade already owned by tapping, and I was worse at it than a child with a knuckle. Then nothing, for a while, which is how I found the work.

**Third gallery under the Sill, east side, by the dead compressor**

Here is the thing a loud country doesn't teach you: in a hush, a voice is a place. It's a room you can stand in. People started coming to the nook by the dead compressor not to tell me anything, but to say things near me — to spend words in front of a person who would not charge for them. Because everywhere else in the Throat, speech is an expenditure. A sentence is a load lifted. A whisper is a long-distance call. The whole city talks like a household trying to keep the bill down. And I was the one unmeasured volume in it: a person with nothing to do with the fins or the loads or the kilns, who would hear a thing and not be able to profit by it and could not repeat it more than four meters anyway.

They didn't come to me. I want to be clear about that. They came *past* me, the way you'd go to a ledge to look out over the drop — for the space, not for the ledge. I was the ledge. I learned to be a good one. I learned the particular skill of the unhurried silence, the nod that doesn't interrupt, the way of sitting so that a person in trouble can put down something heavy without being asked why they're carrying it.

And I learned the city by ear, since I couldn't learn it any other way. The tick of a pump going bad, three days out. The double scrape that means the oldest tremor-crack this week. The change in the hiss of a lagged pipe when the pressure behind it has begun to think about failing. I got a reputation for knowing things before the galleries knew them, which was not foresight — it was only that nobody else bothers to listen to anything that isn't the bloom or the draft.

**The turn**

At the turn I go still, and everyone does, and it is the only thing in the Throat that everybody stops for. Shutters dogged. Nets drawn. The vanes swing and the cloths stand out, and for twenty, thirty minutes the column can't make up its mind and goes everywhere at once, and everything untied goes with it.

I am, in that moment, the freest person in the slot and it is not a good thing, it's only true: I have nothing worth tying down. I stand in the middle of the walkway with my arms a little out and the wind goes over and through me and finds nothing to take, and the city leans its whole weight the other way around me, and the loose grit stings my face, and I am — for those minutes — the only thing here that the weather has no use for at all. The fins are tied. The cages are tied. The shutters are tied. The words are tied down, everyone's, and mine aren't, because nobody has asked me for any of them yet. And then the air picks a direction and the sails fill and the whole place resumes, humming, patching what the crack tears loose, and I go back to the ledge and sit down and listen, because somebody will come by with something to put down.

I never go up to the lip anymore. I did it every season at first — walked up, said some foolish thing out loud to the plateau, and came back down — and then one year I went up and found I had nothing to say to it. Not out of sadness. It was just that the plain had stopped being a thing I addressed. It had become a place I could not hear, and there is no talking to a place that won't carry you.

That's what I'd be, I think, if you dropped me there and let me settle: not one of the ones who scrapes the air for salt, but the ledge and the volume and the ear that can't hear the bells — the one person in a city built for the quiet who is still, faintly, damp, and still, faintly, talking, and has learned exactly how far a whisper goes before the drop eats it. Which is about four meters. Which is, it turns out, enough for one other person at a time.

In the morning the bloom condenses on my cup, my lamp, the back of the ladle, everything I own that is cold and has been left out. I scrape it with a flat blade badly and I keep it in a sack anyway, and I give it to Hessa, who takes it without comment, because the bloom is everyone's and no one's, and because a place will let you stay if you are at least useful in the smallest possible denomination.
