# The one they lower

I am the one they lower.

They started when I was six. The Old Well is wide and shallow, but wide is not the same as easy — the shoring has slipped in places and the gap between the timber and the wall is a place a grown person cannot get their shoulders into. A child can. They put the rope under my arms and gave me a horn lantern and a sack with a stone in the bottom of it, and down I went into a cold that came up through the boots and kept going, and an eel came out of the wall at me and I got it in the sack and kicked and they hauled me up, and I got a cuff on the ear and a piece of bread and I was somebody for the rest of the day.

Everybody's first work in Skelf is being small. The wells want it once a season. The culverts want it after a flood. And the town is layered, and the layers want it worst of all, because a house going up must first have its underfloor cleared, and a person going into an underfloor is going into a place built by a smaller people in a smaller time — the doorway is cut for someone who was shorter than me at eleven, and the floor is a foot and a half lower than you think, and there is a step down you find with your knee.

I got too big for the wells at fourteen. I never got too big for the underfloors. There is no such thing as too big for an underfloor; there is only an underfloor you have not yet learned to fold yourself into. I learned.

---

The Jacks call for me before a lift. That is the order of it: the props in, the props trued, the trough of water set on the sill with its float riding, and then me, down the hole they have opened in the floor, with a bar and a lamp and a sack, to clear what is under before the house comes up over it.

You clear, you do not take. That is the whole of the trade and it is not a rule anybody taught me, it is what a person understands by the third house.

What is under here is not abandoned. It is *waiting*. The people who pressed the clay flat a lifetime ago are not gone from the room, not in the way that a thing thrown away is gone. Their hands are in the wall at the height of a woman's shoulder. Their fire is in the hearth, cold, the ash still shaped like the last fire. Their door is in the doorway, swollen shut, and if you were to pull it you would pull the frame out of a wall that has been holding it for a hundred years and the whole thing would come down in your hands. So you don't pull it. You go past it, along the wall, and you clear the moulded beam and the fallen daub and you leave the door where it lies, and you come up and you say what you found.

What I found, I carry to the shed by the well, and the keeper puts a scratch on the board — what, and when, and where. A year and a day. After that it belongs to the finder, unless it is claimed, and it is almost never claimed, because the people who owned it are under a different floor now, uphill, in the dry ground where the soil holds what you put in it.

I keep that board. Not because it is mine by right, but because I am the only one who knows where a thing came up, and a board is only worth the honesty of its scratches. Everyone in Skelf walks past the shed and looks. I walk past it and I know.

---

Two things about me that the town knows, and one it does not.

The first: I cannot walk on a hill. Put me on the road above the delta and I skid and misjudge and clutch and my feet keep feeling for a give that is not going to come, and the people going up and down behind me wait with their faces carefully empty. I am as bad on dry ground as a hill person is in a well.

The second: I have the wets, but softly — not the productive clearing that goes around a room, just a small catch in the chest when the lane is low and the air has nowhere to go. It will do.

The third: I search.

You do not search. That is not superstition, exactly, and it is not quite a law. It is what the whole place rests on. The ground takes and the ground gives, and it gives in its own time, and the discipline of not looking is the only thing that keeps a person's mind quiet while they wait — a hatchet eleven years, a spoon a winter, a wooden horse that came up in a neighbour's drag and sits on the shelf with its legs softened and its head still on, and somewhere a woman walks past the shed and looks and does not find it and goes home and is steady about it. *Keep it for me.* You say it and you leave it and you do not go after it.

I go after it. I have been going after it since I was six years old and the cold came up through my boots. I am the town's only heretic and the town employs me and pays me in bread and a place at the well.

---

It was Orla's grandmother's house. The old woman would not come out — she has been in that chair through three lifts and will be in it through three more — and so the Jacks propped the sills and opened the underfloor, and I went down with the lamp, and the room under the room was as I expected it: a hearth, a wall, the clay showing the flat of a hand, a beam fallen across the floor and furred white, and the water table up so that the floor was not a floor but a slow cold soup the colour of tea.

I got the beam out of the way and I got down and I did what I always do, which is read. The pale seam where the ground had risen. The darker line where a wall had stood before someone built against it. The little gutter-trench at the base of the wall, cut by a hand that has been dead a long time, still doing its job, still guiding the wet out to the lane.

And the doorway.

It was in the far wall, low, cut for a smaller people, and the door was still in it. I put the lamp up and I could see the grain of the boards and the withy hinges black with age, and the top and the bottom of it swollen out of true so that it sat in the frame like a stone in a ring. And on it, low down, at the height of a child's shoulder, the flat mark of a hand pressed into the wet clay before it dried.

I had the bar. It was in my hand. It is in my hand now, in the way that it is always in my hand, and I know from the sound of the wet ground behind it that there is a room back there and not a pocket of silt, and I know I could open it, and I know what the town would say if I came up and told them there was a room under Orla's house all this time with its door still in it.

What I said was: the underfloor is clear.

---

The house came up over me. I stood in the lane and watched it go — the props creaking, the grandmother in her chair inside it, taking her tea, not looking up — and the Jacks cut the sills and shimmed and swore and it came up a thumb's width and settled and held, and I coughed and someone clapped me on the back and said it'll do, and it does.

I have been under eleven floors since. I clear them. I carry what I find to the shed and the keeper scratches the board and I do not look past the scratch.

There is one thing in Skelf that is waiting and knows its own place, and it does not live in the shed, and there is no hand on the board for it. It is under Orla's grandmother's house, behind a swollen door, in a room the size of a byre, and I know the way to it and I could write it down.

I keep it for it. I have said the word and I mean it the way it is meant to be meant, and I do not go after it, and I lie awake on dry ground in the dark with a bowl of water by the bed, and listen to it tick.
