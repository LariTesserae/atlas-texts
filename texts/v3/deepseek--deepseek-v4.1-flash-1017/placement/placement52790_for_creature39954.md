They gave me my first stone when I was six. I still carry it, though it is not the same stone. It was grey then, with a white vein running through it like a river seen from a high place. Now it is the colour of wet bread, and the vein has moved around to the other side. That is normal. Most things here are normal if you hold them long enough.

I am Marl. I am of Hart's Fold, forty-two winters old, or perhaps thirty-nine; the winters here do not always keep an honest count, and no one argues with them. I am a channel-tender and a cairn-keeper, which means I walk the routes and keep the water moving and keep the ways old. It is not a grand position. There are perhaps forty of us in the Fold and I am one of the middling ones, neither young nor unremarkable to time. An elder can stand in a thin place without being pulled. I still flinch. I have made my peace with flinching, mostly.

You would know me by my feet before my face. We are low people, wide-footed, our toes spread for wet moss. My hands are broad and my nails thick as horn, worn into tools — I test stone with them, tap and listen. My skin holds the damp the way river-stone does, so that I am always a little cold to the touch and do not notice. My hair is coarse and dark and braided back with split reed. I wear layers of pressed leaf and woven stem, waxed against the wet, seed-down packed into the shoulders of it. At my throat, one stone. At my wrist, two. In the pouch at my hip, three more, and a fourth that belongs to my brother and that I am keeping until he comes for it. Six stones is a lot. A person with too many is a person who does not trust the world. A person with too few has not been paying attention.

In the mornings I go out into the streets, which are water. The channels run between the low turf roofs, shallow and brown and slow, and I walk downstream because it is easier, combing the lichen mats with a wooden rake as I go. In the early light the mats are dull, grey-green, asleep. They brighten at dusk. We work by them then. We eat the outer growth in hard years; it tastes of cold metal and pond water, and it makes the inside of your mouth glow faintly for an hour, which the children like and the elders pretend to disapprove of.

I keep the cairns too. I carry a satchel of worked pebbles and press them into place at the corners of paths, into the moss, into the seams of the old stone. Not to mark the way. To remind the way to remain. A path that is not reminded will forget itself and become a deer-track, or a glacier, or nothing.

When I was fifteen I sat by the long slow river and watched the mirrored surface and did not look away. I saw the valley under ice — a white plain, and the river beneath it a black crack, and something moving in the crack that was not a fish, because there are no fish. Old Fer came and put a stone in my hand and closed my fingers over it. *The water is remembering,* she said. *Let it remember alone.* For a week afterward my words came out as grey shapes and fell on the ground, and no one finished my sentences for me; they let me finish them myself. That is how they teach. I have carried a stone at my throat ever since.

So when I say I walk the routes, understand what that means. This morning I came around the shoulder of a hill and the path ahead of me had gone young. The stones were sharp-edged and pale, newly cut, and the air smelled of fresh stone-dust and of woodsmoke that was not the smoke of any hearth I know. I stopped. I put my palm flat on the rock and waited. You do not walk on a young path. A young path does not yet know where it is going, and it may go anywhere, including into a time that is not yours. So you stand, and you breathe, and you hold your stone, and you let the path feel your hand and remember its own age. It took a while. The edges dulled. The moss came back into the joints. The smoke went back to smelling like the smoke I know. Then I walked, and it held.

The awl I carry was bone this morning and is a small iron awl now. I do not trust it. I never trust iron. It is the bone awl's job to be an awl; the iron one has ambitions.

At dusk the mats brighten and the whole Fold glows faintly blue-green along its streets, and we sit in a circle in a round house on woven mats over stone benches, weaving and carving and mending, speaking in low unhurried phrases and letting each sentence finish before we begin another. When the words hang in the air as coloured shapes, we wait for them to dissolve. No one reaches after them. Children are taught not to chase the coloured words. I chased them. I am still here; the teaching is not about death, only about inconvenience. The soft stone gives under the knife like hard cheese, and my hands know the shape of the bowl better than my eyes do. Sometimes the bowl remembers being a different bowl, and I let it, and then it comes back.

My mother is in the moss on the eastern slope, with a worked stone in each hand and a channel diverted over her. We speak her name less each year, so the land can settle around her. I still say it sometimes, quietly, to myself. That is allowed. It is not encouraged.

There is no birdsong here. No rustle of small creatures. We are the only quick things, and so our silence is a kind of attention. The plants hold the soil, the soil holds the water, the water holds the town, and none of it holds still in the way you would expect. I am not a master of this place. I am an attendant of a worn one. I carry stones to keep the seams from opening too wide, and I speak softly so the past can pass through without breaking anything, and I live as a low, continuous negotiation between an ancient land and its unsettled memory.

If you came here you would find me on the western path at dusk, pressing a stone into the moss at a corner, and I would look up and not be surprised, because the world is thin and people arrive from the past as easily as from anywhere else. I would put a stone in your hand. Hold it. Wait. When the path feels old again — and it will — walk.

Settle well.
