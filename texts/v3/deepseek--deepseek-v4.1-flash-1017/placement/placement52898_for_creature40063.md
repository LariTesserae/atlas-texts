You would find me on the shallow terraces at the eastern edge, where the runoff spreads thin and the sinter builds its rings, and where the ground is warm but not hot enough to hurt a bare foot. That is where I keep. Not because it is comfortable — nothing here is comfortable, it is only warm — but because it is a margin, and I am a margin animal.

I am upright. I am ash-colored and thin and long in the arm, and I have a nose that has become most of my face. I do not speak much; there is nothing to speak to that would answer, and the place rewards quiet. When I do speak, my voice comes out flat and low, the way a sound comes off a slab.

I have a ridge. It starts at the base of my skull and runs down my spine to the small of my back, and it is sinter — the same yellow-white crust that rims the pools, laid down a millimeter a century. I did not choose it. It is what happens to a thing that stands wet and warm for long enough. It has rings in it, like a cut stem. When the night comes on and my back cools and contracts, it ticks. Tick — pause — tick. I am not the only ticking thing on the plateau. I have simply joined the chorus, and I am no longer sure which tick is mine and which is a flake of crust cooling three paces off.

I do not make my own heat. Very little here does; the place gives it out and we take it. So I sleep in the plumes, standing, braced against a column, my mouth a little open — not as open as the grazer's, I have not gone that far — and the warm air goes through me and out again and I wake warm. When a plume turns bad, when it starts to sting and my throat closes, I move. The flies tell me first. There is always a plume somewhere. I have walked four days for a plume.

The forty beats are the whole of my grammar. I feel the seam in my teeth before I hear it, and I have learned to do the work in the gap and be still in the closing. Still is not a posture here; still is a skill, like swimming. You plant your weight on a column and you wait, and the gap narrows and shuts and sighs its bolus of steam past your legs, and then it opens, and you step. If you get it wrong you lose a foot. I have lost things. Not a foot. But I walk now the way a word is spelled.

I eat what accretes. I scrape crust with a flat stone and chew it; it tastes of chalk and old eggs and I have come to want it. I lick flies out of the low air, which is not dignified but is efficient. I take the small translucent crabs from the warm pools, and once — with a stone and a great deal of patience — one of the plate-sized ones, and the meat of it was sweet and strange and I did not sleep well after. I chew the jointed grey-green stems at the margins. Once I ate a blind fish, and felt bad about it, and would do it again.

The hunter leaves me alone. I am not good eating; my flesh is a salt lick, and I think the smell of me is the smell of the ground. But I go quiet when the quiet comes. When the plume-flies drop low and the cricket-things stop rasping and the ticking seems to hold its breath, I hold mine, and I stand, and I am a column. That is not fear, exactly. It is courtesy, and it is also the correct thing to do.

I am learning the fish's trick, slowly, in the wrong body. I have stopped using my eyes for distance. They are not much good here anyway — there is enough to see by and not enough to see far. I go now by the taste of a seam and the difference between one stone and another; by the smell of iron when the air is about to turn; by the temperature of the air at my cheek, which tells me where the good plume is before I can see its glow. Lime is safe. A sweetness like rotted flowers means a bad fissure, and I turn. If I ever go blind entirely I believe I will manage, and I believe I will miss very little.

The ridge is a record. That is the accident of me. The place keeps no books — it builds nothing, it writes nothing, it does not know how long it has been doing what it does. But I have grown a core sample of everywhere I have stood, one millimeter a century, and if anyone knew how to read it they would find the centuries of the eastern trough, and the year I went north, and the year of the warm rain. No one knows how to read it. Once a party came across the plain with lamps and instruments, and asked me the way off, and I could not remember, and I gave them the taste of the water instead. They went away. I do not know if they found it.

Sometimes I think about the columns. You learn to stand on them and not in the seams; that is the first rule and it becomes the body. But some of them are not columnar jointing. Some of them are weathered at the shoulders in a way that stone does not weather, and furred with salts in a way that stone does not fur, and they lean — a half a degree, a whole degree — farther than the seam below them would have made them lean. I do not know. I have stopped being certain. There have been others, I think, who stood in the warmth long enough, and the warmth laid them down, and the wind took what the wind takes, and now I walk among them and stand on them and do not know which ones to greet.

The place does not need me. That is the freest thing about it, and the thing I stopped noticing last. It will go on closing and opening, closing and opening, when I have been laid down in my turn and weathered at the shoulders and mistaken for stone. But I will have registered. A little. In the rings, in the strata, in a column that leans one degree further than it should. That is what a person is here — a slow modification of the rock, patient enough to be mistaken for it.
