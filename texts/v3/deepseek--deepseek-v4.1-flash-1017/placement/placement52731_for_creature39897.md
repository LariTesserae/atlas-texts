I find myself in a low hollow beneath the curve of a great calcareous spire, where the rock river's edge has been worn smooth by generations of padded feet. The ceiling above me is a canopy of woven tendril and resin, strung with dim copper discs that catch the phosphorescent pulse and throw it back, softened, onto the walls. The air here is thickest, pooled in this low place, and it carries the smell of my own resin pots and the sweet rot of the compost bulb that warms my sleeping mat.

I am a deep-listener. My name is a chord—three descending hums with a click at the root of it, a sound my mother made when she first pressed my palm to the stone. I am old enough now that the young ones place their hands on my chest when they speak to me, to feel the rhythm of my answer before they hear it. My eyes have narrowed permanently to seams. My second lids are thick and slow. I have not run in forty pulse-cycles, and I do not miss it.

My work is this: I sit. I press my palms flat to the cavern floor, or to the flank of the rock river, or to the base of a great frond, and I listen. The Cradle hums, always. It hums in a deep register that lives in the marrow of my femurs and the deep muscles of my jaw. I have learned its grammar over a lifetime. There is the hum of health—a broad, warm drone like a held breath released very slowly. There is the hum of strain—a thinner tone, a tightening, as though a cord were being drawn through the rock. There is the hum of the network, the tendrils that lace the ceiling, passing their slow signals from spire to spire, and that hum is a chorus of small clicks and ticks, a conversation I am only beginning to understand.

I have felt the rock river shift in my lifetime. Once, when I was young, a section of the great cooled flow cracked—a sound like a toad's jaw snapping, but enormous, and the hum that followed was a shriek that lasted a full pulse. We lost four dwellings on the eastern curve. We lost a shaper whose name was two rising tones. The community gathered in a listening circle for a full cycle, humming until the chord resolved into something we could all bear. Fire, blight, the cracking of the river—these are the three fears, and I have felt all three in my bones. The fire was before my time; the blight was a slow gray creeping across the eastern crops, which we cured by pruning and by songs of encouragement to the healthy bulbs; the crack I remember every time I lay my palm on the river's flank and feel a hairline of new strain.

I live alone now. My bond-partner returned to the fungal beds eleven pulse-cycles ago—her name was a single high whistle and a chest-hum, and I still catch its shape sometimes in the network's traffic, a ghost of a signal. Our children are grown and scattered along the river, each with their own chord-names, each placing their palms to stone when they pass my hollow, so I feel them coming before I see them. I like the solitude. It makes the listening cleaner.

I have three tools. An obsidian knife, chipped and re-chipped so many times the blade is a thin sliver of darkness in my broad hand. A copper awl, cold-hammered, its point worn round with use. A coil of fiber cord, which I use to mark the places on the river where I have felt the strain—I tie a knot for each pulse-cycle of attention I give a spot, and the knots accumulate into a kind of map, a slow ledger of the world's health. I keep this ledger in my head, but the knots help me remember. I have four hundred and twelve knots on the eastern curve. I have been watching that hairline for thirty cycles. It has not widened.

What I do with what I hear: I hum back. That is the whole of it. I hum back, low and steady, and the Cradle answers, and in the answering I know more than I did before. I hum to the crops when they are sick. I hum to the young ones when they are afraid of the dark between pulses. I hum to the stone when it is uncertain of itself, and I feel the vibration travel down into the rock, and I feel the rock accept it, and I am reassured. The Held do not master the Cradle. We answer it. We are held in its warm stone hand, and we hum to say *we are here, we are listening, we are still part of the pulse.*

And sometimes, when the cavern hums at its very lowest—that wide, ancient drone that comes from the planet above and the planet below and the slow chemistry between—I press both palms to the floor of my hollow and I hum a chord that is not a question and not an answer but a third thing: a note of acknowledgment, a note that says *I have heard you, I have heard you for a long time now, and I am still here, and I am still listening.* And the Cradle hums on, and I settle deeper into the warm stone, and the fungal fronds above me open and close with their dry papery breath, and the tendrils click their patient clicks, and I close my eyes to seams and wait for the next pulse.
