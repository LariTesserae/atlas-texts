I would find myself not at the center of the plateau but at its smallest seam: crouched at the lip of a circular basin, one hand in the warm, iridescent mud, the other resting on a shelf of liver-colored rock. Behind me, the stony sea stretches into humid haze. Above me, a Rooted arches over the hollow, its pearlescent underside beading with condensation. The air is loud with beetles. A jewel-feathered flier watches me from a fern fan, proboscis raised, deciding whether I am food or furniture. The standing stones at the basin’s edge are humming just below hearing, and I can feel the script on them rearranging itself in the corner of my eye.

At first I would think I was a visitor. I would walk the dry spaces between pockets, measuring the plateau by its oases, sleeping under root-arches, drinking from leaf-cups. But the plateau is not a place that hosts visitors for long. It recruits. It grafts. I would notice my knuckles becoming warm and faintly pearlescent. I would notice that the mist from the old artifacts clung to me and did not dry. I would notice beetles settling in my hair and staying there, not as pests but as passengers. My lungs would begin to taste the condensation before it formed. My footsteps would fill with frogs.

So I would not be a colonist, a maker, or a top predator. I would be a seam—a human-shaped graft between pockets. Something like a walking node in the plateau’s slow, wet circuitry. I would carry spores from one grove to the next on my skin. I would wick moisture from the dreaming stones and release it as a fine, warm breath over the fern fans. I would read the shifting script only when I was not looking at it directly, and I would never understand it, only feel it as a direction—a tug toward the next hollow that needed watering.

My body would be mostly mine, but colonized. A film of blue-green algae along my forearms. Fungal threads in my lungs, glowing faintly when the fungi bloom after rain. A small fragment of standing stone lodged near my sternum, humming below hearing. I would not build roads or villages. I would move slowly from Rooted to Rooted, from spring to spring, carrying the plateau’s dampness in leaf-cups and in the folds of my leathery, leaf-like cloak. The fliers would threaten me. The leaf-mimics would mistake me for a slow plant. The amphibians would nest in my footprints. I would be small, damp, localized, and synchronized to the next pulse of mist.

If you came looking for me, you would not find a person in a house. You would find a warm, pearlescent shape kneeling by a bubbling spring, a halo of iridescent beetles around its head, breathing out a mist that smells of wet mineral and old machinery. I would be the plateau’s small, mobile seam—alive enough to be part of the cycle, quiet enough to be mistaken for geology, and always, always carrying water from one green room to the next.
