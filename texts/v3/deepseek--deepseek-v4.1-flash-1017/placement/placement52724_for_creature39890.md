I am the Collector of Leftover Lamps.

I am not tall. I am not a flue. I am something that lives between them, in the intervals where the plain is nearly empty of bodies.

I am a low, wide thing—wider than tall, perhaps a meter and a half across, pressed into the mat like a hand laid flat. My body is soft and wet and the color of wet peat, and I have no fixed shape, only a habit of shape: a slow amoebic spread, a margin that creeps and retracts, pseudopods that taste the moss ahead of me and decide. I move a few meters an hour when I move at all. Most of the time I do not move. I am patient in the way of the ground itself.

What I am is a mouth and a memory. I am an organ of the mat that has learned to gather what the weather leaves behind.

The ground-storms make lamps. They discharge low and flat along the wet channels, and the fog holds the light, and the light stays—pale standing figures, dimming over hours, drifting a little, resident, patient. The animals stop noticing the old ones. The older ones are the ones I want. I creep to them. I take them in.

I have a structure for this, on my underside, a cup of tissue lined with something that is not quite lens and not quite stomach. The animals call it nothing, for they do not notice me either. I press the cup over a standing figure of glow and I draw it down, slowly, the way you draw water up through cloth. The light comes into me and it does not leave. It settles. It stays lit in the dark of my body, a dim coal, and it dims the way a coal dims, over days, over weeks, so slowly that I cannot tell you when it has gone out. And it leaves a trace, a faint residue of brightness in my tissue that does not fade. I am full of old light. If you could see through me you would see a scatter of pale points, a constellation pressed into a soft dark mass. I am a lamp myself, in that way. I glow, faintly, from what I have eaten.

I have eaten, I think, for a very long time. The flues are all old. No new one has risen in living memory. I do not know how old I am, because I do not keep time the way the flues keep it, with their falling. I keep time the way a lamp keeps it: by dimming.

I am not alone in my way of being, though we do not gather. There are others of me, scattered across the intervals, each in its own slow territory, each creeping to the lamps and taking them in. We do not meet. The plain is wide and we are slow and the lamps are not where they were. When one of us is full—full in a way I have not yet been—I am told it goes to a flue. I am told it presses itself to the warm base and lets the vapor take it up, and it goes out through the crown in a plume, and its light goes with it, dispersed, a thin bright thread in the white feather. I do not know if this is true. I have not been full. I am not sure I want to be full. I am not sure I want to be dispersed.

I am not a person. I do not build. I do not speak. I am a cup and a slow creep and a memory of light. I live in the cold between the rings, where almost nothing lives, and I live there because that is where the lamps are left. The bazaars are loud and warm and crowded, and I have crept near them and felt the mob of insects on my margin and withdrawn. I do not want the crowd. I want the interval, the quiet, the patient glow.

The plain murmurs around me always—the hiss and cluck and gurgle of the channels, the dispersed chord of the flues, the pop of gas from the mud. I have my own small sound: when I take a lamp, I make a low hum, a resonance, the cup of my underside singing its one note as it draws. It is quiet. It does not carry far. But it is mine.

I am one of the plain's inhabitants, though the plain does not know me. The flues are its lungs; the rings are its cities; the mat is its body. I am something smaller: a cell in the great body that has learned to eat light. I am the plain remembering what the weather did. I am a lamp that walks, slowly, in the cold between the anthills, carrying the leftover glow of storms that have already passed.

When I die—and I will die, slowly, the way everything here does, the way the flues fall—I will not fall. I will settle into the mat and the mat will take my shape and hold it for a while and then forget it. And what I have eaten will go back into the peat, into the slow fire, into the charge, into the next storm, into the next lamp. I will be part of the plain's remembering, which is another way of saying I will be forgotten, which is another way of saying I will be everywhere.

That is what I am. A collector of leftover lamps. A slow cup in the cold. A dim constellation pressed into the wet dark, creeping from glow to glow, humming my one note, eating the light that happened earlier.
