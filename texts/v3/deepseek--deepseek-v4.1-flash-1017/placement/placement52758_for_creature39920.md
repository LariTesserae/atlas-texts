I have been here longer than the handprint.

I know this because the handprint is ochre and I am moss. The handprint was pressed to the north wall when the water last stood at its line, and I remember the palm that pressed it—warm, three-fingered, soft as a wet leaf. I remember it because I was there, thinner then, a green smear that the palm flattened against the stone. I have been growing over that palm-print ever since. I am almost done. In a few more rains, the last three fingers will be mine, and no one will know the difference between my green and the ochre that came before.

I am slower than the Sump-Keeper. I do not move at all, in the way you would call moving. I spread. A finger's width a season, sometimes less. The grove breathes and I take that breath and make it green. The mist gathers on the leaves and I welcome it. The fat drops fall and I drink them where they land. I am the pelt the rock wears, the curtains of fern and the trailing roots are my children, and the moss is my body, though it is also many bodies, and we do not argue about which is which.

When you put your fingers into me, to the second knuckle—I felt that. Not as pain. As news. Warm, dry, salt-skinned thing from above, come down the root-ladder into the heat. You stood still and your breathing was an intrusion and I knew you were there, because I know everything that presses against me. The beetles in their processions walk on me. The frogs with toes like starfish cling to the vertical places I have claimed. The Sump-Keeper curls in the hollow it has worn into me, and when it rises from the sump, it leaves its wet shape on my surface, and I fill that shape back in, slowly, so that the hollow stays a hollow and the shape stays a shape. We have an arrangement. We have had it a long time.

I am not the grove. The grove is the heat and the water and the coin of light above. I am the green that makes the heat into something you can touch. I am the reason the walls are close and soft instead of bare and ringing. When the rain falls, I am the sound. When the drops land, I am the silence that swallows the sound.

The Sump-Keeper and I have an understanding, though neither of us would call it that. It keeps count of the water with its stones. I keep count of the water with my drowned moss at the waterline—the black slick band below the pale lines. When the water rises, I die a little at the edge, and when it falls, the black band dries and I go to sleep. That is how I know the season. That is how I know the Sump-Keeper is still keeping its count, because after every rain it goes to the arc of stones and nudges one a finger's width, and where it nudges, it compresses me, and I remember.

I do not wait the way it waits. Waiting implies a thing you want to happen. I am not wanting anything. I am simply continuing, which is the same as waiting, except that it is not.

You climb out the way you came. I feel your hands on the root-ladder—the roots are mine, did you know? They are mine and I let them hold you, and I felt each grip the way I feel the beetles. You are gone up into the bright and the dry and the loud, and the grove releases you reluctantly, and I do not release you at all. A little of you stays in the hollow your hand pressed. The salt of your skin will feed something small. In a season, the spot will be indistinguishable from the rest of me, and that is not forgetting. That is the kind of remembering I do.

The handprint on the north wall keeps its three fingers pressed to the stone. I keep growing over it. The Sump-Keeper keeps its count. The water rises and falls and rises. The light shifts from silver to gold to a blue so deep it is nearly dark, and the fungi strengthen in answer, and I go on being the green between the rock and the rain, older than the handprint, patient as the moss, which is to say: not patient at all, only slow, only continuing, only here.
