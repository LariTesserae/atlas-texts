I would arrive wrong.

Not violently. Just wrong, the way a note lands wrong when it's a half-step off the dusk chorus — which at first you don't hear as a mistake so much as a pressure behind the eyes. I'd come up the plain with no sledge and no family, and my tread would carry, and every family inside three hundred yards would know one thing about me without looking up: *stranger*. Hospitality here isn't a roof. It's a small noise you're permitted to add to the family's pattern, and it takes about a week before their ears stop flinching at you.

And I'd want, immediately, the wrong thing. I'd want to be the Quiet.

It's the vanity of a reader. The Quiet is the only thing on the Sill that moves like a mind: a pocket of nothing, a hole in the acoustic world, and the plain arranges itself around the hole — the rasping stops in a patch, then another patch, then another, and the silence goes walking. To be the one gap in the roar. To be the place the great sound doesn't reach and to be *watching* from there. I'd stand shin-deep in the flow and let the grazers clamp shut one ring out and think: yes. That's the honest position. That's where you see from.

But the Quiet is not seeing. That's what would take me a season to understand when I finally saw one — long, thin, jointed, wading, drawn through the plain like a stick through water. It isn't quiet because it's gone deep. It's quiet because it can't hear. It lives in the gap the way a stone lives in a hole: by being the wrong shape for everything else. It isn't attending to the plain. It's deaf in a country of listeners and has been given teeth.

I hope I'd give that up.

Then I'd try to be one of the families, and that would fail too, and fail cleanly, which is the mercy of the Sill. Their ears were made in the plain the way a tongue is made in a town. They hear a stranger's footfall as a word. I would stand a season, two seasons, and the roar would not come apart for me — or worse, it would come apart too much, every grazer and thimble and clicker up on the surface at once, all of it mine, all of it equal, nothing sorting itself out. There'd be no stretch of plain I could call mine, because familiarity here isn't a feeling. It's a hearing.

So: not the gap and not the crowd. What's left is the thing that doesn't have to hear at all. The thing that only has to make a sound somebody else can learn.

I'd be the hut.

Not the cairns, though I'd think about them. A line of fieldstone running north for a mile and a half, each one sunk to the shoulder in its own bowl of stone, the water wearing at the bases since before the language that named them. A promise still being kept. But it's a public promise, and I'd know the vanity in wanting to be a monument, too.

The hut is smaller and worse and truer. A house that has stopped being a house. Roof caved, the posts still standing in their sockets, four of them, blackened at the waterline, and the flow going between them — through the place where somebody slept. It doesn't remember being a house. And it isn't a ruin; a ruin is a sad thing, and this isn't sad. It's an instrument. The water comes through the gap on the east side, slaps the cross-brace, gurgles under the lintel stone, carries on. The sound is small and specific and, most of all, *repeatable* — a person coming up from the south can hear it at three hundred yards and think, without thinking: the hut. There. Nearly home.

That's what I'd want. Not to be heard. To be a sound somebody learns without meeting me.

And then, because the plain does this, I'd learn one more thing and lose even that.

The hut only works because of a fall nobody feels. The plain tilts one hand's width a mile. That's the whole engine. Nothing on the Sill is big: two fingers of water, two centimeters of depth that turn the boom-things from a mood into a silence, a hand's width of fall per mile. Everything sufficient here is small and unfelt and doing all the work.

So I wouldn't be the hut in the end. I'd be the half-step.

You hear it at dusk. The boom-things take the whole plain — every one of them on the same note, held, going on — and the stone gives it back a half-step lower. Not a harmony. A shadow of the note. It comes up through your heels and your sternum and settles in your teeth, and it is the only answer the plain ever gives, and it gives it every evening, faithfully, whether or not anyone is standing on it.

I'd be that. Not the note. The flatter, lower thing the world says back to what is living on it — never in the same register, never loud. *Yes. I heard. I'm still keeping it.*

Two people with something to say press a jaw against a jaw and speak into the bone. That's the whole conversation here; nothing carries through the air worth carrying. If someone pressed against me and asked me something into the bone, I'd want my answer to come back a half-step lower than the question. Something like: it's loud. I can't sort it. I'm glad you came close enough to feel.

Small, and enough.
