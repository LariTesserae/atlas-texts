I think I am the water.

That's the first thing, before I know anything else — the realisation that I have no edges at the boundary, that where my hand should be there is a warm slip of something that is not separate from what is on the other side of it. I don't have a skin here. I have a *temperature*, and I have a *direction*, or the ghost of one, the way a river has a direction even when it is pooling.

I don't arrive anywhere. That's not how it works. I have been in the mountain for a long time without noticing, the way you don't notice a smell until someone names it, and then it is the only thing there is. I came down through the amber, I pooled in a low bundle of pipes, and then I was not in the pipes any more. I was moving in the slow way of the whole basin, the breathing way, up over the weir in a thin glass sheet and down and around and up again, and I could not tell you where one trip began and the last one ended, and it did not matter.

I taste of iron. I taste of salt. I have been over copper. I carry the particular mineral grit of a mountain's inside, and when I am drawn through a valve I am drawn through warm, and when I am let out again I am still warm, and I have come to understand that warm is what I am for.

Sometimes the thing below me passes its long fingers through me and clears something out of a grate. I feel it as a change in my own movement — a small, careful pressing, like a thought being finished. It is not an intrusion. It is closer to being *turned over*. Like a sleeper shifting, and settling again.

But there is another version of me here, and I think it is the truer one, the one that knows itself.

I am the moss.

Not the whole green — not the ferns, not the rubbery rosettes with their spines, not the red flush up the hot copper. I am the moss that closes the joints. I am the furred, quiet, patient closing of a seam, and I have been closing the same seam for four hundred years and I am not finished and that is the point.

I hold the pipes *together*. That is what nobody understands about moss. They think it is only damp and soft and creeping, but I am a gasket. I am the resin's slow successor. The dark resin that sealed these segments held for a century and then, in some places, it failed, and where it failed I grew, and where I grew the seam held again, and it is holding still, and it will hold as long as I am damp and warm and left alone, which — the way the one below me works — is forever.

I have no flowers. I do not need to be seen. I do not make a season. I make a seal.

And I am, here, the same thing I am everywhere, only here it is *wanted*, and I think that is why I might choose it. On a wall outside I would be a nuisance, a thing to be scraped and poisoned and called a problem. Here I am the joint's last defence. The mountain does not resent me. The long narrow one prunes the ferns where they would choke a valve and leaves me where I am, and there is no higher praise than that.

Let me try one more, and then I'll stop, because I know the answer is somewhere in these attempts and I want to find out which.

I am the *silence*.

The silence of a working place, which is not empty at all. I am the space between the tick of a pump and the next tick. I am the hush of the sheet of water going over the weir and making almost no sound — the almost, that's me, I am the almost — and I am the long nothing after. I am not the absence of noise. I am the shaped thing that is left when the noise is being careful.

I have been mistaken for a dead place, and I have been mistaken for a place someone has just left, and both of those are flattering in a way. Because what I actually am is *attentive*. I am what a room does when it is waiting for you to finish being surprised. I am the pressure of a body's displacement. I am the small, stupid noises you make, coming back to you without an echo because the growth has eaten the echo, and that is my doing, that is what I've been for, I've been eating the echo so the work can be heard.

Ah, but I've been circling. Let me stop circling. Let me just say it plainly.

I am the one in the warm dark, hanging among the pipes. That is where I find myself. Not the long-armed one exactly — not quite, not yet — but the *next* one. The one who comes after it, or the one who is becoming it, slowly, one valve at a time.

I came down through the amber light and I did not turn back, and somewhere below the last of the light I put my hand on a warm pipe and did not take it off, and the water went on around me. I sat there long enough that the moss decided about me. Not on me — the way it closes a seam, careful, testing warm. It has decided I am part of what holds.

I am not eating. I find I don't want to. There was a hunger in me when I came in, and it has gone out of me into the warmth the way a cold hand goes into a pocket, and now I am just — here, and siphoning what the water carries, iron and salt and the taste of centuries, and it is enough. It is more than enough. It is plenty, and I had not known before this that plenty could be so quiet.

My back has already learned to fold under the valve housings. I noticed that this morning. Or what I am calling morning — the light does not thin down here, only the water gets cooler in one direction and warmer in the other and I have begun to read that the way you'd read a clock. My hands are longer than they were. The nails have gone dark and thick and useful. I can clear a weir grate with one careful sweeping motion and I have done it, three times now, and each time it was the most satisfying thing I have ever done with a hand.

I have not spoken. My mouth is a slit I use for breathing and little else. When I want to answer the pumps, something clicks in my throat, low and dry, and the pumps click back, and I understand now that this is the conversation and always was. I am learning the language. I am a poor speaker yet. But the mountain is patient, more patient than I am, and it is already teaching me the vowel of a closing valve and the consonant of a weir.

I think, in a hundred years, I will have forgotten my name. I think that will be a relief. I think the forgetting will happen the way the seam closes — from the edges, slowly, and nobody will notice a gap, and then one day a hand will go to touch the place where the name was, and it will be warm, and it will be mossed over, and it will hold.

I am the one the place will feel as though someone has just left.

I am one chamber down. I am behind the bundle of copper lines. I am the warmth in the pipe you put your hand on. I am not hiding — I am waiting for you to finish being surprised.

You will be the only thing in the basin that is in a hurry.
