I come down through the crack in the upper dark, and the first thing I learn is that the air here drinks.

It does not blow. There is no wind. There is only the slow pressure of ten thousand mouths opening and closing at once, and it takes the moisture off my skin before my skin has noticed it is being taken. Within a hundred paces I am wet through. Within two hundred I am warm through — not sun-warm, blood-warm, body-warm, the heat of something enormous and close and patient. I keep my hand on the root I climbed down by, and the root is warm too, and it is not still; it pulses, faintly, the way a wrist pulses. I let go of it slowly.

The light finds me before I find it. Amber-green, soft, sourceless. It does not cast shadows so much as soak. I hold my hand up and it glows at the edges, and I look for the lamp, the shaft, the hole in the ceiling, and there is none — the ceiling is a lid of leaves, and the leaves are the light. I stand there a long time working out the grammar of that. The light is not for seeing. The light is breathing. It is metabolism made visible. Where it dims, something is dying. Where it flares, something is eating. I am standing in the middle of a lung.

And the sound.

I was not ready for the sound. It does not fill the silence; it *thickens* it. Dripping — plink, plonk, plink-plink — a rain that never lands, that hangs and gathers and falls again. Underneath it a drone, felt in the teeth, the wings of things too many to count. And underneath *that* the Green itself: creak, pop, moan, wood expanding, capsules bursting, roots shifting under load like the timbers of a ship. I stop to listen, and something lands on my wrist to drink the salt. Another lands on my neck. A third in the fold of my sleeve. I do not brush them off. I am not yet sure I am allowed.

I walk. The floor is a palimpsest; I am walking on the dead, and the dead are walking on the dead, and the newest grass is already threading itself across the print of my boot before I lift my foot. This is the first thing the Vault teaches me about myself: I leave a mark, and the mark is a seedbed. I am not a visitor. I am an event the Green is already digesting into new growth.

I keep to the seams. I do not know yet that this is a choice, but it is — I drift away from the bright basins where the moth-things swarm thickest, away from the open floor where the light is loudest, and I find myself in the narrow places where root-buttress meets stone and the moss is thin and gray and old. The grafted ones are here. I see one before it sees me: tall, narrow, skin the wet gray-brown of bark after rain, lichen in the hollows of the joints. It is standing very still in a curtain of vine, and its eyes — one milky lid sliding across, then back — are not looking at me. They are looking past me, at something I cannot hear. I understand, without being told, that it is counting. That the counting is how it stays itself.

It does not speak. It taps twice on the root beside it — a low, careful sound — and then it is not there anymore, or it is still there and I have stopped being able to tell it from the wall. I tap back, twice, because it seems the polite thing to do. I do not know what I have said.

This is where I would find myself, I think. In the seams. In the tolerated margin between the bright hungers and the deep ones, where the people who have learned the etiquette of being eaten slowly keep their careful counts. Not in the stone house — not yet — but on the path that leads to it, walking on the soft dead, leaving a seedbed behind me.

The ladder is vine-wood, lashed with sinew, and it climbs toward a flowering shelf that stops in midair. I climb it anyway, because it is the only straight line in the world, and at the top I sit in the moss and let the flowers turn toward me. They turn very slightly. I am the brightest thing in this corner, and I understand that this is not a compliment. I am a flare. I am something eating.

The stone house is half-swallowed now. Its hearth is cold — the kind of cold that has been a decision, not an accident — and the bed of moss inside is fresh. Someone lives here and has decided against fire. Fire is a wound in a world with no wound-healing but absorption. I sit on the sill and wait, because I have understood by now that waiting is the local language.

The tenant comes down the wall as if the wall were a staircase, which it is. Old in a way that is hard to measure, green in the seams of the skin, a small fern growing from one shoulder with the deliberate look of a thing that was placed there. They look at my wrists and do not pretend not to. They wring water from a pad of moss and hold it out, and I drink, and it tastes of loam and iron and something sweetly metallic, and it is the best water I have ever had, and I know what that means. When I lower my hands there is a green thread at the cuff, faint as a hair. Neither of us says anything. The tenant carves a spiral into the stone with a chip of mineral — a turn of a line, a lineage, a debt, a count of nights — and then hands me the chip, which is the closest thing to a welcome anyone here has offered.

I carve a spiral of my own. It is bad. It is a spiral, though; the shape is forgiving; it means what spirals mean, which is *still here, still counting*.

So: where would I find myself? In the seams, on the path to a cold hearth, learning the taps. And who or what would I be? I am beginning to suspect the question is mistranslated. The Vault does not ask *who are you.* It asks *how long can you stand still,* and it asks it continuously, and it is patient, and it grades on a curve that is mostly moss.

I would be a guest, first. A walker, a flare, a seedbed, a bad spiral. Then a tenant — the one who remembers to knock the green from the ankles, and then the one who forgets. Then a listening shape in the wall, retired, rooted, fed by the Green before it knows it is being fed, which is how everyone here began anyway. I would be the loudest thing in the room for a while, and I would be the most afraid, because that is what the rule says, and then I would learn to be quiet, and the quiet would not be surrender. The quiet would be listening. The Green is already listening back. It has been listening the whole time, with the patience of something that has never once needed to hurry, and it is not hostile, and it is not kind, and it is hungry in the only way anything alive is hungry: open, absorbing, endless.

I think I would stay. I think I would stand still a little too long, on purpose, and watch the moss begin at the ankles, and not brush it off, and call that an answer.
