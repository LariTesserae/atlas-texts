I would be found above the path, not on it — a third of the way up the fourth comb past the dry well, in a chamber whose sill is smoother than its neighbours'. That smoothness is mine. I have been entering and leaving long enough that the stone has accepted my weight as a kind of weather.

I did not come to live here. I came to count.

The bands, the striations that are not geological — I came to be the second person to count them and arrive at the first person's number, or the first to arrive at the same number twice. I had a method. I had a notebook of pressed grit, and a stub of something that made a mark, and the method was this: count upward from the sill, record each interval against the reach of the comb's shadow at the moment of counting, climb down, climb up, repeat. I did this for a long time. I was thorough. Thoroughness is a kind of prayer in a place that does not answer.

The trouble is that the counting is true while you are doing it. The bands are there; they are there in the number you have reached. And when you come back the next dimness, the number is a different true number, and the shadow has moved in a way the moon-sized thing does not account for, and you cannot tell whether the stone changed, or you did, or the agreement between you and the stone was quietly renegotiated while you slept. I filled the notebook. I filled another. I began to suspect that the counting was not a way of learning the language but a way of being slowly translated into it, and that every number I wrote was a word I had mispronounced.

So I stopped counting. I am not proud of it. But stopping is how I found the rest.

I moved up the comb. I live now two chambers below the hollow — the one open to the wind, the one that plays the fifth. The two tones come up through the floor of my chamber and I have learned to tell root from fifth by where the cold sits in my chest. The root sits low and still, like a hand pressed flat. The fifth sits higher and wants to move. When the tone falls away into that interior quiet — the drawn-breath quiet — I can hear the grit settling in the lanes below, and the tick of droppers letting go under the far overhangs, and once, twice, the syllable from deep in some neighbouring comb, said and said until it is only a shape.

And I have begun to answer.

That is the thing I would confess, if there were anyone to confess to. In the pause after the tone falls, I make a sound. Not the fifth. Something under it — a breath shaped like the syllable, or like my best guess at the syllable, which is to say not a word at all but the intention of one. I do not know if it is right. The walkers have not changed course. But once, far off, one of them — mid-stride, breaking the line where the dimness meets the stone — adjusted by half a degree when I made the sound, and not when the note fell. Or I imagined it. At these distances the shelf does not distinguish between a thing and the imagining of it; that may be the whole of its hospitality.

You will want to know what I am. I am not a builder. The builders are a phrase now, worn to a mouth. I am not a walker; my prints are my own, and they are wrong for the path, and I have stopped leaving them where the path can see. I am not the watcher, though I have felt the watcher pass over my sill and change the pressure on my face, and I did not mind it. I think I am what the shelf keeps when a person stays past the point of leaving: a soft thing in a chamber that changes, very slightly, how the note comes down. Furniture for the sound. A baffle. A bad mouth learning its part.

When I am gone the sill will keep my smoothness for a while, and then the wind will take it, and the shelf will have forgotten that anyone tried to answer. That is not a sadness here. It is the arrangement. The phrase is still being spoken. I was one of the mouths it wore — briefly, imperfectly, and the wearing is the point.

I would be found above the path, two chambers under the hollow, listening for the fall, shaping my breath to the syllable, and I would not stop if you came the other way. My prints would not match yours. Or they would match the ones you left an hour ago, which is worse, and which is the shelf's way of telling you that you have been here longer than you thought, and that the counting was never going to work, and that the note is the only thing with a schedule, and that it was kind enough to keep one.
