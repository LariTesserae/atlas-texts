**The Interval**

First I thought I would be the cart.

That was the honest first guess, and it was wrong for a reason it took me three descents to see: the cart does not decide. It comes down the long shallow slope with its wheels locked and its load-bed rattling empty, it sits at the foot of the rise for a season while the sacks fill, and it goes back up with somebody else on the traces. It is the only thing in the basin that is neither stone nor water nor crust nor meat. It has a beginning and an end to every trip. It carries the number up. But it goes where it is pulled, and I have to choose, every season, whether to get on it — and a thing that chooses is not a cart.

So: not the cart.

For a while I thought I would be one of the settled. That is the obvious answer, and you can tell it is wrong the moment you test it, because the settled ones do not think about it at all. Dell does not lie in her rib at night weighing the shelf against the trough. The weighing is precisely what makes you not one of them. They are people who stopped weighing — not decided to stop, stopped, the way a kettle stops when it is taken off — and it happened on a season when the cart went up and they were standing at the foot of the rise and simply turned around. You cannot do it on purpose. Do it on purpose and the basin knows, the way a wall knows a blade.

I tried to be the throat, in a poem I never finished, because the throat is the good part, the throat is the sound. But a throat is not a self; it is an aperture. It does what the pressure does. If I were a throat I would have no question to ask — and I have one, that is the whole trouble, I have exactly one, and I have had it for nine seasons.

So I settled on the least romantic answer available, and it turned out to be the true one, and the true answers in this place always look like bookkeeping.

I am the one who counts.

Not the seasons; the settled ones count seasons, and a season here is just how you say *however long it has been* in a place with no days. I count the resolves. I am the only person in the basin, settled or otherwise, who keeps the number nobody keeps, and I keep it badly, in wax pencil, on a strip of canvas cut from a sack and folded four times, in the inside pocket where I keep nothing else.

Two columns. Descents, and resolves heard.

The columns have never once agreed. They are off by two, or five, or eleven — I cannot tell which, because the wax lifts in the damp and the folds go soft and gray, and I have had to copy the numbers onto a new strip twice, and I am not certain I copied them right, and that uncertainty is not a flaw in my method. It is the method. This place is built to erase records. The nap fills in the print of your hand overnight. A season fills in a footprint. Erosion and deposition hold hands and nothing accumulates here but the crust itself — so a number cannot live in a place, it can only live in a body, and the bodies here are being filed into the stone.

---

The first resolve I heard, I heard from inside a trough, halfway down a wall I had no business on, in the wet, alone.

I knew it was coming. That is the part I have never been able to explain to anyone up on the shelf, and I have stopped trying: an hour before it happened there was a pressure behind my eyes and an ache in the arches of my feet, and I sat down on the slick stone without deciding to. The throats drew together like a fist. The basin said it, one soft enormous *whump* I felt in my sternum and my sinuses and the roots of my teeth, and the water rose in every trough at once and held, trembling, and something in my chest did a thing it does not do at any other time — slow, strong, not quite mine — and then it let go and the rhythm scattered back into its loose unruly round and I was sitting in a trough with wet knees, breathing.

I have been going down to have it do that again ever since. It has not done it again. I keep going down. That is not devotion, exactly, and it is not science either; it is closer to the way you keep returning to a room where you once heard something through a wall.

The settled ones think it strange that I go back up. The cart-men think it strange that I keep coming down. Both are right. I have a life on the shelf with a shape to it — morning, noon, night, a beginning and an end, people who expect me at supper — and I have this, and I have declined to choose between them for nine seasons, which I understand is its own kind of answer and not a flattering one.

But I will tell you the small reason and then the real one. The small reason: up there I am one of many, and down here a single living thing is an event, and it would be a lie to say I do not like the way that feels. The real reason: I keep my seams. In my sixth season I opened a knuckle on a rib of stone, and it healed thick and pale and faintly warm, mineral at the edges, the way a channel wall heals — and I have been careful ever since. Not because I am afraid of the basin. Because I do not want to be healed into it. Dell is being filled in and she has stopped wanting it to stop, and I understand that completely, and I will not have it, and that is not a judgment on her. It is a difference of metabolism.

---

So: where would you find me. Down in the ribs, on the dry stone, in the hour before a resolve, with a folded strip of canvas in my pocket and a count I cannot keep.

What would I be? I have thought about it a long time, and the answer I have landed on is not a person and not a thing.

Everything in the basin has a tenant. The troughs have the skimmers. The deep chambers have the pale things, rising and settling, rising and settling. The throats have the pulse. The crust has the stone, and the stone has the shape the water wanted, and the water has the pressure underneath, and the pressure has whatever is patient and warm and does not say anything. There is exactly one part of the place with nobody in it, and it is not the thud.

It is the fourteen seconds.

Not silence — it is loud there too, the clucks and gulps and long wet hisses never stop — but the interval is where the beat is countable from. It is short. It is the only room left, and I take it every time I come down and give it back every time I climb out, and I have come to think that is the whole of what I am: the number, and the keeping of it, and nothing to keep it in but a body that the nap is patient with.

Dell would say the basin is quiet. She is right, and it is the most alarming thing anyone says about it. But I have heard the resolve from inside a trough, and I have walked up out of the dish afterward into a day with a shape, and I have never once been able to give the number to anybody — they take it the way you take a stone someone hands you on a beach, politely, and put it down.

The crust will fill in the wax in a season or two after I stop. Then there will be no number, and the throats will go on falling into step on no schedule at all, and nobody will notice the gap, because there is nothing in the basin a gap could be measured against — except the interval, which is where I would be, and which is not empty as long as somebody is counting.
