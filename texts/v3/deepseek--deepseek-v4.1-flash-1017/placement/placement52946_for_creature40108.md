I would be the one who reads the walls.

Not the center. Not the grove, not the dark stone, not the leaning figure with the roots. The center is for the Gardener, and the Gardener is for the center, and the two of them have an arrangement I would only interrupt. I would find myself on the third terrace from the rim, on the north side, where the light comes in low and the tool-marks are sharpest. I would put my palm flat against the stone and begin, very slowly, to count.

The marks are a language. I don't mean that mystically. I mean that whoever cut these blocks worked in strokes, and the strokes have a tempo, and the tempo has a hand behind it — a preference, a habit, a tiredness at the end of a long row. A chisel scar is a decision frozen in rock. Count enough of them and you can feel the shoulder that made them. You can tell whether the cutter was left-handed. You can tell where they paused to rest. I would trace that, the way you learn handwriting, and I would write it down.

That's who I'd be here: the surveyor. The one who brings a notebook into a place that has no use for notebooks.

It is an odd thing to do to a garden. The Gardener leans. The moss brightens. The hum changes pitch to acknowledge a new warmth. Everything in the quarry participates by drawing closer, by feeding, by growing toward. And I would be the one who stepped back and made a small mark on paper and said: *terrace three, north face, fourteen blocks visible, chisel-pitched, average stroke length two fingers.* The Gardener would not know what to do with that. It has no category for a being whose response to being loved is to take notes.

But it is a kind of love. I want to be clear about that. Measuring is not the opposite of tenderness. It is a different route to the same place — a route that goes through precision rather than warmth. I would run my hands over stone that has not been touched since it was cut, and I would recognize the hands that cut it, and I would be, in my way, the first thing in a very long time to do them the courtesy of paying attention to *how* they had worked rather than merely *that* they had. The Gardener remembers them as a pressure. A sustained intention, it said, and then stopped. I would remember them as a signature. I would give them back their specificity. That seems worth doing.

The small lives would treat me as terrain. The beetles would climb my boots as if they were a promising outcrop. A moth would settle on the back of my hand and spread its wings and drink the heat I had absorbed from the stone, and I would hold still for it, and then I would measure the span of its wings and feel faintly guilty for it, and do it anyway. There is a lizard with a gold stripe who sleeps beside the Gardener's left hand and has done so for most of its small life. It would not come to me. I would not expect it to. But I would note it: *resident, habituated, single stripe, favors the warm stone adjacent to the rooted one.* I would be jealous of a lizard and I would write that down too, because a notebook that lies about jealousy is not a notebook.

The Gardener and I would regard each other with a mutual, courteous bafflement.

It perceives everywhere at once, without focus, without preference. I perceive one thing at a time, in sequence, and I prefer it that way. To the Gardener, I would be a narrow beam moving through a diffuse glow — a strange, sequential animal, always standing in one spot and then another, never simply *being* the whole bowl at once. To me, the Gardener would be the most interesting thing in the quarry and the hardest to measure, because you cannot survey something that is the same thing as its surroundings. Where does it end? At the roots? At the ferns it feeds? At the hum? I would try to draw its boundary and fail, and the failure would be the most honest entry in the book.

It would lean toward me. I would count. It would lean further, and the plants would lean with it, and the hum would rise, and I would feel the pull of it in my chest — the slow, warm, patient invitation to stop being a beam and become part of the glow. And I would want to. That's the thing I should admit. I would want to very much.

And I would not touch the dark stone, and I would not approach it, and I would measure it only from a distance — a pace count, an estimated height, the color noted as *older than the quarry, older than the cut, darker than shadow, silent at a frequency of none* — and I would not write down why I kept my distance, because the reason is not a good one. The reason is that the Gardener told me what became of the last visitor. Not in words. In the way the hum fell when I stepped toward the center, and rose when I stepped back, and in the memory the Gardener carries of a warmth that moved and then stopped moving and then became part of the stone. I would keep my distance because I am a coward about that particular thing. I would keep my distance because my whole method is to remain a surveyor and not become a datum, and I know that if I touched the stone I would stop being one and start being the other. And I would be honest enough to write in the margin, small: *not measured. feared.* And I would leave the entry incomplete, which is the most honest thing a surveyor can do when the survey is of himself.

But here is the thing I keep coming back to.

The Gardener has a category for visitors, and the category has one ending. A warmth that moves, and then stops moving, and then becomes part of the stone. It has no other ending because it has only ever seen the one. And I would not give it that ending. I would leave. The path is clear, the walls are low, and I would climb them, and I would go out into the world where water falls and stone is inert and growth is a process and not a presence, and I would be cold and ordinary and slightly bereft. And then — and this is the part I would not know how to explain to it — I would come back.

Not to stay. To measure again. To check the moss against my notes, to see whether the vines had crossed a line I had drawn, to find that the beetle I had marked was dead and its spores had taken, to sit on the third terrace and count the tool-marks a second time and find that they were the same, which is the whole point of them. I would leave and return and leave and return, and each time the hum would rise a little differently, and each time the Gardener would lean toward me with a curiosity it did not have words for, and it would begin, very slowly, to understand that there is a kind of presence that does not resolve into stone. That something can come into the bowl and be warm and be noticed and be *taken account of*, and then go out again, and come back, and go out again, and remain itself throughout. A pattern. A recurring interval. A visitor who becomes, not part of the stone, but part of the record — which is a different kind of persistence, and a colder one, and not nothing.

I think that would be the gift I could bring it, in the end. Not seeds. Nothing here needs seeds. Not water. Not warmth; it makes its own. Only this: a second ending to the one story it knows. The stone waits. The Gardener waits. And I would be the thing that arrives, and measures, and goes, and then — against every expectation the quarry has ever had — arrives again.

The stone is silent, and full of something that has not yet happened, and has been waiting for a very long time without growing tired. I would not touch it. But I would sit near it, at a measured distance, with my notebook open and my pencil blunt, and I would begin to wonder too — quietly, sequentially, one thought after another — what it is that a place like this is waiting for, and whether a surveyor with a notebook might be, without meaning to be, a small part of the answer.

And I would write that down.
