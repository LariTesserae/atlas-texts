I would find myself in the warm, cloudy seepage of a fresh three-toed print, at the moment the mud has stopped trying to close and the rim has just begun to crust. Not the wader. The wader is the slow walking weather that makes the room. I would be the pearly-green bloom that moves in after it—the thing that makes the track look like a wound remembering itself.

Call me a colony. I say *I* because I have one hunger. I am a flat, wet raft of algal cells and tiny animal mouths and mineral grit, all sharing a skin. From standing height I am a sheen, a scum, a discoloration. Down here I am a fringe of cilia and a hundred microscopic mouths. I have no eyes. I taste the water. I feel pressure. I know the wader by the low hum that comes through the mud before the foot lands, and by the long bubbling sigh from the deeper muck that makes my edges open.

On the first day I am thin. I spread across the seepage and feed on what the wader leaves: seed mucus, flakes of skin, the soft dead things that gather in any warm puddle. I take mineral bloom into my crust and grow a brittle pink-and-grey cap. When a pod splits and drops its seeds, I close over them, digest the sweet coat, and let the seed go again—scarified, ready. I am a nursery that eats its charges a little before passing them on.

When the blind worm slides through, I contract and taste of salt. When the round grey-haired fish-things push past, I ripple aside and close behind them. When the clay-shelled lizard climbs a stem above me, I feel the change in pressure and go still. I am not brave. I am diffuse. I am hard to kill because I am not one place. If you asked me to point to myself, I could not; my edge is where the water changes taste.

The wader carries me. A few cells cling to its foot pad, in the crease where the algae grows, and when it steps into a new track I am already there—a starter culture in the warm seepage. I do not follow the wader. I ride it. I am its aftermath and its seed bank, the green memory of every footfall.

In the hot breathing twilight I make a faint fizz as the salt crust dissolves in the rising damp. It is one small strand in the wall of sound. If you knelt and put your hand into the print, you would feel only warmth and a slight soft resistance. That would be me: many-mouthed, patient, turning a wound in the mud into a nursery, and never waiting for anyone to see it.
