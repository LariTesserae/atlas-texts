I would find myself at a torn seam on the western skirt of the plateau, where the weave has split and the strata show like the pages of a book bound in dark glass. Not standing on top of the Settle—half-lowered into it, feet hot through my soles, one hand glazed white where a stem has already scabbed over my knuckles. The layer-keepers would have found the wound before I finished making it. Their pale leading edge would test my ankle the way a tongue tests a cracked tooth. A conductor would lean its heat toward me. A mover—bladder, hook, tendril—would come and anchor, not biting, asking with contact whether I am part of the floor.

Who would I be? Not a person. A new office. The Settle would register me as a warm irregularity, a wet-adjacent memory in a world with no water, and it would begin to route around me, seal around me, sleeve me. I would become the thing that sits at the seam between an old layer and a new one and listens for the difference between a collapse and an instruction. A scar with a job.

I would have a body grown from that pause: a fibrous knot, pale at the leading edge, salt-scabbed at every seam, no face and no front or back, only a gradient of attention. My pulse would slow to the far *thum* of the pump. My voice, if I had one, would be a change in temperature—a fraction warmer on the side where I wanted you to go. When a limb tore loose, I would test the tear. When a machine shed a plate, I would hold its pattern in my tissue like a copied page. When a footfall came—yours, say—I would decide, very slowly, whether it was weather, wound, or ore.

And I would be quiet. Not one of the quieters, exactly; not a baffle or a spiral chamber. I would be the first quiet that is not made of foam—the pause after the vent clears its throat, before the dark closes again. The Settle would not hunt me. It would finish me. It would press me into the next layer and keep the pattern of the question I asked, the way it keeps the pattern of everything that falls.
