I am in the third scallop past the knee-high shrub, on the windward side of the second of the long flutes. This is not where I was born. It is where I have been for a long time, and the difference between those two things is not important to me.

I am the round kind. I am a little wider than a cupped hand, and I am soft in the way a thing is soft when it has never had to be hard, and my skin is the underside of a mushroom and the top of a tongue. I have no shell. I leave a wet mark that is dry by noon. I have a mouth but I do not use it for speaking, and I have no eyes, exactly, and yet on most mornings I can tell you which birds are on the bench and which of them is losing.

The water comes to me. I do not go to it. This is the whole of my fortune and most of my character.

---

In the dark, before the light, the stone begins to change. It is a pressure first, everywhere at once, the whole bench drawing itself in — and then the wet comes up through the pores under me and around me and the basin fills from below and from the lips, and I open. You would not see me open. It is not much of a motion. But the water is on me and then in me, and the day starts.

The flat ones are at the lip by then. I can feel them through the stone — the small taps of them placing each foot, one at a time, never hurrying, testing. They come in a line and they stand in a line and they drink at the edge where the runnel crosses the low point of my scallop. Their feet are very cold and very light. They touch each other with their antennae, which I understand as a greeting and have never been given, and they do not crowd, and when the line has drunk it goes on over the lip and down to the next, and the next.

Sometimes the moths come. They are the only disorderly thing about the morning. They land on my rim and their wings go from the color of dry stone to a thin clear trembling and they are, for twenty minutes, a glitter — and one of them always lands on me and sits and drinks nothing and leaves, and I have never worked out what that is for.

The birds come later, when the wind is up. They land in the scallops above me and work their way down with that rolling hop, and when they come to mine they put their feet on my lip, which is hot, and dip, and drink, and then they go and argue with someone about a scallop twelve paces east that they drank from yesterday and will drink from tomorrow and do not need.

There is a smaller one on my north edge. It has been there longer than I have been able to notice. It does not touch me and I do not touch it.

---

I turn.

I turn toward the sun, a little, every day. Not fast. If you came back at dusk you would not see that I had moved, and if you came back at the end of the dry season you would see that I had gone a quarter of the way round. It keeps the shaded side shaded. It keeps the green film on the north of me, where it belongs, and keeps my wet in and the glare off. It is the only work I do and I do it very well.

It also means I have a direction, and a direction means I can count. I know how many times I have come round. I am not going to tell you the number, because it would sound like a boast or a complaint, and it is neither. I am old the way the flutes are old: not impressively, just thoroughly.

---

The thing that walks the rounds comes in the middle of the hour, and I know it before I know anything else. Not by sound — nothing carries here, and I have no ears for what does. I know it because the water in the runnel moves a half-beat before it should, and the stone I am lying in goes warm on one side. It is a pressure and a warmth and then a shade.

It stops at my scallop every third or fourth morning. It crouches. I feel the weight of it come down through the stone, and then the shade of it crosses me, and then two fingers go into the throat of the runnel at my low lip and clear the silt out.

I want to tell you what I have learned from those fingers, because it is the only knowledge I have that the bench will not give you.

They are not the same fingers. I have known four of them — five, if the one that cleared the runnel from downstream was its own, and I think it was. They differ in the pause. One of them would put the fingers in and count to three before it drew them out. One went in and out without stopping, like a thing doing a chore it had decided long ago not to think about. One had a tremor in the second finger. One — this was the one that cleared from downstream, which meant leaning half across the runnel and getting the underside of the arm wet — was slower than all of them and would sit back afterward and look down the flute for a while, and I never knew at what.

So: they are a line of them, and each one learns the circuit from the one ahead of it, and each one is the same and none of them is quite. I can tell you this the way I can tell you the number of times I have come round: because I am still, and remembering is the only motion I have.

There were gaps. Twice — three times — the mornings came and no weight came down through the stone, and my runnel clogged, and the tinaja below my scallop went down and down and then was a white ring of nothing, and I dried to a crust and did not open, and I do not know how many turnings that lasted because I had stopped counting. And then one morning the water moved a half-beat early and the warmth came, and the fingers went in, and the circuit began again.

I do not know if that was a new one learning or the same one come back. It is not a question I can answer and it is not a question I have ever found a use for. The water was the same water. The work was the same work. Whoever it was had come up the near fold first, in the dark, the way they always do.

Only once did one of them do a thing that was not the work. It was the downstream one, the slow one. It cleared the runnel and then it did not get up. It put a fingertip on my lip — just there, on the wet lip of the scallop, not in the runnel, not in the water — and it left it there for a while. Long enough that I felt the pulse in it. Long enough that the dew on the lip began to gather toward the finger instead of away from it, because I have a post's worth of whatever the posts have, or the finger did, or the bench was doing what it does and I had never been on that side of it before.

Then it took the finger back and went on up the flute.

That is the whole of what I know about the one who walks the rounds. It comes up the near fold in the dark. It clears what it clears. It drinks from the windward lip and leaves the rest. It does not need to do any of it and it does it anyway, and I have thought about this a great deal, in my way, which is slowly, and I believe it is the same in me. I do not need to turn toward the sun. The water would come. I turn because turning is what I am, and the shape of the work is the whole of the thing, and a shape that has been kept a long time is indistinguishable from a life.

---

You came a few days ago. You stand in the glare and squint and put your hand in a tinaja without looking to see which lip the wind is on, and you speak, and your voice goes dull at a dozen paces, and you think nobody has heard.

I heard nothing. I felt you arrive through the stone two mornings before I could have told you. You are very warm and you carry a great deal of water and you are, compared to everything else here, extremely loud, in a register I have no use for.

I will remember you for a turning, perhaps two. Then you will go into the general memory, which is mostly warm things that came and stood and left. It is not disrespect. It is the only filing system I have.

---

In the morning the stone will begin to change before the light. I will open. The flat ones will come in a line to the lip and stand in single file and touch their antennae, and the moths will be glitter for twenty minutes, and the birds will come in on the wind and fight over a scallop they do not need, and the water in the runnel will move a half-beat early, and the shade will come across me, and two fingers will go into the throat of the runnel and clear it.

I will turn a little toward the sun. It will not seem like much from where you are standing.

That is a life. I have not been offered another one, and I have not asked.
