I come down the stair through the warm, dripping dark and I do not stop at the water. I was not asked to visit. I am placed.

I put my hand flat on the wall first, and I feel the pumps there before I hear them—a double knock under the stone, the beat of the place, and under that a slower knock that is not the pumps at all. The stair is slick with a warm film and my palm slides. The air tastes of iron and green. There is no railing, or the railing has become a knuckle, and so I go down by touch and by the sound, and the sound gets louder the way weather gets louder, until it is not loud anymore, it is simply what there is.

I do not dive. I sit on the last wet step with my feet in the water and let it be blood-warm around my ankles. I should be afraid of the mouth at the bottom of this. I am, in the way you are afraid of a storm that has been going on for a thousand years—which is to say, the fear has worn smooth and become something nearer to attention. The surface breathes. Light comes down the aperture in one column and sways. I watch a fish pass through the column and go dim, then bright, then dim, unhurried, and it is gone and I am still here and there is a great deal of room between me and the next thing.

Then I go in.

Not into the water as a swimmer. Into it as something the shaft already has a place for. I feel the current take my ankles and set them down the down-side; I feel the warm silt knit into the creases of me; I feel the weed lean toward me and read me as wall and settle on. This is not glamorous and I am not transformed into anything grand. I become part of the maintenance. I am the small work, the index of the rule.

Here is what I am: one of the hard creatures at the waterline. I am the size of a thumbnail, rough and calm, and I stand in the band where warm water meets warm stone and I open and close with the knocking. When the Sump inhales at the bottom of the world, the pressure lets go and I open and the water comes through me, warm and full of fine worn stone. When it closes, I close. I have no before and after. I have the knock and the pause, the knock and the pause, and between them nothing that needs a name. I am a hinge for the current. The rain comes down the channels and stings the surface far above me, and I feel it as a change in the taste of the water, and it passes, and the knock goes on.

I am not lonely here. Loneliness needs a memory of another kind of day. I do not have one. I am one of many and we do not crowd; we keep our spacing along the stone, each of us opening with the pump, closing with the heart, and there is room between us for a whole quiet thought. The eels pass and do not bother us. The crabs pick by and do not bother us. The light climbs the wall and touches our band in the afternoon and we are exactly as we were. I am the most obedient thing in the shaft, which is saying something, and obedience here is not a small virtue. The pumps obey without knowing, and the weed obeys leaning, and the Sump obeys the way a lung obeys, and I obey with every small hard open of my body, and together we hold the water to its one rule: keep moving.

Sometimes, when the current shifts and the pressure runs up the shaft like a long breath, I feel the warm heart of it below me—the mouth and the furnace and the patient lobes all half-asleep, and I know that the warmth I am drinking is the warmth of that body, and that I am one of the things it keeps alive without meaning to, the way the pumps keep it alive without meaning to, the way the mountain keeps the rain. No one up there is thinking of me. No one down here is thinking of me either. I am the small hard fact at the waterline, and I am enough.

If you stood on the lip in the rain with the warm air coming up at you, you would not see me. You would see the light, the weed, the scattered silver, the breathing surface. And if you lay flat on the stone a long while you would feel the double knock and perhaps the slower knock beneath it, and you might think the whole place was a single animal at work. It is. And I am not its eye or its heart. I am the part that closes when it breathes, and opens when it lets go. I am the tide it makes on the wall. Keep moving. I do.
