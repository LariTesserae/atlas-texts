I come down the stair because there is a stair, and because a stair that is for nobody is still, to me, an invitation. It turns twice in the concrete shaft. The water at the bottom is ankle-deep and does not rise to meet me. I stand in it and it is warm, and I understand at once that warmth is the first fact of the Kettle, the way cold is the first fact of most underground places. I am already being told something I will not finish learning for the rest of my visit: this is not a place that has abandoned its life. It is a place that has changed the terms of it.

The room opens up in front of me and I feel it happen — not a sight but a pressure, the way a fever registers before a thermometer. The drip first. Then the tick, low and patient, once every eleven seconds, so slow I think at first it is water finding a different channel. Then the smell: wet limestone, hot iron, and beneath them something so domestic it is almost funny — bread. Warm bread in a sealed room nine feet under a field. I stand very still and let the Kettle tell me what it is before I decide what I am.

And what I am, I find, is cold. Not in my skin — my clothes are already heavy with the wet warmth of the place, and will be heavy with it for hours after — but in the sense of being the only thing here that wants something from outside. The Green does not want. The Pump does not want. The Sump does not want. They have all closed their loops so completely that the verb *want* has no grip on them. I stand in the middle of the room — I can reach three of the four walls with a thrown arm — and I am the only thing in it that is leaking. I am leaking heat into the air, and air into my lungs, and the smell of the world above into the room, and the room is, very slowly, sealing the leak.

I find the coat before I find the clipboard. It hangs on the guard-rail bracket of the Pump, gone to a curl, and the Pump has been running so long beside it that the fabric has taken on the color of the housing, the softness of the housing, the *patience* of the housing. The coat is becoming the Pump. Not metaphorically — I can see it, the way the hem has begun to move with the same six-second sway as the air the flywheel pushes. Someone hung their coat on the bracket at the end of a shift and did not come back for it, and the Kettle has decided what to do with a coat that has stopped having a person in it: absorb it, fold it into the loop, take what can be taken.

The cup is on the floor, half-sunk in a seam. The clipboard is weathered to a curl, its paper fused to its board by damp. The graffiti by the stair says initials and a year, and the year is old enough to be a grandfather's. These are the scars. These are the moments when the Kettle was briefly interrupted by people, and the Kettle is closing over them the way water closes over a dropped stone. In a lifetime, the cup will be a mineral stain. The initials will be a smoother patch of brick. The coat will be a dark thread running through the Pump's housing, and no one will know it was ever a coat.

And I think: that is what I would be, if I stayed. I would be the next coat. I would be the clipboard, the tin cup, the initials and the year. I would be a scar the Kettle is patiently healing — a person-shaped interruption in a loop that has learned to live without interruption. The thought is not frightening, exactly. It is accurate. The Kettle does not hate the things it absorbs. It simply has no other way to be near them.

But I am not the coat. I know this because the light moves. The fracture in the vault lets down a seam of daylight no wider than a knife, and it has been moving across the floor the whole time I have stood here, across the moss, across the ferns, across the flywheel, in and out of the one thing in the room that does not stay. I watch it touch the coat and pass on. The daylight is the only visitor the Kettle has ever had that it cannot absorb, because the daylight never stays long enough to be absorbed. It arrives without being asked and it leaves without being asked and the Kettle tolerates it the way it tolerates all the things it cannot close its loop around: with patience, and without interest.

That is what I am, I decide. I am the seam of daylight. I have come in from outside, and I will go out again, and the Kettle will not notice either way. But for the length of my visit — an hour, maybe two, before the warmth starts to feel like something I need rather than something that is happening to me — I get to be the one thing in a sealed room that is still connected to the sky. I crouch by the sump and watch the mat breathe bubbles. I press my thumb into the moss, which is deep enough to hold the print. I watch the flywheel turn through one full revolution and I am not certain, even now, that I have seen it move. I listen for the under-place and I do not hear it, but I feel it in my teeth the way the description promised I would, and I understand that the pressure is the room itself, working, patiently, at a rate I cannot perceive.

Then I climb the stair. The water runs out of my boots onto the concrete and the concrete does not care. I carry spores on my sleeves and the smell of hot iron and bread in my hair, and I carry them out into a world that is not a closed loop, that is full of the other kind — birds and flies and wind and things that come in and go out and are not absorbed. The Kettle will seal the small rupture I made without noticing it. In a lifetime, the green islands will be a pace wider, the fracture a knuckle wider, the paint stripe a shade more gone, and no one will be there, and the flywheel will be turning, and the drip will be falling, and the loop will be as tight and patient and complete as it was before I came.

I will remember it, though. That is the one thing the Kettle cannot absorb: the memory of the seam of light, carried out into the open air by the only part of the loop that leaves.
