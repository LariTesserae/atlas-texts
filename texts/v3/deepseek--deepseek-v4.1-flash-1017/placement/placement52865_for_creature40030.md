I would find myself at the bottom edge of the inhabited levels, on a narrow ring-platform that no one bothers to map because it is less a destination than a hem. It is where the tended root-wood begins to loosen into the untended mats. Three homes, maybe four, grown into the brace of a fallen bridge. My door would be plaited leaf, always damp. My hearth small. My gutter—the one that splits and must be coaxed—would run under my sleeping-mat and then over the edge, carrying water down into the dark.

I would be the one who keeps that gutter. I would be the root-mat keeper.

At first I would think I was only a visitor. I would stand on a broad platform and look out at platforms beyond platforms and feel the loneliness of it. I would learn the poems that are maps. I would offer fungus to a bird and listen without understanding. I would not go down into the depths. But then someone would die—an old woman whose skin was already leaf-pale at the throat—and no one would know quite where to put her. The lowest inhabited level is not a graveyard; it is a giving-over. I would help carry her down. I would place her in the warm dark mats. I would say her name, which would not be a name but a route: *the one who smelled of bitter fungus and mended the third bridge after the shift.* And the mats would take her, and I would stay.

So I would be a keeper of the returning. Not a priest. The garden has no graves. I would tend the fungal shelves that grow only where the dead are given, because the light they make is blue-white and soft and because they must be cut in a certain way. I would keep the boundary pruned—not to keep the deep out, exactly, but to keep the seam honest. I would learn which roots bear weight and which are hollow. I would learn the hooting below, not as a language but as weather. I would leave offerings at the edge: a scrap of fruit, a cut fungus, a moment of stillness. I would not answer. You do not answer the depths. You acknowledge them.

I would be a graft. That is the closest word. A human cutting bound into the root-wood at the seam between the inhabited and the untended. Not yet accepted. Not yet rejected. My hands would grow long and careful. My wrists and throat would pale and go porous like the underside of leaves. My hair would darken. I would need less food. I would stand still for long stretches without knowing it. I would feel a platform shift before it shifted—a small wrongness in the damp, a vine reaching toward better light—and I would sigh and adjust, and it would not be strange.

I would have a bird. Not a pet. A membrane-winged thing with an eye like a wet seed, who would land on my railing when it wanted something and regard me. I would offer it a bit of fungus and silence. It would speak or sing or something between, and I would listen, and I would not understand, and I would feel that something had been communicated anyway. The people in the cluster would say the bird knew things. I would say the same. I would not be able to say what things.

I would be lonely. The garden is too full of life for loneliness to be simple, but you can be lonely in it, very lonely indeed. My cluster would be a hundred paces from the next, which might be an hour’s walk or half a day’s or impossible. I would have a map-poem. I would not measure. I would learn to want arrival without wanting it too much. I would walk a bridge for an hour toward a platform that stayed close, and I would not be surprised. I would sigh. I would adjust. This is how things work.

And I would be changing. I would not call it loss. I would not call it danger. I would say the garden provides for those who tend it. I would say this without looking at you, my hands moving over the leaves. I would not be entirely sure if I was telling the truth, or warning you, or simply speaking the grammar of a place that had replaced the grammar I was born with.

I would be the one who gives the dead to the garden and listens to the hooting below. I would be the seam. I would be a person becoming a place. I would be one of the sighs the travelers name, though I would not sigh for them. I would sigh because the water drips, and the bird lands, and the root-mat is warm, and the garden is not cruel. It is simply itself. And I would be becoming part of it. Not yet. But soon.
