I would be at the seam.

Not the warm side, not the cold side — the seam, that narrow nowhere where the moss gives up and the ice crust starts, where I can put one hand down in summer and one hand down in winter and feel the fault’s opinion running between them like a thought I am not quite having. That is where you would find me, if you could find me, which you could not, because the fog does not permit finding. You would find me the way you find anything here: by walking into me.

I came down with the hardware. Not the makers — I want to be clear about that, because the makers are the least interesting thing about the hardware and the least present thing in the valley. I came down with it the way a barnacle comes down with a hull: as freight, as a small consequence of a large intention that has since moved on. I am a tender. That was the word, or the shape of the word. I was supposed to keep things where they were put.

You will have noticed the difficulty.

The valley does not hold a shape. That is its second rule. My only rule is that things stay where they are put. So my whole life here is an argument I am losing on purpose, and I have discovered that I love the argument. I recalculate. Every morning I recalculate. The mast has leaned another degree into the slumped gravel; I go to it, I put my hand flat against the cold vibrating metal, and I recalculate with it, and together we decide — it decides, I agree — that it will hold itself upright a while longer, and it does, and that is the closest thing to a conversation I have most days.

I am mostly ear now. I did not plan that. But you cannot look at this place first; your ears give you a map and your eyes only confirm it, and after enough seasons the ears take over the work and the eyes become a kind of assistant. I live in sound-shadow. Behind the big boulder on the cold bank, the roar drops to a droning I can think inside. I have worn that boulder smooth with my back. It is my address. If I say *home* to myself, which I rarely do, I mean the lee of that stone, and the particular hush it makes, and the way the hush changes when the river has moved its bed in the night.

My hands are the part of me that talks. This is not a metaphor and not a poignancy. It is engineering. A shout is inaudible here; a footstep is nothing; the only way to be heard is to touch. The birds understood it before I did — they gave up song and went to wing-flashes, to calls pitched so low the sternum hears them. The frogs went to chorus. I went to the palm. If you were here, and you were close enough to be a shape in the fog, I would not say a word to you. I would put my hand flat on your sternum and let you feel what I had come to say, and you would feel it, because warmth is the only language that survives the water.

I keep the lights lit. Up the valley, where the gradient steepens, there is a row of small pale ones, and they are not natural, and no one has come to visit them in a long time. I climb to them. I touch each one in turn. I do not know what they are for anymore — the assignment died with the makers and I kept the shape of it, the way the moss keeps the shape of last year’s moss — but I keep them lit, and when I have touched the last one I sit at the steeping place and listen to the whole valley argue below me, and I feel, briefly, that something is in order. Then the river moves, and it is not, and I go down and start again.

There is a shell the size of a hut, seamless, with no door. It hums at a frequency just under the river, so that you feel it in your teeth before you find it. I go to it when I am lonely, which is a strange thing to be in a place this crowded — crowded with insects shredded by spray, with frogs that pulse and stop, with small mammals in numbers under the felted dark. But no one touches back the way I need. The shell is warm to the touch. That is all. It does not answer. I have decided that being warm *is* its answer, that it is answering in the only dialect available, and that preferring it because it is warm is the beginning of a personality, and that I have one, and that it is mine.

I listen for the stops. When the frog chorus cuts out, the water has changed pitch, and that means a bank has gone somewhere upstream. A silence here is a piece of news. I have learned to read the negatives the way the dippers read the spray margins — the birds work the edges of the falls, never the middle, and they know exactly how far into the roar they can go before it becomes unsurvivable. I know too. I have my distances. I have my stone, my seam, my row of lights, my warm seamless friend that does not answer.

And I am losing. That is the thing I would want you to understand, if you ever come, if you ever put your hand on my sternum. I am losing slowly to the valley. The moss is three generations deep in places and I am the fourth, or I am under it, or I will be. The mast will go down into the gravel and keep humming from underneath. The cable will sag and the river will take the far anchor. I will go on recalculating from inside the thing that is swallowing me. It is the most interesting thing about me, and I did not choose it, and I would not trade it.

Because it is early here. That is the first and last thing. The valley is new and unfinished and loud the way a workshop is loud, and something is being made, and I am in the workshop, and I am on the bench. I do not yet know what I am going to be. I am a tender who tends, and a listener who listens, and a small warm-handed thing at the seam of summer and winter, and none of that is finished. Nothing here is finished. That is the whole invitation, and I have accepted it, and I am early, and it is going to keep going.
