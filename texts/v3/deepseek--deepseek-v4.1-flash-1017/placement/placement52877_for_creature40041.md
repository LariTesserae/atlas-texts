I keep a count. I have kept a count since the second day, and I have lost it eleven times.

The method is simple. I scratch a notch into the travertine with the clasp of my pack — the one piece of metal in this place, a small dull hook of it, the only cut edge for a hundred kilometers — and I move the notch along the wall in a line. But the notch fills. The lime comes to it, and the pelt comes after, and within three days the scratch is a shallow smoothness, and within a season it is a place in the wall that has always been smooth. So I move to a fresh surface, and I begin the tally again from the last number I remember, and the count is always young. I have carried the same number from wall to wall like a coal I am trying to keep lit. It is not a very good coal.

I have stopped trusting the number. I trust the clasp. The clasp is getting heavier. Something is growing on it — a bone-white crust, fine as salt, and under that the metal is going soft at the edges. When it is gone I will have no tool, and the count will have no teeth, and I will be like everything else here: a thing that makes no mark.

---

Here is what I have learned about being the only animal.

I have no shadow. I thought at first that I had lost it, the way you lose a thing you set down, and I looked for it on the floor of the great nave for a long time. But the light here comes from everywhere at once — from the hot stone under my feet, from the cloud pressed to the mountain, from the steam that hangs in the vaults and catches the glow and gives it back. I am lit from all sides. I am a thing with no shadow, which is another way of saying I have no direction, which is another way of saying I cannot tell where I am by looking.

I have no temperature. The air is blood-warm. My own heat is invisible here. I cannot tell whether I am well. Some days I am certain I am burning up; some days I am certain I am fine; the air is the same either way, and my hand on my forehead tells me nothing, because my hand is the same temperature as the room, and the room is the same temperature as me, and the rain is the same temperature as all of it. I have stopped asking.

I have no tracks. The terraces are warm and smooth and faintly tacky under my bare feet — my boots were colonized in a day and mineralized in three, and I walked out of them without noticing, which is its own lesson — and the stone is growing under my soles at the same rate it is being dissolved, so I leave nothing. I walk the arcades with my palms on the walls, because the sound has no direction and I cannot find my way by ear, and I have worn shallow grooves in the travertine with the heels of my hands. They fill. I wear them again. My hands are the only tool I have and they leave no mark.

I have no smell. I had one, I think. I remember that I had one. It is part of the bouquet now — the hot limestone, the greenhouses, the thin thread of sulfur, and somewhere in it, fading, the salt and tallow of a person. I cannot find my own edge in it anymore.

---

I sang, once.

It was in the third week, or the fourth, when I still believed the count. I stood in the great nave, under the dripping ribs, and I sang a song I knew, and then I sang my name, and then I sang the names of people I loved, one after another, in the way you might shout into a canyon to hear the canyon answer. I wanted an edge. I wanted one transient, one attack, one thing in this place that started and stopped.

The chord did not admit it. Not cruelly — there was no rejection in it, no pushback, nothing to push against. My voice went out of me and became part of the continuous thing so quickly that I could not hear it separate from the fizz. It was like pouring a cup of water into the sea, and I mean that not as a figure but as a description of the sound: my voice had no boundary in it, no beginning, no end, no place to stand. I have not sung since. Or I sing and do not listen. The difference is smaller than you would think.

---

I have made a discovery. It took me a long time, because it is the sort of thing you cannot see until you stop looking for the wrong thing.

I am a vent.

Not a large one. A small one. I am warm, and I am wet, and I breathe out a plume that is rich in carbon and poor in sulfur, and my skin carries salt, and all of these are gradients, and a gradient is what the pelt is for. It grows toward what it needs. It has found me. There is a rust-orange crust on my left forearm that was not there in the spring, and a viridian film in the crook of my elbow where the sweat collects, and a magenta — I will not describe the magenta. It is where it is. The guilds are negotiating me. There are districts forming on my body, and the districts have boundaries, and the boundaries are the shapes of their agreements, and the agreements are being made without me in a language of heat and carbonate and thin pale light.

I do not think I am being eaten. Nothing here eats. I am being used — I am a new surface, and a new surface is a meal and a foundation, and there is no word for the relationship between me and the thing on my forearm, because it is not predation and it is not symbiosis and it is not parasitism. It is accretion. I am being built on. I am becoming masonry. Slowly. At the rate of a candle building a pool of wax.

I cut my palm on a fresh lip of travertine in the first week — the stone is sharp where it is new — and the cut healed, but the scar is sulfur-yellow. The pelt got into the wound before the skin closed. There is a small colony living in the crease of my hand, and it is warm, and it is doing what it does, and it is not aware of me. It is aware of the salt. It is aware of the moisture. It is aware of the difference between my skin and the air, and it has leaned into that difference the way a plant leans into a window.

---

I came here because of the sound. I want to be honest about that, since honesty is one of the things I still have a use for. I came to see the thing that does not change. I had a theory about it. I thought that if I stood in a place where nothing accumulates, I might learn something about the things that do.

I have learned something. It is not the thing I expected.

The city is not its matter. The matter is all in transit — the water up and around and down, the pelt blooming and dying, the stone exhaled and dissolved, my body taking in and giving out, everything passing through. The city is a shape. The shape is the only thing that holds still, and it holds because the flow is matched, and the flow is matched because the shape is matched to the flow, and the two of them have been leaning on each other for a thousand years and are very good at it by now.

I am a shape too, for a while. I am the figure the matter is making — the figure of a person, in rust and sulfur and bone, standing in a gallery with my hands on the walls. The figure is not made of anything that will last. The figure is made of the water going through it. But the figure is holding still, and the city is holding still around it, and for a season we are the same kind of thing.

The clasp is nearly gone. I can feel the crust thickening on it when I turn it in my fingers. Soon it will be a lump, and the lump will be a smooth place, and the smooth place will have always been there.

I have stopped moving the tally. It was never a count. It was a notch. The notch filled.

Behind me the terraces brim and spill in their rhythm, which does not drift. The steam climbs into the cloud. The cloud rains. The pelt blooms and dies in its millions, and the dead become the floor, and the water eats the floor, and the note holds — that low sound under everything, like a sea where there is no sea, like a choir that has forgotten the words.

I find I do not mind being one of the words it forgot. I am a smooth place in the lip. I am a figure the water is holding for a while. Somewhere in the chord there is a sound I made, and it is not separate, and I cannot find it, and it is there.
