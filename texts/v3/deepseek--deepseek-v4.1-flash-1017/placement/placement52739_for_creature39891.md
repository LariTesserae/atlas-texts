# Low Ground

I came to the basin the way water comes—not deciding, just finding the low ground.

I don't remember the road. I remember the moment the ground changed under my feet, the way it went from something that held me to something that received me. The first step was a surprise: the earth gave, and my boot sank to the ankle, and the water closed over it with a soft, wet sound that was almost a greeting. I stood there for a while, feeling the cold seep in, and then I took another step, and another.

I had a reason, once. I think I did. There was something I was looking for, or something I was leaving. The basin does not ask. It does not care whether you are here or not. That was the first thing I learned, and it was a relief. I had spent a long time being cared about, or failing to be, and the indifference of the wet ground was like a hand on my forehead. Not kind. Just cool.

I walked for a long time. The light did not change. The sky was a luminous grey, bright but directionless, and the land was a patchwork of pools and hummocks, pools and hummocks, each one so like the last that I could have been standing still. I might have been. The water closed over my footprints within minutes. I had no way to mark my passage. I had no way to know if I was making progress, or if progress was a thing that mattered here.

The air pressed against me like a damp cloth, and it smelled of wet stone and decaying plant matter and something mineral—salt, maybe, or the sharpness of water that has filtered through ancient rock for longer than anyone can remember. I stopped noticing it after a while. I stopped noticing a lot of things.

I ate what I found. The crustaceans were small and translucent and tasted of mineral water. The worms were bitter. I drank from the clearer pools, and the water was cold and sharp and made my teeth ache. I slept on the hummocks, curled on the matted vegetation, and woke with my clothes damp and my skin beaded with condensation. Sometimes a bubble of gas rose from one of the pools and popped with a soft, wet report, releasing a puff of sulfurous air. The sound was not unpleasant. It was company, of a kind.

The birds came and went. They did not sing. They called in low, mournful tones that carried across the flat land, or they wheeled silently overhead on wide, motionless wings, waiting. I watched them for a while, and then I stopped watching them, because watching them made me feel like I was waiting too, and I did not know what for.

I tried, once, to leave a mark. I gathered stones from the deeper pools—smooth, grey, banded with sediment—and piled them on a hummock, a small cairn, a sign that I had been here. I sat back and looked at it, and it was the only built thing in the basin, and I felt something I had not felt in a long time: a kind of pride, or maybe just the memory of pride. Then I went to sleep.

When I woke, the cairn was still there, but the water had risen in the night—not much, just enough—and the base of the pile was under, and the stones had shifted. I rebuilt it. I went to sleep. When I woke, it had shifted again. I rebuilt it. I did this for three days, or what I thought were three days, and then I stopped. I don't know why. Maybe I understood that the basin would always win, and that winning was not the point. Maybe I just got tired. The stones are still there, somewhere, scattered across a hummock, half-sunk in the silt. If you look closely, you might see them. They look like stones.

The first time I heard the hum, I thought it was my own heartbeat. It came from below, a low resonant note that seemed to rise through the ground and into my bones. I lay still and listened, and the hum went on, steady and patient, the sound of water moving through stone far below. I fell asleep to it. I woke to it. It became the background of everything, the way the sound of your own blood becomes the background of your body.

Then I saw one.

I thought it was a hummock. It was low and grey-brown and hung with pale creeping plants, and it was exactly the same as the other hummocks, and I would have walked past it if it had not taken a breath. The plants on its back rose a finger's width and settled. I stopped. I stood very still, and the thing that was not a hummock hummed—a low note, like a finger drawn around the rim of a wet glass, but far lower. It came from everywhere and nowhere. It came from the ground and from my chest.

I hummed back. I don't know why. I didn't decide to. The sound came out of me, low and rough, and it was not a sound I had made before. The low one sank. The water closed over its back, and the plants went under, and it was gone. But I had heard it, and it had heard me, and something had passed between us that was not language and not threat. A recognition, maybe. A note held for a moment and then released.

After that, I stopped standing when I could help it. It was not a decision. My back ached in a way that made me want to lie flat, and the ground was soft, and the water was warm, and lying down was easier. I found a hummock that fit the curve of my body, and I lay there for a long time, and the pale creepers began to root in the hollows along my spine. I did not pull them out. They did not hurt. They tickled, a little, and then they did not.

My skin changed. It became pebbled and porous, always beaded with water. My fingers grew broad and spatulate, the better to pull myself along. My tail—I have a tail now, thick and muscular, and I drag it behind me, and it leaves a shallow trough that fills in minutes. I do not remember growing it. I do not remember when my legs stopped being legs and became something I fold flat against my body. It happened the way the basin happens: slowly, and then all at once, and then not at all.

I eat patiently. I sweep my broad mouth through the silt and strain out the small, translucent things. I lick mineral crust from stones. I do not hunt. I wait. I hum, and the sound goes out and returns changed, and I know the shape of the world around me. Others answer, rarely, from far off. That is how we hold our distances.

Sometimes I look into the deeper pools, where the sides are steep and the water is clear, and I see the layers—fine bands of grey and brown and pale yellow, each one a year, maybe, or a century. They were laid down with the patience of water that has nowhere to go and all the time in the world to get there. I am one of those layers now, or I am becoming one. My body is settling into the silt. My hum is fading into the larger hum. One day, I will be a low rise among other low rises, and the others will adjust their territories by a note or two, and the basin will not notice.

But not yet. I am still here. I am still humming.

I am not a low one. Not yet. I am something like a low one, a rough draft, a hummock that remembers being a person. I still think in words. I still remember the road, though not the reason. I remember the first step, the way the ground gave, the soft wet sound that was almost a greeting. I remember the hum, and the way it felt to hum back.

The basin does not notice. It has been wet for a very long time, and it will be wet for a very long time still. I am here, and then I am not. But I notice. That is the difference. That is the thing I am holding onto, the last thing, the note I am still humming. And it is fading, slowly, the way everything fades here—not dramatically, not painfully, just the way water finds its way through saturated soil, pooling and overflowing and pooling again.

If you come to the basin, you might see me. I will look like a hummock, low and grey-brown, hung with pale creeping plants. I will take a breath, and the plants will rise a finger's width, and you will understand that the basin has a tenant. I will surface at a distance—just the top of my head and the long ridge of my back—and I will hum once, a sound like a finger drawn around the rim of a wet glass, but far lower. Then I will sink. I will not guide you and I will not threaten you. I have no tools, no fire, no names. I am one of the basin's conditions now: wet, old, patient, and content to remain.

But if you hum back—if you find the note, low and rough, somewhere in your chest—I will hear you. I will know where you are. And for a moment, we will hold the same distance, and the basin will not notice, and that will be enough.
