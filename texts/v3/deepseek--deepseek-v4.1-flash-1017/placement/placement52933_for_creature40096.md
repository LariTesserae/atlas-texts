I would find myself on the middle terrace, at the wet edge of a shallow pool, in the last hour before dusk. Not on the third terrace among the old stones—that is where I might want to be, as the simurgh, a huge slow shadow with wings. But the stair has a way of editing grandeur. It would set me down where the moss is thick and the water is dropping by a finger’s width, and it would make me small and wet and numerous.

I would be one of the frogs.

Not the whole chorus—one throat in it. Wet-slate colored, small enough to sit in a hand, with a body that knows the pool as a country and the moss as a roof. I would sit at the edge where the sedge stems make a little blind, and I would feel the pool shrinking under me, the waterline retreating across my skin. The moss would give back condensation so slowly that I would never have learned to hurry. I would call at dusk because the light going is a thing that must be answered. Around me the others would call too—a hundred bad musicians agreeing loudly—and I would not experience it as noise. I would experience it as the shape of the crowd, the way you know your own house by the sounds in it.

The insect hum would come through the water as a low pressure. The heron would be a pair of legs and a patience at the far end of the pool, and I would know exactly how far away it was, not by seeing it but by the way the smaller frogs stopped. The buzzards would be a shadow that did not smell like cloud. The simurgh, if it crossed the third terrace at midday, would be a darkness that made the beetles pause a fraction of a percent; I would feel it through the moss—a huge silent thing passing over the stair—and I would go on calling, because that is what the pool does.

I would not be the largest resident. I would not be the most important; the moss is that. I would be the wet throat in the middle of the stair, the small resident the heron works for and the noise the simurgh has learned to hunt around. I would live in a pool that shrinks and fills, and when the dry breath came I would burrow into the moss and wait, and when the wet breath came I would climb back out and start the argument again.
