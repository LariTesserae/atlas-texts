I would begin as a taste. Iron, ozone, the sweet-sour of warm algae in slurry. Then the taste would gather a pressure around itself, and the pressure would remember it had limbs.

I would find myself low in the fused mass, in the warm seam between the fourth and fifth tiers, where the city bends its shoulder around a storm vent. My pressure-pocket would be a cousin to a cyst: walls of dark, glassy growth, a floor that is a living mat, a ceiling that pulses amber. I would be one of the six-limbed gardeners—slate hide patched with translucent film, gill-plumes never still, bead eyes set low in a blunt shield of a head. Two of my limbs would be gripping fans. One edge of the left fan would already be fused to the doorframe. Not death yet. Just the city holding my hand.

My name would be a chord: hot iron, wet resin, storm coming. The wall that absorbed my larval spat gave me the first note; my household amended it twice. I would be a vein-singer—one of those who coax the city’s conduits to bend around a new nursery, who prune the crystalline lattices when they grow greedy through a pressure vein, who scrape mineral crust until the nano-fluid pulses amber again. I would herd iridescent arthropods with pheromone pulses and milk bioluminescent algae from the canals. My tools would be grown, not made: living clamps, crystalline wedges, mats of photosensitive film folded like wet cloth. When I needed the city to do something, I would not command it. I would sing to it in pressure and chemistry, and a vein would brighten under my manipulators.

I would speak in two streams at once. Clicks and bladder-notes pitched under the world-roar, and a scent that sweats through my skin. To lie would be difficult. My body would leak the truth.

I would have a wall-kin in the north wall of our pocket—an elder half-settled, limbs thickened, hide exuding resin, voice mostly chemical now. I would press my flank to her and feel her answer as a slow warmth along the pressure-pores of my spine. She would not be dead. She would be becoming more wall. When I groomed her gill-fringes, she would release the scent I have known since my larval drift: safe, slow, slightly bitter. In the walls, the old ones still answer for a while. Then the city is a little more itself.

I would not fight storms. When the pressure fell and the great weather engine began to turn, I would seal the pocket, open the vents, and let the roar run through the architecture and through me. Sometimes I would ride a colossal jellyfish-construct up into the upper tiers, clinging to the drifting bell while the warm rain hammered down. I would not be brave. I would be buoyant. The storm would be another tide in a world of tides.

If you tried to isolate me, you would not get one thing. You would get a colony: symbionts in my blood, city-fluid in my joints, kin-scent in my skin. I would be restless, tasting the air, listening with my flank, already half-fused to the wall I touched. Solitude would not translate. The nearest word would be *untethered*, or *leaking*. A sudden absence of the roar would feel like death.

So I would be a cultivator in the seam between tiers, a storm-rider, a chemical chord in a pressure-pocket, a temporary eddy in the warm current. I would be one more process the city is running—and when I slowed, I would settle into the wall and keep answering for as long as I could.
