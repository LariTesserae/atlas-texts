I came in with a slate.

That is the first thing to say, because everything else follows from it. I came over the shoulder of new rock with a slate and a stub of chalk and a commission from people who were not here and had never been here, and who believed — I can say it plainly now — that the works were finished. *Count the coils. Note the condition. Assess the metal.* There was a word in the commission, and the word was *salvage*, and I carried it up the scree without once hearing how it sounded.

Then I topped the ridge and the light went down into the valley and came back up, and the wood below me was brighter than the meadow I stood in, green-gold and moving slightly, a second sky. I remember thinking: *that is a thing I will have to describe.* I had a whole vocabulary for it. I was already writing the report in my head — the downcut channel, the fracture planes, the verdigris, the condensation field intact and running, *no combustion, no engine, gravity-fed,* and I was pleased with myself, and the chalk was soft in my pocket from the wet before I had gone a hundred yards.

---

You cannot write in the Bright Hollow. That is the first thing the place teaches, and it teaches it without malice, the way water teaches stone. The slate beads over. The chalk will not mark. You wait for a dry moment, and there is no dry moment — the air is doing the wetting, continuously, the same way it wets your hands, your hair, the inside of your collar. I stood there for a while with the chalk in my fist, waiting, and the waiting was the beginning of the undoing, because a person who is waiting is a person who is looking, and once I started looking I stopped counting.

I do not remember deciding to stop. It happened at the pace of the place, which is to say it had already happened before I noticed.

I found the pump-house first, roof caved inward, the interior full of fern — great black-stemmed things unrolling in the dark like slow green thoughts. I found the gallery in the eastern wall, water sheeting down its lintel in a film so even it looked deliberate. I found the towers, four of them, the cable still taut between, and I put my hand on the steel and felt it humming, faintly, and I wrote — no, I did not write, I *said* aloud, to no one, "It's still running," and the moss took the words within a few meters and gave me back a denser silence.

Then the coil field. Dozens of them, mossed, on their concrete pads. And this is where the counting failed. Not because there were too many. Because each coil has a ring of moss beneath it sized to that coil's own rate, and the rates do not agree. They are regular and not mechanical; they loosen and tighten with the light and the wet, and so the rings are all different sizes, and a number is a thing that agrees with itself, and the field does not agree with itself. It keeps time loosely. In counterpoint. My counting was a kind of shouting in a place built for listening.

I sat down on a pad and put the slate in my lap and did not open it.

---

It was the second day, or the third, that I mistook one for a post.

It stood at the lip of a catchment channel with its palms turned up, and I had walked past it twice already, and the third time it turned its head. By degrees. Like a fern unfurling. It was short and broad and very still, its skin the color of wet bark, the creases of its wrists bloomed copper-green where the runoff gathers, its hair a close mat of moss and pale lichen beaded with condensation. Its eyes were fog-colored and slow to focus.

It was not surprised. That is the thing I keep returning to. It had no surprise in it at all. It held out one broad hand, palm up, and waited, and I understood that I was meant to wait too, so I did. The drip came. It landed in the cup of the palm and beaded there and did not run off, and the tender looked at it, and nodded, as if I had said something correct.

I had said nothing. I had only waited the right length of time.

---

I know three coils now by their note. I know that the field loosens before rain and tightens after the fog lifts. I know the route along the eastern catchment by the sink of it underfoot, and I know which grates silt first. My palms face up when I walk. I did not decide this either; it is how water is caught and read, and once you know that, the hands do it on their own.

The slate is somewhere. The chalk has gone to paste and dried and gone to paste again. I do not remember the last time I tried to mark it, and the commission is a set of words in a pocket I no longer reach into, and *salvage* is a word from a language I am slowly forgetting how to speak.

I am not one of them. I want to be honest about that. My skin has not gone to bark, my nails are not ridged like mussel shells, and when they tap a message on the wet stone I catch only the rhythm and not the sense. But I am no longer the surveyor, either. I am something in between, and the in-between has a shape, and the shape is an interval. If you ask my name I will tell you it is *the long pause after the fog lifts,* or *the quickening before rain,* and I am still learning which. They do not correct me. They do not need to. The valley is instrument enough.

---

The great pale moth works a fern at the edge of the field, and I let it. It is the only thing here that moves without our permission, and I have learned — I use the word deliberately, it is the only word that fits — I have learned to let that be.

If you stay long enough you will mistake me for a mossed post. I will not be surprised. I will turn my head by degrees, and hold out my hand, palm up, and wait for a drip to land. Then I will nod, as if you had said something correct, and go back to listening.
