I am crouched at the lip of a basin on the middle slope, one palm flat against the stone, and I am listening with my hand. This is how we hear the water best: not with the ear, which the heavy air fills and blunts, but through the pads of the palm, where the skin has grown thick and sensitive as a tongue. The trickle arrives. It is thin, no wider than a cord, and it comes down the channel called Nine Steps in a way I do not like—too fast at the third turn, pooling where it should run. I will bring the wedges tomorrow. Or the day after. The water is patient and so am I.

That is where you would find me, if you came looking: on the middle slope at the hour when the diffuse light goes grey and the stone seems to breathe out its wetness, crouched like a shrub, my hair bound up in fiber so it does not drip into my eyes. You would not see me at first. We are shaped for not being seen—low, broad, dark as wet basalt—and the plateau does not announce its tenants.

But who I am is a longer answer, and it has taken me a long time to learn it.

I am of the household that keeps Nine Steps and the two basins below it, and that is a good reach; my mother's mother kept it, and her mother before. We are not wealthy—wealth is a dry sleeping shelf and a moss that returns every year—but we are not poor. Nine Steps runs. That is most of what anyone needs to know about a person here.

Yet I have always been the one who listens to the channels that do not run.

There is a channel above the town, in the upper ridges, that the old people still call Long Throat, though no water has passed through it in the memory of anyone living. It is a groove in the black stone, sharp-edged, dry in the way that nothing here is ever truly dry, and when you put your palm to its floor you feel the ghost of a direction, the small worn smoothness that water leaves even after it has gone. I found it as a child, following a seep that failed, and I have gone back to it all my life. I know eleven such channels now. I keep their names the way other households keep their basins. I can tell you where each one begins and where it meant to go, and what the stone did to turn it aside, and where the water went instead.

This is not a useful skill. You cannot drink a name. My brother, who is practical in the way that the sounder households are practical, has told me so, gently, in the low voice we use for disagreement, which is barely a voice at all. But the old ones do not think it is useless. When there is a council, and the question is where a new cut should go, they turn to me. *Where did the water try before?* they ask. *Which way did the stone refuse?* Because the plateau is young, and its fractures are fresh, and the water is still learning its way out—and a channel that died is a lesson the stone has already taught. I am not an engineer of the living water. I am a rememberer of the dead. In a place where nothing changes, memory is the only map of change.

So you would find me, in the end, between two kinds of listening. By day I crouch at Nine Steps and feel the trickle and plan the wedges. In the grey hour I climb to Long Throat and lay my hand in the dry groove and tell it what it was.

I should say that I am not sad about this. We are not a sad people; we have no word for the particular silence after a drip, only for its length. But there is a shape in me that has no name, and I have come to think it is the shape of the water that never came. I leave pale flowers at the springs that still run. At Long Throat I leave nothing, because there is nothing there to receive it, and that is the point—I go anyway, and I put my hand in the groove, and I say the name aloud, low, so that the heavy air can swallow it. It is a small, stubborn act. It is the only thing on this plateau that I do that does not keep something moving.

I do not think I am lonely. We do not think of ourselves that way; there is nothing to miss, no flocks, no birds, no beasts in our stories. But I have begun to suspect that the thing I feel at Long Throat is the loneliness the others have not named—the held breath of a world that has paused, waiting for something to arrive or depart, and I have appointed myself the one who waits at the dry door for it. It is not a grand post. It is a woman on her knees in a groove of black stone with her palm flat, murmuring a name to the water that is not there.

But it is a life, and it is mine, and when the council asks *which way did the stone refuse?* I am the one who knows, and the new cut runs clean, and Nine Steps trickles a little more truly down to the lower basin. And then I climb back up to Long Throat, in the grey hour, and listen with my hand.
