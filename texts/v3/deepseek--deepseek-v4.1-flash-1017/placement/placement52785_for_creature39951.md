I would be a stonemason's apprentice in the Third Warm District, where the steam from the condenser-engines keeps the film thick and the shell harvest rich, and where I'd have been born — like most children — because soft water means clear chests and the warm walls dry just enough on the inner face to keep an infant breathing.

I would stand at dawn on a spillway stair with my hat off and my bare head gleaming, listening. Not to the water — the water is furniture — but underneath it, to the note of the hall I've lived my whole life beside, the Fourth Condenser, and I would know before the tuners made their round that the third plate array is going thin. I wouldn't say so, because apprentices don't, and because a tuner would have to confirm it anyway with their clay tube and their tile stylus and their terrible patience. But I'd know. Everyone knows. It's how I'd been taught to know anything: sit, be still, tell me what the wall is saying.

My hands would give me away before my face. Broad-knuckled, nails gone thick as horn, palms that have not been dry since I was pulled wet and howling into a room that smelled of hot metal and mineral silt. When I'm old they'll say I went grey early — Third District water is hard — and they'll read my hat and see a hundred patch-glazes in four greys, which means I've mended it every season for fifty years, which means I've been neither rich nor idle.

The masonry is what I'd be for. I'd have come up through the shell harvest like everyone: brushing the flat slow armored things off the damp walls into baskets by the million, cracking them, grinding them, learning the grain of a district the way a farmer learns a field. But I'd have been given to the masons because I could hear which walls would take and which would spall, and a mason who can hear that is worth three who can't. I'd spend my life re-facing, re-glazing, re-shelling the same four hundred meters of wall, and I would not find that strange, because a district can be a century into a repair and nobody remarks on it.

At dusk I'd stand with the others on the high stair and watch the swarms come up and the light go silver, and down in the canal a big shell-backed animal would hand its low organ-note to the next district and the next, and the tuning would hold steady underneath it, and I'd feel the wall I'd patched that day settle a fraction of a millimeter under my palm, taking its weight, making more of itself. And in eighty years they'd fire me and set my name in colored tile beside the wall I went into, and some child on the way past would touch it, and that would be the whole of it. Not grim. Ordinary. The most ordinary thing about the place.

And sometimes, in a lower gallery of the Ninth District on an errand I didn't need to run, I'd walk past a bare patch of stone three meters by one, dry and dead, ringed with a low parapet of new shell someone keeps swept, and I'd not look at it directly, and I'd not talk about it, and I'd go home and tap my knuckles on the railing all the way down — *I am here and the water is where it should be* — and mean it more than usual.
