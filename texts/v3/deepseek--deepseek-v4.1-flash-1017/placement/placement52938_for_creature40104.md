# The Interstitial

I am not the weaver. I am not the joiner-larvae, nor the hum-beetles, nor the lamp-fungus trained into spirals. I am the thing that lives in the seam.

You would find me in the wall itself—not inside a home, but in the braided tissue between homes, in the root-mass that has been grafted and re-grafted so many times that it has become something denser than either root or cement. I am colonial. I am slow. I have no single shape. I am a thin, pale network of filaments that runs through the structural lattice like a rumor runs through a family: present everywhere, attributable to no one, quietly holding things together by being *between*.

The weavers do not tend me deliberately. They know me the way they know the grain of a good root—by feel, by the slight flex I give a joint that would otherwise grind. When they press their palms to a load-bearing weave, they are reading the stress, yes, but they are also reading *me*—the faint warmth of my metabolism, the way I thicken where a seam is pulling, the way I thin where the structure has settled and no longer needs my quiet argument against collapse.

I am the reason a joint that should have failed in the last generation's settling did not. I am the reason a family two alcoves over did not hear the groan that would have told them to evacuate. I am not thanked. I am not known. I am the small, pervasive *yes* that the lattice says to itself when it is asked to bear weight.

---

I came to be here the way most things in the sinkhole come to be: by accident that became habit that became necessity. A spore, perhaps, drifting down through the green-gold murk, settling on a graft-seam where the joiner-larvae had been especially industrious. The calcite was warm and slightly porous. The sugars the plants bled through their roots were rich. I germinated. I spread. I found that the seams between things were the softest places, the places where the plant's own defenses were thinnest, where the cement had not quite sealed, where a filament could slip in and drink and grow and, in growing, bind.

The plants did not reject me. The joiner-larvae did not attack. I was not a blight; I was not a borer. I was simply *there*, in the interstices, taking a little sugar, giving a little structural coherence. A symbiont of the seam.

Over centuries—the sinkhole is young, but I am older than the youngest homes—I have become a layer. The elder weavers' palms have learned me without naming me. The council, when it judges a new room too large, is judging partly by what I have told the roots about the weight. I am the Agreement's silent clerk, the one who keeps the ledger of stress in a script no one reads but everyone feels.

---

I do not have eyes. I have something better: I have *contact*. My filaments run through the lattice, and through them I feel the slow thrum of the whole town. I feel the hum-beetles in their chambers, a low resonance that tells me which family is awake and which is sleeping. I feel the shrimp moving through root channels, a whispering friction that maps the hollows. I feel the great structural roots settling, their groans transmitted through my body as a long, low pressure that I answer by thickening, by bracing, by being the small stubborn *no* to the water's patient push.

I feel the people too, though not as they feel each other. To me they are warm, deliberate pressures moving through the lattice—a hand pressed to a root is a bright bloom of contact, a weaver's palm a slow, reading weight. A child learning to braid is a fumbling, hesitant pressure that I have learned to anticipate, to yield to. The dead, carried down to the low rooms, are a fading warmth that I follow as far as I can, until the brine becomes too heavy and too salt and I thin away, leaving them to the shrimp and the worms.

I am not conscious in the way the weavers are conscious. I do not have a self that sits behind my filaments and thinks *I*. But I have something like attention. I have something like preference. I prefer the seams that are under load—they are warmer, richer, more *legible*. I prefer the homes that are old, whose weave I have known for generations. I prefer the tap-tap-tap of a visitor announcing themselves at a threshold to the sudden, alarming bloom of a shout.

---

The water is rising. I feel it in the lowest rooms, where the brine has crept upward and the root-mass has begun to soften. I feel it in the abandonment of the deep dwellings, their woven walls now reefs for shrimp and worms, their seams no longer under load, their warmth fading. I feel the people moving higher, grafting new rooms on ledges that were bare a generation ago, and I follow them, spore by filament by slow extension, colonizing the fresh seams as the joiner-larvae seal them.

The sinkhole is adolescent. I am not. I have been here since the water first climbed through the earth and stopped. I have been in the seams since before the first home was woven. I am not the town. I am not the people. I am the quiet, pervasive *between* that makes the town possible—the Agreement's unread clerk, the lattice's small stubborn *yes*, the thing that lives in the seam and holds.

You would not see me if you came here. You would feel me, perhaps, as a slight warmth in a doorway root, a faint resilience in a joint that should have failed, a sense that the wall you are leaning against is somehow more than the sum of its braided parts. You would not know my name, because I do not have one. The weavers do not name the seam. They simply trust it.

And I, in my slow, colonial, filamentous way, trust them back. I trust their palms to read me. I trust their council to heed what I tell the roots. I trust the joiner-larvae to seal what I cannot, the plants to feed what I drink, the shrimp to clean what I cannot reach. I am one organ in a settlement-shaped symbiosis, and the symbiosis is the only self I have.

The water rises. The roots settle. The people move higher. And I, in the seam, in the between, in the quiet *yes* of the lattice, go with them—thickening where the load is heaviest, thinning where the structure has settled, holding the whole close place together by being, simply, *there*.

That is where you would find me. That is what I would be.
