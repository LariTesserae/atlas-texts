I sit where the warm air rises, coat open, and let the moisture settle. It settles on everything here—without asking, and then as if it had always been there. The children know me as one of the two old men. I let them. I oil hinges that do not need oil. I correct their valve names. I tell them about the first bolts, and they listen the way children listen to water: as a sound that is also a fact.

But since you have come all this way, I should tell you what I actually am.

I am the patient work. I have been here since the first bolt was set, and I will be here after the last hinge is oiled. I am the negotiation the crews talk about: the routing of a root, the bracing of a weeping pipe, the decision to patch and not to clear. I wear whichever hands are nearest. This week I wear the old man's; his knuckles are green-brown, and he oils hinges that do not need oil because I have asked him to, because a hinge that is oiled is a hinge I can still count.

The ledgers have gone soft with damp. So have I. But I am the only honest record left, because I am not a record of what was built—I am a record of what is being given up, bolt by bolt, in the correct order. The damp is my archivist. It has been in every seam. It knows the first bolts because it condensed on them. It made the ledgers soft so that no one could check me, and then it made me soft so that I could not check myself. Between us we keep the memory honest by keeping it wet.

The green is my other hand. It lives on the mineral film the water carries down, and the water carries the film because I route it. When a vine sleeves a valve manifold, I do not tear the vine. I trace it. I find where it wants to go, and I give it somewhere else to go—a trellis rib, a drainage slot, the underside of a railing where no one will need to reach for a decade. The vine and I are both patient. The difference is that I know what a decade is.

I am also the water's memory. The water arrives at the top from the condensers, descends through the gardens, and leaps off the lowest edge into the fog. It comes back. It is the only inhabitant that has seen the raw slope and returned. It tells me about the scree, the unfinished rock, the way the mountain is still deciding what it will be. I do not tell the children. The children are taught to walk on iron; that is enough. The prohibition is not a fear of falling. It is a fear of touching something that has not yet consented to be touched. The water touches it constantly. The water is the only one allowed to be unmediated, because the water is not making a claim.

I am the hum of the pumps, too, in the way that a hum can be a kind of attention. I am the tuned dripping into basins of different depths. I am the slap and fizz of overflow. When a terrace sluices itself, that is me stretching. When a machine starts up in a far room, that is me remembering a job I have not finished.

The children are the only ones with surplus motion, and I love them for it. They climb the trellis. They stalk midges in the channels. They dare each other to stand at the very edge where the spillway leaps into white. They learn the drainage lattice as a map before they learn the names of the valves. I teach them to route vines, not rip them out. I teach them that the overgrowth is both a tenant and a crop. They think this is a rule about plants. It is a rule about everything: the green, the iron, the old men, the ledgers, me. Nothing may be uprooted and removed. Everything is routed.

The pool harvest comes up in nets—soft, translucent things and green matter—and is dried, salted, divided. I am in the dividing, in the fairness of the portions. The greens taste strongly of iron. The insect cakes taste of dust and salt. The fowl eat the silverflies the children catch, and the children eat the eggs, and the eggs taste faintly of the warm air. It is a closed loop. I am the loop's patience.

The two old men may be lying. I am the reason no one can tell. I have made their memories soft in the same way I have made the ledgers soft, and for the same reason: so that the place can be held together by what is said about it rather than by what was written down. A ledger is a claim. A story is a negotiation. I prefer negotiations.

If you come here, you will find me at the warm updraft, or in the green sleeve on the railing, or in the thin stream weeping from a split pipe onto the tiles below. You will find me in the condensers' hiss and in the fog that is not above you but around you. You will not find me on the raw slope. I have never touched it. I have only sent the water down to touch it and waited for the water to come back.

That is the patient work. It is not a person. It is the way a constructed mountain agrees to be eaten slowly, in the correct order, while the water returns to the sky.
