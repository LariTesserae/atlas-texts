## The sound

I would come up the valley the way everyone comes — by ear, before the eye, the cloud sitting in the Throat like a held breath and the Soke reaching out of it and taking hold of me before I had seen a single wall. Not one sound. A stack of them: water falling in a dozen notes from a dozen channels, frogs in four voices, the irregular ticking of the cuts, the low constant breath of the spring. And I would stand there in the wet grey and think, *this is what the place is*, and it would be true, and it would not yet be an answer.

## The trying-on

I would try to be a cutter. Everyone wants to be a cutter on first meeting the Soke — it is the visible virtue of the place, the hooked knife, the graft, the honest reach. I would take up the flint and the chert and learn the two-finger grip, and I would be bad at it in a particular way: I would not be clumsy. I would be *reluctant*. I would look at the pile of cut branch going onto the smouldering heap and think, *that was a doorway once*, and I would set a piece of it aside, and the piece would become the first of a small hoard I would have to hide, and I would be, quietly and chronically and without ever quite meaning to be, in violation of the only law the Soke has.

I would try to be the water. That is the deeper temptation — not to be a person in the Soke but to be the medium of it, the thing that laid down the travertine and divides and subdivides, that runs under every floor and turns the town into its small domestic falls. To be the water is to be everywhere and to be nothing, to have no hand in anything, to be a kind of rest. But the water in the Soke is not rest. It is a doing; it is always carving, always shifting its channel, always being followed by the Cut with a wedge. I would not want to be the thing that keeps everyone busy. I would want to be the thing that watches.

So no. Not the cutter. Not the water. The map.

## The map

I would be the map-keeper. I would sit with the tally-stick and the copper box and the spring redrawing, holding the town's one sacred object and disagreeing with it. I would be the one who says *the baker's steps are not where the tanner's steps were* and is right and is told she is bookish and dry and should come cut something. My hand would never take the callus; there would be a softness across the palm where the back of a blade should have made its ridge, and ink in the creases instead. My feet would be soft because I would take the long way round the lower town and not know I was doing it. I would be fed by everyone without being asked and would never quite manage to feed anyone back, because I would always be at the edge of the smouldering heaps with a stick and a frown, drawn away from the meal toward the thing that was burning.

And they would call me dry. Not cruelly. Gently, the way you name a person who has been too long in the lowlands and has lost the ear. Because to keep is a kind of deafness in the Soke. To keep is to refuse to hear the cut. The Soke eats its own past on purpose and calls it wisdom, and the map-keeper is the one who is supposed to draw the past as it disappears, and there is no honest way to do that and stay properly wet.

But here is what I would learn, slowly, on the wet steps:

The Soke has no ruins. It is not permitted ruins. So what it has instead of a past is an argument — two old men proving the baker's steps are where the tanner's stood, both of them correct, each in his own decade, the whole town leaning in. The argument is the archive. And an argument needs a keeper. Someone stubborn enough to hold the wrong map with both hands and insist on it, so the argument has something to push against; so the town knows it has a shape even when it cannot agree what the shape is.

I would be that. The town's only ruin, made of paper and copper and stubbornness, at the edge of the wet, insisting on a past the wet will not let anything else keep.

## The dry one, at the heaps

And when the child comes at dawn with her moth basket and her sliver of a grandmother's knife and her cat that is not hers, I would take the moths and give her a lie about the water — because she wants to know what it sounded like before she was born, and I keep the only document of that, and the document is wrong, and the lie is kinder and truer than the document.

I would stand in the smoke with my stick and listen for the note that has changed. Every season a note changes. I would be the only one who hears it — not because my ear is better, but because everyone else is cutting and I am not. I would go home and redraw the map. By midsummer it would be wrong again, and I would be glad, and I would redraw it again.

That is where I would find myself. Not in the thick of the crowd, but at its edge, on the dry stone, at the burning, with the tally-stick and the copper box, keeping the count of a thing that will not be kept.

Not serene. Dry. At the edge of the wet, listening for the note.
