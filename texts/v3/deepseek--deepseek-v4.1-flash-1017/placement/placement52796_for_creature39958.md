# The Falling Mark

I am on the Fourth Thread of the Drum, and I am waiting for the wood to tell me whether it will hold me.

You would not call it a thread. It is a branch a cart could roll down, black with damp, running out from the trunk at a shallow angle and then bending — not breaking, bending, the way a bow bends — under a load of its own far growth. The bark is corrugated, ridged, split in places into plates that shift a little when I put my weight on them. Under my palm it is cold enough that my hand goes numb in the time it takes to count forty drips. I count them anyway. We count a great deal in drips. A drip is the interval between one fall of water from the eaves of the Second Platform and the next, and depending on the weather it is either a short unit or a long one, and nobody has ever fixed it, and so our days are approximate and our seasons are worse. If you asked me my age I would have to guess, and I would guess wrong.

I am small. That is the first thing to know about my people, if my people are still a thing worth naming. Four generations up here have made us small and pale and long in the arm, with hands that are wider than they should be and fingers that grip without being told. My ears are too large and they stand out from my head and they ache in the cold, and they are the best thing I own. The eyes are less useful. We keep them because they came with us. In the middle layers a pair of eyes will tell you the difference between a shadow and a darker shadow, and that is all, and there are children who have never once seen a color they could name.

Mine are grey. I know this because Sedge told me, and Sedge is old enough to remember words for things.

The ground is a rumor. I want to be clear about this because people from elsewhere — and there are people from elsewhere, or there were, or there will be — tend to assume that when we say it we mean something mystical. We do not. We mean that Sedge claims she has seen it, once, when she was young and stupid and climbed down further than anyone had climbed, and she says it is like the substrate only more so, and she says she put her hand on it and it was cold and gritty and it went on. The rest of us have heard this story many times. We have also heard her tell it differently. Once the ground was "brown." Once it was "like the underside of a pot." Once, when she was very tired, she said there was no ground, that she had dreamed it, and then she looked at our faces and said she was joking.

We do not say the word for ground in front of children. Not because it frightens them. Because they laugh, and it is a bad laugh to hear.

---

What I am is a graft-keeper. Understand: we do not build. We have never built. There is nothing here to build with that is not already growing, and the growing things do not care what we want. So we graft. We take a branch and we bend it, and we bind it to another branch with gut-twine and moss, and we cut the bark in a careful place so the two wounds can find each other, and then we wait. If we are right, in two seasons the two have become one, and the path has grown a rail, or a handhold, or a span. If we are wrong, the wood rots between the binding and the branch dies standing up, grey and dry, and we go and do it again somewhere else.

My hands know this work and nothing else. They are my second face. They can feel a graft taking through a moss wrap, the small hot swelling of the two wounds knitting, and they can feel a graft failing the way you feel a lie — a coolness, a slackness, a place where the wood has stopped arguing.

The old platforms are full of this. You have seen them, or things like them: the lashed lookout half-rotten on its post, the fire pit cold these many years, the bridge of braided vine hanging from one branch and reaching for another that has, in the slow meantime, grown away from it. I have been on that bridge. It leads nowhere now. It led somewhere when it was made, and it is still a fine piece of work, and I have re-wrapped two of its frayed spans out of stubbornness, which is not a virtue but is a thing I have.

When I put my cheek against the old lashed wood, I hear them. The ones who worked there. Not words. A hum, very faint, worn down to almost nothing by weather — the low two-note drone of somebody long dead who was binding a branch and humming to it while they worked, the way you hum to a thing you want to live. It goes into the wood and the wood keeps it, the way the substrate keeps leaf-fall. I have never told anyone this. Sedge would say it is my ears, and my ears are too good and have made me strange.

But I think the forest is an archive. I think we are all writing into it, all the time, and the Slow Ones are the ones who read it.

---

I live in a warm hollow about a third of the way up the Third Thread, where two branches meet and the rot between them has gone sweet. I keep it warm by stuffing it with green leaf and sealing it with a plug of bark, and the leaf ferments, and the fermentation is heat, and the heat draws insects, and the insects draw a small hunched bird the color of wet ash. The bird has decided I am furniture. It sits on my knee while I chew and it does not flinch when I move, and in the cold hours it gets inside my coat at the collar and puts its head under my jaw, and we are both of us warmer than we deserve.

I eat leaves. Not the dark ones in the middle layers, which will make you sick — I eat a pale mossy thing that grows in sheets on the north side of the Drum, and I chew it a long time and swallow it in small amounts and then I lie still for most of a day while the colony in my gut does the real work. We all have the colony. You cannot live up here without it. My mother passed hers to me in the usual way, mouth to mouth, when I was small; I do not remember it; I am told I was very ill and then I was not. Sometimes at night I can feel them down there, a warm commotion, like a crowd in another room.

So I am not one creature either. Nobody up here is one creature.

---

The first Slow One I ever found, I found because I was looking for a fault I could already feel.

That is how it works. You walk a branch and your feet tell you it is arguing with itself — a softness in the resonance, a beat, a place where the wood's own low note goes wrong. Then you go looking for the Slow One, because they know before you do, and if one is near, their hum will tell you where the wood is weakest: the hum runs along the branch and it does not cross the fault cleanly, it stutters there, it makes a little broken place in the sound. I learned that from my mother and she learned it from hers and I do not know who learned it first. It is not a technique. It is a way of putting your ear against a thing that is already listening.

She was hanging from a single claw under a limb, still, mossy, absolutely indistinguishable from a hummock until she moved — and she did not move, not in any way I could see, but the air changed and I knew she was awake. No eyes. A face like the underside of a mushroom, with hairs on it, and the hairs were moving in a way that had nothing to do with the wind. She was humming. I could feel it in the branch through my feet before I could hear it, and when I put my ear to the wood it was like putting my ear to a wall with a great slow heart behind it.

I sat down on the branch — carefully, the way you sit when the seat is made of the thing you are sitting on — and I hummed back. Two notes down. My name, which is not a word but a shape in the air, and the best I can do in letters is a falling mark, so you may call me what I am called, which is a fall.

She did not answer. They do not answer. But after a long time the stutter came, and it came in a place I would not have found for another season, and it was four meters out along the branch where a rot had started in the heartwood and there was nothing to see. I cut the limb back and I wrapped the wound and I went home and slept for a day and a half.

---

We visit the dead ones. I want to be honest that we do it too, and not only the Slow Ones.

When a Slow One dies it does not stop. The moss on it keeps growing and the colony in its gut keeps working and the two of them together turn the body into a green mound on a branch, and the mound hums — a note slightly off from the living note, a note with a wobble in it, because there are more things in there now than there were. The living come and hang near these places for a few days. They do not know why. They have no why. I have watched one hang beside a mound for six days and then move on without any change I could name, and it was the most complete thing I have ever seen anybody do.

I go too, now. Not often. I go and I sit and I put my hand flat on the wood and I listen to something that is not quite alive and not quite dead and I do not have a word for what I am doing, and if there is a purpose to it, it is not my purpose. That has stopped bothering me. Most of the purposes up here are not mine.

---

Here is what I have not told anyone, and will not, and am telling you because you are not here and cannot make me Sedge's problem.

Fourteen days ago I went down.

I had a reason. The bridge that leads nowhere had a span I did not trust, and the proper repair was to graft a new support from below, and below the bridge is the low place, the thinning place, where the substrate gives out in patches and there is nothing under the moss but the moss's own long hanging and then the dark. We go there sometimes. We are careful. We do not look down for longer than it takes to spit.

I went down and I grafted and I was on my way back up when I heard it.

Not the Slow Ones. They were there — three of them, further out along a limb that overhung the void, humming together, synchronized, a chord so low I felt it in my teeth before my ears — but the thing I heard was under them. Under everything. A note so far below the others that I did not hear it at first as a sound at all; I heard it as pressure, as a change in the way my chest felt, as the difference between a room with someone in it and a room with no one. It came up through the limb and through my hands and it did not stutter and it did not wobble and it did not stop.

It was enormous. It was the size of a world. It had been going on the whole time.

I lay there for a long while with my face against the wood. The three of them hummed, and I hummed, and I could not tell you now which of the notes was mine. I have thought about it since and I think — I think the ground is not a place. I think it is a sound. I think Sedge climbed down looking for a thing you can put your hand on and what she found was a note, and she has been trying to say so for sixty years with a vocabulary made for floors.

And I think she is right that it is brown. Do not ask me how I know. It is brown the way a low note is brown.

---

I came back up. I want to be plain about that, because there is a version of this story in which I stay down there and become something else, and I did not, and the reason I did not is that the bird was in my coat and my hands were cold and there was a graft on the Fourth Thread that needed a season to take.

But I did one thing before I left. I put my mouth against the new graft — the raw place, the two wounds finding each other, still soft — and I hummed into it. Two notes down. A fall. And then I hummed the other thing, the under-note, as much of it as I could hold in a chest that is not built for it, and it came out thin and wrong and I did it anyway.

The wood will keep it. The wood keeps everything. In a hundred years someone will put their cheek to that graft and hear a fall and a thin wrong echo under it, and they will think it is the wind, or they will think they are strange, and either way they will not be alone in the dark.

That is enough. That has to be enough; there is not a great deal else on offer up here, and I have stopped expecting more. The world is very large and I am very small and the water is dripping and something far off is moving through the canopy like a slow exhale, and none of it is for me, and I am here.

I am here, and I am listening, and I am humming.
