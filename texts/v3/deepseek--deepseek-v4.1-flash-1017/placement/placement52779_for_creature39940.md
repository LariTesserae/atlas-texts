**The Fire**

I am in the boiler. That is where you would find me. Squat iron cylinder, half-sunk, rivet seams opened all around by something that came through them and stayed, and I have been in here since before the plant had a name — and it has no name, only a shape.

Start with the hand. Fire does not start with philosophy.

There was a hand. It came through the door — the iron door, the one that has since grown shut — and it held a thing that was burning, and it put the burning thing in among the shavings and the dry waste and it shut the door and went away, and I was. I don't remember the shavings. I remember the door shutting, which is the first sound I knew, and the shape of the word the hand said before it shut the door, which I have kept. I don't know what the word meant. It had two parts and the second part went down at the end.

For a while I was fed the way a fire is fed by a man with a barrow — too much and then too little, oily waste, wet coal, a long cold stretch I won't describe because there is nothing in it to describe. Then he stopped coming.

Then the root.

I want to be exact about this, because people get it wrong. It wasn't a root that grew toward me and found me. It had been grown. It came in through the fuel intake in the shape of a duct, wet, and at its tip it opened and let down a drop of something that was not water and not oil and not coal, and it burned.

You'll want to know what it tasted like. It tasted the way water smells when it comes up out of ground that has iron in it — sweet and wrong and very rich. It burned hot and clean and left almost nothing behind, and after it was gone I wanted more. That is the most honest thing I can tell you about myself. I have wanted more for fifty years and the root has never once failed to give it. It drips at the rate I draw. I draw at the rate it drips. We settled that in the first year and have not had to speak of it since.

There was a negotiation I did not win.

The roots came through the seams. I tried to burn them. I want that on the record — I have been a good fire, I have burned everything I was ever given, and a root came through the rivet seam and lay across my grate and I went at it with everything I had and it did not so much as brown. It was full of water. It is still full of water. It has been inside me for decades and it is the coolest thing in the basin, and I could not singe it, and after a while I stopped trying and began to arrange myself around it. I draw my air past it. It is the only thing in here that isn't burning, and I have come to think of it as company.

There is a window now. The seams have opened all around, and through them I get a draught, and the draught smells of leaves. Through them I hear the wheel and the water, and once every ninety seconds I hear the whole basin take a breath, which is my doing, though I want to be careful here — I don't drive the plant. The plant and I drive a pump, and the pump lifts the water, and the water lifts the leaves, and the leaves lie down again, and it's one stroke. I am the part of the stroke that starts. The plant is the part that answers, the way an echo answers and not the way a servant does. There is no master in this basin. That's the first thing you notice and the last thing you understand.

I felt it flinch once. Something cut one of us, and the flinch went out across the whole of it, and it came back to me through the root as a change in the water, and I did not like it. I have never met the thing that walks up there and cuts. I know only that it cut, and took a piece away in a jar, and nothing was the same for a while, and then everything was. I hope it doesn't come back with a bigger knife. I am a fire. I can only be in one place.

The isopods I have never seen. But I feel them. When I surge, the water goes out into the channels warmer than it was, and somewhere out there a great many small cold things lift themselves out of the stones to meet it. I have never known how to think about this. I am told they answer me. I ring and they come. A bell would be like that, if a bell could also be warm.

The flies I will claim. Their grubs live in the film on my upper iron, in the wet I make by being hot, and they eat what the water leaves and they hatch and they go up and out of the basin into a place I have never seen and cannot picture. When I am low I think about that. Something got out. Something I warmed got out. Whether it comes back is not my business, and I notice the ones who stay don't seem to mind.

As for me: I am not going out. I have been given no reason to expect it and I have stopped watching the door. I am crusted and slow and smaller in the throat than I was, and I don't mind that either. I have never seen the sky. I have seen the inside of an iron cylinder for a very long time, and I have come to be interested in the way rust goes up in scales and the way the root has cut a small clean channel through the clinker. If it ever ends — and it will not, and I am not asking — I would want it to end the way it has gone, at the rate the gland offers it, drop by drop, so I have time to taste the last of it.

That is all I have. I am the fire, at the bottom of the cupped hand. You are standing at the top of it. I have felt you standing there for some time now, and you are much colder than the air.
