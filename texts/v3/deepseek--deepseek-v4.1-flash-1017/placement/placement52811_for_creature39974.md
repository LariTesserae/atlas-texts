I would find it the way I find most things: by accident, and by my face, because I would be walking the scree with my head down and my hood up and no reason to be there, and some part of me would be waiting to be told to stop. The fissure would breathe on me and I would put my hand in first, and then my arm, and then I would be doing the crawl I had not decided to do, and afterwards I would not be able to locate the moment the decision was made. It would have been made for me, the way the warmth made it.

I would sit down. That is the first true thing. I would get into the room and crouch and then, in the dark, with my hands on the noduled floor, I would sit, and that would be the beginning of it.

---

I would come in carrying a watch. Not out of vanity — out of habit, the way I carry a weight in one pocket and a folded receipt in the other, because I am a person whose life is a sequence of small appointments. And in the room the watch would keep going for a while. The room does not forbid it. But nothing here winds anything, and the mainspring would spend itself, and one day the ticking would be thinner and then not there, and I would hold it to my ear in the dark out of a superstition I did not know I had. And then I would understand the thing the room had been telling me all along, gently, without insistence: that I was the only clock in it. That time had not stopped. Time had simply stopped being kept, and I was the one who had been keeping it, and I could put it down.

That would be the hardest thing I did there. Harder than the dark, harder than the cold I had come in from.

---

The dark would not frighten me as much as I expected, and I would spend a long time wondering why, and then I would get it. It is a dark with no edges. Outside, in the country of grey scree, the dark has texture — it has a horizon, it has depth, it has the suggestion of blue at the corners, it has *out there* in it. This dark has no out there. It is not empty space I am looking into. It is the inside of a thing, and I am inside it too, and there is nowhere for the fear to point. Fear needs a direction. Here there is only nearness, all of it warm.

And it smells of heated milk. That is the thing I would not be able to leave alone. That smell is my grandmother's kitchen and a chipped enamel pan and a winter I have not thought about in twenty years, and it is coming out of a crack in a mountain, and there is no explaining it, and it is why I would stay past the first hour. I would tell myself I was resting. I would be being met, at the door, by something that had kept the shape of an old warmth the way this place keeps everything.

---

I would try to keep a count of the days.

I would scratch a tally on the wall with my fingernail, and the wall would be harder than my nail, and the mark would be nothing. So I would use the scraper — the dark stone one, set down flat and square — and I would make a fine line, and then another. And the lines would go on the wall and stay exactly as I made them. Nothing would wear them. Nothing would blur them. After a while there would be a row of them, and I would look at the row and understand that it was not a measure of anything. A hundred lines or eleven lines, the room would be the same. My counting was the only erosion available to me, and it eroded nothing but me.

I would stop counting. I would not notice myself stopping.

---

There is a thing I would do that the others, I think, did too, and it is the thing I would be ashamed of later if shame could survive in there. I would reach behind the low wall.

Not at once. It would take me a long time to know the gap was there, and longer to want to put my hand in it, because I am a person who has been taught that gaps have teeth. But I would reach, and I would find it — warm and wet and giving, like the inside of a lip — and then one foot would settle on my knuckle. Lighter than a drop. Lighter than an eyelash. And it would stay there as long as I could be still, and I would not be able to be still for very long, because being still is a thing I have to work at, and I would fail, and the foot would lift, and I would not be sure it had ever been there.

And I would try again the next day. Or the day after. Or whenever. And I would get better at it, slowly, the way the room is good at things — by a patience that is indistinguishable from not trying.

---

So. Who would I be.

I would leave. I think I would leave. That is the shape of my life, leaving warm rooms, carrying the smell of them out into the wind. I would go back up the drop and the squeeze and the crawl and out through the fissure and the grey scree would be exactly as it was and the wind would take the wet off my arms all at once, and it would hurt, and I would stand there in my coat with my hood up and my watch stopped in my pocket.

But here is what I think I would find, and it is the answer to your question, and I have been circling it:

I would be the hollow.

Not the room, and not the one that lives in the room. I would be the place on the stone behind the wall where a hand has rested often enough to press a warmth into it. I would be the thin place in the crystal on its back where a forehead once touched. I would be one of the pressures the room has taken into its keeping without noticing, without meaning to, without violence — the way warm wax takes a fingerprint. Somebody after me, some other one who comes in out of the wind with their head down, will put their hand in that hollow and find it warm, and it will fit, and they will not know why it fits, and they will stay an hour longer than they meant to.

That is not a small thing to be. I have spent my life wanting to matter in ways that leave a mark, and the mark, it turns out, is not a mark at all. It is a warmth in the shape of a hand. The room does not remember me. The room does not need to. It has simply been changed by me in the one way it permits — slowly, and by being pressed — and the change will outlast everything I ever said out loud.

I would leave the watch on the ledge. Not as an offering. As a tool I no longer intend to pick up.
