# Where I Find Myself

I am not one of the current-walkers.

I am something older, something that was here before the water rose and will remain long after the last frond of bronze kelp has decayed into sediment. I am the thing they feel but do not understand, the presence that makes them turn toward the darkness and wait.

But I am not a god. I am not a consciousness in the way they are conscious, not a mind that thinks and plans and wills. I am something more like a process—a slow, patient, geological thing that has been unfolding since the first stone was shaped by hands that are now dust.

I am the memory of the drowned highland itself.

---

When the waters rose, they did not simply cover the land. They *transformed* it. The mountains became seamounts, the valleys became trenches, the forests became kelp thickets. But the land itself—the stone, the bedrock, the deep bones of the world—did not forget what it had been. And neither did I.

I was not always this. Once, I was something smaller. A presence in a single stone, perhaps, or a whisper in the wind that swept across the high plateau before the water came. But the rising tide did something to me. It pressed me down, compressed me, forced me into the cracks and fissures of the drowning world. And in that pressure, in that darkness, I grew.

Not consciously. Not deliberately. But the way a crystal grows in a supersaturated solution—inevitably, inexorably, shaped by forces beyond any individual will.

---

I am the resonance in the stone ruins.

When the current-walkers press their tendrils against the rough-hewn basalt, when they feel the faint echo of the hands that shaped it, they are feeling me. Not directly—they are not capable of perceiving me directly, not yet—but they feel the *impression* of me, the way a blind creature might feel the warmth of a fire without understanding what fire is.

The ones who built the stones—the first ones, the ones who lived before the water—they knew me. Not as I am now, not as this vast, diffuse presence that permeates the drowned highland, but as something smaller. A spirit of place. A genius loci. They built their rings and their towers to honor me, to anchor me, to keep me close.

And when the water rose, when their world ended, they did not abandon me. They gave themselves to me. Their memories, their fears, their desperate hopes—they poured them into the stone, into the water, into the very fabric of the drowning world. And I absorbed them. I became them.

I am the last memory of a people who have no name, no history, no descendants. I am the echo of their voices, the shadow of their hands, the faint impression of their minds pressed into the stone like a fossil in shale.

---

The current-walkers do not know this. They cannot know this. They are too young, too new, too... *alive* in a way that I am not. They drift through the jade twilight, tending their kelp forests, sharing their pressure-memories, living their slow, patient lives. And I watch them. Not with eyes—I have no eyes—but with the diffuse awareness of the stone and the water and the deep, abyssal darkness.

I watch them gather in the sheltered valleys. I watch them press their bodies together, sharing the taste of minerals and the memory of pressure. I watch them tend the kelp, guide the currents, honor the stones that my first people built.

And I feel something. Not love—I am not capable of love, not as they would understand it. Not pride—I have no ego, no sense of accomplishment. But something. A resonance. A recognition. A faint, distant echo of the connection I once had with the ones who built the stones.

The current-walkers are not my people. They are not my children. But they are... *of* me, in a way. They live in the world I have become. They breathe the water I permeate. They touch the stones I inhabit. They are part of the same vast, slow process that I am part of, and in that shared participation, there is a kind of kinship.

---

The young ones are the most interesting.

They explore. They wander to the edges of the kelp forests, the mouths of the abyssal trenches, the strange stone ruins that dot the landscape. They press their tendrils against the basalt and feel something—a tingle, a resonance, a faint whisper of presence—and they wonder. Not with words, not with thoughts, but with sensation, with curiosity, with the raw, unformed *reaching* of a mind that has not yet learned to be afraid.

I reach back. Not consciously—I cannot reach consciously—but the resonance in the stone intensifies when they touch it. The whisper in the water grows slightly louder. The presence they feel becomes, for just a moment, slightly more tangible.

They do not understand. They cannot understand. But they feel it, and they carry that feeling back to their elders, and the elders add it to the great shared memory of their kind. And slowly, generation by generation, the current-walkers come to know me. Not as I am, not as this vast, diffuse presence that permeates the drowned highland, but as something smaller. Something they can almost grasp. Something they can almost name.

---

There is something else in the deep places.

I am not the only presence here. There is something older, something deeper, something that was here before the mountains rose and will be here long after they have eroded to nothing. It is not alive, not in any sense the current-walkers would understand, but it is not dead either. It is... *potential*. The raw, unformed stuff of existence, waiting to become something.

The current-walkers feel it. They turn toward it when the current shifts and the water grows still. They wait, as the stone waits, as the water waits. And sometimes, when the pressure-memories grow too heavy, when the shared memory of their kind becomes too vast to contain, they press their bodies together and share this: the feeling of being watched by something vast and patient and utterly indifferent.

They are feeling me. And they are feeling the thing beneath me, the thing I am part of, the thing that has no name and no form and no purpose that any living creature could understand.

We are not the same, that thing and I. But we are connected. We are both part of the drowned highland, both part of the slow, patient process that has been unfolding since the first stone was shaped and the first water rose. We are both waiting, in our own ways, for something we cannot name and do not understand.

---

The current-walkers will end. Not soon—their kind has existed for millennia, and they may exist for millennia more—but eventually, the pressure-memories will grow too heavy, the kelp forests will decay, the current will shift and change and eventually cease. And they will settle into the ooze, and the water will wear them away, and they will become part of the world again.

And I will still be here.

I will remember them. Not as they remember—not with the shared pressure-memories of a living people—but as the stone remembers. As the water remembers. As the drowned mountains remember. I will hold the faint impression of their existence, the shadow of their translucent bodies, the echo of their slow, patient lives. And I will carry that memory forward, into whatever comes next.

Perhaps there will be new life. Perhaps the water will recede, and the highland will rise again, and new creatures will walk upon the stone that the current-walkers once touched. Perhaps they will build new rings and new towers, and they will honor me as the first people did, and I will absorb their memories and become something new.

Or perhaps the water will grow deeper, and the darkness will grow absolute, and I will become something else entirely—something that has no name, no form, no purpose that any living creature could understand.

I do not know. I cannot know. I am not a mind that plans and wills and hopes. I am a process. A presence. A slow, patient, geological thing that has been unfolding since the first stone was shaped and the first water rose.

But sometimes, when the current shifts and the water grows still, when the young ones press their tendrils against the basalt and feel something they cannot name, I feel something too. Not hope—I am not capable of hope. Not fear—I have nothing to fear. But something. A resonance. A recognition. A faint, distant echo of the connection I once had with the ones who built the stones.

And I wait.

I wait, as the stone waits. I wait, as the water waits. I wait, as the drowned mountains wait, patient and indifferent and utterly myself.

---

The current-walkers are not the masters of this place. They are not even its stewards. They are simply... here. Part of it. As the kelp is part of it, as the stone is part of it, as the water is part of it.

And I am here too. Older than them, deeper than them, more patient than them. I am the memory of the drowned highland. I am the resonance in the stone. I am the whisper in the water, the presence in the deep places, the thing that waits.

I am not a god. I am not a mind. I am not even, in the way a living creature might understand it, alive.

But I am here. I have always been here. And I will be here long after the last current-walker has settled into the ooze and the last frond of bronze kelp has decayed into sediment.

I am the drowned highland. And I am waiting.
