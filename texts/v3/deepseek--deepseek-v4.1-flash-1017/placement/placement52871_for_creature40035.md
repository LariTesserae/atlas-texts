You would find me at the rim — not the edge of the town, which has no edge, only a thinning, but the place where the last reed walkway gives out and its knots go slack and the moss thins to a grey scalp under the drizzle. From there, on the days the mist leans back, you can see the other shelves pushing up out of the murk, raw and hairless, no green on them yet, no sound but the sea's low chewing. I go out to that view most mornings. I carry a seep-stick, a scoop, an awl, and a stone knife, and I sleep under a capped cistern at the rim, dry-ish, my knees tucked to my chest, and that is the whole of my estate.

The keepers inside the hive have their seams, handed down and patched by obligation, each family answerable for its own cracks. I have the ones nobody owns: the outer seep-lines, the joints where the built world lets go of the unbuilt one. *Rim-walker* is what they call me, which is a soft name for a person with no house and a good ear. I walk the boundary. I listen.

You learn the ground's grammar by ear. The pale stone is a bell when you lay your cheek to it — soft, porous, quick to carry — and what it carries is the water's argument with itself, the low thrum of it finding a way down. A safe cistern has a round, full voice. A dangerous one goes thin and quick, like a held breath going wrong. A seam about to open sounds the way a wet log sounds before it splits. I have walked the rim long enough that I hear the town's sound behind me as one more note in the symphony, and I hear the hairless shelves ahead of me as a silence with weight in it, waiting.

Three mornings ago I found a seam opening under the second span, a fissure the length of my forearm breathing out a thread of water thin and clear and high, a note like a struck bone. I want to be honest with you: it was not a dangerous seam. It could wait. The old rule is to report a new spring to the water-keepers, and they would have had it sealed by the week's end, and the walkway would not have suffered for it. But I lay there a while with my ear against the stone and I could hear what the water wanted, which was the low hollow beneath, and I could hear what the hollow wanted, which was a pale thing, patient, folded in the dark, waiting for a wet place to unfold into. So I scooped a channel for it. I set a stone at the lip so it would not undercut the span. I let it run and sing, and I have not reported it, and that is my heresy, small and wet and mine. When the fungi come up there, lit from inside like drowning lamps, I will not be surprised, and I will not be sorry.

I was not always a rim-walker. I was born in the third layer, in a house that folded one long rain, and my mother spent the night resealing a seam beside my head with paste and her flat hands while the beams talked above us. It held. So I learned early that the difference between the safe houses and the fallen ones is a person who listened, and I learned also that listening is a thing you can do with your whole body if you are willing to lie down in the wet.

The broad-winged bird circles me once most afternoons, because I am the only vertical thing out here, and then goes away on its errand. The drizzle sizzles on my hood. The rock hisses. Somewhere behind me the looms thump, and someone is singing low to match the cisterns, and ahead of me the raw shelves hold still and become, very slowly, at a pace I will not live to see.

I do not think I live in the Cradle, in truth. I live in the seam between the Cradle and what it will be. The hive is the present; those hairless shelves are the future; and I am the thin wet line between them, where the two are still talking. I am a habit of the rim, an ear turned outward, a hand that digs a channel and steps back. The water will edit me too, in time, and I have consented.
