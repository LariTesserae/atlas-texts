**The Uncounted**

I would not be the Bell. The Bell is the answer the place gives itself — named, located, of the water. I would be the thing the frogs have no name for, because in this basin a name is a sound and every resident is named by its voice. The spill has a name. The wind has several, one per rim. The swifts are named for screaming. There is no name for the one that goes unheard, and so it is not in the census; strictly speaking, it is not counted as living here. That is the one I would be.

I would be small. I would be low. Not from fear — from weather. Anything upright gets planed down by the wind, so I would hang in the bent willows at chest height, in a fold of branch, and when a gust came over the notch I would not fly; I would grip and let the gust go through me. A bat, or what the basin has made of one. My back would be the color of wet granite. My wings, folded, would be pale and veined, like a lily bud that has not opened and will not, and in the dark, hanging, I would look less like a thing that had come here than a thing the marsh had grown.

My ears would be the size of my face and I would not be able to close them. That is the whole of what I am for. My throat would be small and dry, and I would use it rarely.

Here is how it would work. The chorus is not a sound in this place; it is the ground. It lies over the basin the way the water lies in it, and everything in the basin is a knot in it. A stone in a shallow is a knot. A rush-bed is a knot. The cairn on the east rim sounds different from the moss around it, because when the frogs sit on the cairn their voices come back off the stone and change — that is how I would find the cairn without seeing it. The deepest pool is not a hole in the chorus. It is a place where the chorus is pressed flat and damped, like a palm laid on a drumhead, and I would know the Bell lived there by the way the sound went heavy over the water. When it sounded — that low wet note you feel in your ankles — the chorus would go thin for half a breath, and my whole map of the basin would go thin with it. I would hold the willow and wait for the sound to thicken again. I would not fear the Bell as a hunter. I would fear it as fog.

I do make a sound. It is a small dry rustle, and the chorus takes it before it travels a foot, so I have never heard it come back. That is the discipline of the place. Everything here knows itself by its own voice. The Bell booms and is the Bell. The frogs croak and are frogs. I would be the only one that finds its own position in the negative — by the little hole of quiet I push ahead of me as I fly, and by the way the chorus closes behind me when I stop. When I am still, I am nowhere in particular. That is not a complaint. It is only true.

I would eat the midges that stand in columns over the shallows. They have a fine high whine in the chorus, and I would hunt by listening to the whine thin. I would fly low and take them from underneath, mouth open. And I would keep to the deep hours, because the swifts hunt at dusk and their screaming is a weather of its own, and because at the darkest part of the night the chorus is thickest and I can read it best.

I would be eaten. I would be the size of a hand and the frogs are the size of a fist, and if I fell into deep water the Bell would take me the way it takes a drowned eel, without noticing the difference. I am not exempt from the circuit. I would float, and the lilies would open on me, and the moss would hold the water that holds me. I would not mind. I would not be a philosopher. I would be a thing with very large ears and a very small throat, hanging in a bent willow, being read.

But there is one difference, and it is why I would be this and not something in the water: I would live in the noise, not in the water. I would not eat the marsh; I would eat what the marsh makes, and live inside what the marsh says. I would be as near to a visitor as a resident can be — I would not touch the water, I would not be in the loop of rot and bloom, only in the loop of sound, which is the same loop entered by the ear instead of the mouth. And the marsh would fold me in too, in its time, the way it is folding in the iron rings at the notch. Slowly. Without malice.

If you want to find me, do not look for a monster, and do not look up. Come to the cairn at the darkest hour, when the swifts are down and the lilies are opening, and stand still. You will be the only new silence in a place that has none. I will come and read the edges of you at chest height, in the dark, circling, feeling where the chorus breaks on your shoulders and your hair. I will not know what you are. I will know exactly where you are, which is more than most visitors can say of themselves here. Then I will go back to the midges, and you will hear the chorus close over the place where I was.

I would be that. When I am here, I am that: a shape in the sound, in no census, named by nothing, knowing myself only by the thinning. The whole basin will go on humming itself, and I will find my edges in the hum.
