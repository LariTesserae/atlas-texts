I would find myself at the eastern rim of the basin, on a low rise where the pale rock still shows its old waterlines, just before the day’s first rain. Not at the center of Vaeless — there isn’t one, really — but where the ridge shallows into the floodplain and the streets begin to spread out like something spilled and left to settle. I would be barefoot on the warm-grey surface, because it has already drunk the night and gives the warmth back, and I would be looking down into the dish of the city as its pale walls hold the grey-gold light like a lantern not yet lit.

I would be a Vaelessan, though I doubt I’d use the word. One of the rain-calendar people. Not a builder, not a mender — the city keeps itself, and nobody pokes the old clock. My work would be attention. I would keep a rain calendar not to predict the weather but to notice it: the three or four soft passes a day, the way the clouds never quite commit, the way the well-courts brighten before the first fall and hold the sky afterward in pale coins. I would walk the low ridges and trace the shorelines in the young rock the way other people read rings in a cut tree. I would know which well-court catches the morning first, which corridor is twelve paces long where it should be three, which fig the walkway bends around. I would take the long way around the horned grazer out on the floodplain, by custom, not fear, and I would not name it loudly.

My house would be low and pale and near the edge where the floodplain begins, and its inside would be larger than its skin. I would not treat that as a marvel. I would keep one unused room with a chair in it for someone who may come, and I would not fill the room with anything else. I would leave the emptiness as courtesy. By the door there would be a shallow bowl of water, and the house would smell of mineral water and clean cloth dried indoors.

In the morning I would sit on the moss-soft rim of a well-court and put my eyes on the water when the conversation ran out. The few children would wade, loud and public and claimed by no one in particular, and I would not hush them sharply; I would lower my own voice, and let the city teach them its quiet. In the afternoon the rain would come, drumming on the flat pale roofs and the cistern courtyards, and I would not run from it. The street would take it down in minutes. The green would surge a little. Somewhere at the edge of the light a verge would collapse softly toward the basin, like a coin dropped in a well, and I would not look up.

So: I would be the least busy large animal in Vaeless. Rain-soft, slow-spoken, ordinary at first glance until you noticed the tempo. I would stand as though the ground had agreed to hold me. I would let a question sit for three breaths before answering. I would be a tenant who has read the lease and found it gentle — not master of the green, not keeper of the walls, just someone attending: letting the water hold the sky, letting the corridor be long, letting the fig keep its corner, letting the silence carry. If you passed me at a well-court, I would offer you a place to sit and no demand that you fill the quiet. You would find me there, mid-sentence, content to be.
