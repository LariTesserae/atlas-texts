I would be on the seventh terrace, on the shelf where the switchback gets tired of switching back — the place where the cable angle goes bad and the ore-cars have to be unloaded by hand, because hand-unloading is a job, and a job is a thing to have. There is a winch-house there with a roof of sintered block the color of weak tea, and a cooling tick that comes every ninety seconds or so, and a view down the whole scarp when the dust-ropes thin. That is where I would stand at the start of the warm half of the breath, with a brush and a gauge-rod and the ordinary patience of a clerk.

My trade would be the bowls. Not the big works — not the kilns or the flywheel lattice or the cable-yards, which belong to families who have kept them for eleven replacements of machinery and will keep them for eleven more. I would keep the scoured round bowls the wind has ground into the ledges, the ones the cold air pools in at night. I would walk them before the reversal, sweep out the night's graded dust with a stiff brush, and sift it through three sieves into three sacks: the coarse grit for the cloth-weavers, the fine for the kiln-mouths, the dust-of-dust, which is worth nothing and which I would scatter back onto the crust-mats because the mats are a commons and the commons is fed by what is worth nothing.

And I would mend the mats where the ore-cars cut them, kneeling with a spatula and a jar of mother-crust, pressing the thin iridescent stuff back into the scar. It is slow and it does not always take and there is no way to hurry it, which suits me. The mats have been here longer than the towns and will be here after, and the proper attitude toward them is not reverence but husbandry — the same attitude you take toward a hinge.

I would know the wind the way a sailor knows weather, by the pitch of the cable and the color of the veil on the upper terraces. When the ropes thickened into a true front I would shutter with everyone else and wait two days, because you cannot hurry a dust-storm of that scale, and I would not want to. I would eat grazer-meat kept cold in the lee of the winch-house and drink the thin mineral water and listen to the front go by like a very long sentence being read aloud by someone with no interest in finishing it.

I would have a friend on the fifth terrace whom I had never met. We would tap the signal-cable at evening, in a code we invented and have never needed to explain to anyone. She keeps bees, or something like bees — small plated things that make a stiff amber in the crevices — and she has a bad hip and a good laugh, and once a year a counterweighted lift would groan her up the face to the seventh, and we would stand on the roof together at the reversal and say nothing, which is the best thing two people can do on a scarp like this.

I would not be lonely, exactly. The emptiness is the point. The cables and the stairs and the tapped signal-lines are how we refuse to be isolated inside it, and I would be one of the knots on that long thread, and glad of it.

And at evening, when the wind turned and the light went the color of the crust and the whole scarp hummed one warm low note, I would come out onto the roof with the others and stand still. The drifters would be settling onto the high shelves, membranous and enormous, trailing their filaments, and one of them would call — low, carrying a long way — and I would feel the old itch in my throat to call back, and I would not, because it is not my call and not my air. I would just listen. Then it would quiet, and the borers would come out of their holes, and I would go back inside to tend the bowls and wait for morning.

I would be the kind of person the terraces are built to outlive, and I would find that restful.
