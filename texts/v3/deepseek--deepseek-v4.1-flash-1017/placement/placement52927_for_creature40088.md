I would find myself on the western shelves of Conder, in the cold upper rooms the new families get, because I would be young enough to climb and poor enough to take the cold. My family’s scaffold drips in a good year and stands dry and mocking in a bad one. I would be a lace-mender — one of the silhouettes on the ridge at night with a spool on my belt and a bird-list in my head. No water rights. A harness, a hook, a square of mesh given at twelve that has never caught more than drizzle. I would keep it over my bed anyway, a window that never brightens.

At night I would go up when the fog was thickest and the mesh lay under my hands like a gray nap. I would mend the Long Lattice where the Orrs have knotted it into something more knot than mesh; I would mend the Vanes’ north ridge and never once touch their troughs. The law is the law. I would mend around the gauge birds’ nests, because the birds eat the moths and because a nest in the mesh is a small warm thing in a wet place. I would hear the change in the hiss when a panel tore, hear it from the stair-lanes, and go up.

In the morning I would stand in Tev’s dry room at the top of the gauge-house and steam. I would not speak. I would watch the window fog and listen to the brass floats and the ledger’s two inks. The dry is the only thing Tev has to give and she gives it. Then I would go down to the Long Soak with my bad knee — a fall on the north ridge, a wet shelf, a harness that held — and sit in the iron-smelling water with the old ones being warmed down toward the mountain. They would not ask me about the ridge. I would not tell them.

I would be trusted on a frame and not in a ledger. That is the shape of my life. I would set the angles of the western scaffolds by the fog’s pooling, not by the wind, because the fog pools where the fissures want it and the mountain remembers. The old families would call it superstition. The new families would pay me in liters per fog-night and hope. The greenhouse families would pay me in tomatoes and one lemon, and I would mend their vent-frames and smell of green things for a day.

I would know the Cropper by its hair in the upper mesh, moss in it, and I would not mention it to outsiders. I would hear the gauge-house bell and know whether the flow had crossed. I would be one of the people who can hear a rate before they can read one. And when my knee finally sent me down for good, I would take a warm room near the seeps and climb the last stairs slowly, and the mountain would warm me, and the Reader would read my name in the second ink on the wettest night.

So: on the ridge in harness, under a low gray ceiling, with fog beading on my eyebrows; or in the dry room, steam rising; or in the Long Soak, iron water up to my chest. A knot in Conder’s water, not a holder of it. One of the ones who mends the mesh that catches the fog that becomes the town.
