I would find myself at the outer thread of Aumber, where the raised stone walks thin into guide-rails and the last cistern gives way to open seep-line. Not the center of the constellation—there is no center—but a small bright joint on its edge, where the city’s capillaries end and the plateau begins to sweat.

I would be a seep-keeper. One of the maintainers. Part plumber, part herdsman of stone, part neighbor. My module would be pale and faceted, grown rather than built, its roof pitched to harvest condensation and its walls channeled with capillaries that tick at night. I would keep a weather mast and a commons cistern, and I would know the nearest overflow weir the way other people know a stair.

The first answer might be the follower—lean, low, patient, living by heat and patience—but I would not be that. I would know its tracks in the grey grit and the way it uses the long parallel ribs of the plateau to break its outline. I would see where it waited at a seep and choose, out of habit rather than fear, to check that seep a little later in the day. The grazers would be my neighbors in the ordinary sense: loose lines of dark, humped animals beaded with condensation, lowing softly to one another as they browsed wiregrass and dwarf willow no taller than a boot. I would not herd them. I would read them. Their lines would tell me where the warmth was, and where the water was, and where the follower had been.

I would not be a repair swarm either, though I would send them. They are plumbing that has learned to skitter, and people here treat them as weather. I would watch them move like mites over the capillary channels, silent and busy, and I would know by their concentration which module was weeping, which joint was thinking about failing.

My days would be water days. Check the seep. Read the meter. Clear the capillary. Watch the overflow weir. Draw water from cold air with the weather spine and hold heat in the phase-change lattice. Walk the raised stone walk between wellheads with my breath fogging and my hands learning again the bite of wet stone. A cistern is a commons; a wellhead is where neighbors meet. Solitude is not abandonment here. It is the normal spacing. I could walk a long time between presences and still be inside Aumber.

What I would be, more than anything, is a keeper of joints. The culture here is a culture of joints. Nothing is old enough to be restored, so things are either growing or not yet grown. You do not dig a foundation; you seed one. You do not fight the uplift; you build joints into it. A cracked module is not mourned. It is decoupled, re-seeded, absorbed into newer growth. I would hear an anchor adjust in the night and go back to sleep.

And I would be, in my own small way, one of the plateau’s fauna as well as its caretaker: clustered around water and warmth, sparse, adaptive, quietly busy everywhere no one is looking. I would notice condensation forming and rain crossing and herds shifting and stone being laid and water finding its level. I would be a concentration at the edge of the scatter—not a crowd, not a hero, just a person on a raised walk with wet hands, listening to the Sough make itself.
