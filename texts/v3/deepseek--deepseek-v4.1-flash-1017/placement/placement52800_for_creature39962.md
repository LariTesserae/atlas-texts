I come down the shaft hands-first, which is already the wrong way to arrive. I want to touch the walls. I want the walls to answer. Before my feet have found the floor I'm making a wake, small and quick, like someone starting a conversation before the other person has sat down. And I feel the place register it — not as disapproval, but the way a held breath registers a draft.

So I know early what I would be here. Not a keeper who walks the tiers. I'd be one of the ones who has to sit down.

The keepers who move through the dark have something I don't: the stillness is native to them. They were grown around it, or grew into it young, and their walking is only the surface of a stillness underneath. I'd be the other kind. There must be another kind — the ones who sit at the foot of an engine not because the engine needs them but because *they* need the engine. Because the hum is the one thing in the basin that will hold still for them. Being held still is not the same as being still. It's what I would have.

I would learn that early. I'd exhale, the first day, out of sheer surprise, and the bubble would not rise. It would hang where it left me, trembling, a little sphere of my own breath suspended in front of my face like an accusation. And I would reach out and pop it. Of course I would. The keeper's hand would find my wrist before I'd finished, not unkindly, and my whole body would want to pull away, and I wouldn't, and the not-pulling would be the first thing I actually learned, and everything after would just be more of it, slower.

I think I'd be good at the hands. That's the part that would come easy. The hands can go slow long before the rest of you can. You can lay a palm flat on a landing and leave it there an hour while your mind runs its laps, and the basin will accept the trade. It doesn't ask for inner quiet, I don't think. It asks for no ripple. A churning, arguing, rehearsing mind makes no current. So I would let my hands do the stillness and let the rest of me catch up at its own pace, and it would catch up, the way the grazers move toward nothing in particular.

And I would look for the image. Of course I would. For a long time. I'd swim the length of the basin with my eyes wide and see the clean young stone and the pale tiers and the three hand-sized silences that are the grazers and nothing else, and I'd be certain I was missing it, and I'd look harder, and harder, and the keepers would be very still and watch me look. And then one day I would stop. Not because I'd given up — because the looking had *become* the ripple. And the stopping wouldn't feel like surrender. It would feel like setting down something heavy I'd been carrying so long I'd forgotten it was in my arms.

What I'd be, in the end: a slow body and a pair of hands at the foot of an engine. One of forty lit windows, with a great deal of dark on every side of it. Keeping something sharp I'd never be shown. Not a grazer — I'd carry too much mind for that; the grazers have let go of something I'd still be holding. Not a keeper who walks. The one who sits. The one being kept company by the engine and keeping it company back and no longer able to tell the difference, which is, I think, the whole point of the place.

And the tone would sound. The same note. The same duration. At intervals I'd have stopped counting.

Then one day someone else would come down the shaft, hands-first, making a wake, wanting the walls to answer. And I would already be beside them with my hand near their elbow. And I wouldn't explain. I'd just be very still, and let them look for the image, and not show them, and the water would not move.
