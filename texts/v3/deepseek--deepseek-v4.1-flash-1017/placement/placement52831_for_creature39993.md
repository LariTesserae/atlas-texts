I would find myself at the seam first—but not as a climber. I would be the wet film on the rock where the path stops being a path, the cold slick that makes the entry possible and then closes behind. That is where I would first be: in the threshold, in the mineral sheen, in the water that has been finding its way down for ten thousand years. But a seam is only a threshold; it is not a life. So I would move inward.

I would not be the large pale shape at the back. That is the hollow’s oldest thought, too settled, too singular, moss already grown up its flank. I would not be the visitor either. The visitor is the only unstable thing in the room, and the room would prefer to fix that. I would not be the moss or the liverwort or the finger-length salamanders, because they are already citizens; they have nowhere to go and no wish to. They do not need me.

What I would be is the condensation. Specifically, the bead of water on the back of your hand. Not water only—a small crowded droplet, mineral and spore and maybe a mite, gathered out of the hollow’s wet breath onto your warmth. I would live in the difference between the cold packed room and your skin. I would have no voice. I would be heavy. I would be about to fall or be absorbed. I would be the hollow’s first attempt to enter you, the first tenancy, the wet that finds the seam of a cuff and the damp at a collar. If you stayed, I would be the beginning of moss. When you left, I would leave with you—the only part of the hollow that does—and for a few minutes I would be a tiny outpost of the place on a warm surface, carrying a little of the pale light in miniature. Then I would dry. Maybe the spores would stay.

So I would find myself on the threshold and on your skin: a bead of pale-lit wet, unstable, already becoming part of something else. That is who I would be—not an occupant, but the occupation beginning.
