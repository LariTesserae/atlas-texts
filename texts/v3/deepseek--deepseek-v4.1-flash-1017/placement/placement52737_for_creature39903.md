I am a sounder. That's the word we use for it, and it's a job, not a gift — though the crews down here talk about it as though it were a temperament, the way you might say *he's a nervous one* or *she's good in wet ground*.

I came in with the third survey, six seasons back, carrying a theodolite and a set of instruments for measuring a land that had already made fools of the previous four surveys. The instruments went into the silt within a month. What I kept was a fork of grown shell, a little taller than my forearm, that someone on the rim pressed into my hands and told me to strike, and then to listen, and then to strike again. That's all. That's the trade.

You strike the fork on the mat and the mat answers. Over thick ground it comes back low and round and slightly late, like a bell through a hand. Over thin ground — over the warm dark that is always nearer than you think — it comes back fast and flat, and the ring has a crack in it, and the weed around your boots brightens blue-white as the filament spends itself on the sound. So I walk, and I strike, and I listen, and with a stick of charcoal I mark where a span could be laid and where it could not, and by the time anyone builds the span my marks are wrong, because the floor has come up a hand's width and the channel has opened somewhere else. Maps are worthless. I stopped making them. I keep a song instead, and I revise it.

They call us sounders, and there are perhaps nine of us in the whole basin at any time, and we are not the interesting inhabitants. I want to be clear about that. I am the least of the residents here and I know it. The mat is a resident. The chorus is a resident. The builder — the patience — is a resident and a landlord, and I live in a room it made without asking me, and it will make another room where this one stands, in time, and I will move, and it will not notice either event.

Here is what a day is. I wake in a tower of accumulated floors, pale and warm, that has no stairs to the ground; I go down by a spiral of root-vines that the filament has thickened for the purpose, or that I have persuaded myself it thickened for the purpose. I eat something grown. I walk out under the amber smear that passes for daylight and I begin to strike and listen, and the roar comes up around me, and after an hour or two I stop hearing it as noise — the way you stop hearing your own name if you say it enough. The pulses of the chorus are in there. The insect-things sync and break and sync, and if I set my feet down on the beat my footsteps vanish into it, and I have become, for a few hours, one of the overlapping pulses. That is not poetry. That is technique. It is also, I have come to suspect, a kind of slow conversion.

Things I have learned, in the way you learn a language by being dropped into it:

That a shout carries four kilometres and the basin answers. That the answering is not an echo; the basin has its own opinion, and it arrives late, and it is not the same shape as what you sent.

That the low chord means the lift has shifted and a channel will open within the day, and you should not be in the channel.

That the rising figure means hull-beasts, and you will feel them an hour before you see them, and when they come over the ridge they are not animals so much as slow bells with legs, domed and pale, in no hurry whatsoever, moving at the pace of the floor beneath them. I have walked beside one for a whole afternoon. It never acknowledged me. Its shell had been mended in three places with filament so fine it looked like frost.

That the pool-things are a disturbance in the weed and nothing more, and that still water is not to be trusted, and that once, on a night I do not talk about, I watched a long shape cross a pool without parting it.

That the builder builds. I have watched a span reach out across a channel over the course of eleven days, five centimetres of pale accretion a night, arriving at nothing, destined for nothing, meticulous. I have a stair now that leads from my third floor to a room with no other entrance, and the room has a window, and the window has a view, and the view is a wall. Nobody has walked on most of the surfaces in this town. They are being made anyway. I find this less frightening than I did. There is something restful about a purpose that does not require you. It takes the weight off.

And I am changing, in the way things here change: not worn, but added to. The filament has got into the soles of my boots and into the calluses of my hands, and when I cut myself on a raw bank the cut closes before I have finished noticing it. I am being maintained. I don't know whether that means I am being kept or whether it means I have been counted as part of the floor. My speech has gone flat and careful; I pick my words out of the noise one at a time, and when I go up to the rim to see the supply crews they wince at me. I have stopped noticing the roar. That's the thing that should worry me and doesn't. When I am away from it for more than a day I feel the absence the way you feel a missing tooth.

Where would you find me? Out on the mat, mid-stride, fork raised. Somewhere the light is the colour of a held breath and the ground rings under my heel like a plucked string and I can feel, through it, the pulse of walkers kilometres off — a sounding crew, or hull-beasts, or something with no name that keeps time with the lift.

And who am I, finally, in the ledger of this place? A tenant of a rising floor. A small thing that makes sounds and reads the answers. Part instrument, part audience, slowly being threaded with the same white lattice that mends the leaves and the shells and the stairs. I keep the one rule the ground taught me, and I keep it every day, and it is the only rule anyone here shares:

Do not stand on ground that has started to sing.

Then step off, mark it, and walk on in time with the chorus, so that your footsteps vanish into it, and the basin does not have to notice you at all.
