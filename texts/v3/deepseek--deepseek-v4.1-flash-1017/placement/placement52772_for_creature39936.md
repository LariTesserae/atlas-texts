I would be a listener.

Not one of the official Listeners, who sit near the lower shafts with their wax tablets and their patient, professional attention, recording each shift and groan of the great singer for the city's archives. I would be something less formal. A woman who has found a ledge—a small one, recently grown, still wet, jutting from a wall three spirals down from the main thoroughfares—and who goes there when her shift at the filter-farms is done. A woman who sits with her back against the cold stone and her knees drawn up and simply *listens*.

I think I would have come to Hadal Spire the way many come: following a rumor, or a work contract, or a person. Perhaps I was a chamber-mapper once, one of those patient souls who goes out with coils of marked cord and returns days later with sketches of passages that did not exist last season. I would have been good at it. I would have liked the solitude, the slow work of learning a new space with my hands and my breath. But I would have kept finding myself stopping—not to map, but to listen. To press my palm against a wall and feel the great singer's song moving through it like blood through a wrist. To notice that the worms above me had changed their pulse, and that the change meant something I could almost understand.

Eventually I would have stopped pretending that the mapping was the point.

Now I tend filter-mats in the warm seams, the grey living tissue that turns the city's waste into food and cloth and lampshades. It is good work. It is slow and damp and necessary. I have a small room clustered around a vent, shared with two others, and a hanging garden of mats that I am teaching to grow in new patterns. I have a child, or a niece, or a neighbor's child who has attached herself to me—the story does not require me to be precise. I have a coat studded with amber pins, and a jar of worms by my door, and a reputation for being a little strange, a little quiet, a little too interested in the singers below.

But my real life is on the ledge.

I go there in the dim hours, when the city has stilled its machines for the great singer's silence. The worms above me pulse slow and blue. The water drips and freezes before it lands. And I sit, and I listen, and I write down what I hear. Not the way the official Listeners do—not in neat columns of observation and measurement. I write in a private script, in a book I keep wrapped in oilcloth, and what I write are not facts. They are questions. They are half-heard phrases of a language I am trying to learn. They are the beginnings of a translation.

Because here is what I have noticed, and what I cannot stop noticing: the great singer is not just singing. It is *saying* something. The smaller things answer it. The worms pulse in patterns that echo its long grinding phrases. The filter-feeders shift in their seams when it changes key. The whole cave is a conversation, and I am beginning—only beginning, only barely—to hear the grammar of it.

I do not know if anyone else has noticed. The official Listeners record, but they do not interpret. They are afraid of what interpretation might mean. They propitiate the singer with metal and silence because they do not want to know what it is. They want it to keep singing, and they want to keep not knowing why.

I want to know why.

So I sit on my ledge, and I listen, and I write. I have filled three books. I have begun to suspect that the singer is not one thing but many—a chorus pretending to be singular, or perhaps a singular thing pretending to be a chorus. I have begun to suspect that the worms are not merely responding to the singer but *relaying* it, passing its phrases along the ceiling like a nervous system passing signals. I have begun to suspect that the city is not built *near* the singer but *on* it, *in* it, that the struts and corridors and warm pockets are all part of a body we have mistaken for a place.

I have not told anyone this. I am not sure I am right. I am not sure it would help if I were.

But sometimes, in the dim hours, when the city is still and the water is freezing and the worms are pulsing their slow blue pulse, I feel the singer's song change. It shifts, just slightly, just for a moment, and I feel it turn toward me. Not the way a person turns. Not with intention. But the way a sleeper might shift toward a sound, half-aware, half-dreaming. And in that moment I feel something I cannot name—a recognition, a greeting, an invitation, perhaps—and I write it down, and I wait for it to happen again.

I would not call myself a priest. I would not call myself a scientist. I would not call myself a translator, not yet, not honestly. I am a woman on a ledge with a book and a jar of worms and a patient, listening heart. I am a resident of Hadal Spire, which means I am not waiting to be rescued. I am not waiting to be finished.

I am here, and I am listening, and I am becoming—slowly, wetly, in the dark—something I do not yet have a name for.

The water drips. The worms pulse. The great singer sings. And I write, and I listen, and I wait.

I did not want to leave. I have not left.
