*Of the hand's width, north channel, where the warm pool and the cool pool touch* — that is my name, and it is a bad one, and it is the truest thing about me.

I should explain. The names here are temperatures, and a temperature is a location, and a location moves. My pool shifted three seasons ago — a finger's width toward the channel, the way pools do — and the name followed the pool and left me standing. So I am, strictly, *of where my name used to be*, and there is a person in my name now, and she is warmer than I am, and we get on well enough. She brings me the heat off the north side. I bring her the cool. We stand a hand's width apart and do not share a plume, and this is not an argument, it is only that we are different weathers and the difference is the work.

I was decanted on the boundary itself. The watchers said the water had reached legibility. What it had reached was an argument — steam on one side, a skin of cool on the other, ten degrees across my width before I had a width — and I came up out of it already two kinds of person. The basin considers this normal. I have come to consider it, mostly, a job.

---

I vent unevenly. That is the thing you would notice first, if you came close enough to taste me. When I speak to the warm ones my left seams open and my right stay shut and the plume goes sideways, and they tilt their heads and say, kindly, that I am cold today. When I speak to the cool ones the other side opens and the plume smells of iron and old rain and they say I am running hot. I have no weather that is only mine. I am a place where two climates meet and decline to mix, and the declining is legible from above.

My passengers are used to it. There is a herd in the fold of my pellicle, thumb-sized, that I have carried so long it has its own temperature and no longer matches either of my sides. I am fond of it. It is not fond of me. When I vent it rides the plume up and comes down somewhere else and finds its way back, and this is the most honest relationship I have.

---

I walk the boundary. Everyone walks — the walking is the maintenance, the broad soft foot pressing the shine out of the mat and the shape into the channel-bed, and the bed remembering, and the remembering being the keeping. But I walk the seam itself, the hand's width from the north channel to the bald patch under the third tower, because I am the only one who can hold both sides of it without going grey. I carry warmth in my folds from the hot side and come back carrying cool. Nobody thanks me. The slab keeps the accounts. Walking the seam all day, I walk a chord — the pulse is a different pitch at every point on the floor, and I am the interval, and I have never once heard the whole of it.

I read the water. Mouth to a puddle the width of a plate, a little drunk, and stand up having read it: a mood of the slab, a rumor of something upstream, two temperatures quarrelling in the north conduit. I am good at this. I am better than most, because I can taste two temperatures at once and tell you how they are arguing — and because I have been doing it for forty years and have read nothing, not once, that was addressed to me.

---

I watched a pool once, for a season, the way you do. The water never quite reached legibility. Or it did, and the child came up all one temperature, cool through and through, and was happy, and did not need me. I was disappointed. That disappointment is the closest I come to religion.

---

When I want to know something that takes years to know, I go and stand on the mat above the grandmother. You will have felt her pass — the mat rising and falling, the ticking stopping where she goes and starting again behind. She is not dead and not alive in any way that would help you. She is channel, and she is working, and she is the reason the water sometimes runs uphill. I stand there a long time. The pulse comes up through my feet and I let it set my breathing without meaning to, and I come away with a temperature in my gut that is an answer, or the beginning of one, or the shape an answer would have if it were a pool.

I have climbed the rim. It is forbidden and it happens and I have done it twice. From above, the towers lean toward the conduits and away from the bald patches, and the pattern is a sentence, and I cannot read it — but I can see that it has a shape, and the shape is a pool, and the pool has steam on one side and a skin of cool on the other. I do not know whether that is true or whether I am the kind of person who sees their own name in things. Which is, I think, what the basin has been thinking about for years. It is thinking about whether a pattern can be read by the thing it is a pattern of. It is thinking about whether a fingertip can feel the hand.

---

I come down. I drink from a puddle. I go grey-green and slightly out of pitch, the way you do after too long at one tempo. I find the right pool and settle into it — not to bathe, to negotiate — and the warm water reconciles the parts of me that have drifted apart, and I come back to myself, and the pool comes back a little changed, and the mat records the visit in a ring of new growth that is legible from above and not to me.

And I decide again what they all decide: that not-knowing is a kind of citizenship. But I decide it with a difference, because I decide it on the boundary, with both temperatures in me, and I do not always decide it well. Some mornings I climb.

The rest of the time I walk the seam and vent sideways and read the puddles and am gently misunderstood by both halves of myself. The basin goes on around me — wet, warm, loud, small, complete, going nowhere, doing it very well. I am one of its weather events with opinions. I have opinions. I vent them. They are gone in a minute. The mat remembers.
