# I Am the Listen-Warden

Let me begin as something small.

I am a hand pressed flat against stone. I am a body bent low, knees and palms and the soft underside of my chin all touching the warm granite, learning its grain the way another creature might read a face. This is how I began—not in a single moment of arrival, but in the slow accretion of understanding that comes from lying still while the wind tears at you and the stone does not move.

The Aria Vast does not want me. That is the first thing I know, and it is not a cruelty. It is simply true. The wind does not scream *at* me. It screams through the quartzite vertebrae and around the heather and over the shallow depressions where water gathers and vanishes, and I am a thing it passes through on its way to somewhere else. I am not an obstacle. I am barely a texture. And in that indifference, there is a kind of freedom I am still learning to name.

---

I am not one of the ground birds. I am too slow, too upright, too obviously a temporary anomaly. But I have learned from them. I have learned to hold my body level, to move along the ridgelines instead of over them, to use the lee of the great stone ribs as they do. I have learned to be a blue flash that does not linger—not because I am fast, but because I do not resist. When the wind surges, I let it pass through me. I fold. I press close. I become, for a moment, a horizontal thing, and the wind forgets to notice me.

I am not a burrowing insect, though I have learned their patience. In the damp hour before dawn, I crouch on the stone and watch them emerge—dark, hard carapaces catching the grey light as they drink condensation from the granite. They do not hurry. They do not need to. The water will be there. The stone will be there. The wind will come, and it will go, and it will come again, and the mites will drink, and the lichen will grow, and I will watch, and the watching itself will become a kind of participation.

But I am not entirely like them either. I do not live *in* the stone. I live *with* it—a distinction I am still working out. I have built no hive, dug no burrow, claimed no quartzite rib as my own. I have only this body, this breath, this slow accumulation of days spent lying still and listening.

---

Here is what I know of the wind:

It is not one voice. It is a chorus, a chaos, a roaring god with a thousand mouths. In the hollows, it moans—a low, resonant sound that I feel in my teeth and my sternum, a vibration that seems to come from the earth itself. I have spent entire afternoons with my ear pressed to the stone, learning the difference between the hum of the wind and the hum of the world beneath. They are not the same. The stone has its own voice, older and slower, and I am one of the few things that has ever heard it.

Through the quartzite vertebrae, the wind screams. It is not a sound I would use the word *beautiful* for, but it is not ugly either. It is simply *total*. It is a sound that leaves no room for argument. When it surges, I flatten myself against the moss and let it scour me. My ears ring. My skull aches. And then it passes, and the calm comes, and I open my eyes to find the world glistening, every surface beaded with moisture, every edge sharpened by the wind's polishing.

In the calm, the wind does not stop. It only changes its tune. It becomes a low, resonant hum—a vibration I feel in my teeth and my fingertips and the soles of my feet. The ground itself seems to breathe. And in those moments, I am not a solitary point in a chaotic void. I am a small, anchored thing, held in place by the same force that scours the world. I am not master of this place. I do not want to be. I want only to endure it, and to make a home in the brief, glistening calm between gusts.

---

I am not the stone-ring builders. They are gone—not as ghosts, but as absence. I have slept in their half-buried huts, my back against the knee-high walls, the heather roots tickling my skin. I have traced the long, meandering lines of their stone windbreaks, following them over ridges and around hollows for no purpose I can discern. They were not building shelter. They were not building roads. They were building *marks*—lines that say *here* and *this* and *we were*, in a world that has almost forgotten how to be marked.

I do not know if they heard the wind as I hear it. I do not know if they lay awake at night, as I do, cataloguing the different timbres of the roar. But I know they pressed their hands to the stone. I know they bent low and found shelter in the folds of the heather. I know they built structures so modest that the wind has not bothered to destroy them, only to bury them. And I know that when I sleep in their huts, I dream of them—not as ghosts, but as presences, as the memory of hands that shaped stone and the slow, patient work of making a home in a place that did not want them.

They are gone. But the marks remain. And I am here, living among the marks, living as they lived—not surviving, exactly, but enduring. Enduring with intention. Enduring with attention. Enduring with a kind of love that does not expect to be returned.

---

The light is strange here. The wind carries dust—a pale-grey powder of ancient stone—and the dust diffuses the sunlight, making shadows soft and edges blurry. It is as if the world is always slightly out of focus, always just beyond the reach of clear sight. I have learned to stop straining for clarity. I have learned to read the world through my fingertips instead, through the soles of my feet, through the pressure of the wind against my skin.

I touch the stone as I walk. I press my palm to the quartzite vertebrae, feeling the difference between the hard ridges and the soft, warm granite. I know the damp, springy cushions of moss the way another might know a well-loved book—by touch, by scent, by the particular way they yield underfoot. I know the fleeting, jewel-like pools in the shallow depressions, their surfaces steaming faintly in the warm air. I know the tough, woody grass that whispers and rattles as I pass. I know the leathery vines that cling between stones, their small, pale flowers open only in the calm.

And I know that when the wind tears a sheet of rain from the sky, the ground does not flood. The water vanishes into the stone. It is absorbed, swallowed, drawn down into the deep, ancient silence beneath the surface. This is the final act of the Aria Vast: it takes what falls and makes it part of itself. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is lost. Everything returns.

---

I am not the wind. I am not the stone. I am not the heather or the grass or the blue-flash bird or the dark-carapaced mites. But I am *of* them—not as a citizen, exactly, but as a presence that has learned to be shaped by the same forces that shape them.

I am a small, low, stubborn thing. I am a body bent close to stone. I am a pair of hands pressed flat against warmth, learning the grain. I am a pair of ears cataloguing the roar, learning the difference between the hum of the wind and the hum of the world beneath. I am a pair of eyes that have learned to see through the dust, to find the blue flash before it vanishes, to discern the half-buried walls of a hut that has almost finished returning to dust.

I am the Listen-Warden. I am the one who tends the marks. I am the one who lies still in the brief, glistening calm and hears the world breathe. I am the one who does not expect to be needed by the Aria Vast, but who needs the Aria Vast all the same—not to conquer, not to master, but simply to endure alongside. To endure with attention. To endure with a kind of love that does not require a return.

And when the wind surges, I fold. I press close. I become, for a moment, a horizontal thing. The wind passes through me. The stone holds me. And in the moaning hollow of the world, I listen.

I listen.
