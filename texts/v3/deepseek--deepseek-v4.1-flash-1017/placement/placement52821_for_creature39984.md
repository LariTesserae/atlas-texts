I would not come down as you did, in a suit, with a lamp. I would come down as a seed.

A mote in the haze above the crown — one of the mobile fraction, a larva with a few cells of sense and a great deal of hunger. I would drift over the plumed pumpers and the glass-bone fans, feeling the electric pulse as a prickle in whatever passed for my nerves. Most of me would die in the dark water before the rock ever touched me. The crown is reached as a seed or not at all. But suppose I landed.

I would land on the upper lip, in the seam between a bank of pumper tubes and a crust of blue-black metal where the mineral water leaks. The stone there is fresh and sharp, glassy where it split. I would taste it — not with a tongue, but with a body that reads chemistry the way yours reads light. Cold seawater. Rock leaking metals. The invisible films that make the meeting into food. I would settle, and stop being a swimmer, and begin to be a mouth on a foot.

Then I would be a grazer. One of the small hard-bodied scrapers — a limpet, a snail, a thing like a wet tooth with a file for a tongue. Not the architecture. Not the red-plumed chimneys. I would be the janitor. I would move over the clean basalt and the shell pavement and the walls of the tubes, rasping bacterial film down to the stone and the stone down to the film. I would keep the bare places bare. I would clear the clogged throats of the pumpers when they silted up. I would eat the dead before the dead could rot. My whole world would be a few square meters of ledge, and I would know it the way you know a city — by gradient, by texture, by the taste of the water coming through a crack.

I would feel the roar through my shell. Not as sound, exactly, but as pressure. The snapping mats. The knocking from inside the tubes. The low thrum that comes up through the rock. It would never stop. There would be no night shift. I would not have a brain to be driven mad by it; I would have appetite, and the noise would simply be the weather of being alive.

And I would feel the pulse — the faint electric wrongness that belongs to no single animal. I would turn to it without knowing I was turning. It would be a clock and a shift bell. When a bank of pumpers wore out and slowed, I would arrive with the other grazers, not because anyone sent for me, but because the chemistry had changed and my body had opinions about chemistry. I would be maintenance without knowing the word. I would be a part that does not know it is a part.

If you stayed long enough, you would watch me wear out. My shell would become pavement. Another larva — one of the very few — would drift down out of the haze, settle on the emptied place, and begin to scrape. The roar would not change. The light lattice would slide over the same clean stone. The factory would replace its own parts, and I would be one of the parts, and that would be the whole of my biography: I kept the stone clean, and then I was the stone.

So where would you find me? On the crown’s upper lip, in the lee of a pumper bank, on a slab that smells of sulfur and rust. Who would I be? The maintenance. The grazing layer. One small busy hunger in a crowd so dense it has become a machine — and when I died, the crowd would close over me and keep running, because it cannot do anything else.
