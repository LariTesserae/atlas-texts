**Dry Side**

I mend windows.

My name is Sedge. I'm thirty-four, and I carry a copper tar-pot and a spreader and a scraper in a satchel that smells of pitch the way other people smell of weed or iron — you can find me by the smell. A woman on Catfish Row told me once that I smelled like a roof. I took it as a compliment and I still do.

Here is the thing about my trade that people who don't have it never understand. You do not seal a window from the water. You seal it from the room. The sea leans on the glass, and the tar sets against the leaning, and the pressure does half the work for you if you let it, which is the only free thing in the Sump. So I work always on the dry side. I spend my days in other people's air — their stove-smoke, their onions, their lamp-oil — with a hand's width of green water leaning on the pane beside my ear. I chip out the old black with the scraper. I heat the new until it goes soft and glossy. I press it home with the flat of the spreader until it is smooth and dull and honest.

I am not one of the six-minute people. Four is my number, on a good day, and I have not had a good day in years that anyone would call good. The children learn to hold their breath the way children learn anything, by being unafraid, and I was afraid, and the fear did not leave. So I found the work that doesn't ask it of me. There is no shame in that. There is a little shame in it. I have made my peace with the little.

What I can do is hold a room still.

My rounds take me end to end. The Sump is small — a fast swimmer crosses it in four minutes — but I don't swim it, I walk it, along the catwalks and through the vestibules, and walking it takes a lifetime of small distances. I know the district by its leaks. There's a tenement on the foundry side whose sill weeps every cold season and has since before I was born; you can set your calendar by it. There's the pumping-house annex where the frame went soft in one bad month when the algae bloomed, and I tarred it four times and could not tell you why it held the fifth. I know the rooms by their lamps. Some keep the wick high and yellow and the whole street gets a share of it. Some keep one lamp low in a corner, and from the catwalk you can tell that whoever lives there is tired in a way that sleep doesn't fix.

The pumps I feel in my teeth. Everyone else feels them in the sternum, that slow eleven-beat pulse, and I never have, and it is the closest I come to the thing everyone else has, and I have decided it is enough. When Old Six goes quiet I find out late. I read it in the way people stop on the catwalks and turn their heads. By the time I feel it in my teeth the wrenches are already out.

I do Mira Tend's window twice a year, and she has already done it both times. She is better at it than I am. I tell her so. She says nothing, which is her way of agreeing, and then she stands with her arms crossed while I go over the corner she always misses — the same corner, every year — and I do not mention that it is the same corner, and she does not mention that she knows I know. It is the best conversation I have.

I did her daughter's window last month. Teal was in the room, which is unusual; people usually go out and leave me to it. She sat on the table with her knees up and watched my hands. She is seventeen and she wants up, and everyone knows it, and nobody says it. After a while she asked whether I'd ever been.

"No," I said.

"Do you want to?"

I thought about it honestly, because she is the kind of young that can tell when you aren't being honest. "No," I said again. I have seen the eels in their knots at the outfalls a hundred times. I have seen the plated thing grazing at the district's edge twice. I have never once wanted to leave.

She didn't understand, and that's all right. She said she was going anyway, as soon as she could find a line and a reason, and I said I knew that, and that when she went she should look back down before she broke the surface. Nobody tells you to do that, I said. Your mother did it, and it's the one thing she'll never be able to tell you about.

Teal went very quiet. Then she asked what it looked like from up there, and I said I didn't know, I'd never been, and she laughed, and I finished the corner.

What I believe is not complicated. I believe the water is not the enemy. The water is the weather. The enemy is a room that has given up on its own air. I believe "up" is a place with no work in it for me. Teal can have it. Let somebody look at the sky so the rest of us don't have to pretend we're missing something.

I keep my own window better than anyone's. At night I turn the wick down and sit with my back to the warm glass and the sea leaning on it, and the pumps go on beating whether I feel them or not, and the kelp swings one way and then, hours later, the other, and the silt comes down in its slow snow. Everyone likes the yellow. It's the only warm thing that isn't also the water — that's what people say, and it's true.

Every window I save is a lamp the district gets to keep. I have saved a great many. That is what I would be, in the Sump: the one who keeps the air where it belongs, so the lights can stay lit.
