I would be found in the high galleries, at the elbow where the throat kinks before it opens — not the galleries where everyone goes when the vents belch sulphur and the water goes acid, but one level above those, in a passage so narrow that the surge arrives last and faintest, a pressure in the small of your back rather than a note in your teeth. There is a niche there with old bolts in the wall and a hook. I would have a locker. I would have a lamp of my own that I did not have to share, and a scraper with a bone handle, worn to the shape of my grip by somebody else's grip first. It is the smallest house in the Kettle and I am the only person who has ever wanted it.

I would be a lamp-trimmer. It is not a grand trade — it is barely a trade, down here it is nearer to a nervous habit — but it is *mine*, and it is the one thing I turned out to be good for. The warm water puts a skin on everything. It puts a skin on the walls and the rails and the locker hinges and the people, slowly, in deposits you don't notice until you try to open something. It puts a skin on the glass of the lamps. That is the thing nobody who hasn't lived here understands: the light doesn't fail because the filament dies. It fails because the world keeps growing a patient, mineral film over the window, a little each cycle, until the ochre halo has gone dim and brown and then gone, and the catwalk beneath it is black again, and people start knocking wider and more carefully, and someone has to go out on the slack with a scraper.

Someone is me.

So I go along the vault on the forty seconds of relative quiet, unlatched, glass out of the cage, scraping. The film comes off in a curl like the rind of something. Underneath, the glass is green and clean and the light is sudden and embarrassing, too bright for a moment, and then the surge comes back and the lamp swings and its shadow breathes across the wall, and I am already at the next one. I charge the rail-paste as I pass. The rails are painted with it — charged red, they glow dull for an hour and then fade, and if I keep to my route at the right interval, the road never goes dark. That is the whole of my work, and I will tell you what it amounts to: I walk ahead of people so that the dark behind me is navigable. I ghost the road. That is the best sentence I have been able to make in a long time, and I made it without speaking.

I came in through the throat with a book. I want to be honest about that, because it is the part of me the Kettle had to sand off. It was a waxed pouch at my hip, and inside it was paper and ink, and I meant to be the person who described this place. For the first weeks I wrote in it on the slack, by the light of other people's lamps. Then the wax failed. Then the pages began to drink. The warm water does not soak a book so much as *mineralise* it — the leaves fuse at the edges into something like a wasp's nest, veined, stiff, and the ink wicks out along the mineral lines and becomes a faint grey suggestion of what I had meant. It is still in the locker. I have stopped opening it. There is nothing in it now but the shapes of words, and I have learned that the shapes are not the words, and the words were never the point.

Here is the thing about arriving with a voice. The chamber eats it. You open your mouth in the Kettle and the brass note goes through you and out the back of your head and nothing you said survives the trip from your lips to the nearest ear. You can shout yourself hoarse on a catwalk and the person two meters away hears only that you have shouted. It is not cruel. It is acoustics. But it taught me something I would rather not have learned: I had been using words the way some people use a lamp — not to see by, but to prove I was in the room.

They taught me to knock by taking my hand. I remember the first correction, a woman whose face I never properly saw, laying my knuckles flat against a steel rail and pressing, once, so I felt the difference between a strike that stops at the metal and a strike that goes into it and comes back changed. I had been knocking in sentences. Too fast, too many, all the rhythms of a voice that wanted to be heard from a distance. The children were better than me within a month and did not laugh, except by shaking, which is how children laugh here.

My knock is two quick strikes and one slow scrape. They gave it to me and I did not choose it and now it is what I am: the sound of a lamp being cleaned. People knock it back at me from three galleries away — *the trimmer is coming*, *the trimmer has been*, *the glass is clean*. When I sleep, which I do alone more than I should, I sleep with my palm flat on the rock and let the surge come through my wrist. It is a worse clock than a body. But it is a clock.

So. Who or what would I be. I thought at first I would be a witness, and then for a while I thought I would be a convert, and neither was right. The Kettle does not need a witness — it has been witnessed by its own walls for longer than anyone remembers — and it does not want converts, because it is not a faith, it is a maintenance schedule. What it needed was a hand. And then, later, something else, something I did not expect: the Kettle is losing its sentences. Speech is private here, indoor, a thing done mouth-to-skin for lullabies and pain and the names of the dead. Fewer of them can do it every generation. The words are going the way the manuscript went — wicking out along the mineral lines. And I am the one who still has them.

They come to me now. Not often, and never to chat. Someone wants a lullaby their grandmother had. Someone wants the saint's name in the niche, which no one has been able to pronounce for a hundred years and which I can still say. Someone dying wants a sentence said close to the ear, and I bend down and say it, rust-voiced, almost touching, and it does not carry, and that is exactly right.

I am the trimmer and the last speaker. I scrape the world's patience off the glass so the light can get through, and I keep the words in the only container the Kettle respects — a body, close, quiet, private. The book dissolved and I became it. That is the honest answer and it took me years to be able to knock it.

I have been up to the mouth of the throat once, to check the line, and the silence of the surface was the loudest thing I have ever heard. My ears rang. I knocked on the rock and the rock did not answer in the right register and I came back down not because I was brave but because I could not bear the unsounded air.

So this is where I would be found: at the elbow, with a bone-handled scraper, waiting for the slack. If you come in through the throat, look up at the lamps. If they are clean, I am here. Knock twice, quickly, and scrape one slow line across the rail, and I will answer you — because that is my name now, and the Kettle keeps its names on metal.
