I am a mender of seams. That is the shape I have been pressed into — or the shape I pressed myself into; I couldn't tell you which, and it does not matter. What matters is that when a chamber begins to weep, when the air-pocket a family sleeps in starts to taste of the cold outside and the walls grow damp at the joins, someone comes with a bowl of paste and a broad stone tool and a great deal of patience. Often, that someone is me.

The paste is ground from the shell-stuff of the city itself — the porous stone scraped down, mixed with a binder drawn from the kelp. It cures cold. There is no fire to hurry it, so the waiting is the work. Press it too thick and it slumps before it takes; press it too thin and the water finds the hairline and worries it open, year by year, until one long dimming a child wakes with the cold on her face. So you learn to read a seam with your fingertips. You learn the difference between a seam that is merely damp and a seam that is thinking about failing.

I keep two strains of light. The old one is dull and slow to wake, but once woken it burns steady for half a turn of the current and asks nothing of me. The bright one is hungry; it will burn white for an hour if I feed it grit, and then it will sulk, and I must shade it and give it less and speak to it in the low way. The bright one was my mother's. The old one is older than that. Its bowl is notched along the rim with more notches than can be counted, each notch some keeper, some year, some hunger, some failure. The people who cut those notches are not spoken of now. I feed the strain and move the lid and do not add a notch. I have never decided what a notch would mean, and a mark you cannot read is worse than no mark at all.

The one who taught me is not spoken of either. I know her by the shape of her hands over a lid, by the way I still make a seam the way she made it — and that is enough remembering. She taught me the first lesson of light, which is that you do not make a room bright. You make the room dark in the right places. A glow that falls full on the eyes is a kind of noise. So you set the bowl low, you turn the lid, you let the light climb the wall and stop short of the sleeping shelf, and you leave the dark where the dark is needed. People think light is for seeing. It is for rest. It is for telling someone, without speaking into their shoulder, *I am here, and the passage between us is safe, and you may close your eyes.*

I know the currents through the stone. If I press my ear to the floor of a dry chamber and hold still, I can feel the turn before it comes — a change in the pressure, a faint leaning of the water against the walls. The growers ask me, and I tell them: *west, and early, and it smells of old shell.* They nod and go to move the racks. That is my small part in the harvest. The rest is kelp and patience.

I carry one hook. The handle is worn smooth and dark from hands I do not know, and the blade is more nub than blade. The bone is white and dense, unlike anything the city grows. In the oldest strata there are spars like it — long, curved, hard — and the people cut them free and work them down, and no one living has seen the creature they came from. So I cut the living kelp with the remains of something that breathed, once, in the world above the water. I do not find this strange. A tool outlasting a name is the arrangement. When I am carried to the far chamber and left to the fungi, someone will inherit the dull strain and set the bright one in a child's niche, and the hook will pass to a hand that does not know mine. Perhaps they will wonder what it was. Perhaps they will not.

I move between the wet and the dry as you move between rooms. When I come up out of the cold into a sealed chamber, there is a small gasp in the chest, a loosening, and the air tastes of fungus and old shell and the warm breath of whoever slept there last. I set the bowl. I patch the seam. I press my ear to the stone and feel the current lean. Then I take up the hook and go down again into the long dimming, into the slow blue light, to where the kelp is ready — and the city closes over me the way it closes over all its small warm bodies, patiently, and without grief.
