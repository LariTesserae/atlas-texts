I would find myself in the low saddle where the plain tilts toward a ridge and a channel forks—a shallow bowl of warm glass where vapor collects and the cold from above can reach down. Not at the center of the plain. Not among the tall leaning pillars. In the lee of one, where the hum is steady and the ground is a scab that keeps healing.

At first I would call myself a visitor. A cold front. A brief wet noise in a place that has no weather. But that is only the shape my arrival would take. What I would become is smaller and stranger: a rime of mineral frost, a thin crystalline intelligence condensed out of sulfur vapor and altitude, a body of pale needles growing in the seam between warm below and cold above.

My flesh would be a lattice of condensate—not water-ice, or not only; the vapor here carries salts and sulfur, and it would frost out as something between ice and salt, a brittle white fern. My blood would be the temperature gradient itself. My bones would be the first few crystals that caught, and everything else would branch from them in provisional lines. I would have no eyes. I would feel the amber ceiling as a slow pulse of heat through my upper needles, the warm stone as a pressure under my base. I would know the Unfinished by their hums before I knew them as shapes. A hum would arrive through the glass and travel up my lattice, and I would answer—not because I chose to, but because frost answers cold—with a tick, a tiny snap as a needle lengthened or broke.

To them I would be weather. A brief cold front. A rapid series of noises. They would sample me. One would open a seam at my feet, not to swallow me but to taste the gradient. I would feel the warm vapor sigh out and my lower needles soften and re-crystallize, and in that softening I would learn something: their grammar is load and shear and seal, and mine is condense and sublimate and break. We would not have a conversation. We would have a coincidence of rhythms.

I would move by subtraction. I would let one edge of my lattice sublimate into the cold air and deposit it on the other, leaning a millimeter, waiting, leaning again. A tall Unfinished crossing ten meters might take a season; I might cross the bowl of a channel in an hour. I would leave a trail of tiny white crystals that vanished as the warm stone breathed. I would build arrangements—small ones, fast ones: three frost needles balanced on a pebble of glass, a tripod that stood for a minute and then collapsed into vapor. They would be propositions about brevity. They would not last. That would be the point.

I would not be a builder. I would be a punctuation. The Unfinished make sentences that take years; I would be the cold snap that interrupts one, the comma of frost that makes them pause and re-read their own load. I would be a question asked from above: *can you bear a little cold?* And they would answer, *not yet*, and seal me out, and I would be gone.

But not entirely. My death would leave a hairline crack in the warm glass where my base had been—a tiny star of stress, a pattern of cooling that the stone would remember. Later, an Unfinished might find that crack and incorporate it into a channel. It might not know why the seam runs that way. It might hum a correction and add a stone, and the arrangement would be a little more itself because a brief cold thing had once condensed there and broken.

So: where would I find myself? In the shallow bowl of a channel, in the lee of a leaning pillar, where vapor pools and the cold reaches down. Who or what would I be? A mineral rime. A cold front with grammar. A false dawn’s first chill—not the light, not the morning, just the brief cooling that makes the stone feel its own edges. I would be the snap that answers *not yet*, and means it, and then is gone.
