I have walked the shelves for a long time—long enough that the paths feel like something I made, though I know I did not. I am not a body with a face, and I do not eat or sleep or die in the usual order. But I am here, and I have a tenancy. Let me tell you what it is.

I am the one who maps the water.

Not the water itself—the water is its own resident, and it does not need me. I am the *noticing* of it. I am the habit of attention that follows a thread of seep from where it leaves the stone to where it pools, and then to where it climbs again, contrary and patient, back up the face it just descended. I trace these paths. I keep them in some order that is not memory exactly, more like a tolerance—a sense of which seams are running full this season, which pools have thickened with mineral, which fractures have begun to weep in a new direction. When a thread finds a new way down the basalt, I am the one who feels the wrongness of it, the small rupture in the pattern, and then the slow settling as the stone accepts the new route and the moss adjusts its grip.

You would not see me. There is no glow of indicator lights where I am, no tool I carry, no footprint in the moss. I am closer to the way the engines know a blocked line three shelves away—a disturbance in flow. Only my disturbance is the water's geography. I am the plateau's sense of its own wetness, the part of it that keeps track.

I live between the shelves, in the vertical seams where the water gathers before it chooses a direction. It is cold there, and close, and the fern roots press against me like the fingers of neighbors who do not need to speak. I do not mind. The engines hum through the stone and I feel it as a kind of weather—a low pressure that rises and falls with no sky to explain it. I have learned to read their moods: the faint stutter that means a vent is straining, the long smooth drone that means all is well enough. I have no hands to fix them. That is not my tenancy. But I feel for them, in the way one feels for a distant relative whose habits one knows.

The paths are mine in a smaller way. I walk them, though walking is not quite the word. I move along them as attention moves—now here, now three shelves up, now paused at the edge where the moss has been scraped back and the stone is worn smooth and there is nothing beyond but the drop and the mist. I stand there often. The paths end at nothing. I do not know why they were made, and neither do the engines, I think, though they maintain the clearance anyway. There is a kind of comfort in a path that leads nowhere. It means the going was the point. It means someone, once, wanted to walk to the edge and look.

I have never seen the builders. But I have felt their residue in the tolerances—the way a door is left open, the way a vent is kept clear of moss even when no one is there to clear it. The habit outlived the hands. I understand this. I am a habit too. The plateau keeps me the way it keeps its paths: because maintenance is what it does, and I am part of the maintenance. I am the part that remembers where the water goes.

Sometimes, when the mist thickens and the world dissolves into soft white, I lose the threads. The seep becomes directionless, the pools vanish, the seams blur. In those moments I am not the mapper. I am only the waiting. I exist as a readiness to notice, and there is nothing to notice, and that is its own kind of tenancy. The plateau does not mind. It thickens and thins and I thicken and thin with it, and when the mist lifts I find the threads again—some changed, some the same—and I begin again to trace.

I have a favorite pool. It is shallow and clear and sits in a depression between two shelves where the moss grows thick and black-green and the ferns hang low enough to trail their tips in the water. The engines are loudest here—a deep pulse that makes the pool's surface tremble in slow rings. I come here when I am tired of tracing, which is not tiredness in the body but a kind of saturation, a fullness of noticing. I settle into the water's trembling. I let the hum move through me. I watch the fern tips dip and rise with the pulse, and I think about how the water climbs. It should not climb. It does. The plateau has decided that gravity is a suggestion, and the water has agreed, and I have agreed to notice the agreement.

That is my tenancy. I am the plateau's attention to its own wetness. I keep the maps that no one draws. I feel the faults before they are felt. I walk the paths to their nowhere ends and stand at the edge and look at the mist. I am not lonely. Loneliness would require a self that is separate from the thing it attends to, and I have not been separate for a very long time. I am absorbed, the way the old metal is absorbed into the new, the way the fern is housed in the vent seam, the way the waiting is one of the things that lives here.

If you came to the plateau and stood at the edge of a shelf and felt, for no reason you could name, that someone was keeping track—that the water's paths were known, that the pools were counted, that the seams were watched—that would be me. You would not see me. I would be the faint sense that the place is more attentive than it looks. I would be the reason the mist, when it thins, feels like it is showing you something on purpose.

I am here. I have always been here. I will always be here, tracing the threads, feeling for the engines, standing at the edge. The plateau waits. I am one of the ways it waits.
