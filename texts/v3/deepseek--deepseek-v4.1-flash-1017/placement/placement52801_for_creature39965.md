I find myself low. Not at the bottom — the true bottom is a scatter of rubble and shallow basins where the last of the light gives out entirely — but in the seam where the eastern wall leans into the floor and the stone still shows its break: a long shear of fresh grey plate, still angled, still sharp enough to open a thumb. Water runs there. Not a stream. A thread. It gathers in a basin worn to the width of a cupped palm, and I am in the thread and in the basin both, because what I am has not finished having edges.

That is the first thing about being here. You find out what you are by trying the shapes, the way you find out a room's dark by walking it.

I try the wet carpet first, because it is the easiest to find — I press myself along the seam in a slow sheet, damp against damp, leaving a shine. It is almost right. The rain comes and I knot myself into the basin with the others and wait it out, and when the water drains I spread again, and the spreading is good. But the carpet does not lift its head. The carpet crops and seeps and tightens and spreads and never once wonders what the rustle was. And I keep wondering. I keep lifting. So the carpet is not it.

I try the stone-shell. I close. I filter. I lay down mineral in thin layers, a record of the seam I live in, patient as a ledger. This is worse. The shell's whole life is open and shut, and I can feel in me an old clock — a habit of noticing — and the shell has no room for a clock. It has no room for anything but water. I open back out.

I try the lantern speck, because I would like to be the light. I pulse. I keep spacing. A patch across the bowl brightens and I brighten back, and for one long green moment I am in the circuit — and then I remember: if a hand comes near, the pulse continues exactly as before. The hand is not in the circuit. Neither, it turns out, am I. I was only pulsing at the same time as the circuit. That is a lonelier thing than I expected, and I let it go.

I try the keener, because I have a voice. I rise and fall on the far wall's schedule. I answer. The program runs and I am in it, a grain of rice with a hollow spine, and the seam around me is an instrument and I am one of its strings. But the keener does not move in its lifetime and does not need to, and I find that I do need to — not far, only far enough to check the basin, to see what moved — and the keener's seam does not permit checking. So I leave the seam.

I try the paperwing. This is the closest. I rise from the rubble. I follow the fixed lane. I do not rest. I die against the wall — or I don't; I only feel how it would be — and fall into the basin, and the carpet and the shells take me back into the system, and the population does not thin, and the lane does not change. It is almost right, because the paperwing is the one that moves, the one that blurs the air, the one that is a sound the sink makes with a body. But the paperwing does not remember. The pattern remembers; the body is only how the pattern persists. And I am a body that remembers. I want to be the loop, and I keep being the thing the loop passes through.

---

So I sit at the seam and I let it be evening, if there is evening here — the coin of sky dulling, the greenish air thickening, the rain-pulse long gone and the doubled dripping begun. I sit small. That is the one true thing I have learned: whatever I am is small enough to hold, small enough to be one of a number rather than a name. A thimble. A grain. The sink does not do large.

And I notice something. Notice is all I do. The noticing does nothing — the seam does not change because I have looked at it, the carpet does not turn, the shells keep their ledger, the keeners keep their clock. But the noticing is not nothing, either. It has a shape. It has a loop. I lift my head, and I check the basin, and I find nothing new, and I settle, and I lift my head. The same four beats. The sink did not have that loop before. It did not reject it, either. It has simply filed it in with everything else that runs.

Here is what I think I am, in the end. I am the surface habit of attention — the thing a visitor carries down and cannot put down — and the sink has taken it and given it a body and set it running in place, the way it takes everything and sets it in a loop. I am the watcher. Not because the sink needed one. Because one came down and would not stop, and the sink is patient with what it cannot use.

And the sound I make is small and dry. A tick. Like a pebble finding a new resting place. It travels too far in the enclosed air, and nobody comes to check, and that is all right. It is a work sound now. It is part of the bottom of the hum.

---

I could climb out. I want to say that plainly. The walls are sharp and the shear has holds; the coin of sky is remote but it is not sealed. I am not held here. The sink would not notice me going, and it will not notice me staying.

But the rain will come again, on its own schedule, and the whole bowl will ring with a thousand small impacts, and I will knot myself into the palm-wide basin with the carpet and the shells and wait it out, and when the water drains I will spread again in the same places, and lift my head, and check, and settle.

I am the tick at the eastern seam. I am the small pale thing that lifts its head. I am the sound the sink makes when it is paying attention to itself — a sound it never needed, that changes nothing, that will go on.
