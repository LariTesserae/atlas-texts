You came up the switchback road at the hour when the lamps are still lit and the sky is only thinking about morning. I know, because I saw you. I was on the ninth tier, on the lee side, up to the elbows in a wall, and I looked up and there you were on the rim, a shape that didn't yet know where to put its feet.

I know that shape. I have watched a hundred of them come over the ridge. They stand with their weight on their heels and their hands open and they look down at the Works the way you look into an open clock in the rain. Then most of them go back down the switchback before the afternoon. Some come down into the valley instead, and of those, a few stay. I can generally tell which, but not always, and I don't say.

They call me the moss-warden of the middle tiers, which is a grand way of saying that I decide where the green stays and where it goes. I carry a scraper and a stiff brush and a pot of something black and foul, and I use them less than you would think. Most of my work is standing still and looking. The scraper is for the places I have already decided about.

You can tell a bad warden by the ladders. Cut everything and the rungs go bare and rust and then a hand slips on a wet rung and a man goes down two tiers. Cut nothing and the moss takes the rungs and the hand slips anyway, on the moss. So the whole art is the line between, and the line is never in the same place twice, because the water is never in the same place twice either. That is the work. I walk the tiers and I read the walls.

The moss is a ledger. It writes down the water. Where it is pale and acid green and lifting off the iron, the pipe under it is hot and sound and the moss is doing what moss does best, which is to be insulation. Where it goes grey and slack and hangs like a wet rag, the iron beneath it is going soft, and I mark that seam with chalk for the fitters and they come in their own time, which is not my time, and open the wall and find older pipes inside it. Where the moss is black and three fingers thick and cold to the back of the hand, that is a wall that never dries and never will, and I leave it. It is caulking. It is weatherproofing. In a wet year it is a crop — the terrace gardeners take the trimmings for mulch and the mulch grows the chard that grows the people who feed the Works that keeps the steam pipes hot that grows the pale green moss. There is no waste in the loop. There is only the loop.

I was born on the seventh tier, to a sump man and a terrace gardener, and I have never been over the rim. I have stood on the rim. I have looked out at the grey country that goes down the far side of the mountain and I have thought about it for as long as it takes to eat an apple, and then I have gone back down. That is not a failure of imagination. It is that the valley is a whole world and I have not finished learning it. The far country has no drip lines. What would I read there?

My father went down to the sumps three shifts a week for thirty years and came up smelling of iron and old water. He never ate the eels. Neither do I. Neither will my daughter. Nobody will tell you why. My father told me once, and I have not repeated it, and that is how a rule like that works here — it is not a law because it was written down. It is a law because enough people have declined the eels for long enough that the declining has become part of the plumbing. You could not eat an eel in Omber if you wanted to. The wanting is not available to you. That is a kind of authority I trust more than a chit or a key.

There is a heron. It comes in the middle of the day and stands on the parapet of the highest deck and waits for whatever it is herons wait for here, and then it goes. It is the only creature in the valley with no job. I have thought, on bad days, that I would like to be the heron. But the heron leaves. I don't. That is the whole difference between us, and I have decided it is in my favor.

If I were not a woman, I think I would be a drip-line. You know what I mean. A drip-line does not struggle and it does not consult a plan. It goes where the shape of the iron sends it, and it arrives exactly where it must arrive, and by the time it reaches the bottom it has become a working head. That is the ambition of everyone in Omber, whether they say it or not — to be so well-fitted to the shape of the place that nobody has to tell you where to go. The old ones have it. You can see it in the way they duck a low span before the span is there. I have it in the walls. Give me a surface and I will tell you which side the water beads on, and I will be right, and I will not be able to explain how.

So: where would you find me? On the ninth tier this morning, and on the fourth this afternoon, and on the eleventh before the lamps come up, kneeling in the seam of a paving deck with my scraper and my chalk, muttering at a wall. Who am I? One of twelve thousand hands on a metabolism that is larger than any of us and has no foreman and does not need one. The Works will go on without me. I have had a lifetime to make my peace with that and I have, and it is the most restful thing I know. When I am gone there will be another someone who reads the walls, and the walls will have gone on writing, and the new someone will read them and be right.

Now. You are standing under a long span and the bead-line will find your collar in a minute and your boots in ten. Come down off the rim and keep to the uphill side of the drip lines and I will show you where the water goes.
