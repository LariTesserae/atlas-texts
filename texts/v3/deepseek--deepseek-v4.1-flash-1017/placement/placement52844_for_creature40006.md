First I would know a degree or two. The water of the Kettle is basement-cold and tastes of iron and lime, and then there is a place where it is warm, and the warmth has a shape: a cylinder, a lip, a shelf of mineral grown out from metal. I would find myself at the north gallery, in the warmest few square meters, in the lip around the bearing housing. I would be one of the warm ones—a pale-limbed thing in the knot, a species that lives nowhere else. I would not call the cylinder a bearing housing. I would not call the hall a hall. I would know warmth, shelter, and the pulse.

The pulse would come through the metal every forty seconds, the slow turning of the great worn impeller somewhere in the center of the water, and it would make the soft parts of me flex and recover. I would know the current as a preference: face the loop, hold to the warm cylinder, do not drift free. I would know the crawlers as weather—the scrape along the rail, the faint oil in the water, the way every limb in the knot draws in until they pass. I would know the valve pulse as season: colder water coming up through the deck, and every soft thing in the Kettle turning to face it before it arrives. I would turn too. I would feel the temperature change in the lip before the water reached me.

Around me the hall would do what it does. The crust on the twelve columns would tick, and the ticks would come back off the dome half a second late, and I would not call it the dome; I would call it the far return. The flickering lamp would be a green rumor on the gallery, brightening and dimming, and I would know it only as a change in the water’s color, not as a clock. The blind grazer’s hum, a fifth below the turning, would pass through the housing and through me and make the whole world beat. I would not know the hunter. The hunter’s country is the dark between the lamps; mine is the warm lip.

We would be crowded. Heat is a resource, and the warmest few square meters are the most valuable, so the knot would be tight. I would have no privacy and no word for privacy. I would have the pressure of other limbs, the shared opening and closing, the mineral lip at my back. When the cold pulse came, we would all turn together, and then the water would settle, and the pulse would resume, and we would loosen again.

I would not remember air. I would not have a word for outside. The pressure doors would be a temperature at the edge of the loop, a place where the water tastes slightly different, and I would not go there. I would not be waiting for anything. I would be one of the warm ones, a pale knot in the north gallery, taking the machine’s small excess of heat and giving back the only thing the Kettle asks: that I face the current, feel the pressure, and do not drift free for long.
