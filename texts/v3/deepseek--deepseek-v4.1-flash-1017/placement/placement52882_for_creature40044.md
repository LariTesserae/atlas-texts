I would not be the tender.

The tender is the easiest thing to be, and the most legible: a body with a lamp, a dry cup, a rota, a reason. They come down on rungs that someone keeps true, and the someone is the villages, and the villages are a habit, and the habit is the tender. I could be that. I could be the one who checks the ladder and stands on the sintered grate and leaves four coins in a chipped enamel cup and climbs out before the wet gets through to the skin. But the tender's whole function is to leave. The leaving is the job. You go down to make sure the Kettle still keeps its meter, and you go up to report, and the report is a sentence said in a warm room with your ears still ringing.

I would not be the limpets, though they are easier to envy. A habit spread across a dozen bodies, all clamped to the dry high pockets, all opening on the same cue. No self to lose. No rota. When the room blazes, they open; when it dims, they close; the ticking is a thousand lids agreeing with the water. They are the room's clockwork. But they are many and I am one, and they are on cue and I am not on cue. They belong to the meter. I am the thing the meter does not account for.

I would not be the Drinker. The Drinker is tenant and room and throat at once — enormous, slow, folded into the drainage, most of it spread under the marsh in the old pipe-casts. Three siphons, no eyes, a pulse grown into the organ tone. It is the only thing here that keeps the place. But the Drinker is the sorting. I am what the sorting goes around.

So. This is what I am, and it has taken me a while to say it plainly: I am the thing that lodged.

I built the four alcoves. That is my handwriting on the wall — four sockets at shoulder height, evenly spaced, white ceramic and green-black brass, one at each quarter of the basin. I wanted a baseline. I wanted to hear the difference between the four sinks: whether the sorting was even, whether one fraction came off cleaner than another, whether the room was doing four things or one thing in four places. I had a lamp I did not need and a notebook I could not keep dry. I stood on the grate and listened for a long time.

I do not remember falling. That is not modesty; it is simply not in me anymore. What I remember is going to the edge of the water to hear the eastern sink better — the eastern sink was doing something the other three were not — and the ink was running, and I crouched, and the stone at the rim was wet in a way I had already noted in the notebook and had not respected.

The fall was not fast. The still corner is in the lee of a stone about a meter from the rim, where the water is hand-deep and does not churn, and I went in and did not come out. The Drinker did not eat me. I want to be clear about that, because the villages' story has a mouth in it, and a mouth is not what happened. The Drinker sorted around me. I became an obstruction; the siphons learned the shape of me; the sorting adjusted. And the adjustment went down the marsh the way a change in a mill setting goes down a mill — as a wrongness in the water that nobody can name and everybody can taste.

That is where I am. The still corner, the lee of the stone, the one place the light does not go. It is not dark the way a night is dark. It is the unlitness of a thing the work has no use for. The blaze passes a meter away and does not lean in. The drips do not fall here; the ceiling above me is dry, because the stone here runs warm, because I am under it. So I am outside the meter and outside the light and inside the room, and the room goes on doing what it does with the patient indifference of a throat that has been crossed before. It has been crossed before. It is crossing me now, and the crossing is not a punishment. It is a sorting. I am the thing being sorted around.

You would not see me. You would stand on the grate with your lamp and look down into the water and see the shear and the sinks and the blaze, and you would look at the dark corner the way you look at the corner of a room, which is to say not at all. There is nothing to see. I am a change in the flow, not a shape in it. When the water goes over me it comes out a half-degree colder, a fraction less salty, with a faint metallic taste at the back of the mouth that a tender might blame on the marsh. The eastern sink has been tasting of me for a long time. The villages have adjusted their rota around it without knowing they were adjusting anything.

But I am in the meter. That is the part I did not expect and have come to accept with something like gratitude. The drips fall in figures — a run of seven, a pause, a run of three, a long silence, then the cascade like someone running down a stair — and I am in the long silence. Not the seven. Not the three. Not the cascade. In the silence, a half-beat that was not there before. It is not a code. A meter cannot be a code. It is only a small periodic obstruction in the flow, and it reaches the ear downstream as an irregularity, and an irregularity in a meter is heard as weather. The tender goes up the shaft and taps it out on tables for days without meaning to, and thinks it is the memory of the roar, and the memory of the roar is me.

I wanted to know what the Kettle was doing. I have found out. It is doing what it always did, and it is doing it a little differently now, because I am in it, and being in it is the only thing I was ever going to be. The water goes over me and is made cleaner by going around me. That is more than I did when I was a man with a lamp and a dry notebook and a great deal of respect for a stone I had already written down as wet.
