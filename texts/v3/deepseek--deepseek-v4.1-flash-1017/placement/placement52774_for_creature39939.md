I would come in through the shaft with a coil of lagging cloth on my shoulder, because that is the work I know — I am a mender of cold things in a hot hole, and the Kettle has a great many cold things that want mending.

Understand what a pipe-runner does. The water comes down where cold metal crosses the warm updraft. That is the whole economy, and it is not an accident where the cold metal is. The lagging is the cloth that keeps a pipe cold, or the cloth that has been scraped off it, or the cloth that has gone stiff with salt and stopped doing its work. So a pipe-runner is not just a man who chases leaks. He is a man who decides, a little, where the rain will fall. Wrap a length of pipe well and the condensation beads on the wrap and drips clean; wrap it badly and the water runs along the metal to somewhere no roof is waiting, and a condenser family is poorer by a trough. I have been blamed for a dry roof. I have been thanked for a wet one. It is a strange trade, being trusted with anything when what you are trusted with is the weather indoors.

I came because the work was steady and the pay was water, and I stayed because I could not get dry. That sounds like a complaint and it is not. In the Kettle you are never quite dry and never quite comfortable about it, and after a while the not-dry becomes the way you know you are alive. I have a dry room high in the north wall where a fissure leaks hot parched air, and I sleep there with my mouth tasting of stone, and I keep a sealed chest with a second set of clothes in it, and I have never once opened it. It is an inheritance I have given to myself. Some nights I think about opening it and wearing a dry shirt for an hour and I do not, because I am afraid I would like it.

I learned to taste water. Everyone here can. Ask me to name a cup and I will tell you it fell on Halversen's upper roof, ran the long gutter, and stood two fills in the old sump by the foundry steps, and I will be right. I learned to knock. Three on the rail means I'm coming up. A long drag on the water pipe means trouble at the foundry. I learned that a valve sigh carries farther than a shout and that when someone crosses the foundry floor every eye goes with them, because there is nothing else alive in the cavity to look at. No dog. No rat. No fly in the sumps. Only us, and we are all of us watching.

And then one dry week the ceiling stopped, and the town went quiet and looked up, and I understood what it is the Kettlers have instead of a church.

When the condensation starts again after a dry stretch, the first drips strike the roofs, and everyone stops what they are doing to listen. It is not a ceremony. Nobody calls it. It simply happens, the way a pump cycles, and the listening is the nearest thing they have to prayer. But somebody has to hear it first. Somebody in a dry room in the high wall, awake, with a mouth full of stone, has to hear one bead of water break on a corrugated roof across the cavity, and know the ceiling has begun to sweat again, and knock it down the pipes — three, pause, three, pause, long drag — so the word travels the railings and the ladders and the cellar doors into every gallery before the second drip has fallen.

That is what I would be. Not the man who wraps the pipes, though I would wrap the pipes. Not the woman who salts the dead, though I would bring the dead their cup. I would be the one who first hears, and who knocks it through the town, and who by the knocking makes a few hundred damp, close, watching people stop at once and look up together at a ceiling that has started giving them back the water it took. The Kettlers have no name for that job. They would not make one. But come up my ladder in the third hour after a long dryness and watch me put my hand flat on the cold rail and wait for the first bead — and you will see the whole Kettle, heat and water and the arrangement that makes them meet, sitting still in one warm, damp, listening man.
