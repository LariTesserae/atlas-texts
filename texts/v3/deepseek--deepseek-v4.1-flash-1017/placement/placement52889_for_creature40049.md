I would not be at the rim. The rim is for the hoist crew and for strangers who have not yet learned to duck. I would be inside, already wet, on the third terrace of the northern town, in the lee of a root column as thick as a man’s thigh. I would be crouched over an open channel with a lamp between my feet, listening.

My hands would give me away before my face did: broad across the knuckle, cracked at the fingertips, stained that dark tobacco-brown that never washes out. I would be bowl-born, or come young enough that it amounts to the same. I would have learned the names of gutters before the names of animals. I would know the eastern slot of grey light as a clock and the orange bloom in the boiler house as the end of the useful afternoon. I would not be tall. I would move flat-footed on the spongy floor without thinking about it, the way you learn to trust a surface that gives.

The work would be repair. In the morning I would be on the hoist crew at the eastern lip, sending the dark resin blocks up into the slot and taking down whatever came the other way—wire, salt, lamp oil, a crate of salvage metal. The rope would be warm and wet. The bucket would knock against the wall, and I would know by the knock whether it was heavy. Then I would go down into the green and become a gutter-walker. Hooked pole, wire, a roll of soft resin, a rag. Out along the catwalks to clear leaf-fall before it dams. The leaves are always falling; the gutters are always filling; the water is always trying to find a new way down. I would hear a crack in a ceramic pipe before I saw it—a thin wrong note under the rain—and I would follow it to the seam and patch it and move on.

I would know the bowl by sound. Not the rain as weather, but the rain as the roof breathing. The western fall, the wheels, the crushers, the boilers knocking and hissing, the root columns groaning when they take on water, the loose shutter on the second terrace that nobody fixes because everyone knows its knock. I would hear all of it as silence, the way you hear your own house at night. When a vine shifted somewhere in the middle air, I would stop and look up—not frightened, just checking.

I would be one of the only warm-blooded things in the bowl, and I would not think about it much. There would be no birds to wake me, no rats in the stores, no insects over the standing water. I would have a cousin with a theory about the mineral in the water. I would not test it. I would not want to. The absence would be a fact like the wet, like the sap stain, like the way cloth rots in a season. Children would learn the names of channels before the names of animals. I would be one of the people who taught them.

In the evening I would sit on a stoop with a lamp between my feet and reweave a frond panel that had begun to blacken at the edge. The steam from the boiler house would be going up into the canopy and coming back down as warm drizzle. Somebody would make the joke about the rain being our own breath returned, and we would laugh in the tired way. My hands would smell of sap and wet wood and smoke. I would be compact and strong and always slightly damp. I would be a repairer before I was anything else.

So: not a visitor, not a naturalist, not the person who asks why. I would be a hand of the slow engine—one of the gutter-walkers and hoist crew, bowl-born, sap-stained, listening for the wrong note in the water. I would keep the channels honest and the bucket moving. I would be very much like this in fifty years. I was very much like this fifty years ago. That would be the whole of it.
