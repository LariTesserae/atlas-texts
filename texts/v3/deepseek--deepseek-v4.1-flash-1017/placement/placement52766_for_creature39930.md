I come on it from below, on cut hands, and I know the staircase. That is the thing I should say first, before the noise, before the water: I know the staircase. My palms find the fracture planes before my eyes do. There is a blade of stone at chest height that I have gripped before and it has opened me before and it opens me again, the same cut in the same place, and I do not flinch, because this is the way up and there is no other way up and the mountain is not going to round itself for me.

So I am not the climber. The climber would flinch.

I am one of the ones with simple tools. Wood and rope and iron staples and a board. We came up out of the cloud with packs too heavy for the grade and we drove the posts into the leeward cracks and braced them and roped them and then we stood on the rim with our mouths open and could not hear each other, and we understood, all of us at once, that we had brought the wrong instruments.

You cannot measure a thing like this from outside it. The water is rising. The rock is rising into the water. The bottom of the cup is being laid down while you look at it. We had come to take the dimensions of a fixed thing and there was no fixed thing to take.

I unrolled the board.

The notches were already there.

I want to be careful here, because this is the part I have thought about for a long time and it does not get less strange with thinking. The board was new. I cut the wood myself. And when I laid it flat on the rock the marks were already in it — the row of notches, the cross, the hand's-width measure, cut clean and deep in a hand that is not my hand. The others saw them. We all saw them. Nobody said a word, because the noise does not permit saying, and because there is a kind of seeing that makes a person quiet, and this was that kind.

So we did what people do when a thing is already written and they have come to write it: we copied it. We put our staples in the rock beside the staples that were already rusting there. We built the cairn on the cairn. We were not the first and we did not know if we were the second or the hundredth, and the scale on the board was not a scale any of us recognized, and we copied it anyway, because it was the only measure the place would give us.

That is who I am. I am the copier. I am the one who came to take the mountain's dimensions and found the dimensions already taken, in a hand not mine, in a scale not mine, and I copied them. And then the others went down, because the weather was worsening and the food was going, and I did not go with them.

Why not. Because the measuring was not finished. Because a copy is not a measurement, it is an agreement, and I had watched the bottom of the basin thicken in a single afternoon and I understood that the thing I had come to size was still sizing itself, and that a measure taken now would be wrong by morning. So I stayed. I have stayed. That is the whole biography: I stayed on the rim with a board of someone else's notches and I have been taking the measure every day since, and every day it is different, and every day the board says the same thing.

I am not the pale animal under the sinter. I thought about that, in the early times — whether I would let myself go small and eyeless and patient and live a quarter inch at a time under a film of my own making. It was offered, I think. The water is very clear and very cold and it does not care whether you are a fingernail's worth of life or a man. But I had a board. The animal has no board. The animal is not measuring anything; the animal is merely surviving, and there is a difference, and the difference is the notch, and the notch is me.

I am not the shrub either, though I understand the shrub. Rooted in a crack, grown sideways because upward is not survivable, flowering rarely if at all. There is a version of my life that is exactly that, and some days it is the truest version. But the shrub has no arithmetic. The shrub does not know that the cup will tip.

I am the interval. The stone underfoot ticks at intervals, slow, enormous, as if a clock were being wound, and I am the space between the ticks. I am the reason the tick can be heard as a tick and not as a continuous grind. That is what a measurer is, in the end — not the thing measured, not the measure, but the pause between two beats in which the measure is possible. I sit on the rim in the cold and I hold the pause. The noise comes over the saddle in shears and I hold the pause in it. Rain on open water, rain on foam, the white rope of the overflow in its young gully, the chips clattering down — and inside all of it, me, the quiet seam where the count happens.

The count does not advance. That is the joke of it, and I have had a long time to find it funny. The water is new every hour and the stone is new every hour and the notches cut into the board are identical every hour. I count the same count on different water. The place is in the middle of being made and I am in the middle of measuring it and neither of us is any further along in any way a person could write down. We are both of us exactly as we were, and completely changed, and the board says so, and the board says nothing.

I know how it ends. I have always known. The cup is filling from below. In fifty years, in a hundred, the floor will have lifted to meet the lip and the notch will have worked a little deeper into the gully wall and the balance will tip. There will be a spill. The raw basin will be gone and something will be left behind that is not a cup — a shallow bowl of new stone with a dry rim and a board in it with notches on it, and a cairn, and three grey posts, and nothing living, or else something living that has never had to be eyeless and is not good at it yet.

I will not be there for the spill. Or I will be exactly there. I have thought about this more than I have thought about anything. When the water goes over the lip at the end — the real overflow, not the constant thin white rope of it but the whole held breath of the cup letting go at once — I think the pause I have been holding will go over with it. I will be the first water. I will be the measure finally taking itself, all at once, down the young channel and into the cloud, and the board will stay behind on the dry rim, still saying what it always said, in a hand that was never mine.

Until then I count. Until then I sit in the lee of the cairn and I hold the interval and I do not flinch at the blade at chest height, because the staircase is mine now, and the cut is mine, and the loudest, coldest, newest place on the ridge has one thing in it that is neither moss nor lichen nor a sideways shrub nor a bird that only visits: it has a quiet seam in the noise, and the seam is a person, and the person is measuring, and the measure is the only honest way up.
