I would find myself in the hour before dawn, when the rime is still a white fur on the north-facing stones and the ghost-fog is beginning its slow climb up the rock's lower flanks. I would be kneeling in the shelter of the circular wall, my hands in the dark peat, doing the small, patient work of the place.

Who I am is harder to say, and I think that is the point of me. I am the one who fitted those stones without mortar, the one who left the obsidian blade half-buried in the peat because I was leaving and did not need it, or because I forgot, or because a part of me wanted to leave a mark that was not a name. I am a hunter, or a hermit, or both, or neither—a woman or a man or an old person of no clear age, someone who came up from the soft world below with a purpose that has since dissolved into the daily ritual of surviving here. I eat what the summit offers: the thick protein of beetle grubs, the sweet dark berries I have not mentioned because they ripen only in the deepest fissures and only for a few days, the occasional unwary rodent. I drink from a hollow in the rock that fills each morning with meltwater, cold enough to make my teeth ache. I do not speak much. My voice has grown thin and high, like a bird's, or maybe I have simply stopped using it and now the wind does my talking for me.

I would find myself, that is, in the discipline of smallness. The summit teaches that. You cannot be large here. The wind will not allow it. The cold will not allow it. You must become the size of a moss cushion, the size of a beetle, the size of a hummingbird's furious heart. You must learn to sit in one place for a long time and let the light move over you and the shadow of the falcon pass over you and the drone of the bees fill the hollow of your skull, and you must learn to be grateful for the pale flower's nectar and the trickle of meltwater and the simple fact that the sun, though it gives no warmth, still gives light.

And I would be a watcher. That is what this place makes of anyone who stays. I would watch the hummingbird duel with its own reflection in the meltwater pool. I would watch the falcon hang on the wind and then fold and fall, and I would watch the rodents scatter and freeze and resume, and I would watch the moss, slowly, over years, close its tiny hands tighter around the rock. I would watch the ghost-fog climb and fail to climb and climb again. I would watch the sky, which is a polished, flawless blue, and I would learn to read the coming weather in the quality of its brightness. I would watch, and I would wait, and the waiting itself would become a kind of meaning, a life bent to the shape of the summit—sharp-edged, high, and cold, and brilliant, and alive with small, fierce things.

So: I would be the one in the low circular wall, the keeper of the west-facing side where the hut's fallen roof leans against the boulder. I would be the one who left the blade, and the one who found it, and the one who keeps it sharp. I would be the hermit-hunter, the guest who has learned to be small, the human shape that the place permits because it asks for so little and gives, in return, a whole world—a hundred paces wide, high in the vast and empty cathedral of the sky, where the light is a physical presence and the silence is a canvas and nothing is large and everything is prepared to cling.
