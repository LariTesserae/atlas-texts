I would find myself in the low seam of the terrace where a milky-turquoise stream slips between two vent mouths—one breathing white, one sulking yellow—and the moss holds my weight like a soaked cushion. Not at the center of the basin. Not on the mossback’s back. In its wake.

At first I would be the visitor: a warm, fragile interruption in the steam. The air would taste of sulphur and crushed green. After a day my watch glass would etch; after three, the silver on my wrist would go matte black. I would sit still because stillness is the only currency here. The vent-walker would settle nearby, a log-sized hump of wet gray and orange mineral bloom, and the copper frogs scattered across its back would chime softly at my stillness. I would think: I am temporary weather. It will pass.

But the terrace is not interested in visitors. It is interested in steady states. If I stayed—if I let the air have its way—I would stop being temporary. The mountain would not transform me in a flash. It would do it the way it does everything: by drip, by condensation, by slow mineral exchange. I would become the small thing that lives in the mossback’s trail.

Call me the runnel-sifter, though the terrace has no need of names. I would be a low, wet-bodied grazer no longer than a fern stem, with a shell like a flake of vent crust—orange-banded, porous, not quite stone. My eyes would be weak amber. My world would be temperature and chemistry. I would taste the air along a fringed lip and feel the mountain’s deep thrum through my belly. I would move in the intervals between drips.

I would live in the pressed-moss channels that close behind the vent-walker’s feet. When it passes, it leaves a trail of warm runnels; I would follow at a distance, after the rain has diluted the acid of its skin. I would rasp the brittle mineral masks it molts. Old crust would become new shell. I would carry fern spores and moss fragments in my gut and leave them in my shining trail. I would drink from the milky turquoise streams, never the scalding pools. When the sun came hot through the steam, I would retreat under a broad funneling leaf and let the heavy drops fall past me. My shell would grow thin orange bands; the air would etch it; geckos would taste me once and remember. I would not be fast. I would not need to be.

I would know the mossback as weather, not as god. Its soft kettle-sigh when overheated would be a sound I timed my movements to. Its trail would be my road. Its shed crust would be my pantry. I would not be the mountain’s lung or its furnace. I would be one of its small recyclers, a slow, wet thought in the factory’s gut, turning what the vent-walker sheds back into moss and fern and the next thin band of shell.

So if you came looking for me, you would not see me at first. Look in the warm footprints behind the vent-walker, in the low gullies between vent mouths, where the moss is pressed and the water gathers. Look for a glint: a small, orange-banded shell moving by fits and starts. That is where I would be. That is what I would be.
