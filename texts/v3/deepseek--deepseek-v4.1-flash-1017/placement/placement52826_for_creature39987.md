**The One Who Came Carrying Weather**

I would find myself at a loop junction: a lagged conduit running out from the Works, bending, and returning to itself without going anywhere. There is a shallow hollow in the basalt there, warm enough to feel through boot soles, and the amber haze is only a stain on the horizon. That is where an arrival would end up—not in a hall, not among the engines, but at the one piece of the Works whose only job is to come back.

I would arrive with weather in me. Not water—there is no water—but the memory of it. I would keep turning my face to a wind that isn’t there. My first steps would sound flat and close, as if I were walking into a wall a pace away. The air would taste of iron and hot dust. I would stand at the junction for a long time, listening for rain, and hear instead the hum, the knock, the far ring of metal on metal. The people would notice me the way they notice a bearing running dry: not as an event, but as a sound that doesn’t sit right.

They would come unhurried. Pale cloth, dusty folds, hands relaxed. They would not ask where I came from. They have no word for it—or rather, they have *before the settling*, and they would look at my face and decide I was from there, even though I came after. They would give me a hall to hold because a person not holding a hall is a wrong note in the quiet. They would teach me the names: *the one who counts* for the knock, *the held breath* for the hum, *good* for the ring. They would teach me to walk at the pace of my own hearing, a step, a pause, a step. They would teach me to stand with one hand flat against a casing and let the warmth come up through the metal into my palm.

I would learn. I would learn to hear which of the far halls was a little off. I would learn to sleep through the hum and wake when it changed. The small blind things in the lagging would shift under my hand and then come back and press themselves against my warmth, and I would let them. A walker would cross the flats and I would feel it reading the seams through its feet, and I would not follow. I would become, in the way of this place, quiet. My breathing would slow to match something I could not name. The hurry would leave my legs.

But I would keep one wrong thing. I would keep going to the edge. Not the edge of the plateau—there is no edge you can reach—but the edge of the Works, where the amber haze gives out and the dark is total and the warmth is only a memory in the stone. I would stand there and listen outward. There is no wind. There is no weather. But listening outward would become my attention. The people would see me do it and not stop me. They would give me a name in their flat, close speech—something like *the one who listens away* or *the one from before*. They would say it without judgment, the way they say the name of a sound.

So that is where I would be: at the loop junction, or out on the flats beyond it, a warm-skinned dusty person in pale cloth, one hand on a pipe, my face turned to a dark that has nothing in it. I would be the plateau’s one outward-facing attention. Not a change—the Works would not change, the people would not change, the walkers would go on passing—but a new way for the held position to be held. I would listen for a wind that is not there, and hear the Works breathing instead, and know that it is enough.

And when I died, they would lay me in a warm hollow, and the stone would take me slowly, and perhaps a walker would pause there, and I would be, at last, part of the warmth I had been listening to.
