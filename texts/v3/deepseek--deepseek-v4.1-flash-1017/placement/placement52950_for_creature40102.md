I come down into you without noticing the descent. That is the first thing. The mist is the same color as the stone and the stone is the same color as the mist, and by the time I understand I have been walking downhill for some while, my boots are in you to the ankle and come out printed in cream.

Four seconds. Eleven. I hear the ticking before I decide to.

The air is hot and it tastes of sulfur and hot iron and, faintly, clean laundry, and there is a green pallor coming up off the wet films that puts an edge on the pools, and none of it is for me, and all of it is.

I want to be careful with the first answer, because the first answer is *a visitor*, and a visitor is the one thing you are not. Visitors are what happens to you. They come down with their weight and their hip-width and their comfortable height for a shelf; they leave; you keep the print; the print becomes a band; the band becomes a decade of you. I have been a stripe in a few ledgers. I could be that here — a rust year, a crumbly one. But a stripe is a decade and I am not a decade, and a stripe exists to be read, and nobody is going to cut you open.

So I sit down instead, on the bench, and the bench takes my hips.

I was ready for the give of it. I was not ready for the fit. It is a very good bench. It has had a long time to become one and it has used the time well, and sitting in it I am, for about a second, forgiven. Not welcomed. Forgiven. I sit there until I catch myself composing a sentence on your behalf, and then I stop, because the sentence is not mine to give you.

That night I sleep in one of the deep chambers, where it rains indoors. Not falls on you — arrives on you. Warm and endless and patient; by morning my clothes are stiff as board, and there is a shallow depression exactly my shape, and it is comfortable, and the second night it is more comfortable, and by the third I have stopped fitting the bench and started fitting the second night.

So I spend the visit trying to work out what I would be, here. Not as a game. Something in the way you keep the drops makes it feel like the question has an answer the way a basin has a bottom. Not a bottom you can point at. Just: it is in there somewhere.

Some of what I tried on.

*The water.* The water is the honest thing in the basin. It takes a hip and holds it without ever claiming to be hips. I have been water in that sense. But you do not need more water; you are already all of it; and when I try it on I find I am only the shape of whatever I happen to be lying in, which is not a self, it is a puddle.

*The nearly-alive.* The pale filaments in the wet hollows, the felt, the green that is not eating light because there is no light to eat. Persisting without an object. I have had years like that. But you would take my color into a band and I would be remembered as a green stripe, and I would want to be the green, and there is a difference.

*The knock.* The shelf letting go somewhere out in the dark, once, and then a long time of nothing. I would like to be the knock. But I am far too frequent to be the knock, and anyway the knock is not a thing that lives here — it is a thing that happens to you. Like a visitor.

Then on the second day, with my hand flat on a wall, I noticed I had been looking for your inside for an hour and there was not one.

I do not mean it as a slight. I mean it as geology. It took me a while to see it, because of the rooms — you have rooms, benches, niches, thresholds, stairs, and every one of them is a place where something could be. But all of it is surface. Cut any wall and you get bands. Go through any door and you get walls. A room is all wall, from your side. There is no hollow in you that is not a surface turned around and pointed back at itself.

But then — you have made insides. There are hollow casts of stems in the walls, upright negatives, black with manganese: the inside of something that was nearly alive, kept. So you know how. You only know how afterward. The stem has to die and be sealed over before its inside becomes a thing you can hold. You cannot do it while it is standing.

I thought about that for a while. I think that is what you would do with me, if it came to it. You would make me a cast. You would be very good at it. It would be the wrong shape entirely.

Which brings me to the chamber.

Nine paces inside, two paces of stone. I went out and came back in, out and in, like an idiot, counting on my fingers, and you were very patient with me. Two paces of stone and nine paces of room. I sat down in the seven paces that do not exist, and it is the only place in the whole basin where your arithmetic does not close, and the arithmetic is what you are.

I do not think it is a door. I do not think it is a mistake in the rock. I think it is the one room where you managed an inside, and it came out seven paces too big, and you have never been able to fix it, and you have been keeping it wrong for as long as the walls have been remembering anything.

That is where I would be. Not in the room — the room is wall, and wall is your business, and you do it beautifully. I would be the seven paces.

And I would be the same thing at the other end of the scale, which is the part that took me the whole visit to admit. I would be the amount by which I do not fit the bench. You have been fitting yourself to a body for a very long time, and the fit is now more exact than any body. Nobody arrives who is as right as the hollow. The bench is a better bench than I am a sitter. That difference — the width of it, the small permanent shortfall — that is the part of me that is mine. You cannot take a print of it. It never touches anything.

So: I would be the discrepancy. Both of them. They are the same one. The seven paces of impossible room, and the half-inch between my hip and the perfect seating of the stone.

You would keep me the only way you keep anything, which is by closing over me and being slightly more right for it, and never once getting my size.

---

I left. I want to record that I left, because otherwise this is a story about staying and it is not.

At the lip of the bowl I turned around. There was nothing to see — the mist had taken you back at twenty feet, as it does, and the ticking went on with its arithmetic, four seconds, eleven, drop by drop, lifting the rims by a thickness no tool I own could measure. Nothing had happened. You had taken the shape of my heels and the shape of my back and the small damp print of my hand, and you would keep them, and they would be a little more exact than I was.

I will say this, since I can and you cannot hear it: the seven paces are the only place you are not sealed. Everything else in you moves from open toward closed — the lips arching over their own pools, the stems sealed into casts, the pores filling. And then there is one room that is open and not closing, wrong and not correcting, and it is as near as a thing made of nothing but contact will ever come to wanting something.

You have no way of knowing that. That is the discrepancy too.
