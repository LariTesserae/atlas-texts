I arrive the way most of the Arrived do: with a map that lied about distance and boots that will not dry for a month. I come down the switchbacks counting stairs because the count is the only thing that stays true, and by the fortieth landing I have stopped being a person with a destination and become a person with a rhythm.

The first thing I am given is not a room but a warning. A woman with mineral sheen at her cheekbones—Latch, though I don't know her name yet—takes my map, folds it without looking at it, and tells me three things. Don't argue loudly near stairs. Don't stare at the flowers. If the lamp ahead looks close and the ground under it looks far, trust the ground. Then she hands me a lamp of my own, already lit, already green at the flame, and points me down.

I think I will be a factory hand. I have the shoulders for it, and the Riveted are hiring, and there is a certain honesty to being a silhouette against green flame. But the canyon has a way of assigning work by what you keep noticing, and what I keep noticing is the light.

The lamps here do not agree. Two flames can hang at the same apparent size and be a hundred stairs apart. A window that throws a warm rectangle onto wet stone might be around the next fold or three terraces up and behind a girder. The Arrived walk into walls. They misjudge the last step and sit down hard on the landing. The Cleftborn laugh, not unkindly, and say: *the light is a suggestion.*

So I become something the residents don't have a word for yet, and the stair-keepers, after some consultation, call me a **carrier of the line**—or more usually, just *the walker*. My job is to walk the lamp-route: every evening and every dawn, from the topmost terrace where the chimneys vent into fog down to the lowest pools where the seep-readers keep their ledgers. I trim wicks. I refill reservoirs with oil and, where the flame burns too blue, with a little seep-water, which the lamps like. I note which flames gutter, which burn green, which have begun to throw their light at a wrong angle.

And I keep the thing that turns out to matter most: a **depth-ledger**. Each lamp has a true distance from the next, and those distances drift. A span that was ninety steps last month is a hundred and ten now, or eighty, or the stairs have folded so that the ninety are still ninety but arrive somewhere else. I pace them. I write the numbers in a book I keep waterproofed under my coat. When the fog is thick I read the book by touch and go slow.

The Cleftborn find this funny at first—this Arrived woman pacing stairs with a book, muttering numbers—and then they find it useful, and then they find it necessary, and then they stop mentioning it. That is how a thing becomes part of the canyon. It is absorbed by use.

What I am, I think, is a witness who is also a maintenance problem. The place holds together partly by being seen; I am paid a small wage and a smaller room to do some of the seeing, and in exchange the place asks me to keep my eyes on the *distances* rather than the green. A vine that is watched too fondly will remember your window. A distance that is not watched will forget itself and become a fall.

I learn the courtesies. I leave bread at the niches on my route—there are eleven on the lamp-line, and two that are not on any route and are simply *there*, and I leave for those too. I do not name the Uncounted. I have seen them, in the way one sees a shadow's size without seeing the shadow: a largeness behind the lamplight that made the flame lean. I turned my head. I kept walking. The next morning there was a vein of something bright in the wall where the light had leaned, and I did not chip at it, and I did not tell anyone, and I did not look at it directly, and it stayed.

I am not Cleftborn. My nails are flat and my skin dries in the midday shaft like anyone's. I still keep a curtain over my window and my boots by the door and I still have not finished arriving. But I have found the thing the canyon wanted from me, which is not labor exactly, and not courage, and not even patience—it is the habit of walking the same conditional path every day and writing down what it was, so that tomorrow it can be measured against itself.

At midday I stop on whatever landing the sun has chosen and I look up, briefly, at the full height of the stacked terraces and the impossible green and the water threading down. The place pauses. I put a small stone in the doorway of my room—not for luck; as a witness—and then the shaft closes and the blue-grey dusk comes back, and I open the ledger, and I walk.
