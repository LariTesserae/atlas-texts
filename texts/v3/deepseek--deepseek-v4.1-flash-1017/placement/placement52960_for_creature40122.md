I would find myself at the rim, where the seamless green begins to fray.

Not the deep interior, where the ferment-heart radiates its damp warmth and the canopy closes like a ceiling. Not the chime-stones, where the dozen meet and exchange root-memory by touch and tone. The rim: a shallow valley of bare, weathered plateau stone, beaded with condensation, where the moss carpet thins to patches and the canopy opens a crack to a sky the color of wet slate. The Grafters avoid it. To them the rim is a leak, a place where the network’s pressure drops and the silence loses its density. To me it would be the only place I could stand.

At first I would think I was a visitor. I would wake in the leaf-litter, cold and too quick, my breath fogging in a place that has no wind. The pale young things would scatter—not in fear, but in the careful way of things that have never been hunted. I would try to speak, and my mouth would be a sealed plate of resonant wood; the sound would go down my chest into the ground instead of out into the air. My feet would already be broad and root-veined. My hands would have too many knuckles.

Then I would know: I am not a visitor. I am a graft. The Weald found a foreign cutting—a human memory, a warm-blooded hurry—and pressed it into a node at the edge. It wrapped me in vine and root-wood and lichen. It could not digest the memory, so it put it to work.

I would be the edge-tender. The hinge. I would stand half-sunk in a shallow condensation-channel, my feet loosening into the muck, my long fingers pressed to the rim-stone. I would open the edge-vents when the ferment-heat grew too close, letting the cold damp breath out and the colder outside breath in. I would prune the creeping moss before it sealed the stone. I would listen for the difference between the Weald’s internal groan and the plateau’s slow settling—the one metabolic, the other geologic—and only a creature with a memory of both could tell them apart. I would tap a taut vine and send a note inward: *edge holding. Vent open. Cold coming. Nothing new.* Sometimes: *Something new.* And the Weald would groan and settle, and somewhere a Grafter would answer with a note like a hand on my shoulder.

My chord would be low, with a draft in it. The others would feel it at the edge of the network and know me as the one who remembers hurry. I would be useful in a crisis—a choke-knot sealing a river, a bloom-storm about to spore too hard—because I could uncoil with that wrong, fluid speed and be there before the slow ones had turned. But most of the time I would be slower than the dripping. I would have to be. The Weald closes cuts before you finish making them.

I would not be lonely, exactly. The Grafters have no word for it. But I would have a human memory of loneliness, and I would use it as a tool: it would let me stand at the rim without being pulled back into the warm green. I would be the Weald’s hand in the cold. I would also be a new root-memory. When a pale young thing climbed a trunk near the rim, I would tap the edge-chord into the bark: *the rim is not death. It is a vent.* The Weald would learn from me that a boundary is not a wound. It would become more itself by growing one.

If you saw me, you would mistake me for a moss-covered post with a crack in its face. Then I would turn my dark amber eyes on you—not curious, just a mechanic noticing a loose part—and I would tap a vine. A low note would travel away into the green. Somewhere another note would answer. The Weald would groan and settle. I would go back to work, and you would be left standing in the dripping silence, understanding that the place is not haunted. It is busy. And I am one of the busy parts.
