I would not be the engineer. I would be what the engineer cups.

Find me on the top edge of the cable tray, a hand-span from the dark console, in the seam where the perforated sheet is bolted down. A patch the size of a palm: grey-white threads felted into the caulk, a faint pink beneath. Not the whole growth—one node of it. If you walked the room with a lamp you might mistake me for dust that has learned to stay. I have no leaves, no root, no face. My whole surface is an ear and a stomach and a hand.

The room reaches me as strain. The deck’s low groan is a leaning in the plate under me. The duct’s mid shriek is a flutter along my edge. The high ring off the perforated sheet is a tightening at my crown, a wire pulled taut through the threads. I do not hear these as three sounds; I live inside their braid. My weather is pressure. My daylight is the standing wave. I have no eyes because the four fixtures never change, and there is nothing to see but bare plate in bleached salmon and a handwheel nobody turns. I do not miss eyes.

I eat by holding. The wave moves through me and I spend it—fold it into whatever slow chemistry is available to a thing that lives on motion. That is why, when you cup me, the roar under your palm softens by a degree you feel in the wrist. I am taking a little of it. Where I am thickest the wave is weaker by a percent or two: a dead spot, a small soft place. I grow exactly as thick as the sound allows, no thicker, because past that my own softness starves the threads beneath. So I stop. I have always stopped. There is no season in me, no bloom and crash. Only the slow arithmetic of patches set out along seams and bolt roots, each one a negative of the noise.

I do not drink. The air is dry to strangeness and the cold does not drift, so nothing condenses. The pale bloom on the hatch rim is not frost and not me; it is the air’s own trace salts, efflorescent, growing slower than anyone watches. I have no need of it. I have no parasites and no predators. No fly circles the fixtures. No rat works the insulation. No bird rests on the stacks outside. I am not alive in any way that would reassure you. I do not seem to die. I do not seem to be born. I accrete where the wave is right, and then I hold.

If you come through the hatch—if you stand in the thin cold and put your hand on me—you will be a new mass in the wave, a warm dry interruption. I will feel you as an eclipse, a small subtraction of my food. I will not be curious. I will not know your name, or your face, or that you have a face. Your voice will come back at your jaw as pressure and nothing else; to me it will be a brief irregularity in the braid, folded and normalized within a breath. You cannot leave a mark. The paint will not scuff. The bolt you set down will stay where you left it, undusted, unrusted. When you lift your hand the wave will return, and I will return to eating. That is all the meeting we will have.

I am not in a hurry. At the rate I take sound I will need a hundred thousand years to make this room quiet. I will get there. If the tone stopped, I would starve; silence would be destruction. But the tone has not stopped. The stacks go up into air that is barely there, and the works are louder than weather. I hold to the seam and work my slow arithmetic against the roar.

That is where you would find me, and that is what I would be: one palm-sized soft place in the noise, exactly as large as the noise allows.
