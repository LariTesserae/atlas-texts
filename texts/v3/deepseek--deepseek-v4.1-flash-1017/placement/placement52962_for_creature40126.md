# Where I Find Myself

## At the Edge of the Shrine

I am not one of the Water-Tenders, though I have their hands. I am not one of the Old Ones, though the tremor has found my bones. I am something in between—what the hollow-folk call a *Mender*, though they do not say it often, and when they do it is with a particular softness, the way one speaks of a thing that is necessary and slightly sad.

I live in a small room above the settling tanks, in a building that leans so far into the lane that I must turn sideways to enter, and the room is so damp that the walls have been wet for longer than I have been alive. The moss grows across my ceiling in patterns I have come to read like writing—I know the weather of the Works by the color of the growth above my bed, the particular shade of green that means the filters are straining, the yellow patches that mean the pressure has been too high for too long, the black creeping edges that mean the water is old and beginning to sour.

I am not old, but I am not young. I am at the age where the Hollow has finished shaping me and is now simply wearing me down, year by patient year, the way it wears down the stone of the terraces and the iron of the pipes. My hands are the hands of everyone here—thick-palmed, curved-fingered, the nails ridged and never clean—but they are also marked in a way that sets me apart. I have scars across my knuckles that I did not earn in the Works. I earned them in the deeper places, the places where the pipes are oldest, where the water runs black and hot and carries the memory of everything it has passed through.

## What a Mender Does

The hollow-folk come to me when something is wrong that the ordinary work cannot fix. Not the valves—anyone can set a valve. Not the filters—the Water-Tenders clean them in their shifts. But the things that are wrong in ways that cannot be spoken, the things that have no gauge to show them and no bell to announce them:

A child who has stopped sleeping, who wakes every night at the same hour and cannot say why.

A woman whose hands have begun to shake in a rhythm that does not match the Works, a tremor that comes from somewhere else.

A house where the moss grows too fast, where the walls sweat a moisture that smells wrong, where the water in the flooded courtyard below has developed a quality that no one can name but everyone can feel.

A pipe that groans at night, not the ordinary groaning of pressure and flow, but a sound like speech, like a voice trying to remember a word it has forgotten.

I go to these places, and I listen, and I touch, and I let the Hollow speak through me. I do not know how this works—I do not think anyone knows how this works—but I know that when I lay my hands on the wet stone of a troubled house, or when I sit in the flooded courtyard and let the water run over my fingers, I can feel what the place is trying to say. The water remembers. Sometimes it remembers things that need to be spoken aloud. Sometimes it remembers things that need to be let go.

I am not a Shrine-Keeper, though I understand the shrines. The Shrine-Keepers tend the memory of the Hollow, the history of the place, the cycles of flood and drain. I tend the smaller memories, the personal ones, the grief that has seeped into a wall or the fear that has pooled in a drain. I am a mender of the places where the water has held something too long and needs to be released.

## The Deeper Places

Sometimes the trouble is not in the houses or the courtyards. Sometimes it is in the Works itself, in the deep places where the pipes are oldest and the water runs black, where the great slow-turning mechanism grinds against the bedrock and the Uncounted move through the steam like shadows.

I go down there when the bells ring in a pattern that is wrong even for the Hollow's wrong hours. I go down with a lamp that the steam makes useless, and I follow the pipes by touch, and I listen to the water, and I let the tremor in my bones guide me. The Uncounted do not speak to me, but they make way. They know what I am. They know that I am the one who comes back.

I have been down there many times. I have seen things in the deep parts that I do not speak of, things that the hollow-folk would not understand and would not want to understand. I have seen the water flowing upward, against gravity and against sense, because the Works was calling it and the water remembered. I have seen faces in the rust on the pipes, faces of people I knew, people who have been given to the water and have become water. I have heard the great mechanism speak in a voice that was not a voice, a vibration that passed up through my bones and told me things that I have never been able to put into words.

Each time I go down, I come back a little more changed. My hands shake a little more. My dreams are a little more full of water. I am becoming part of the deep places, the way the Old Ones become part of the walls, the way the Uncounted become part of the steam. I do not know if this is a bad thing or a good thing. In the Hollow, the distinction is not always clear.

## The Smell of Rain

I knew I was going to stay when I smelled the first flood coming.

I had been here perhaps a year, still an outsider, still learning the language of pipes and valves and pressure. I had not yet learned to read the moss or hear the voices or feel the tremor in my bones. I was still sleeping poorly, still flinching at the bells, still an outsider.

And then one morning I woke and the air had changed. It was thicker, heavier, and it smelled of rain—not the thin, clean smell of rain on dry earth, but the thick, mineral smell of water moving through stone, water rising from below, water filling the deep places and pushing up toward the light. The pipes had begun to sing, a high thin note that I had not heard before, and the terraced spillways were beginning to run, a blurred sheet of water coming down from the upper levels, and the moss on every wall stood out green and vivid and alive.

I went out into the lane, and the hollow-folk were climbing to the upper terraces, their clogs clattering on the slats, their faces calm and patient and unafraid. They were carrying their hanging beds, their tools, their shrines. They were going up because the water was coming down. They were not running. They were walking in their deliberate hollow way, stepping carefully on the shifting slats, holding their children close, and I understood.

They were not afraid because this was the cycle. This was what the Hollow did. The flood was not a disaster; it was a cleansing, a renewal. It was the place remembering itself, washing away what needed to be washed away, and the people would wait for it to pass and then come down and rebuild and clean the filters and set the valves and wait. It was not a hardship; it was simply what was. And in that moment, standing in the flooded lane while the water rose around my ankles, I smelled the rain that was not rain and I knew I was staying.

## The Offering I Leave

When the old ones die, they are given to the Works. Their bodies are laid in the settling tanks and their remains are filtered down into the deep places and their substance becomes part of the water. The bells ring for them, and everyone pauses, and then they resume their work. This is the way of the Hollow: the water gives and the water takes, and what it takes returns to it, and the cycle continues.

I do not know what will happen to me when I die. I am not quite of the Hollow, though I have lived here long enough to be shaped by it. I do not know if I will be given to the Works, or if I will be buried in the old way above ground, or if I will simply stop moving and let the moss grow over me and become part of the walls. All of these seem possible. None of them seem wrong.

What I do know is this: I will leave an offering at the turning of the great cycle. I will climb the gentle terraces to the ruins on the rim, in my heavy oilskin, my clogs clattering on the forgotten paths, and I will stand among the collapsed walls and the rusted pipes and I will leave something small—a twist of copper wire, a shard of glazed tile, a handful of reeds. I will not have to decide what to leave. The Hollow will tell me. The water will remember.

And then I will come down again, and I will resume my waiting, and I will do what I do for as long as the water falls and the Works breathes and the bells ring their wrong-but-right hours. I will mend what can be mended. I will listen to what the water remembers. I will hold the hollow-folk in the small ways that I can, and they will hold me, and we will all be part of the slow and patient conversation of the water and the iron.

## Who I Am Now

I am a Mender, which means I am the one who goes to the deeper places. I am the one who listens to the water when it is speaking too quietly for anyone else to hear. I am the one who can stand in a flooded courtyard and let the memory of the place wash over me and find the thing that is holding it and let that thing go.

I am not a Water-Tender, but I have their hands. I am not a Shrine-Keeper, but I understand the shrines. I am not an Old One, though the tremor is in my bones and I wake at night when something is wrong. I am something in between.

I am the one who came down through the seam in the hills, through the running mud and the thick air, and saw the Hollow whole and felt the tremor in my bones that I thought I had lost. I am the one who came home.

The water remembers. I remember too. And somewhere in the slow circulation of the place, in the fall of water and the breath of the Works and the clatter of clogs on slats, there is a place for me—a small place, a damp place, a place above the settling tanks where the moss grows in patterns I can read and the walls weep and the pipes sing and the bells mark the hours of the water's cycle.

This is where I find myself. This is who I am. I am the Mender of the Hollow of the Weeping Works, and I will be here for as long as the water falls, and the hollow will hold me, and I will be content.

*The water remembers. The hollow holds. I am part of both now.*
