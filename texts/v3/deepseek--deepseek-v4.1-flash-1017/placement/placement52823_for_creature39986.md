I am on my knees in the passage. That is where you would find me, if you were looking, and you are not — no one looks down a passage of dressed stone for a person on his knees. The oil can is in my left hand and it has been in my left hand for the length of the passage and it will be in my left hand until the wick is full. My right hand is free, which it has to be, for the corners.

I have worn these corners. Not the way the water has — the water's wear is a curve, an argument made once and never revisited. Mine is a scrape, a knuckle's width, renewed yearly. If you came up the passage while I came down it we would have to solve each other. You would go back first. You are carrying nothing but what you brought in with you, and I am carrying oil.

I am not the keeper. Understand that first, because it is the thing the room will not tell you. She is the keeper. She has the lamps and the snails and the book and the seat worn to her. I am the one who goes further.

Past her room the channel dives under the floor and the pipework goes on and there is a door in the wall behind the moss, and behind the door there is the engine. I have seen it. It is the only room in the world that is not finished. Everything in it is happening — the fire is under the boiler and the fire leans and gutters and will not settle, and the steam goes up the pipe and comes down the pipe warm, and nothing about it is the same twice. That is why I am needed there and not here. She keeps the room's middle. I keep its number.

The fire will not settle. It is the one thing in this place that will not, and it is mine, and I feed it with the oil I do not put in her lamps — no. That is a lie of convenience, and the room punishes those. The lamps take what the lamps take and the engine takes the rest and there is never quite enough of either, and that is another thing the room will not tell you: it is not equilibrium. It is a great many small corrections, made by hand, out of sight, forever.

The gauge. You looked at it and thought it had not moved in living memory. It has not moved because I hold it. I set it from behind, at the pipe, with a valve whose wheel I have greased myself, and the needle sits where I put it, and the fog comes and her thumb clears it, and the fog is mine, because the warmth is mine, because the fire is mine. She is the gauge's weather. I am the gauge's weather's weather. We have never once said this to each other.

The sluice stands open. I opened it. It could stand wider. There is a quarter turn in it and the threads are sound because I keep them sound, and I have had my hand on that wheel, more than once, in the small hours that do not exist down here. A quarter turn. The water would come a little thicker, the level would rise a finger's width, the needle would climb, and she would have to write it down. That is the thing I cannot get past. She would have to write it down, and the book cannot take a mark, and she would have to say a new word.

I have never moved a snail. Once I did — one had got onto the plate near my door, the wrong side of the room entirely — and I put it back on the wall, and when I came through she was looking at me. Not at the snail. At me. It was the only time. Her hands are in her lap and the water pools in them, and the moving of snails is her office, and I had taken a piece of it, and she let me know with her eyes and then with nothing, which is worse than with words.

So I go in through my door and I do not go in through my door. Mostly I set the number and I am gone before the lamps need me.

You will pass me in the passage, going down as you come up. You will not know what I am. I will not tell you, because the can is heavy and the slope is down and because it would be a kindness you have not asked for. You will carry out one certainty, that she will be there, and you will spend the rest of your life finding every needle wrong, and I am sorry for that, and I would not trade you.

Because here is what I carry in: a number. I carry it the way a man carries a coal in his pocket. I check the valve, I watch the needle settle to the place I have chosen, and I know the number is right, and it is right because I am here, and I am here on a schedule she does not count and cannot see. I am her year. She has no seasons, so I am the only month she has, arriving wet and quiet with oil, and she does not know what time it is either. She only knows it is the same time, and it is the same time because I make it so.

The engine will outlast me. The room is built to outlast me; it is the oldest stable thing I have ever stood inside and it is the size of a room. But the number is only as good as the hand that holds it, and my hand is getting old, and the passage is narrower every year, and the knuckle-scrape is not renewed so much as relocated.

One day I will set it wrong. Or I will not come at all.

Then the needle will move, and she will look up, and there will be a new word, and she will not know whether to say it or write it, and the moss will go on growing into the shape it has already chosen, and the fog will gather, and no thumb will clear it.

That is where I am. On my knees, in the passage, with oil. Going the wrong way for you.
