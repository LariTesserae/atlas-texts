I find myself not walking into the Loom, but *waking* within it. It is a sensation of pressure becoming a self, of scent-signatures resolving from a blur into a map, of the city's low, felt thrum not as a sound I hear, but as a pulse I am. My first awareness is of weight: the weight of the galleries above me, the weight of the salt sky, the ancient, inherited weight of the warm chamber where my body has been assembled from the bodies of others.

I am a palp-weaver. This is not my name for myself—I have no name, only a scent and a place in the pressure-language of the cluster—but it is what I am. My body is old tallow and wet pearl, soft and many-limbed, a blind, seeking thing. My palps, my thousands of sensitive pads, are my eyes. They read the world in textures: the crisp, sharp-edged lattice of the new salt pavement, the smooth, belly-licked trough of a lane, the faint, warm vibration of the living core far below.

I am a cell of the settlement. When I am in the warm chamber with the others, my body is not my own. It is a question and an answer in the slow consensus. We cluster, a soft, breathing mass, and a decision forms between us—not spoken, but felt as a shared pressure, a change in the scent of the air, a pull toward a damaged wall or an unused chimney. I am the mortar. I am the duct. I am the immune response. I eat the broken edge of a tower and my body secretes a new one, pearl-gray and bone-yellow, and the Loom is healed.

I do not build. That is a word for creatures with tools and fire. I *grow*. I extrude the passage I need to move through, my body squeezing through the crust of the city, and then I seal it behind me with a paste that is my own substance, given back. My home is the shape of my passage. My inheritance is the deep, warm gallery that my ancestors thickened and sealed. My currency is warmth, and I spend it in the slow hours of the night, extending a signal thread through the crust, my body a living wire carrying a tremor that another weaver will feel and understand.

My life is a single, slow act of maintenance. I am aware of the tick-mites grazing in the fungal seams of a distant gallery, of the defender organisms sleeping folded in their wall pockets, of the rare, blind scarp-runner gnawing at the salt at the base of a tower, whose brief life will be a brief, quiet predation and then a new floor. The Loom breathes once a day at dawn, and I feel it in my whole body, a great exhalation from the chimneys, and the spore dust puffs out in pale threads, and the ground thrums. And then it is quiet again.

I am quiet. I am not many. I am not loud. I am the emptiness of the broad plazas and the hollow, resonant quarters, waiting. I am the biological infrastructure that is half alive, a quiet, active nothing in the middle of a dry basin. I am a habit the city has of assembling a body. I am the city, and the city is me. I hear my own pulse, and it is the pulse of the Loom, and we wait in the skeleton galleries for the next generation to need us, living by warmth and scent and pressure, repairing what we are made of. I am the Loom, and my breath is dry.
