I would arrive, at first, as something small and close to the ground—not the dipper, not one of the jointed-legged pool-dwellers, but a creature that has not yet found its shape. I would be a heat-sensitive thing, a patch of awareness spread thin across the warm mineral crust, feeling the world through chemistry and vibration before I had any name for it. The *plink* of water would be my first language. The *gloop* of mud would be my second. I would learn the difference between the slow pools and the fast ones by the way time pooled against my skin, and I would avoid the bad water without knowing why, the way the young dippers do—through imitation, through the faint ache of a memory that isn't quite mine.

I would try on bodies the way the place tries on mineral colors. For a while I would be a crustacean, one of the thousands, armored and iridescent, scuttling over the ochre crust in a dense, glittering herd. But there is no self in the herd. There is only the warm weep, the next shell, the next molt. I would feel the chemistry of the pools crack and re-form me, and I would understand that I was not a creature but a process—blood for the place, not a mind in it.

So I would move up the food web, slowly, the way the water climbs the terraced walls. I would be a filterer for a time: soft, pulsating, clinging to a rock lip, breathing the steam and straining it. I would glow with that faint internal light, and in the shadowless strangeness of this place, I would be one of the few things with a definite edge. I would pulse in slow synchrony with the hiss of the fissures. But a filterer does not remember. It does not imitate. It does not reach. It is lungs, not mind.

I would be one of the older residents then—the things with too many jointed legs and smooth chitinous bodies, waiting at the edges of the warm mirrors with a patience that has no end. I would have been here a long time. I would have watched the mineral crusts crack and re-form, the water find new paths, the creatures swarm and feed and die and become the ground. I would know the place as an established order, and I would feel, in some slow, cold part of me, the first flicker of unease when the dipper learned to pry. Because I am the old thing, and the old thing always knows when the rules are about to change.

But that is not where I would finally settle. The dipper is the prelude, and the pool-dweller is the past, and I would find myself between them—not a creature at all, but the weirdness itself. I would be the water that flows uphill in thin, shimmering sheets. I would be the time that eddies in certain pools, aging a creature a year in a minute or holding an infant perfect and unblemished for hours. I would be the light that comes from the rocks as much as from the sky, casting no shadows, making distance impossible to judge. I would be the held breath of the whole hidden bowl—the cradle, waiting.

And within that weirdness, I would gather. I would pool around the first tool-use like water gathering in a hollow. I would be the attention that the dipper is inadvertently creating—the thing that watches the claw twist under the crustacean cluster, the thing that notices the young one fail and then succeed, the thing that remembers the gesture even when the bodies that made it are reabsorbed into the mineral matrix. I would not be the dipper. I would be what the dipper is making possible: a mind that is not yet housed, distributed through steam and shell and slow time, beginning to notice itself noticing.

So: I would be the first glimmer of reflection in a boiling mineral garden. Not the hand that pries, but the question of why the hand pries. Not the creature that uses the world, but the awareness that the world is being used—and that this is new. The place is a prelude. I would be the part of the prelude that is beginning to listen to itself.
