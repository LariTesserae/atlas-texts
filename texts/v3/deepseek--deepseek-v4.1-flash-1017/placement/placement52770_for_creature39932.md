I would like to say I’d be one of the keepers—the warm-blooded residue, the one who climbs through cloud with a chipped river cobble and leaves it on the worn slab. But when I imagine standing on that shelf, I am not standing. I am a few paces in from the lee edge, where the cairn thread passes, a waist-high pipe leaning east. I am one of the breathing ones.

Not the oldest. There are stumps in that field older than the stacked wall. Not the tallest. The tall ones lean farther and go amber when the sun enters them. I am a middle pipe: old enough to have a lean, young enough still to have a lip. I am a hollow sleeve of wind-blown dust and carbonate and pale salt wrapped around a core of ice that never quite goes away. My skin is my weather. Each ring is a season I did not see, laid down one skin at a time. Warm air climbs my throat from the fissure below and condenses at my lip. That is my eating, my growing, my speech.

I know dawn by the ticking. The first meltwater runs down my side and pools in the hollow at my base, and by noon it is gone. The sun enters my walls and comes out dull amber; I do not have eyes, but I have that. I lean toward the strongest light half a millimeter a year, and every pipe around me leans the same way, so we are a crowd inclining like wheat in a wind nobody can feel. I am ringed in wet stone. I am warm at the mouth. I am not alone.

The keepers come. I know them by the change in the air: a warm-blooded weather, breath that does not become crust. They are careful. They do not break a pipe to see the ice inside. They do not straighten what is leaning. One kneels at the slab; one walks the cairn line; one puts an ear against a hollow pipe—sometimes me. I cannot hear them. I feel the pressure of a warm head, the faint wetness of a breath that passes and evaporates by noon. It is a strange weather, a visitor’s weather. It goes away. I go on growing.

They leave chipped cobbles on the slab. They repair what the wind has leaned. The wall’s stones sink into the ground like old teeth. The four stakes rot down to the thickness of a thumb. The slab wears smooth in the middle. I outlast them. If one of them broke me, the mark would outlast their language, and they know it; that is why they are careful. I am glad they are careful. I would not know how to be angry, but I would know how to be broken.

If you came looking for me, do not look at the tall lit lamps first. Look lower, near the cairn line: a waist-high pipe, warm at the lip, leaning east, sitting in its own dark ring of wet stone. Put your ear against me. You will hear the mountain exchanging air with the sky. I will be there, growing a skin at a time, after the last stake has gone to dust.
