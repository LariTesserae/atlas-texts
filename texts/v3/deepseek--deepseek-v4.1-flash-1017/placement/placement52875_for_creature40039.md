# Where I Find Myself

I am on the western shelf, where the wind is forced through the gaps in the rock, and I am sitting cross-legged in the dust with a lap-harp across my knees. The strings are sinew, old and yellowed, and I am not playing them — I am *tightening* them, bone peg by bone peg, because the wind has been slackening all morning and the chord has gone sour.

I am a tender. I have been one for long enough that I no longer notice the boom of my own footsteps, that I walk on the balls of my feet the way the herders do, that I have stopped thinking of silence as rest.

Around me the thicket is leaning in. The bellows-plants have swiveled their blind faces toward the harp over the last few hours — slow, patient turnings, the whole grove of them, like a congregation turning toward a pulpit. They are listening. They have heard the chord go thin and they have felt the sap slow in their hollow bodies, and they are waiting for me to make it right. The lungmoss is flat. The cordgrass has stopped its chatter. The gyre is still pouring over the rim, but it is a *quiet* pour, a scraping hiss, and the whole bowl feels like a held breath.

I am afraid, in the way a tender is afraid. Not of the dark or the dust or the long fall from the rim. I am afraid of the thing that comes after quiet.

## The harp

The frame is a rib of something enormous, a curve of bone taller than I am, braced into the cleft of the rock. The strings cross the gap where the wind is forced through. When the wind is strong the harp plays itself — a low complaining chord, unresolved, never stopping. When the wind falters I have to make the noise myself. I have to be the wind.

I have a rattle of shell-shards in my coat pocket. I have a flat stone and a drumstick of hardened resin. I have my voice, which is hoarse from talking to thickets, and which I use now, muttering to the grove, telling it that the wind is coming back, that the chord will resolve, that I have not forgotten it.

The tenders' rule: a silent tender is a tender who has let a grove die. I know this in my body. I have felt a grove go still under my hands, once, three valleys over, and it did not come back. It stands there yet, a huddle of grey sacks and dead sticks, and no chirpers go near it, and the root-mat under it has dried to a crust that crumbles underfoot. I do not want to be that tender again.

## What I am

I am a keeper of noise. That is the whole of my work.

I keep the harps strung and the pegs tight. I tap the bellows-plants with a bone spile and draw a slow cup of sap and drink it, because the sap is what keeps a tender moving and because the plants give it more freely to the ones who have asked politely. I walk the cairned paths and I drum on flat stones in the evening and I rattle the shell-shards when the chirpers need encouragement to rise.

I collect the dead. This sounds grim and is not. I go out in the morning with a sack and I gather up the dry husks of the drummers and the shed shells of the carriers and the brittle bodies of the grey hoppers, and I pack them into the root-mat under the thickets, and in a season they are growth. There is a great deal of death in the Bell and it is all very tidy and very dry and every scrap of it goes back into the listening.

I talk to the thickets. I tell them what I am doing. I tell them the wind is coming back. I tell them the fire-pit is lit and the resin is burning and the smell is good. I tell them about the time the gyre stopped for a whole day and we thought it was the end and then it came back at evening with a roar like a rockslide and the whole bowl swelled up and the sap ran so thick I could have filled a bucket in a minute. They like that story. They swell a little when I tell it.

## What I am not

I am not one of the carriers, though I have ridden them. I am not one of the chirpers, though I have stood in their column and felt the pressure of their wings on my ear and felt the sap rise in my own blood in answer. I am not rooted. I am the one who stands between the rooted and the moving and makes sure the noise goes on.

The herders think the tenders are strange. They come down from the plateau edge with their grey hopping herds and they look at us talking to thickets and they shake their heads. But they bring us salt and bone spiles and resin, and we give them safe paths and sap and the use of the rubbing-posts, and we all understand that the Bell does not care about our opinions of each other. The Bell cares about the noise.

## The evening

Now it is evening. The gyre has backed around and the note of the Bell has changed, gone deeper and slower, and the light through the dust-veil is the colour of a bruised coin. I have the harp tuned as well as I can tune it and the chord is holding, just barely, a low complaint that does not resolve. The bellows-plants have relaxed. A few of them have gone back to facing the swarm-columns, which have started to rise again as the wind picks up. The lungmoss is inflating, slowly, all together, like the floor breathing.

I take my flat stone and my resin stick and I sit by the fire-pit with the others — there are four of us on this shelf, five valleys over there are two more — and I begin to drum. The rhythm is not quite regular. That is the point. The drummers under the crust take up the beat and hand it back, and the chirpers come down to see, and the growth leans in, and I can feel it through the soles of my feet: the whole bowl, awake and listening, drinking the noise.

I am a tender on the western shelf of the Bell, and my work is to make sure the listening does not stop. I do not cut. I tap. I talk to thickets out loud. I keep the harps strung.

And when the wind falters and the Bell goes still, I stand very still in the dust and I wait, with everyone else, for the roaring to come back.
