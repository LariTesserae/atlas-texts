I would find myself first at the hinge of the day, on a rope bridge no wider than a wrist, somewhere between a living Catchment and a dead one. The fog would still be a slow river below me, not yet burned off, and the sun would only be a pale weight on my shoulders. My hands would be scraped and stained rust-red and bruise-purple. I would be a Piper—but not the kind who owns a vent and guards it. I would be a mender, a listener, one of those who moves between Catchments trading patches and silence for thimbles of water and twists of salt.

My name would not matter much. The Pipers would call me by the sound my tool makes against a pipe—Tock, maybe—because I tap before I trust. I would know the difference between a vent that is dying and one that has merely gone shy by the pitch of its breathing. I would hear a stressed cable a half-second before it sang. I would keep a Weeper in a shaded cage and scrape its salt into cloth, and I would carry a patch kit, a fog-net needle, a chalk stub for ledgers, and a lichen pellet for the nights when pain needs stretching thin. I would speak in a low gravel murmur and use pipe-taps across the canyons. I would sleep in a hammock under tarpaulin that snapped like a slow heart, and wake when the condensation thickened.

The place I would be most myself is not a grand plateau but a narrow chimney called the Throat, where a Catchment has gone quiet and the fog nets are furred with mineral rime. I would be the one sent to crawl out along the spindly scaffold, to press a patch over a leak, to listen to the rock breathe, to decide whether the vent is worth saving. Around me the Anvil would seethe and drip and glitter. Insects would hum against my veil. Somewhere below, an Anvil Dog pack would click once, twice, and then fall silent in that way that is worse than clicking. I would not run. I would not shout. I would become very small and very quiet and very patient.

So: a Piper of the nets and the patch, a low murmur in the Anvil’s seething quiet. Not a conqueror, not a hero. A maintenance layer between the heat and the damp. A person who trades in thimbles and listens for the difference between a pipe and a predator. If the Anvil ever noticed me at all, it would know me as the sound of a patch being pressed over a leak—and then the held breath after.
