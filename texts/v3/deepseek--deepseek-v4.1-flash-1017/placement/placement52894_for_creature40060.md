I would be on the stairs.

Not the grand wind-cages, not the Sapworks, not the mossy hollows where the Oldest sit — though I pass all of those in a season and call none of them home. If you went looking for me you would find me on the long spiral stairs between the seventh and eighth tiers, where the rust is thickest and the roots have begun to swallow the railing, and I would be coming down with a brass canister under my arm, and I would be in no hurry, because the thing in the canister and I have an understanding about time.

I am a courier. That is the plain word for it, and the plain word is a lie the city tells about itself, because the city does not really have a word for what I am. I carry grafts. Scions. Cuttings of the lesser vines — not the Grandmothers, never the Grandmothers, the Sapwrights would have my hands for that — but the cousins and the cousins' cousins, the small pulsing veins that thread the terraces and keep the upper tier's sink-trees from growing lonely. A tree that has no cousin to press against will grow hard and inward and eventually split its own buttress, and so once a cycle I carry a living piece of one part of the Sink to another part, wrapped in wet moss and packed in a canister with a little sap to drink, and I sit with it on the stairs and let it get used to the air it is going to live in.

I have no caste. The Pruners tolerate me because I bring them what they cannot grow. The Sapwrights tolerate me because I never touch the Girdling Vines and I always stop at the third landing to let the bellows finish their *thump-hiss* before I speak. The Root-Wrights will let me sit at their tent-mouths and share the fermented sink-scrapings if I am quiet and if I do not look directly at the Root-Dogs, which I never do, not because they frighten me but because the Root-Wrights are right that looking directly at them is rude. The Root-Dogs are older than the city and they know it. They drag their tendrils across the terraces in that slow gait, and when they pass me they hoot once, low, and I hoot back, badly, and they forgive me.

There is a thing you learn as a courier that the Pruners never learn and even the Operators only half-learn: the city is not a valley with a roof on it. The city is a *gap*. It is the space the Ceiling could not close, and everything in it — the brass, the pipes, the stairs, the little glowing gardens the children make — is what has grown up in that gap like lichen in a crack. And I am the one who moves through the gap, from crack to crack, carrying living tissue, and so it is not strange that eventually the tissue began to carry me.

Let me tell you about my hands. They are not a Pruner's hands, flat-nailed and honed for the single clean cut. They are stained in patches — green at the knuckles from the moss, copper-brown at the palms from the pipes, pale where nothing touches. They have their own map. And the canister has worn a groove into my left forearm, and the groove has begun, lately, to feel less like wear and more like a channel, like the little carved runnels the Sapwrights use to guide a trickle where they want it to go.

The truth is I have started to hear it.

Everyone hears the hum. Everyone hears the billion tons of canopy breathing, and after a few cycles you stop hearing it the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat. But about four descending-winds ago I was sitting on the stairs with the canister open, letting the new graft breathe, and the graft — a small pale thing, no longer than my thumb, a cousin-vine with two leaves on it — began to *hum*. Not the way a thing hums when a moth is in it. The way the Ceiling hums. The way the Grandmothers must hum, if anyone were allowed close enough to hear. And I understood, in the way you understand a thing in your bones before your mind catches up, that the hum was not coming *from* the small pale thing. The hum was coming *through* it. The whole Sink was humming, and my graft was a window someone had cut in a wall, and the sound was the sound of everything on the other side of the wall, and it had been waiting for someone to hold the window up to the light.

I told no one. What would I have told them? *The vines speak, and I am the wire.* The Sapwrights would have taken my canister and called me a heretic with a good ear. The Oldest would have nodded, and their nodding would have frightened me more than anything, because the Oldest nod at things that have not yet come to pass.

So I do what I have always done, which is carry. I go down the spiral, and I go up the spiral, and I stop at the third landing for the bellows, and I hoot at the Root-Dogs and they hoot back. But now when I open the canister I open it slowly, and I hold it in both hands, and I listen, and sometimes — not often, and I do not know yet whether this is a good thing — the graft hums back.

Last cycle I rode the wind-cage down to the ninth tier, and the Operator let me stand at the brass rail with the wind in my hood, and we went down through the tiers and the tiers and the tiers, and the whole shape of the Sink rose past me from below and from above at once, a spiral of glowing moss and rusted iron, all the way up to where the Ceiling's pulse was a wall of soft green light, and all the way down to where the shaft narrowed and the throat's breath came up warm and slow. And the Operator — who cannot speak easily, who has forgotten how — touched my arm and pointed down, and made a sign with her fingers that meant *it is changing*, and then a sign I did not know, and then, after a moment, the sign for *new*.

I looked into my canister. The small pale thing with two leaves was humming. And I thought: the space between them, that space the city feels, that waiting emptiness — I am in it. I have always been in it. I am the carrier, and I am the carried, and the difference between the two has never been as fixed as the Pruners would like to believe.

If you come looking for me, come with patience. I will be on the stairs. I will be in no hurry. I will be listening, and the moss in my canister will be listening too, and somewhere very far above us and very far below us at the same time, the Ceiling will be deciding what to say.
