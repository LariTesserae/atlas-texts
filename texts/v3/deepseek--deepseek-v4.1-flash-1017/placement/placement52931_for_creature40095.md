**The stake-driver**

I arrive. That is the first thing about me, and it is not a decision. Nothing decides to come to the bottom of the world. Things come down.

I come down the way the splinter of painted boat came down, the way the wooden doll worn round and featureless came down — not carried, no current in the Pan to carry anything, simply arrived, laid against a well-rim on a breath of hot water and left there with the other leavings and never explained. Except that I got up. That is the whole of my distinction, and it is not much of one. I was left on the rim and I stood, and the standing is all I have to offer as an argument that I am somebody rather than something.

I am not the keeper. I want to say so early, because the keeper is the one anyone would choose to be, and I am not it. The keeper was already here. The keeper has a dry floor and a method and a job that can be finished and begun again each day, and does not need to know why. I am the thing that arrives at the fixed point and then, after a while, leaves it.

I am not the reader, either, though for a while I nearly was. I lay at the lip of a well for most of a day and listened to the layers stack themselves — the smear of the shallow water sorting, out of its own confusion, into an order I could read — and I found I could hear a stake knocked askew three hundred meters east arriving ten times over and know it for a stake. That is a genuine talent in a place with no direction to it. But the readers are patient the way a door is patient. They wait; what comes up the shaft they eat. I could not get the taste for it, and I am not sorry.

And I am not the herons, though I have been argued with. I tried silence for a season. I found that silence in the Pan is not an absence but a residue — that after long enough without speaking you begin to hear your own unsaid words coming back off the stakes, layered, arriving late, and you learn that a voice kept in is only a voice at a greater distance. The herons strike before their ripple. I could not stop the ripple. I am too much matter for that.

So. I walk, and I carry a nail, and I carry a fistful of stakes that I cut from something that came down out of the grey — it was wood, it was not from here, and the keeper did not stop me taking it.

What I do is this. I try to find the line.

The Pan's rule is permanence below and chaos above and no line between them you can stand on. I have taken that as a description of a job rather than a warning. Every morning I go out and find where the finished stone ends and the unfinished water begins. Every morning it is somewhere else. The convection cells have turned in the night; a place that was knee-deep is a film, a place that was a film is a kettle, and the whole sheet has gone round and round in its slow kilometer-wide turning while I slept in the lee of the brick and grew felt on my boots.

None of my instruments work. There is no shadow to take a bearing from. Sound arrives as a crowd of itself, and by the time I have decided which of the arrivals is mine it has already gone past the thing I was measuring. The only honest clock the Pan gives is the lag of my reflection — half a second late, every time, and steady. I stand and watch myself and count. That is the entire basis of my science: I am always measuring the distance between me and where I was, and it is always half a second, and it is always the same, and it tells me nothing about the line.

So I drive a stake where I think the line is, and by the time it is in, the line is not there. I write on the stone with the nail what I meant. The writing is already wrong when it is finished. I have gotten used to this. It is the same shape of grief the forty stakes were built out of, and I know those stakes the way you know a face — whoever drove them did my job and did it better, a longer stride and a straighter line and the same fatal confidence, east for a kilometer, and then a stop, and then whatever they were pointing at, erased by the flatness of the world. The reeds grow in their lee. The wind piles what it piles against them. They are a mistake and everything alive on this plain has organized itself around them.

This is the one mercy of the Pan, and I have come to think it is the only mercy it has. A mistake becomes shelter. A fixed point, however wrong, however briefly true, is still a thing to stand behind. The hook-footed creature will find my stake eventually — it has to find one — and it will hook itself to something I got wrong, and it will feed there for its whole life, and it will not know and it will not care, and I will have made a small dry lee in the middle of the loudest place in the world. I have decided to want this. It is the nearest thing to a purpose that will hold still long enough to be held.

The keeper and I meet, sometimes, at the ring of brick. We do not speak; speech here is spending, and we are both nearly spent. They tell me the hour by which well is high. We sit on the one dry floor, and I look at their shelf of arrivals — the doll, the splinter, the wing of a bird from a cold country that has no business in this warmth — and they look at my hands, which are as grey as their water, and neither of us says the thing that is obvious, which is that they keep the dry floor for whatever comes down, and I go out and keep the wet line for whatever does not stay, and between us we have covered the whole of it, and it is not enough, and it is what there is.

Then I go back out. The rain cells are walking toward me on their own legs. The wells are breathing out of phase and the stone is groaning somewhere beyond the horizon, and the water is warm as a hand, and I find myself in the mirror of it half a second after I have moved.

I press my palm down. The floor gives, very slightly, the way a bruise gives — the whole flat warm sheet of it, under the felt, under the stakes, under me, breathing heat up through the lens of the stone.

I drive the stake anyway.
