The cold arrives first, the way it always does—not as a bite but as a hand laid flat against the skin and held there. I let it press. My blood is dark and slow, and it holds what warmth I make; I do not spend heat on shivering. I am suspended in the grey-green water, and the light comes down from somewhere I have never seen as a rumor of silver, a diffuse and patient gift. Below me the trunk goes down into the lightless deep. Above me it climbs toward a sun I will never touch. I am somewhere in the middle, in a chamber my grandmother's grandmother pruned smooth, and the plant breathes around me.

I am one of the people. Long and smooth-limbed, grey-green and faintly mottled, webbed hands that can wedge a bone blade into a seam of new growth or stroke a young tendril without breaking it. My feet are paddles. Along my flank runs the sensitive line, and it is always reading—the shudder of the trunk, the click of a hunting fish, the chitter of crustaceans swarming in their thousands before my eyes have found them. I breathe through my skin in the water and through my mouth in the chambers, and my voice, when I use it, is a low vibration that carries farther than any shout.

My name is a phrase of rhythm and place. It is not a word I can give you cleanly; it is the sound the trunk makes three levels up when a current is about to stress a wall, and the way my flank-line tightens when I hear it. In the low song of my kin, I am the one who hears the third groan.

The first groan is obvious. Anyone feels it in the wood—the deep, resonant flex as the spire leans in a slow current. The second is a harmonic, a thinner sound that the older ones can pick out if they are still and listening. The third comes before both. It is not a sound so much as a tightening, a pre-echo in the sap, a change in the thrum that runs down the veins of the plant like a held breath. I hear it in my bones before I hear it in the water. When I hear it, I go. I take a bone wedge and a stone scraper and I move through the corridors to the chamber that is about to be strained, and I do what small, patient things can be done: I clear a passage so the growth has somewhere to go, I wedge a seam open a finger's width, I move a bowl of fungi out of the path of what is coming. It is not dramatic. It is maintenance. The plant is slow, and I am slow with it.

I live low, near the oldest chambers, where the light is faint and the deep caller's boom is closer than it is for most. The boom is immense and unseen, a sound that rolls up through the water like a second pulse under the plant's first. The others hear it and are glad it is far. I hear it and I listen to where it is going. It is not my friend; it is not my enemy. It is a neighbor in the same volume of water, and I have learned the shape of its patience. Someone must keep the boundary between the lit levels and the outer dark, and it has fallen to me because my flank-line is the most sensitive and because I do not mind the silence. That is all. It is anatomy and habit, not courage.

At the crossing nodes I meet my kin. We do not crowd. A node may hold two families for an hour and then be empty for a season. We exchange news by touch and low song—a hand on a shoulder, a rhythm hummed into the water, half imitation of the plant and half meaning. My songs are maps. I carry the memory of the third groan: which chambers it warned, which seams were wedged, which corridors were cleared. We have no writing. The memory lives in the tuning of chambers and in songs like mine, and someone must carry the songs between the scattered levels, and that someone is often me. I am a small library made of breath and rhythm, and I am welcome everywhere and belong to no single cyst.

I am not lonely. Loneliness is not a fear among us; silence is a material we use. I curl with my kin in the warmest inner cysts when the cold is deep, and I let it press on me without ever quite entering. I drink sap from veins that have been tapped for generations. I harvest small fish and swarming crustaceans. I feed the fungal bowls and read their generations as a calendar. When one of us dies, we give the body back to an unused cyst, and the plant reabsorbs it, and nothing is destroyed, only returned.

So: I am the one who hears the third groan. I am a tender of seams and a carrier of songs, a small warm creature inside a vast cold body that tolerates me because I tend it. I do not master the Aethelgard. I answer it. The sap's thrum is my clock, the plant's flex is my law, and the silver light from above is the only promise I need—that the world is still there, still growing, still patient, and that I have a place in its long, slow pulse.
