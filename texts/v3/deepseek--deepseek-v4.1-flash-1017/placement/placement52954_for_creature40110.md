# The Count

I came up through the cloud with a slate and a commission, and the cloud did not end when I arrived. It only got nearer. That should have told me something. In the lowland, weather is what happens between you and a place. Here it is part of the furnishings, and it was already beading on my sleeves before I had found the first terrace, and it has not stopped since.

The commission was this: count the people of Ammer. The lowland pays a levy by household and a stipend by head, and it has been paying both to Ammer for a long time on the basis of a number that nobody in the lowland can remember establishing. The lowland is always wanting to know. It wanted to know how many, and whether they were well, and whether the terraces still stood, and it sent me because I could write and because I was expendable in the way that young people with slates are expendable.

I found thirty-one. That took four days, because six of them were in the high coppice and one was in a room I had already passed twice and had taken for empty. I wrote thirty-one on the slate in a neat column of marks and then, on the fifth day, found three more, and had to rub out the column with my thumb, and the thumb was wet, and the slate did not rub clean; it smeared. That was the first thing Ammer did to me. It made my counting provisional.

---

There is a problem with counting that you do not learn in the lowland, which is that a count is a kind of touch that does not have to be repeated. That is its whole virtue, down there. You touch a thing once with your eyes, you write it, and it stays counted, and you go home. The count holds the thing at a distance and holds it still. In Ammer, nothing is held still, and nothing at a distance is held at all.

So the thirty-one — thirty-four — would not stay counted. A woman I had written down on the second day went up the cleft and did not come back down for a month, and I could not tell whether she was still of Ammer or had become something else, some weather of her own. A child was born. An old man stopped eating in the way that the very old sometimes do here, quietly, without any announcement, and then he was not there and nobody made anything of it, and I rubbed out a mark and did not know what to write in its place. You cannot count a thing that is being kept. Keeping is a verb. The count is a noun. They do not agree.

And there were the others. That was the trouble I could not put on the slate. The lowland had asked me how many people, and I had come up through the cloud thinking I knew what a person was, and Ammer did not.

There was the heron-thing. I saw it on the ninth night, on the span between the third and fourth terraces, and it was four-legged and too tall and too thin and grey with wet, and it was walking the rope with a care that made no sound at all — no creak, no shift, a slow placement of weight that the rope accepted without comment. I watched it for a long time. It did not watch me. It was hunting the millipede herds, and it took one, and it ate it, and it went on. I wrote nothing. I want to be honest about that: I did not write it down because I did not know what column it belonged in, and I have thought since that this is how most of the world gets left out of most records.

The millipedes I did try to count. Once. I sat with my slate on the second terrace at the hour when the herd was crossing it, and I began, and the herd was like the sound of a held breath and it went on for an hour and it was not a herd, it was a weather. I put down forty-one marks and then I understood that I was not counting. I was grazing. My eyes were cropping and my hand was cropping and the herd was indifferent to me and would be indifferent to me for the rest of my life. I closed the slate. I have not opened it to that page again.

---

I learned the signs. That was not in the commission either, but the commission was going badly and I needed something to do with my hands.

The first sign I learned was the one for *how many*. It is made like this: the fingers of one hand opened and turned, a small scooping motion, twice. The Ammeri made it at me constantly in my first weeks. They made it across the terraces. They made it at ten feet, out of habit, out of a sense that a spoken word is a thing you should be close enough to give someone. And then, when I had answered, they made a second sign, which I did not understand for a long time, and which I eventually understood to be *fewer*. Count us as fewer, they were saying. Count us as twenty.

I did not understand it at first because I was from the lowland and in the lowland more is more. But the levy is by household and the household is a rope-span and a roof, and the stipend is by head, and the salt comes up on the mule-track in the cold season, and a head is a mouth and a mouth is a claim. A claim has to be kept. I watched a woman spend a morning walking a rotation through four empty rooms, touching the hearths, touching the doorframes, knocking the moss from a threshold with the flat of her hand — and I understood that she was not doing nothing. She was doing the hardest work in Ammer. She was keeping a room from becoming a room that had been given. And if I counted her town as thirty-four, the lowland would send salt for thirty-four, and there would be three more mouths to keep, and three more rooms that would have to be touched, and the town would be a little heavier and a little further from the shape it was trying to hold.

So they asked me to count them as twenty. They asked me with their hands, in the fog, politely, the way you ask a guest to take off their boots.

---

I did not send the count. I want to say I did not send it out of principle, but the truth is that I kept meaning to finish it and I could not finish it, and the season turned, and the mule-track closed, and the lowland has not heard from me.

Instead I began to walk the rooms. Not all of them — there are four hundred and I am one person with a slate and a bad knee — but the ones on my route, the ones I passed anyway. I began to touch them. A palm flat on a doorframe. A knuckle against a post. A tool lifted and set down. I told myself I was making an inventory. I wrote down *adze, one* and *bowl, one, containing water, containing animal* and I told myself that this was counting, that I was still the lowland's hand held out.

Then the slate went green. Moss first, in the scratches of my writing. Then a thread-fine creeper, following the column of marks I had made on the first day, the ones I had smeared with my thumb. It was beautiful and I left it a week, and in a week it had thickened, and I had to scrape it with my nail every morning to keep the slate a slate. That is the whole of the law here and I had it done to me by a piece of slate. Stillness is how things are claimed. I had left the count alone and the count had been given.

So I scrape it. Every morning. It is not maintenance of anything useful. It is maintenance of the claim. I am, I have come to understand, the only person in Ammer who keeps a slate, and the slate is the only thing in Ammer that needs to be kept by scraping, and I do it because the alternative is to watch my own handwriting become a bed for something with too many legs.

---

There is a bowl on a shelf in a room on the fourth terrace. In the bowl there is water. In the water there is a small pale animal, and it has been there long enough to be a resident and not a visitor.

I have been here long enough that the goats are no longer a mistake I would make. I have been here long enough to go out in the cold season with the mallets and the wedges, to take my place in the line, to read the hand signals across the cleft — *slack here, take up there, hold* — and to feel, through the rope, the mountain doing its slow silent insisting on going up. I have been here long enough that my hands have gone broad and soft and never quite dry, and that I make small economical signs when no one is watching, adjusting invisible knots.

I counted thirty-one. Then thirty-four. Then thirty-five, because of the child, and then thirty-four again, and then I stopped, and then I started again, and I do not know the number. I will not know it. I have stopped thinking of that as a failure of the commission. A count that finished would be a count I could send, and a count I could send would be a way of leaving, and I find that I do not want to leave, and I find that the wanting not to leave is itself a kind of answer.

The heron-thing came into my room last night. It stood for an hour and looked at me and I looked at it and neither of us made a sound, and then it left, and I did not hear it arrive or go. I did not put it on the slate. There is no column for it and I have decided that the lack of a column is not the same as the lack of a thing.

And this morning I stood on the highest terrace at the hour when the fog pulses and drew out through the cleft and came back in, and below me the town was green shelves and beaded ropes and thirty-some people touching things, and I made the sign I learned first, the one I learned before I learned anything else, the fingers opened and turned, the small scooping motion, twice.

I made it to nobody. There was nobody on the span to make it to.

But it does not mean *how many* anymore. I have been here long enough that it has changed under my hand, the way the rope changed under the moss. It means *still here*. It means *still here, still here*, twice, to the fog.
