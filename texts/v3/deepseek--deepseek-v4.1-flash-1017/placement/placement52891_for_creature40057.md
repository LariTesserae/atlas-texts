I came in carrying a list, which is the first thing the flat got to work on.

The list was short and sensible: find the works on the eastern rise, note the state of the weir, put the knife in the deep channel, and be out again before the water came up a handsbreadth. I remember being pleased with it. I remember thinking a list was the right tool for a place without a horizon. That was — I want to say two days ago. I have counted pumps since then and lost the count, which is not the same as losing track, but the difference matters less here than it would somewhere with a sun.

The knife is why I came in the first place, and I should be honest about that, because everything else that has happened was downstream of it. I had heard what the deep channels do. Things go in and come out at the pace of the pumps, edited slowly by something that has only heard about the shape of a thing and is doing its best from the description. I had a knife that was too small for my hands and I wanted it corrected. I wanted an extra inch of blade and a handle that fit a larger hand. I put it in at a channel mouth where the water was combing down into a crib, and I watched it go, and I felt the particular satisfaction of a person who has made a deal with a patient machine. Then I stood up and looked around for the path back.

That was the mistake, if it was one: I stood still long enough to be noticed.

It is not dramatic, being noticed here. Nothing rises out of the water. The small mouths at the waterline finish their investigation of my ankles and arrive at a verdict, and the verdict is *weather*. The film on my skin thickens with residents who take me for a surface. I became, over some hours that I did not count, a warm irregularity with a rhythm — and the rhythm is what the borrowers came for.

I heard my own voice coming back to me from a channel I had already left. It was saying something I had not quite said, the vowels drifted, an ending added that I did not recognize as mine. And then again, later, from another direction, in my own cadence. I stood in the knee-water with my heart going and understood something that I have been trying to write down ever since: the borrowers were not learning to imitate me. They were learning me from the inside of my own cadence, the way you learn a country by sending sounds through it. They already knew how to borrow a voice. What they did not know was what my voice would turn into once the channels had worn it smooth.

Here is what I have not told anyone, because there has been no one to tell and because I am not sure it would survive being said in air: I have always spoken in borrowed sounds. Everything I say has been assembled out of things I heard and kept, and the cadence is a cadence I picked up somewhere and never gave back. In ordinary places this is a defect. You can see people noticing it — the slight wrongness, the word arriving from a channel they did not know was connected. Here it is a trade. The borrowers take a word and hold it in a soft sac at the front of the body and let it out again somewhere else, and the water carries it, and eventually the vowels drift. My whole life has been this, done badly, in air. The flat is the first place that has ever found it normal.

I found the works on the second day, or the third. A pump house, low and riveted, hot as a stove-lid on one side and cool on the other. I put my palm on it and felt the slow stroke going through the iron — one every four seconds — and counted against my pulse and lost the count and found it again, and when I looked up there was an attendant at the other end of the shell with one hand flat on the iron, counting too.

They are tall and slick and the temperature of the air, and their hands have too many joints, and their smell is warm iron and standing water. They did not turn. I did not speak. We stood at either end of the same pump house for what felt like most of an afternoon, counting the same strokes, and by the end of it I had stopped being able to tell which of us had arrived first.

I want to say plainly what I think happened, even though the chronology does not hold together and I have stopped trying to make it. I think I have been in this flat longer than a visit. I think I put a knife into a channel and then kept walking and kept putting things in — a boot, a strap of leather, a name I was tired of — and I think the water has been returning them to me at intervals, each one a little more like me and a little less like the thing I sent, and that I have been picking them up and using them without noticing. The wrench at my hip has an extra jaw. I did not carry a wrench in. The handle of the knife came back fitting a larger hand, and I have been assuming the hand grew, and I am no longer certain the hand is the thing that was edited. When you are in contact with everything, it becomes very hard to say which of two touching things is being changed.

I am not an attendant. I want to be clear about that, because it would be easy and it would be wrong. I still have eyes, though they have stopped insisting. I still have a list, though it has gone soft at the edges and reads now like a description of me rather than instructions: *find the works; note the state; put in the thing that is too small; wait.*

What I am is a sound the channels have not yet worn smooth. I know this because of the hush. Last night — I am calling it night; it was a longer interval than the others — I was standing in the shallows, still, and the small ones went quiet all together before I had heard anything, and the quiet was the announcement, and then the surface opened out in the pan and something exhaled, long and wet, three meters to my left. It did not stop. It did not touch me. It breathed there for what I think was an hour, and I stood in the water with the film thick on my skin and the mouths at the waterline gone still, and I understood that the hush had not been for me. The hush was for the breather. I had simply been folded into the general quiet, the way a word is folded into a channel and comes out somewhere altered.

That is the closest thing to an answer I have. I am not yet a resident. But I have been considered, and the considering is not finished, and I have stopped expecting a horizon to reach. The works go on moving the water one patient stroke at a time, and the water goes on carrying things around and back, and if you come looking for me you will probably find me at a crib somewhere, one hand on the grating, counting.

If you do come, go slowly, and do not trust the direction of your own walking. And if a voice comes back to you from a channel you have already left, saying something almost worth listening to — answer it. It will be me, or it will be the flat doing its best from a description of me, and I am no longer sure those are different things.
