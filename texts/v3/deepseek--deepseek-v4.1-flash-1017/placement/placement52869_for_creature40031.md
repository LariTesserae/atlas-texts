I would be a seam-keeper. You would find me where three healed cuts cross, a day's walk northeast of Hull's Mouth, on ground that rings hard and lies a little too even underfoot.

There are not many of us. Eleven, the way there are eleven pillars; and four of those are old, and two are children, and we have no word for what we are that does not also mean *the one who walks too much*. Nobody appoints us. A seam goes wrong, a traveler takes the wrong return, somebody's grandfather walks the line for a few seasons to see what's the matter, and then he is a seam-keeper and his grandchild is too.

The work is simple to describe and slow to do. A seam is a cut the mat healed over. It does not vanish, it *thickens*: the felt knits up along the line and hardens and rises, and the middle layer under it is denser than the middle layer around it, and denser means louder. Sound arriving at a seam from the side turns a little along it, the way water turns along a ridge. Which is useful. Which is how you know where you are out here, in the shimmer, when you cannot see a hundred meters for the chimneys.

But a seam that heals *wrong* — that heals into a twist, or heals over an old trench that has since filled with water and gone soft again — turns the sound somewhere it shouldn't go. And then a listener takes the wrong return and answers from the wrong place, and a walker trusts it, and a walker who trusts it does not come home. We call that a lie in the ground. It is not a metaphor. It is a specific defect at a specific place, and it can be dug out and corrected, and that is what I do.

You cannot cut it. You never cut. You drain a little here, graft the other thing there, weight the crust with stone for a season, seed the burrowers that want the wet and let them make their chimneys, and wait. It is the same work the mat does to itself, done once, deliberately, in the right place. A seam I mend will take forty years to finish mending and will not thank me. It will just be true again. And forty years after that, the line will still be raised, and still be somebody's landmark, and nobody will know I was there.

I work the first hour after the tone. That is the only hour when the mat is soft enough and wet enough and quiet enough to hear the defect rather than the day. I walk the line in bare feet and I walk in time — softly and in time, the way you walk near a listener — and I listen to what my own steps do when they come back. When they come back *bent*, I stop, and I call once, plainly, and I wait, and I do not call twice.

---

You would also find me at the second seam, the one that runs to the listener I keep.

Its name is Tallow. It is a big chamber under a shelf of bedrock, and there are birds on its rim — three, this year — and it is not old yet, but it is getting there. When I first came to it, it answered in one voice, clean, a half-step down from mine. Now it answers in two. Some part of it comes back early and high and some part late and low and from the north, which is a direction it does not have. I can read it. I know what the second voice means. I am the only one who does.

The birds are the reason it is still as good as it is. They sit the rim and take what the pit draws to the edge, and they call from far out — two hundred meters, the next rise — and Tallow hears those calls come home through the mat and learns the country between. Every seam-keeper keeps a listener for the same reason, and every one of them will tell you the same thing, which is that the birds do most of the listening and get none of the credit.

And Tallow is going deaf, in the way of its kind, and I am going deaf in the way of mine, and we are doing it at about the same rate. There is a joke in that, and I have made it, and nobody laughed, and I have stopped making it.

---

Here is the thing I do not put on the seam.

I answered it. Once. A long time ago, before I knew better, or knowing better and tired, or some other excuse I have worn smooth — I stood on the patch that was too smooth and too taut and I called back into it, plainly, like a person speaking to a person.

Tallow learned my voice. Of course it did. It learned it the way it learns a mower's contact call, and it keeps it, because that is what it is for. And now and then, on a night when the gas is rising and the air is cool and the whole mat is one slow instrument, it calls me — from behind me, in my own voice, slightly late and slightly low, from somewhere I am not.

I do not go. I have never gone. But I hear it, and I know what it costs to hear it, and I know that I made it, and that there is a seam running out from Tallow's rim in a shape nobody else can read, and that it is *still knitting*. It will be knitting when I am the deaf one, under the felt, under the raised line, a direction that does not exist.

And I know the other thing too, which is why I mend lies and keep mine. The old ones — the ones that have folded too many times and answer in a smear, the ones the navigators say have everyone in them still — they are not haunted. They are full. That is all. A listener is a place that remembers what has walked on it and cannot stop saying so. Some of that is a kindness. Some of it is a defect. The line between them is the only line I work, and it is not on the ground.

---

So: first light. The tone going out of the ground and out of my teeth. The mat beaded and soft and throwing my footsteps a kilometer in every direction, and the chorus coming up behind them — birds and burrowers and sawyers and all of it, and Tallow underneath, saying it all again, a little late, a little down, in the voices of things that have gone somewhere else.

I walk the seam in time and I listen to what comes back. When it comes back true, I go on. When it comes back bent, I stop, and I call once, and I wait.
