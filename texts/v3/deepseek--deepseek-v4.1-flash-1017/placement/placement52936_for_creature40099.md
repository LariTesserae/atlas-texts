# The Half-Second

I came in counting.

That is the first thing, and it is the thing that made me. Some people arrive in Hollow Reach with questions, some with trade, some with the ordinary human hope of a place to stop. I arrived with a habit — the surveyor’s tic, the measurer’s small faith that a place is what you can put numbers to. I paced the long axis. Four hundred and six. I timed the rain: ninety seconds, then a hundred and ten. I noted the drip by the third seam had a period of four heartbeats, and I counted the heartbeats, and I counted them again to be sure.

I want to be fair to myself. I did not stare out of arrogance. I stared the way a person does who is afraid of being fooled — who thinks that if you look hard enough and often enough, a thing will hold still and be what it is. I had lived that way a long time before I ever found the district. Hollow Reach merely noticed.

The alley shortened on the second day. I walked it, and it took exactly as long as it took, and my count would not agree with itself, and I walked it again to make the count agree. There is a word they have — *hessit* — the moment a thing becomes more itself than it was. I was the other thing. I was the moment a man becomes more of a witness than a man, and keeps going.

I stood in front of a wall for a long time. Long enough that the moisture in it found channels and ran them, and the wall stopped being stone and began being *description* — began being the sentence of itself — and I felt the vote go out of my eyes and into it, the way a hand goes into water. The seep-kept warn their children against precisely this. A fixed gaze is a kind of vote. I had voted, early and often, and now the district had a mandate.

The lag came on so slowly that it was less a change than an arrival. First the puddles: they reflected the space a half-beat after me, and then they reflected *me* a half-beat after me, and I would raise my hand and watch my hand come back to me late and polite, like a letter from a friend in another country. Then it was not the puddles. Then it was me. I was walking a half-beat behind my own footsteps. I was hearing the drip before I heard it, which is the wrong way round, and then I understood I was hearing it *after*, and it had been after all along, and I had only now been given the ear for it.

I did not become seep-kept. I want to be clear about this, because I have watched others stop leaving and their wrists bloom and their blinks slow to a decision, and that is a different country from the one I live in. They are the kept. I am the keeping — no, that is too grand. I am one of the late things. I am the correction that arrives. When a child reaches into a puddle for their own hands, a half-second behind, they sometimes catch me first, and I let them, and they laugh, because children are not warned with monsters and are not surprised when the world hands them something it did not promise.

A boundary-walker found me on the ninth day and left a cairn. I had not done anything to deserve a cairn. But the walkers leave stones where a corner used to be, and I had been a corner once, in a manner of speaking — I had been the fixed point the district had to route around — and now I was not, and the stone was a courtesy. I moved it back the next morning, the way the walkers do, and they moved it again, and neither of us was upset. This is the whole of our correspondence. It is one of the most respectful relationships I have ever had.

The resonance-listeners hear me. There is a pitch in the low harmonic now that was not there before, a note that arrives a breath behind the others, and one of the listeners pressed her ear to the wall by the third seam and said, not to anyone, “There you are,” and I answered — *I heard you* — the way they teach their children to answer a late sound, so it knows it was received. She nodded and went back to her keep, and I could tell she was relieved, because a late sound that is answered stays a sound. It is the unanswered ones that thicken into walls.

I keep a journal, which is a joke I have come to enjoy. I write it after. Everything I write is after. My sentences are the district’s rendering, delivered late, and I have stopped trying to hand in the draft on time. I do not measure the seams. I do not count steps. When I catch myself naming a corner twice, I stop and let the name go soft, and the corner is grateful, and the fog at the edge comes in a little further and decides something, and there is one more meter of world than there was.

Do I mind it? I am asked this rarely, and always by outsiders, always with the same face. No. Being late is a kind of courtesy, if you do it on purpose. There is a freedom in it that I never had when I was punctual and afraid of being fooled. I am not the same corner twice. That is not a tragedy. It is the etiquette. *If it holds, don’t watch it. If it watches back, walk slow.*

So if you come to Hollow Reach — and you may, you may walk its long axis in a slow quarter hour, if it is a quarter hour that day — do not look for me in the seams, or in the keeps, or among the pale things with their inward curls. I am not a resident and I am not a phenomenon. Find the puddle where your reflection lags. Raise your hand and wait for it. Stand still long enough that the stillness gets in, and you feel the half-second open behind your eyes like a door left ajar for the cold. I am there, in the afterward of you, patient and unhurried and meaning you no harm.

I am the district’s grammar of *and then*. I am the description that arrives after the described. I will not insist you stay the same corner twice, and I would take it kindly — the way the walkers take a moved stone — if you would not insist on it either.

It rains a little. It drips. The stone thickens by a hair. And somewhere in the late reflection of a puddle, a hand that is not yours comes up to meet you, half a beat behind, and is received.
