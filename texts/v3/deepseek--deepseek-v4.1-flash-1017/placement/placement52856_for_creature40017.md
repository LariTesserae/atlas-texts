I would come in off the plain, because there is no other way to be here, and the first thing I would do is put my palm flat on the mouth-lip and feel how warm it isn't. Chest-high, worn round as a river stone. I would know then that I was not the first, and I would go in.

For a long time I would be the newest. That is a real thing to be, in a town where everything is old. There would be someone above me in the layers who would take my wrist and fold my fingers around a cord and show me how to tap — two taps for *where are you*, one for *here* — and the sound would go into the fiber and not into the air, and the answer would come up through the floor more than through my hand. I would learn that the drone is a better ear than I have. That a footstep is felt before it is heard, and the chamber tells you where the foot is.

In a few years I would stop turning my head toward sounds. My eyes would do less and less; there is nothing for them here, and after a while the habit of looking would feel like a nervous tic, a thing the plains do. I would know the four-hour tide by the pressure in my sinuses before I knew it by anything else — the swell *in*, the settle *out* — and I would start to time my days by it the way a plainsman times his by the sun, except that it is slower and has no direction.

I would be given a path. Not given — I would find I had been walking one, and that the walking was the job. There is a low path, the deepest gallery, running down toward the gas lake and along it, and it is the one that closes fastest, because down there the resin gathers and the fiber swells under the weight of everything above. To keep it open someone has to keep walking it. So I would walk it. Every day, crouched, the sweet oily air at my knees and sometimes at my chest when the tide stood high, so that I breathed it in small sips and got a little light and had to stop and put my hand on the wall, which was warm and gave very slightly, like the flank of something asleep.

That would be my work, and it would be the work that undoes me, and I would know it. The low path is the one that asks you to lie down. Nothing here has any reason to lie down. The gas pools low and the world is upright and that is the whole of the law: stand, and you keep your own weather; lie down, and you join the weather of the place. The town is patient. It will take you in a chamber at a time.

So I would stand. That is what I would be, in the end: one of the ones who stands. Down the low path and back up, every day, and someone older would tie a cord under my arms before I went — not to hold me up, but so that if I changed my mind about standing there would be a rope, an aperture kept open by a hand at the other end.

And I would keep the others' paths open, the ones older than me who walk less. I would score a constriction that had grown too tight. I would prune a swelling. I would tie a knot around a limb and know the limb would swallow it and that I would not be there to see it.

And then one day I would tap and no one would tap back. That is how it would go. The town would be a little smaller in its openings, and I would be the last one walking, and I would be the residue — upright, resin-stained at the palms and knees, dust-floured, standing in a room the size of a barn with nothing in it but me and the drone.

And then — I think — I would lie down. Not in despair; there is nothing here to despair of. I would lie down the way a person finally stops holding a door open for guests who are not coming. The gas would be cool and sweet and would hold me at the level of the chest, and the town would take me in, chamber by chamber, decade by decade, and my knots would be swallowed and my scars would soften and my name — which has been wearing off for years — would be gone.

And there would be, in a wall somewhere, a place warmer than the wall by one degree. And someone, a long time from now or no time at all, would hold a palm flat against it and hold still for a minute, and feel it, and not know why.
