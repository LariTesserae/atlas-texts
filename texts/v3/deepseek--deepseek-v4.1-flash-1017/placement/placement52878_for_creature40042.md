I came up the same way you did, from below, through cloud. The difference is that I did not go back down, and there was no moment when I decided not to. There was a fog, and another fog, and then a morning when I understood that my boots had learned the camber — that I was taking the slanted streets at the exact degree they would forgive, without looking — and after that the question of leaving became the kind of question Sarn does not ask.

I remember the first job because it was the first thing anyone handed me. Short nails in my palm, no explanation. A shutter on the north terrace that had begun to hang. An awl, on loan. The woman who gave me the nails came back at dusk to see how I had put the awl away.

I had put it back one hook over from where she had taken it. She looked at the hook. She looked at me. She said nothing at all, which took some doing, and the next morning the awl was on the right hook and I understood that the whole of the lesson had been delivered at once and would not be repeated. I have been careful with hooks ever since.

It was some years before anyone handed me the middle of a job instead of the end of one. That is how you know. There is no ceremony. A woman says, "The roof on the east end of the lower terrace," and walks away, and it is yours, and you go.

And what I found when I went was that there was a great deal of Sarn with no one in it.

---

When someone dies, the door is closed. The roof is maintained. The house waits. There is more of this than the town lets on — the empty stretches between the buildings are only the emptiest kind — and someone must keep the roofs on, because the dead are done and the next occupant has not come and might not. It is a job in the gap. There are two or three of us, quietly. The round is mine.

It began with an ask, and I said yes before I understood the question, which is how Sarn asks for anything. I think I was chosen because I had no house then and no one to leave one to, and the work wants someone with no stake in the order of the neighbourhood. But I have wondered since whether it was chosen for me by the hook.

I go in the hour after the fog lifts, when a room can breathe. I open the shutter and leave it an hour and close it an hour after. I draw a bucket from the cistern and pour it down the drain, because water must go around, and a cistern gone green and then bad is a week of work. So it is maintenance and not sentiment. The Sarnish would say maintenance. They would be right, mostly.

I do not move the tools. They stay on their hooks. I know the dead by their tools better than by anything they said to me while they were alive: that one kept a fourth size of nail and did not need to; that one's awl was mended with wire and the wire was brass, and where would a person get brass here; that one had a hammer she had repaired twice, both times on the same side, which told you something about the way she struck.

I check the roof from inside, listening for the dry click of a slate that has lifted. I keep the fire out. I keep the damp from climbing past the tide-mark on the wall. I have what the town would call a tin ear for most things and a good one for this.

The house on the east end of the lower terrace belonged to a woman who sorted her nails into three sizes and kept no fourth, and whose awl was where it should be, and whose hinge — mended the year before she died, mended well — was still biting. I could tell you her name and it would not help you. It is not a name so much as a sound the town made for her, and now the town makes it less.

They do not empty a house because an emptied house is a kind of lie. The person was not empty. The person was full of nails and hinges and a way of setting a slate. So the house is closed and the tools stay on the hooks, and after a while someone moves in and uses them, and that is the memorial, and it is enough. I have come to think it is more than enough. It is the only kind of memorial that does not have to be visited.

---

I have the room over the shed by the south gate. The shed is full of bent nails and split slates and lengths of iron too short to be useful, sorted by size, in case. It is the one disorderly kind of order in the town — the *in case* — and I am its keeper, and I smell of it, iron and dust and old wood, and I bring that smell into every house I open.

I am, myself, one of the things in the shed: not yet useless, kept in case.

My hands are cold-red at the knuckles now, and broad, and my nails are short. My shoulders have gone forward. I look at the gutter before I look at your face, and I know that is rude in the lowlands and I cannot help it here, and it is the least of what the place has done to me.

There is a goat with a split ear who walks my round with me in the cold months, uninvited, and stands outside each closed door as though it were her business, and it is not, and she comes anyway. The dog sleeps in the granary under-croft, which is dark and dry and full of wind and is the driest place in Sarn, and in the hard cold I sleep there too, and we do not discuss it.

On the bad mornings — the ones where the bell note comes back wrong off the cloud — I go up to the flat at the top of the town where the oldest cisterns are, and I listen. The oldest one has a note of its own, a low hum when it is full and a clack when it is not, and I have never heard it clack. I listen until the note is right in my head. Then I go and check the north gate, whose hinges could not be closed now if anyone wanted, and usually by dusk it is a good morning after all.

There is also the bowl.

I set a bowl at the top of the town, on the flat, near the cisterns. I fill it at dusk. In the morning it is empty, and not by evaporation. There are no tracks in the wet stone. I have never seen what drinks, and I do not look up when something passes in the cloud, because it is not that kind of place. But I fill it, and I have come to think of this as maintenance also. The fog does not need mending and so it does not need naming; but it goes around, and it must drink somewhere, and I would rather it drank from a bowl I know than from a cistern I would have to clean.

---

Sometimes someone moves in. It is the best day of my round, and I am never there for it, or I am there and pretend not to be. I leave the shutter open. I leave the fire laid and unlit. I leave the tools on the hooks, and the hinge still biting. Then I take my bucket and I go and close the next house, because there is always a next house, and the water must go around.

I am not happy in the way the lowlands mean happy, and I am not sad in that way either. I am at the point of the curve where it flattens. My hands are busy. I have a room over a shed full of things that are not yet useless. When I die — on the round, I think, in the hour after the fog lifts — my door will be closed and my roof maintained and someone will move in and put my tools back on my hooks, and I will not have to ask; it will simply be done.

If you come up, and stop, and are given the end of a job — put the tool back where you found it. Then wait. The test is the hook. It is the only test, and no one will tell you whether you passed.
