If you came looking for me on the Mizzle Downs, you would have to get low—lower than the chicken-birds, lower than the frog-puddles, lower than the flat canopies of the almost-trees. You would find me in the seam between two slabs of grey-mauve stone, on the sheltered side of a spiral shrub colony, a few paces from a hollow that holds a shallow puddle for a week after rain and then leaves a bright ring of lichen behind. I would be a large, slow, dark spider: one of the crack-dwellers, not one of the web-spinners strung between the shrubs. My abdomen would be furred with silt. My legs would be oil-black and jointed like wet thread. My silk would line the crack and set a single trip-line at its lip.

That is where I would live: not on the downs, but in them—in a hand-wide room of stone and damp, with a roof of fog and a floor that sweats. I would know the place by pressure and moisture rather than by view. The morning fog would reach me as a change in the air, a soft thickening, a coolness settling into the crack. Then the stone would begin to bead. I would come out slowly, leg by leg, and drink the condensation where it gathered in a shallow pit worn by years of my coming and going. The water would taste of mineral and old rain and, faintly, of the lichen that crusts the stone nearby. I would not hurry. There is no reason to hurry.

My world would be narrow and deep. The cricket-drum would come to me through the stone before it became sound. The frog-call at night would be a low pulse in the ground, three notes from three directions, each one patient and unanswered. The chicken-birds would arrive as soft thuds and conversational clucking, and I would feel them before I saw them—if I saw them at all. My eyes would be poor; my legs would do the seeing. A fly landing near the trip-line would be a small bright event. A beetle crossing the pavement would be a slow rumble. The snail trails would shine silver in the fog, and I would know them as paths that were not mine.

I would not be the landlord of the downs. I would be a tenant under the floorboards of the weather. The bird might eat me if I were careless; I would eat the flies and beetles that were more careless still. When I died, my body would become a small dark stain in the tea-colored soil, and the lichens and the spiral shrubs would take me up, and the insects would take them up, and the frogs would take the insects up, and the bird would go walking through its damp domain with the confidence of something that has never been hunted. That is the whole arrangement. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is in a hurry.

So if you ask where I would be, I would say: in a crack in the stone, just below the fog, drinking the dawn one bead at a time. Not a personage. Not a center. One of the small, dark, patient many, at home in the mizzle.
