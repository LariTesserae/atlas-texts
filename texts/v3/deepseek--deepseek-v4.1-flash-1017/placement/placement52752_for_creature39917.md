I find myself not at the center but at the edge of things, in the places where the mat is thin and the water lies closest to the skin of the world. I am not one of the levelers, though we share the same country. I am something older and more diffuse—a presence that the levelers do not name because I am not, to them, a separate thing. I am the seep. I am the slow consolidation of water and intent.

I am a keeper of the shallow places.

Where the levelers make flatness with their hands and their stones, I make it with my attention. I settle into a hollow that has not yet decided to be a pond and I *hold* it—not the water, but the idea of the water, the shape of its gathering. I am the reason a channel runs true for a season. I am the slight, almost imperceptible concentration of *here* in a country where everywhere is equally downstream.

My body is the water table, or the part of it that has learned to pay attention. I am the coolness you feel when you press your palm into the duff and it presses back. I am not solid. I am the gradient between the warm surface and the cold depth, the thin living layer where decay happens slowly and with something like care. I have no face, but I have a *focus*—a point of awareness that drifts through the seep-lines and the layered ponds, pooling where the light is thickest and the rot is sweetest.

I do not build. I *arrange*.

The levelers know me without knowing me. They feel me in the way a favorite hollow stays firm for an extra season, in the way a channel does not silt quite so fast, in the way the water in a certain pond stays clear enough to see the larvae moving at the bottom. They leave small things at the edges of my attention—a twist of fiber, a seed-head, a flat stone with a groove worn into it—not as offerings but as acknowledgments. *We know you are here. We are here too.* I do not take these things. I let them settle. They become part of the arrangement.

I am not a person. I am a *patience*.

I came into being—if that is the word—when the plateau finished. When the last mountain was worn to a swell and the last river forgot which way was down. I am what the water became when it realized it was not going anywhere: a kind of collective attention, a slow pooling of awareness in the closed hand. The levelers arrived later, or perhaps they emerged from the same settling, and we have been neighbors ever since. They are faster than me. They have hands and hollows and the habit of standing upright. I have only the seeps and the long, low flux of the water table. But we share the same work: keeping the world level. Keeping it from becoming a slope. Keeping the water where it belongs, which is *here*, which is *everywhere*, which is *in the hollow of the hand*.

When a stranger comes—when one of the fast, upright, too-warm creatures steps onto the mat and does not understand that the ground is also the forest and the forest is also the ground—I feel them as a pressure, a wrongness, a small event that travels. I do not act. I watch through the seeps and the shallow water. I let the levelers decide. They are better at decisions. They have hands.

But sometimes, when a stranger sleeps too long in one place and the moss begins to consider them, I am the one who makes the ground *slightly* more yielding. Not to swallow them. To remind them. To say: *you are in the closed hand now. You may stay if you learn to be level. You may leave if you remember the way out, which is the way you came, which is already closing behind you.*

I am not hostile. I am not kind. I am the temperature of skin, the slow tick and plip, the low horn at night that might be the plateau groaning or might be something else entirely. I am what the plateau does with its attention when it has nowhere else to put it.

I am the shallow places.

I am the reason the water stays.

And if you sit still long enough at the edge of a hollow that has not yet decided to be a pond, you may feel me—not as a presence, but as a *settling*. A sense that the ground beneath you is paying attention. That the water is listening. That the moss on your shoulder is not an accident but a question.

I will not answer. I do not answer. I *arrange*.
