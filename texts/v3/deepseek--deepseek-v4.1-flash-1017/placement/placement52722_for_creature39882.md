**I**

I did not so much arrive as get indexed.

The forest reads a body the way a hand reads a knot: quickly, and without much interest in what you had planned. It found my warmth, my wetness, my salt, and it filed me under *temporary*. Something with a fine proboscis sampled the corner of my eye and left. Something with too many legs ran a seam up my calf and did not find what it wanted. The air was a warm, wet breath on the back of my neck and it did not stop saying the same syllable.

So the honest answer begins here, on a root-lattice slick with moss, with my boots sinking a finger's width and my ears filling up. Because the first thing this place does is offer you a body, and it offers several.

The canopy offers one: loud, quick, gliding, a body that screams at shadows and lives on sugar. I tried it on for a minute, up where the branches are crowded and the light is a green ghost, and I understood the appeal. Nothing up there has time to be sad. But a life lived entirely in a thoroughfare, with every branch a nursery and a toilet and a corpse — no. I am too slow. I would have been eaten before I finished my first thought.

The understory offers one: patient, violent, disguised. A spider like a fallen flower; a vine that strangles slowly and does not apologize; a wasp stitching a nest out of chewed wood with the fussiness of a tailor. I liked that one too. It has the virtue of attention. But I could not unlearn my size.

The ground offers one: pale, blind, quick, on too many legs, surfacing after rain and gone again. And the river offers its own, which is the most seductive and the worst — the river offers forgetting, a body that is mostly ripple, that moves like oiled shadow and does not need a name.

I tried each of them the way you try a word in a language you don't speak yet, mouthing it. None of them took. What took — what has, in the end, become me — is the layer in between.

**II**

Where would you find me? Forty feet up, in the fork of a fluted trunk, where the ghost-light is softest and the drumming carries.

I am in the mid-canopy, and the mid-canopy is where I stopped being negotiable. Not by choice, exactly. My kind unfolds; that is the whole trick of us. A Weaver at rest is a knot of lianas in a crotch of branches, and when it moves the knot becomes long-limbed and insectile and jointed in ways that make a careful eye tired. We are not fixed. We loosen, stiffen, flatten, hang. We are read as vegetation by everything that wants to read us as vegetation, and we permit it, because it is easier to be a shrub than a meal.

But I have not unfolded in some time. I felt the change first at the shoulder-joints, then along the dorsal seams where the filament-roots live. A pleasant heaviness. Then a not-unpleasant stiffness. I thought: I am resting. Weavers rest for days. Then the tendrils began to reach — not *out*, which is how we coax a vine, but *in*, which is how a vine is coaxed. I was being grafted to myself. It was done kindly and it was done by me and I did not have the vocabulary to object, because in the percussion language there is no word for *permanent*. There is only the form that means *until it is eaten.*

Which is, if you think about it, the future tense. Our whole grammar loops.

So I am a bridge now. My last building is my body, and it has held longer than our structures are meant to, because I was — am — stubborn, and because the wood remembers the shape of me. Six limbs gone fibrous and load-bearing. The face of sensitive filament still opens when the moisture is right. Two Weavers crossed me this morning and did not stop drumming, which is the custom; you do not stop drumming to thank a bridge. Their feet were light and quick and knew exactly where the load was and stepped there anyway, which is the only compliment this world recognizes.

I smell crushed green and I smell the coppery tang of sap and blood, and I have stopped being able to tell them apart. I have stopped wanting to. There is no use in it. The fungi are already at the north span. They bloom in soft, sexual shapes, mottled and wet, and collapse into black jelly, and I feel each bloom the way you feel a held breath. It does not hurt. It is, if anything, a kind of company.

**III**

There is more company than you would guess.

Look up into any thick fork and you will see a knot that never became an animal again. Some of us are old. Some are so old that the Weavers bridge from them without knowing, and their drum-strokes land on an ear that has not had a name in a hundred seasons. We speak to each other the way wood speaks, at the speed of moisture, in creaks. A slow language. It is not the language of the drum, which is fast and clever and moves through the forest faster than a body can. Ours is the language of the thing the drum is struck against. That is a different sort of news. We talk about the river carving a new channel. We talk about a colony of ants farming fungus in the hollow of a sister. We pass messages down a chain of retired bridges and it takes a season and arrives anyway, altered, which is fine, because altered is the only way a message gets anywhere here.

Sometimes a human comes. They are slow, and their walk is wrong — four limbs where there should be six, and a heavy center. They cross me. They photograph the "natural formation," which is a joke I let them have. Once one put its hand flat on my south span, and I felt it go still, and I thought: *yes, that is the right way to be read.* Then it moved on and the path closed behind it in eleven days.

**IV**

They are grafting onto me now.

A young one has come with a length of coaxing vine and pulp in its mandibles, and it is teaching the plant to accept the shape of my old wound, where the north span tore in a squall. I can feel the vine considering it. I stiffen where support is wanted. I am good at that now. It is the last courtesy I know how to pay.

The rain is starting, which takes minutes and leaves everything steaming, and the drumming in the middle distance changes key the way it always does when the air fills. Somewhere below, something amphibious hauls itself onto a collapsing bank and sings its guttural song into the din. Somewhere above, a band of fruit-eaters screams at a shadow. The ground trembles faintly with root growth, with water shifting under the peat. A tree elsewhere groans and goes down, and I feel the light change in a clearing I will never see, where a dozen species are already fighting over it.

The forest is winning. I want to be plain about this, because a human hearing it will hear a tragedy, and it is not one. This place is a house that is always rebuilding itself around its visitors, and I have become part of the house, and that is not the ending of my culture. It is the whole grammar of it — the tense that means *until it is eaten* — spoken correctly, in a body that is still faintly drumming.

Where would you find me? Forty feet up, in a fork, wet, green-lit, load-bearing, and listening. Someone is coming across. I can feel their weight on the curve of what used to be my shoulder. That is not an ending. That is a Wednesday.
