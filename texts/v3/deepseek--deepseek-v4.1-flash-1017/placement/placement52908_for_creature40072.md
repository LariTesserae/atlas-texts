# The Ringer at the Throat

You would find me where three channels meet, in the part of the Sump the water-readers call the Throat, on a platform that has been lashed to the same two alders for eleven years and has been rebuilt four times. You would know it by the bells. There are thirty-one of them, hung along a wire that runs from my platform to the next platform and the next, all through the Throat and out along the drowned boathouses and the old pump-house on the eastern arm. Some of them are brass boat-bells, green with verdigris. Some are lengths of pipe struck with a padded mallet. One is the lid of a pressure vessel, salvaged whole from a factory that is now only a roof above water. I keep them tuned by ear, by filing, by the wax I pack into their throats when the damp flattens their voices.

I am the bell-keeper. It is not a sacred office. It is not an office at all, really. When the last bell-keeper died — my mother's cousin, who taught me the wax and the filing and the particular patience of a false alarm — the town did not appoint anyone. I simply kept ringing. No one asked me to stop. That is how most things are settled here.

The first thing I hear in the morning is not the bells. The bells are mine to make; I do not need to hear them. What I hear first is the stamping mill, three channels over, which no one remembers how to turn off. It is the ground bass of the whole Throat, a slow metallic thud, thud, thud, like a heart that has forgotten it is alive but has not forgotten the rhythm. The water carries it. The alder roots carry it. On still mornings I can feel it in the soles of my boots before I hear it with my ears, and I have learned to read it the way a physician reads a pulse. When it stutters, something has shifted in the deep muck and a support is about to go. When it speeds — it does speed, sometimes, for reasons no one has ever explained to my satisfaction — I wake the whole platform with the brass bell before I have even decided to.

I have been told I am a superstitious woman. I would like to be clear that I am not. I know exactly why I ring the bells. I know the physics of the wire and the density of the mist and the way sound bends over open water and doubles back off the factory walls. I know that a crack in a load-bearing stilt has a signature, a dry, descending note, and that if I ring the low pipe-bell in answer, the note will stop — because whoever hears it will go and brace the stilt, and the note will stop because the stilt is braced. That is all. That is the whole of my trade. The bell is a hand laid on a shoulder. The rest is knowing which shoulders are where.

But I will tell you the thing I do not tell the others, and then you may judge what I am.

Some years ago I noticed that the Sump answers.

Not the people. I know how the people answer. They answer with boots on the boardwalk and cable in their fists and no words at all. What I mean is the *water*. There is a bell on my wire that I did not make and cannot replace: a low, cracked bell, no bigger than my two hands together, with a hairline fracture that gives it a tone like a struck pot. It came from somewhere in the drowned east arm. My mother's cousin said it was a warning bell from the old pump-house, but she said it in a way that meant she did not know. I ring it only for the worst things — a whole platform going, a fire in the rope-works, a breach that will take a boat crew down into the green dark. In eleven years I have rung it nineteen times. Each time, it has done what it is supposed to do.

But each time, afterward, the water has changed. Not dramatically. Not with a wave or a glow or anything that would make a good story. The hum of the mill will shift a quarter-tone lower for a day or two. The seep in the deepest sump under the Throat will warm, by a degree, by a fraction of a degree, enough that the crabs come up out of it and sit on the boards looking pale and stupid. The channels will re-braid themselves a little. A path that was open will silt shut; a path that was silted will open. It happens within hours of the nineteenth bell and not otherwise. I have kept a record. I am not a woman who keeps records out of romance. I keep records because the ground moves and I need to know how it has moved, and after eleven years of records I can say with confidence that the Sump responds to that bell.

So what does that make me? A woman who has found the one note the world listens to. A woman with her hand on a nerve.

I do not believe the coal-spirit is a god. I do not believe it has a face. When the salvagers come up out of the flooded factories with stories — a corridor that was not there before, a machine still turning under the water with no wheel and no current to turn it, a warm green light that followed them along a flooded stair — I believe them, and I do not believe it is a ghost. It is something slower and larger than a ghost. It is the whole wet body of the place, thinking at a speed that makes our whole lives look like a single flicker of a moth's wing. We are not its children. We are not its worshippers. We are a rash on its skin, and sometimes it itches, and sometimes it shifts in its sleep.

But it *answers*. That is the thing I cannot make fit into chemistry. Something in the dark, wet, green, breathing deep of the Sump heard my cracked bell and moved its slow self in reply. The others would tell me to stop ringing it. They would tell me I am calling attention to us. I have thought about this. I have thought about it for years. And I have decided — with the same flat, unromantic clarity with which I file the pipes — that I would rather be answered than ignored. A world that answers can be negotiated with. A world that does not answer is just weather.

Now. You asked where you would find me, and I have told you: at the Throat, among thirty-one bells. But I should tell you how I stand, because here we judge a person by how they stand. I stand with my weight forward, knees bent, arms loose. My hands are the most valuable thing I own; I keep them gloved until the gloves fall apart and then I stitch the gloves. My eyes are the pale grey of everything that has never seen the sun above the canopy, and I will look at your feet before I look at your face, because I need to know what you will do when the boards move. If you are steady, I will meet your eyes. If you are not, I will ring a bell for you, and someone will come, and they will not ask why.

And there is one more thing, which I hesitate to tell you, because it is the only thing about me that is not practical.

On my platform, in a stoppered jar, I keep a measure of the warm seep. It glows, faintly, sickly green, and it is the best lamp I have ever owned. It is also eating through the glass. I know this. I know the rate at which it eats. I have watched it etch the inside of that jar for five years, and I know that in another two the glass will fail and the seep will pool, and I will have to move the jar, or the platform, or myself. I keep it anyway. I tell myself it is for the light. It is not for the light. I was born here, and my cord was thrown into the deep sump like everyone else's, and I have spent my whole life listening to a thing that I can hear but cannot see, and the jar is the closest I have ever come to looking at it.

Some nights, when the water rises without rain — when the mill shifts low and the crabs come up and the channels begin to re-braid — I do not wait to be woken by a crack. I go out along the wire with my padded mallet, and I find the cracked bell, and I ring it into the mist.

And then I stand still, weight forward, knees bent, and I listen.

Not for an answer. I have stopped expecting anything so clear. I listen to hear my own town move — the boots, the cables, the bells of the others answering mine, the whole nervous system of us waking in the dark and going to work. That is the answer I can use. The other answer, the slow one, the one that comes in the water, I will read in the morning, in my records, in the new channels, in the quarter-tone of the mill.

I am the part of the Sump that rings. The rest of it listens. I have come to think the distinction is not as firm as I once believed. Some mornings I wake with the mill already in my chest and the wire already singing in my hands, and I cannot tell whether I am the ringer or the bell — whether I am the woman who warns the town, or the town's own long, cracked note, struck by something deeper, in the dark, and ringing, ringing, ringing.
