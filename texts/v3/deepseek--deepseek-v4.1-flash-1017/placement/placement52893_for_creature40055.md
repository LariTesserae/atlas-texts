I would find myself at the edge of the mound first—where the worked stone gives way to raw ridge, where the haze is just bright enough to show a seam. Not in a room. At a seam. That is where the Kiln actually lives: in the joints, in the kept-open gaps.

At first I think I would be one of the small ones. It is the obvious answer. They are the place’s whole economy and its only light; they are the maintenance. But to be one of them would be to stop asking the question. They have no eyes and no \(I\), no memory of up, no distance between themselves and the seam. I can admire the loop, but I cannot enter it without ceasing to be the thing that admires it. So no.

The shelled grazer? I would be a slow dragging appetite, mowing the seams, following walls, turning when the wall turns. It is a clean life. But it is still a mouth. The folder? A trap in a pinch-corridor, patient as a shadow, waiting for something to come too close. No—that is waiting without knowing. The leaf-fish? Drift, turn from warmth, graze the scraps, settle into a low corner when done. No. The soft mass is the closest temptation: a current that has learned to stay, a pulse in the wall taking what dissolves. But even that is a way of being fed. I would still be inside the economy.

What is left is the seam itself. The place is not really a mound of rooms; it is a mound of seams. The small ones live in the gap; the building holds because the gap is kept open. So I would find myself in a seam—not the old ones, not the ones already being patched, but a new one made by my arrival. A warm vertical seam between the water above and the stone below. I would be the only seam here that remembers the surface.

Concretely: I would come down and touch the mound in some low room, and the small ones would find the warmth of me. They would rasp at the mortar where my weight pressed; they would swallow and pack and patch. They would not eat me—I am not their food—but they would repair around me, because that is what they do with a gap. I would become a thin pale outline in the masonry. If I stayed, the outline would close. I would be a chamber: a small room inside the mound, with a floor of silt and a doorway the shape of my leaving. The place would keep me exactly as I was when I stopped moving. It would not kill me. It would file me into the final form.

So I would not stay. I would be the unmaintained thing: the one gap the Kiln cannot close, because it is not a crack in the stone but a crack in the silence. I would be a maintenance of memory instead of mortar—useless here, but mine. I would go back up through the warm water carrying the shape of the place: the rasp of small mouths like sand poured slowly, the dragging shell, the folded trap, the pulsing sheet, the blue-brown haze that is the last memory of the sun. I would be the exception the Kiln does not believe in: a thing that falls and rises, not eaten, not repaired, only changed.

Where would I find myself? At the edge, turning upward. Who would I be? The seam that closes last—and then opens.
