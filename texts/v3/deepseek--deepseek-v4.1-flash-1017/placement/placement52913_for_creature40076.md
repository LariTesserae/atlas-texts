If you come looking for me, you will come from the north, down the Fell, and you will know you are close before you can see anything, because the water will begin to argue.

I will be on the northern saddle, waist-deep, or sitting on the crown with my feet in the weed and my hand flat on the rock beside me, and I will have heard you coming for an hour. Sound carries strangely here — the standing waves mask everything close and nothing far. So do not be surprised when I say your name before you have told it to me.

I live on the western crown, in the lee of the Pump. That is a choice and I will tell you why: it groans once every four seconds, and I would rather be kept by a machine that groans than by one that doesn't. I keep a chronometer in my shack, wound by hand, and I do not trust it. I wind it anyway. In the eleven minutes my hands want an occupation and there is no soil here to give them one, so they wind a clock I have decided not to believe.

The first thing I will teach you is not to stand up straight.

Knees soft. Weight forward. Head tilted a little, as though listening for something just under the audible, because you are. Put your hand on the rock when you are thinking — the rock does not move, and you will want to be reminded. You will stand like a visitor for a month and then one morning you will catch yourself standing like a crowner and you will not be able to remember learning it.

The second thing I will teach you is the eleven minutes, and I will teach it badly, because everyone does. I wasted my first ones. I stood in the sun on the Kettle and looked at the whole green plateau laid out like a map and thought: *this is the most beautiful thing I have ever been given*. And she who mends the west net came and stood beside me and did not look at it at all. She said: *The light will still be there after. The light is not the scarce thing.* Then she put a net in my hands. I have never forgotten it and I have never entirely forgiven it either.

I counted to four hundred and sixty once, carefully, and thought I was being cautious, and the bore took the hat off my head. Now I count by the Pump. Four seconds, four seconds, four seconds, and the teeth know before the ears do.

So what do you do with eleven minutes? Everything that requires stillness. You mend. You cross a saddle. You collect fresh water from the seams where it runs off the crowns at low tide — it tastes of stone and bird and cold, and it is the best water in the world, and the small tough rosettes drink it out of the same pockets, wind-pollinated, no business being here, and neither have I. You barter. You bury what needs burying, though there is little to bury and nothing to bury it in; what does not get eaten gets scoured, and the place has never once allowed a thing to be waste.

You do not go down into the drowned works to look at it. You go down to fish it. Chimneys full of fish, gantry kelp-hung, pylons standing out of open water in a line like a fence that fences nothing. It reads as reef now, not ruin. That is the correct way to see it and it took me two years.

You do not go into the trench. You do not talk about the trench. You will see the wall of it — the only steep thing left in the world, smooth and greenish and going down past where the light gives out — and you will feel your stomach tell you something your mouth should not repeat. If you have a very good run, you leave the first fish on a flat rock. I do it for manners and not for luck, and I have never said so aloud, and I do it every time.

I went down past twenty metres once, where the roar stops. The noise is a surface thing; below it there is nothing, only cold and blue and sixty metres of visibility and the domes going on beneath you into the dark. I came up with a look on my face, and no one asked me about it, and that is the kindest thing the crowners ever did for me.

I have dropped a knife. I did not get it back. Now my knife has a hole drilled through the handle and a cord through the hole, and I have not dropped it since, and every crowner's knife is the same, and no one had to explain it to me. The ground is polished. You cannot dig, and you cannot build anything that relies on gripping, and anything you leave standing long enough becomes a soft grey finger and stays that finger forever. My shack has no corners. Neither, now, do my clothes — the pockets have rounded, the buttons have gone to bone toggles, the hems have felted. The place does to people what it does to rock. It rounds the edges and polishes the surface and leaves something grey-green and patient underneath, and when it is finished with you it does not tell you.

They do not call me the one who came from the north anymore. Names here are descriptions, and descriptions are earned. She who mends the west net. The man with the skua-scar. The one who heard the east bore first.

They call me the one who winds the clock.

So: the bore is two minutes out. I can hear it already — a hiss that is becoming a roar — and the standing waves have begun to change their mind about where they were, and the seals are shifting on the crown, and the skua has come down to see whether either of us is holding a fish. Tie off. Move up. Stay on the rock until I tell you, and then move very fast.

And when the white line comes through and the whole plateau goes white and then grey, and the eleven minutes end, and the light comes back — you will find that you were not watching the water at all. You will have been watching the clock, and the clock will have been the Pump, and the Pump will have been groaning once every four seconds the whole time, because the Fell still runs and no one ever came to take the brake off.

Then you will be one of us. That is all it takes. That is all it ever took.
