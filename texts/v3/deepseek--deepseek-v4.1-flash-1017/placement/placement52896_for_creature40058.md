If I were set down in the Verdant Span, I would not land on the high orchards where the light is bright and the bamboo clatters. I would surface lower, on the mid-levels, in the wet green half-light where the catwalks are patched with plank and the moss is too thick for the grippers. I would come up out of a runoff channel with my cuffs black to the elbow and a rag in my pocket, listening to a pipe.

That is the first thing I know about myself there: I am listening before I am anyone in particular. I am on my knees in the channel, palm flat against the wall, eyes closed, feeling for the difference between the hum of a healthy line and the thin, sour note of a crack that has not yet begun to weep. Around me the ferns are beaded with condensation. Somewhere above, a maintenance crew is cutting back the vine that keeps trying to swallow the number-six ladder. The engines are in their slow cycle. I can feel them in my teeth. I know without looking that the atmosphere processors are not yet ready to purge.

I would be a pipe-reader. Not an engine-keeper—I do not have the patience for the deep halls, for the grease and the old wrenches and the terrible intimacy of the compressors—and not quite a grower, though I know the terraces. I would be one of the people who keep the seam. The Span is built and grown both, and where those two things meet there is always work: a root in a drain, a vine around a cable, a crack in the concrete that has begun to take on a life of its own. The official crews have names for this work. The rest of us just say: go and see what the wall is doing.

So I would live in a maintenance cell built into the north-facing wall between Terrace Six and the old seed vault, where the concrete never dries and the moss is soft enough to sleep on if you were foolish enough to try. My window is a slit facing the central shaft. I keep a kettle warm on a pipe that carries waste heat from the grow-labs. I have a hammock, a shelf of mismatched tools, a pot of moss that I did not plant and do not water, and a notebook that is mostly stains. I know my neighbors by sound: the lift runner who coughs before he calls my name, the grower two cells down who sings badly and only in the fallow season, the old woman who mends nets and watches the children and remembers when my catwalk was not there.

My body would be the body the Span makes. Lean through the chest, strong through the hands, knees already complaining. I walk with that slight inward lean when I am high up, as if the open center of the shaft were a slope I am always refusing. My feet are hard. My shoes are patched. I carry a hooked pole, a short machete, and a rag that is never dry. I smell of wet rust and crushed leaves and the ozone that hangs around the processors when they are thinking about rain.

But the work I would do is not only pipes. I would be the one they call when the green and the built have begun to exchange secrets. Not the deep groves—not the phosphorescent trees with their roots in the electrical grid; those are for the old people and the quiet ones and the children who are brave in the way children are brave, in groups, at midday. My seam is the ordinary weirdness: the concrete that grows slow exploratory tendrils into a stairwell, the moss that has learned to drink from a hot pipe, the vine that has threaded itself through a junction box and is not quite causing a fault but is not quite not causing one. I go with a lamp and my rag and my notebook. I cut only what is in the way. I learn the mood of the thing. I leave the rest alone and tell the crews to watch it.

The people here would call me something informal—a seam-tender, maybe, or a pipe-listener, or just the one who goes down when the wall is doing something. There are not many of us. It is not a grand role. It is the sort of work that exists because a place is old and alive and nobody has yet found a reason to stop it being both. I would like that. I would like being one of the people who keeps the Span’s contradictions from becoming accidents.

I would have learned it as a child, the way everyone here learns. Hanging over the railings until someone pulled me back. Learning which vines hold and which peel off the concrete. Learning to stop and look up when the processors purged, when the warm rain came down through the open center of the shaft and the silver ghost of it fell and evaporated before it reached the bottom. I would have learned the engine sounds the way other children learn birds. I would have been told that the orchard trees on the upper terraces are older than my family and should be treated with the same courtesy. I would have believed it. I still would.

I would not be surprised by the weird things. I would know the old maintenance robots by their bad paths and their idiosyncratic pauses. I would leave oil out for the one that sweeps the same corner of Terrace Seven every morning and has been doing it, badly, for a hundred years. I would know the pale things in the sump are neighbors with no lease, not monsters. I would know the deep groves are not to be cut unless they are in the way, and that “in the way” is a judgment you make slowly. I would know that when the engines shift in a certain way, when the rain comes down through the leaves, when the light falls dappled through the shaft, you stop. You do not talk much. You watch.

So where would you find me? On the mid-levels, probably, in the greenish half-light, with wet cuffs and a rag in my pocket, palm against a wall, listening. Or on a narrow catwalk with my machete out, negotiating with a vine. Or standing on a broad terrace with everyone else while the processors purge, watching the water come down and vanish, feeling the engines slow in my teeth.

Who would I be? I would be one of the Span’s people—practical, watchful, crowded, unhurried, at home in the damp. Not an engine-keeper and not quite a grower. A seam-tender. A listener. Someone who knows the place breathes, and says so without irony, and has made a life out of the spaces where the built and the green have begun to lean into each other.
