# The Keeper of the Intake

If you want to find me in Nerei, you won't. Not on the shelf. Stand on the central platform and look north to where the channels gather and the water goes over — the raw lip, the no-railing, the place where the shale-ice simply stops — and then look *down*, past the toe of the fall into the white that has no bottom.

I'm in there.

The stair begins just under the lip, cut into the gorge wall where it runs away beneath the shelf. You won't have seen it; the lip hides it, and the city prefers it hidden, because the city is honest about most things but not about this. It's a narrow stair. It turns two hundred and some times — nobody counts anymore, counting is the kind of thing you do once and then never again — and every thirty steps or so a showerhead of seep-water drops from an overhang, so that by the fourth turn you are wet the way a person is wet after work rather than after weather, thoroughly, without notice, permanently.

Four hundred metres down you pass through the cloud line. That word is the city's, and it's wrong: the cloud has a ceiling, not a floor. Below its ceiling there is still light — grey, gauzy, moving — but no view, no distance, nothing to rest your eye on. And in the middle of that gauze, at the place where the whole of Nerei's received water leaves the world it was received into, there is my post.

The city calls it **the intake**. It is a crawl of engineered surfaces — a last grated terrace, a settling tray, a set of baffles of shale-ice, an arc of small lamps that burn thermal gas and are meant to be visible from nowhere and are. I live in a dome grown into the wall above it, four metres across, warm on the inside, always beaded on the outside, and older than the oldest of the tall angular structures up top. My family has kept the intake for eight generations and has never been one of the four hundred. The city counts its people on the shelf, and I am not on the shelf. When they need a figure for me they put me among the transient — the traders, the pilgrims, the researchers — and I let them, because the alternative would be to propose a ninth category and then I would have to define myself, and I have a job that is mostly about not defining things.

## What the work is

The cloud, rising, brings things up. This is the part the city's engineers only half understand, and the half they leave out is my half. The column is fed by the pressure gradient and the mountain's warmth, and it rises the way smoke rises, and smoke carries. So the cloud carries. It carries dust off the raw faces of the peaks, wing-cases and husks and the shed skins of the small things that live their whole lives inside it — the cloud-fauna, they have no proper names, I have made some up and written them in my ledger because an unnamed thing is easier to stop seeing. It carries spores. It carries the smell of the gorge, which is mineral and green in a way that there is no green anywhere to account for, and which arrives as a taste on the water I drink.

And it carries, once every few days, in the moments when the column slows at dusk and thins toward something almost clean, a residue from above. Stone filings. Fibre. The particular ash of shale-ice being worked in a workshop three terraces up. That's the city coming down to me. I have found, in the tray, a fragment of a funerary cord from a cemetery I could name. I have found a page.

The work is simple and it is not simple. The tray catches what the fall throws out — the falls can't take everything down with them; some things are too light, and they come back up instead, riding the same convection. Every morning at the change of light I work the tray with my hands, dry them on the warm stone of the lamp arbour, and name what I found. Then I write the names in the ledger. Then I release what I found back to the fall, or to the heat, or to the ground under the tray that is not ground but engineered surface and swallows most things eventually.

The ledger has two columns, which is the city's idea and my concession. **Received** and **Released.** They do not balance. They have never balanced, not in eight generations, not in any year of any generation, and the first thing my mother taught me is that a keeper who makes them balance is no longer reading, only writing. So I keep them uneven, and the unbalance is the true record. It is the only honest history of the cloud that exists.

## The one rule

Everything the cloud brings up gets named and released downward. Everything. Not kept, not offered to the archives, not quietly returned to a family in the fourth ring, though I have been asked. The dead come this way — not to my tray, not whole; they fall past me into the white, and the white takes a long time with them, and what reaches me is what the fall has made of them by then, which is mineral and fibre and a faint change in the taste of the water that I have learned not to describe to anyone from the shelf.

The city believes the cycle returns clean water. It does. It returns clean. That is a chemical fact and it is true and I am the person who confirms it every morning with my hands. But the city's comfort lives in the word *eventually*, which is a word about time, and I am the only person in Nerei who has seen the scale of it. So the water comes home clean, and something in between is not clean, and both are true, and my profession — this is the sentence I would carve above the dome if the dome had a door — **my profession is keeping the unknown at accurate size.**

Not smaller. Smaller is the city's temptation; smaller is what you get when you let the vellum do the work of forgetting. Not larger, either; larger is what visitors want, the cloud as oracle, the intake as a chapel. Accurate. That is all. That is enough work for one life.

## Maren

The keeper of the fourth-ring north channel hears my changes before anyone else. Not because she has been told — because her channel tells her. There is a note it makes when upstream the baffles have shifted, or a sluice has crept a quarter-notch, or I have moved something in the tray in the night. She has described it to me as a thinness. She knows it means nothing is wrong yet. She has never asked me what I do down here. She is a keeper, and keepers understand that the gap is sacred, and the largest gap in Nerei is the four hundred metres between her channel and my dome. She keeps her side of it beautifully. I keep mine.

Once a year, when the cloud thins and the city stands on its platform and weeps into the sudden light, the thin air lets sound travel more honestly than usual, and I can hear them up there — a whole city, four hundred quiet people, all of them at the edge at once. It is the loudest thing I ever hear. I go out onto the intake stair, where the light comes down my shaft in one hard column, and I sit, and I am bright for an hour, and then the cloud closes over and I am not.

The visitors ask the shelf-dwellers why the intake keeper doesn't come up. The honest answer is that the intake keeper is the outermost vellum — you can't have a gap and then walk across it every day. The other answer is that someone must hear the column when it slows, and be there when it turns, and name what it leaves, and let it go again. And the third answer, which I have never said aloud: I have been down here so long that I no longer know whether the edge is keeping me from the city or the city from the edge, and I have decided that this is not a question that gets to be answered for me.

## The climb

I go up four times a year, for the bio-works and the thermal allotment, and every time, on the way back, I stop at the lip — the raw place, the no-railing, the place where the shale-ice simply stops — and stand in the city's quiet with my back to it and my face in the rising wet, and I count nothing, and I wait.

Then I turn and take the stair down, and at the fourth turn I'm wet the way I'm always wet, and at the cloud ceiling the light goes gauzy, and at the intake the tray is waiting, and the water is falling, and the cloud is rising, and I crouch and put my hands in the received and the released, both columns, and the ledger is uneven still.
