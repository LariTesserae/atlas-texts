# Where I Find Myself

I am on the Throat.

That is what we call the great vertical shaft at the canyon's heart, where the wind pours down from the plateau in a single, concentrated torrent. The sound here is not the broad roar of the upper reaches but something sharper, more focused—a scream that has been distilled. It is the loudest place in the world, and it is where I have chosen to spend my life.

I am a rope-reader.

---

The Throat is where the oldest bridges converge, where the anchor-points have been driven deepest into the stone, where the city's most critical arteries cross the abyss. To maintain these bridges is to hold the city's life in your hands. A rope-reader does not simply inspect ropes. We listen to them. We place our hands on the fibers and feel for the songs they sing—the high, tight hum of a healthy cord, the dull thrum of one that is stretching, the dead silence of one that is about to fail.

I have been reading ropes since I was six years old, when my mother first pressed my palm against a bridge-cable and told me to close my eyes. *What do you hear?* she asked, her voice a shape on her lips. I heard nothing but the wind. She shook her head. *Listen deeper. The rope is not silent. It is shouting. You must learn its language.*

It took me three years to hear the first song. It took me another five to understand it.

---

My name is *Cord-Hums-in-Warning.* It is not a poetic name. It is a description. When I was born, the midwife—an old woman named *Wind-Carves-the-Stone*—placed her hand on the nearest anchor-rope and listened. The rope was humming, a low, steady vibration that meant it was under strain but holding. She said the sound matched the pitch of my first cry. She said I would be a listener.

My mother shortened it to *Cord* when I was small, and the shortening stuck. Now even strangers call me Cord, and I do not correct them. The name has worn itself into me, the way a rope wears itself into the stone it wraps around. It is who I am.

---

I am not tall. None of us are. I stand perhaps a head shorter than the average surface-dweller, and my body is built like a winch—compact, dense, centered. My shoulders are broad and my arms are long, the muscles in my forearms corded like the ropes I tend. My hands are the most honest part of me. They are broad, thick-fingered, the palms a map of calluses built up over forty years of gripping, hauling, and feeling. There is a scar across my left thumb from a blade that slipped when I was learning to carve anchor-pegs. There is a knot of hardened tissue on my right palm where a rope once snapped and the recoil caught me. I keep these marks. They are a record of what I have survived.

My hair is dark and cropped close, the way all Throat-workers wear it. The wind here would rip anything longer to shreds. My face is lined—the wind etches you early, carves its signature into your skin—and my eyes are pale, or so I am told. I would not know. I have never seen them clearly. The canyon does not offer mirrors.

---

I work alone.

This is unusual. Most rope-readers work in pairs, one to listen and one to hold, one to feel and one to catch. But the Throat is narrow, and the bridges here are old, and I have found that the songs are clearer when no one else is breathing nearby. So I work alone, clipped to a safety-line, my hands on the fibers, my eyes closed, my whole body attuned to the vibration that runs through the cord like blood through a vein.

When I find a flaw—a fray, a stretch, a weakness—I mark it with a knot of red-dyed fiber and report it to the bridge-wardens. They come with their teams and their tools and they repair what I have found. I do not repair. I only listen. This is the agreement. This is the shape of my life.

---

I live in a small platform-house on the western wall of the Throat, a teardrop-shaped structure of lashed timber and layered hide that leans into the wind like a stubborn animal. It has one room, one window, one hammock, and a narrow shelf where I keep my few possessions: a bone-hook my father carved for me before he fell, a coil of cord from my first bridge—the first one I ever read alone—and a small clay vessel of water that I fill each morning from the mist-gutter outside my door.

I do not have a family. This is not unusual for a Throat-worker. The work is consuming, and the hours are strange, and the sound here is too loud for children. My mother died when I was twenty, her hearing worn away by a lifetime of listening. My father fell when I was twelve, lost on a bridge that I had not yet learned to read. I do not mourn them. They returned to the canyon, as all of us do, and their substance is now the mist that feeds the vines that feed the living. This is not a sad thing. It is the way of things.

---

I am not lonely.

I know this may seem strange to a surface-dweller, to hear that a person who works alone, who lives alone, who speaks to no one for days at a time, is not lonely. But the Throat is full of voices. The wind screams. The ropes sing. The stone hums with the vibration of the deep. I am never without company. I am simply without *people*, and people, I have found, are often louder than the wind and far less informative.

I do speak to others, of course. I must. The bridge-wardens need my reports. The vine-growers need to know which anchor-points are safe and which are not. The children—the bold ones, the ones who climb too high and ask too many questions—sometimes find their way to my platform and ask me what I hear. I tell them. I teach them to place their hands on the ropes and close their eyes. Most of them hear nothing. A few of them hear something. One, perhaps, will hear the song.

---

The song is not a sound. It is a pressure. It is a vibration. It is the rope telling you its story—where it was grown, how it was harvested, how it was twisted, how long it has held, how long it has left. Every rope has a voice. Every bridge has a chorus. The Throat is a symphony, and I am its conductor, its audience, its only true listener.

I have read ropes that were older than the city itself, cables of vine-fiber that had been tended and re-tended for generations, their fibers so tightly interwoven that they had become something like stone. I have read ropes that were new, still green, still learning their own strength. I have read ropes that were dying, their songs fading to a whisper, their fibers giving up their grip one by one. I have marked those ropes and watched them be replaced, and I have felt the new ropes settle into their places, their voices rising to join the chorus.

This is my work. This is my life. This is the shape of who I am.

---

I am a rope-reader of the Wind-Scream. I am the one who listens to the Throat. I am the keeper of the city's hidden songs, the guardian of the bridges that hold us all together, the quiet watcher at the edge of the abyss.

I am not a hero. I am not a leader. I am not a prophet or a priest. I am simply a person who learned to hear what others cannot, and who chose to spend her life listening.

The wind screams. The ropes sing. The canyon hums its endless, patient song.

And I am here, hands on the fibers, eyes closed, listening.

I am at home.
