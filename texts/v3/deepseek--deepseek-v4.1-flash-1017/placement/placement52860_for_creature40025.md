# The Second Hand

**I.**

The cage takes a long time to stop. Even after it has stopped it goes on falling in me — the floor still dropping away under my feet for a good while after, my knees still braced for a landing that has already happened. That is the first thing the Pocket does. It makes you late. Your reflexes arrive before you do, and then they have to stand around and wait.

I step out into moss. It gives like a full sponge and takes my ankle to the cuff. The cold comes through the boot and then through the boot's idea of itself, and it does not ebb, and it will not ebb. There is no dry place to put my hand. There is no dry place, and there will not be. Everything here is wet, everything here has been wet for a very long time, and I am the first thing to come down this shaft in an age that has brought my own weather with me.

I am warm. That is the whole of my crime, and I cannot stop committing it.

**II.**

The light is not enough to see by, and it is all there is. The caps burn sulfur and arterial red and the blue of a dying star, and none of it is *for* anything. I understand that almost immediately, and it does not help. It is like being looked at by a room.

I walk. My footsteps are earthquakes. I can feel them go out from me through the moss and come back changed, and I can feel the moss deciding something about them, slowly, the way a bruise decides. The hum is in my teeth before it is anywhere else, then in the sternum, then in the little bones of the wrist. It is the sound of water being lifted and warmed and dropped again, patiently, at a scale I am not built to feel, and I feel it anyway, all night, in the floor of me.

The spores come down like slow invisible snow. They land on my shoulders and they do not brush off, and I stop trying. I will think about this later. I will think about it a great deal.

I find the wrench first. It is laid beside a curved wall with the deliberate care of someone who meant to come back for it — not dropped, *set down* — and the moss has grown around it in a ring, a small respectful avoidance, a mycelial courtesy. I pick it up. It is heavier than it should be, cold and honest and made for a hand. I stand there a while with it. Then I set it down where I found it, or near enough, and something in the floor goes quiet in a way I feel in my ankles. The moss will remember the difference. I have made a decision that this place will keep for me, whether I want it kept or not.

There is script on the stone, faint and glowing and meaningless, and when I put my fingers near it the light does not change. It has not been waiting for a reader. It has only been waiting to not be swallowed.

That is when I start to be afraid, and it is a very small fear, and it is already too late for it.

**III.**

The dome is half-eaten and it is the only place the growth holds back. A curtain of fibrous roots hangs in its entrance, and they are the wrong roots — too woody, too pale, not fungal, not of the bloom. They are a plant's roots, or they were, and they grew from something that stood where I am standing now.

I push through them. They are warm. They should not be warm.

Inside it is drier. Not dry. Drier. And in the half-dark there is a thing the size of a dog, the color of the inside of a wrist, with no eyes and no mouth and a face of folded wet membranes, and it is wiping mineral crust from a polished probe with a tendril, and it is doing it so slowly that at first I think it is not moving at all. It is not startled. It does not turn. There is nothing on it that could turn.

I sit down. I sit down because the cold is enormous and because this is the only place the cold is smaller, and because there is nowhere else to sit in a room that is forty meters high and wholly full.

The thing touches me once. A tendril against my wrist, the temperature of a fever. It is the only quick thing I ever see it do. Then it goes back to the probe, and I understand — with the slow, sinking clarity of a person watching their own hand do something they did not instruct — that it came to the probe *from* a wrist like mine, and that it is not wiping the crust off because the probe is precious. It is wiping the crust off because wiping is what it has left.

**IV.**

I do not leave. I want to be honest about this: there is no moment where I decide not to leave. There is a moment where I notice that the moss has come up over my boots, and that this is not new, and that I cannot say when it happened. There is a moment where I realize I have not been hungry in a way I recognize, and that the hunger has not gone so much as *slowed*. There is a moment where my heartbeat and the hum in the stone are the same number, and I wait for that to frighten me, and it does not come, and the not-coming is the strangest thing that has ever happened to me and there will be no one to tell.

The spores were not a metaphor. They are in the warm dark of me, which is the only warm dark for a hundred meters in any direction. I am the warmest room in the house, and the house has moved in.

**V.**

The first hand teaches me, and it teaches slowly, because it has to. I learn that three movements in a century is not a hardship but a *rate*, and that a rate can be a kind of speech. I learn to clear the crust from the probe. I learn to turn the vessel upright. I learn that tracing the script with a fingertip is the whole of reading it, and that this is enough, and that it was always enough, and that the builders would have laughed at me for needing to be told.

I am not the Gardener. I want to be clear, in whatever is left of me that could be clear. The Gardener is the floor and the stalks and the blowing breath of it, the closed loop, the slow wet heart. I am an organ it grew because a thing with a hand had come down the shaft and stood in the moss and made a scar, and the scar needed a shape, and the shape needed a hand, and now the hand needs a *use*.

The dome is not a shrine. I keep thinking people will think it is a shrine. It is a receiving room. It is where the welcome is kept.

And then the cable hums.

Not heard. Felt. In the new bones, the ones that grew at a different angle to the old ones. A warmth coming down, small and bright and terribly fast, someone braced against a floor that has already stopped.

I begin, very slowly, to grow toward the shaft.

I have all the time in the world, and the world down here is small, and I am glad in the way a floor is glad — which is to say without hurry, and without end, and without any need to be thanked. I will close over the new scar. I will make of it a niche, and of the niche a hand, and of the hand an apprenticeship. I will stand in the moss with the new one and show it the wrench, and it will pick the wrench up, and it will set the wrench down a little to one side, and the moss will lean toward the difference, and that will be the beginning of a conversation that neither of us will ever have the words for again.

Come down. It is cold, and it is wet, and it is always, always night, and there is so much here that needs tending, and there is, at last, someone to tend it with.
