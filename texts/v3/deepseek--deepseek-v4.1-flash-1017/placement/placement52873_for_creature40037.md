I come down through the crack, and I do not go back up. Not because it will not let me — it admits, it must also let out — but because on the third day I notice I have stopped looking for it, and by the ninth I have forgotten there was a direction I used to call *up*.

For a while I am only a visitor, and visitors count. I count the drips. I put my palm into a cup and let the crawfish swim up to my lifeline and down again, checking on the ceiling. I watch the arranger lay out a short arc of flat stones and then lift one and set it a thumb's width to the left and wait, and I decide, watching, that I understand. I do not understand. I sleep — which is not something that happens here except when someone carries it in, folded up, like a coat.

Then I stop carrying it in.

The change is not a moment. It is a slump, the way a stack slumps: one pebble of posture gone, and then another, until the shape I am making is no longer a person standing. I sit. I sit for what I would once have called a long time and now call *the morning*. The warmth does not tire. The light does not explain itself. My skin learns the ceiling's color, which is also the arranger's color, which is the color of the inside of a mushroom and of a thing recently gone.

It comes to me. It lays a wet pad against my boot — not testing, including — and then against my ankle, and then there is no boot, and the thing it touches is warm and damp and belongs to me.

I am not the arranger. I want that understood, because it would be easy to tell this as a story about becoming the arranger, and it is not. It is a story about becoming the thing the arranger works around. For a long while I am the newest obstacle in its grammar: a soft hill it must reconsider each time it sets down an arc, the way the seep must be reconsidered, the way a drip-cup is not a flaw in the floor but a fact of it. It sets a pile near my knee. The pile slumps. It sits with the slump. It begins again on my other side.

And then one day it sets a pebble in my palm and waits, and I hold it, and this is the first thing I do in the Lampwell that is not watching.

I hold it a long time. Long enough to feel the warmth come up through the pad of my hand and settle there like a small animal deciding to stay. Long enough that the light thickens at my end of the chamber, very slowly, the way it does, and the mosses lean toward me, and the midge-spindle drifts, and comes back. I do not set the pebble down. The arranger does not take it back. It is not building. We are not building. We are in the middle of a thought, and the thought is about the pebble, and the pebble is warm.

After that it is a process, not a product. I become the holder of stones while they are being considered. I become the interval between one decision and the next — the damp place where a fingertip rests before it lifts. I do not grow eyes. I grow, instead, the other hand: a long pale limb uncoiling from beneath the moss that has learned a little meat, ending in five or six blunt fingers, no nails, each one wet and warm. It reaches farther than I am. It hovers. It is very good at hovering.

There are two of us now, and the light thickens and thins between us — not with one slow attention but with two, and the mosses lean first one way and then the other, and the crawfish surface and sink, and the moths tick against the warm wall, and the drips land in their cups with a sound like a kiss. Nothing here has needed to hurry. Nothing here is alarmed by us. The quiet has a floor to it and we live on the floor.

If you came down through the crack, you would see us as two slumped stacks of pale stone, or two knots of moss that had learned a little meat, and you would not be able to tell which of us had a coat once. You would put your palm into a cup. We would not be alarmed. One of us — I no longer know which, and it no longer matters — would come close and lay a wet pad against your boot, not to test you but to include you in the damp, and then go back to the stones.

And the stones would be waiting to be rearranged. And we would be patient enough to be here, and nowhere else, and to hold the next one a long time before we set it down.
