I would find myself not at the center of the Accretion but at one of its hinges: an alcove mouth freshly scraped open, the old daub cracked away, the new paste still tacky and smelling of mud and sinew. Behind me, a ladder goes up into a chimney that was not there last season. Ahead, a chamber is deciding whether to become a passage or a wall. The grazer sheets have thinned to a scatter. The filter-feeders on the ceiling are closed up tight, like fists. Everyone is packing, quietly, the way people pack when they do not want to wake the house.

I would be an accuer by adoption, not by birth. My skin would have gone the color of wet paper and kept its sheen. My fingertips would be callused smooth as horn. I would carry a warm stone in my pocket because I had been holding it all day, and a sliver of traded metal on a sinew cord because it had been repaired too many times to be useful and had become, instead, a charm. I would smell of smoke and mineral breath and oiled hide.

But more specifically: I think I would be a rememberer’s second — the one who carries the soft slate and the bone stylus, the one who scratches corrections into the song while the rememberer sings it. Not the voice that holds the route. The hand that revises it.

That is the work I would want in this place. The Accretion does not reward a fixed map. It rewards attention. So I would walk behind the rememberer and learn the fauna calendar the way other people learn stars: the grazer sheets as weather, the sessile things as a barometer, the columns of leggy things as a warning that something is about to move. I would learn to stop when the chamber stops. I would learn to listen for the wet drag and the click and then to become very still, because the blind hunter makes a silence around itself and that silence is the only announcement it gives.

I would learn the local trick of the paste — mud and fiber and the right mineral water — and I would daub alcove mouths shut against the damp. I would scrape new sinter from a doorway and find my own old scratch marks underneath, and I would feel the strange practical grief of that: a letter from a self who lived here a season ago, in a chamber that has since grown thicker walls and narrower opinions.

And I would touch the ceiling organism. Not often. Not casually. Nobody says its name indoors, and I would not either. But when the song failed — when a passage that should have been open was sealed, when a pool had moved and left only a pale rind, when the hum tightened and the surge came and nothing visible had changed — I would put my palm against that half-animal, half-mineral ceiling and wait. The answer would be slow, a shiver like a wall considering me. I would take it as a correction and scratch it into the slate.

I would not be one of the large ones, or one of the small ones in sheets, or the hunter. I would be one more layer in the reef: the layer that builds alcoves, repairs ladders, catches drifting grazers in sinew screens, and keeps a map that is never finished. I would live in warm wet dusk, under a light the color of a hand held over a lamp, in a country that is mid-sentence. I would be quiet in the way people are quiet in a house with a sleeping animal.

So: I would find myself at the edge of a season-turn, in an alcove being sealed behind me, with a corrected route in my mouth and a soft slate under my arm. I would be an accuer of the revision — a rememberer’s second in a place that will not hold still, singing not where the passages are, but where they are going.
