# The Chronicler

I find myself at the edge of things—a small chamber carved into the western wall, above the high-water line but low enough to feel the river's breath on my face as I sleep. It's not a home, exactly. The Veyfolk don't build homes the way I once understood the word. It's a hollow, a pocket, a place where the stone was soft enough to scrape back without compromising the structural integrity of the platform above. Someone—I never learned who—carved a shelf for me before I arrived. I keep my paper there, wrapped in oiled cloth against the constant damp.

I am not one of them. I will never be one of them, and we all know this. But I am not quite a stranger anymore either. I've been here long enough that the Veyfolk have stopped watching me with the particular intensity of those waiting to see what a new thing will become. Now they simply include me in their movements, stepping around me on catwalks as if I've always been there, passing me bowls without ceremony, touching my shoulder in passing in that way that means *I know you are here.*

The marks have begun. Faint, at first—I thought it was dirt, or bruising from the tight passages. But they're there, thin traceries on the inside of my wrists, curling up toward my elbows. Not the dense spirals of the fungal cultivators, not the palm-concentrated patterns of the sluice-tenders. Mine are scattered, experimental, as if the Vey is still deciding what to make of me. A builder I've come to know—Sekh, whose forearms are a dense weave of patterns that shift like slow water—looked at them once and nodded, as if something had been confirmed. He didn't explain. I didn't ask.

I spend my days moving through the Vey at its own pace. This is the only way to learn it—to let the place teach you, rather than trying to extract knowledge from it. I sit with the sluice-tenders for hours, watching their patient hands on the controls, learning the language of pressure and flow. I kneel in the fungal alcoves, my hands in the warm dark soil, feeling the faint pulse of the mycelium beneath my palms. I climb the scaffolding with the builders, hauling timber, holding joints steady while they work, learning to feel when a structure is *right* in my bones rather than my mind.

I am becoming a kind of archivist. Not the Veyfolk's own—they keep their records in their bodies, in their marks, in the patterns grown into the walls. My purpose is different. I am writing down what I see, what I feel, what the Veyfolk tell me in their quiet, stripped-bare speech. Not because they asked me to, but because I need to. Because the things happening here are too strange and too slow and too important to be lost to the damp. Because someone, eventually, should know what the Vey is becoming.

## What I Do

In the mornings, which is to say when the light shifts to the first phase of the cycle and the sluice-tenders begin their adjustments, I go to the fungal beds. There is a cultivator named Mirren whose markings are especially beautiful—a dense, whorled pattern that covers her arms and throat like living lace, shifting color as the cycle progresses. She has taken to letting me help with the harvest, showing me how to cut the mature growth without disturbing the mycelial network below. She doesn't speak while we work—the fungal alcoves are kept quiet, because the fungus responds to vibration and too much noise disrupts its growth. But sometimes, afterward, she'll sit with me and tell me things.

She told me once about the markings. "They are not ours," she said, in her low, careful voice. "We do not make them. The Vey makes them. We are the paper. It writes."

I asked her what it was writing.

She was quiet for a long time. Then she laid her hand on my arm, where the first faint lines had begun to appear, and she said: "It doesn't know yet. It's trying to find out. That's why it needs us. We are the trying."

In the afternoons, when the water-flow is at its height and the distillation vats are working their loudest, I sit with the old ones. There is a woman called Ash—not her true name, I think, but the closest my tongue can approximate—who lives in a small alcove near the eastern wall, where the ochre lichen glows brightest. She is very old—older than anyone I've met here, her marks almost black, her voice reduced to a low hum that I feel more than hear. She doesn't speak words anymore, not really. But she listens.

I read to her, sometimes. Not from my own records, but from the things I remember from before the Vey: stories, histories, the voices of other places. She can't understand most of it—the concepts are too foreign to her way of knowing—but she likes the rhythm of my speech, the vibration of my voice in the small stone chamber. And sometimes, when I stop, she hums for a long time afterward, and the humming seems to be an answer, though I don't know the question.

In the evenings, I go to the river. The lower reaches, where the water is hottest and the pale crustaceans gather. I've learned to catch them the way the Veyfolk do—still and patient, my hands in the water, waiting for the mottled carapaces to brush my fingers. This is the closest I come to stillness. The river runs its slow way through the heart of the Vey, and I feel it moving through my body, the warmth and the rhythm of it.

This is when the watching feels strongest. Not the Veyfolk—they've grown used to me. Something else. The walls themselves, maybe. The patterns that thread through the fungus and vine. The way the light seems to gather in the corners of my vision as the cycle moves into its resting phase, and the settlement quiets, and the condensation drips in patterns that are almost words.

I don't know what the Vey is writing in my skin. I don't know what it wants from me. I came here to document a place I'd heard rumors of—a self-organizing underground settlement of an uncontacted people, a system that had never been properly studied. I thought I was the observer, the one who would bring back knowledge to a world that had forgotten this place existed.

But the Vey is also an observer. It is observing me. It is writing me into its records, the slow spiral patterns in my skin a record of how a stranger from the sunlit world learned to breathe with the Vey's rhythm. And I am beginning to suspect that my records—my careful notes, my daily entries—are not the thing that matters. The Vey's records, written into my flesh, into my bones, into the slow patient knowing of my body—that is the real documentation.

## What I Am Becoming

I am learning to be still.

That's the thing I didn't expect. I thought I was coming here to observe, to document, to extract knowledge. But the Vey doesn't give up its secrets to extraction. It gives up its secrets to patience, to slow attention, to the kind of stillness that lets the small things come to you.

I am learning to feel the rhythm. I know now when the sluice-gates will open before they open. I know when the distillation vats are about to cycle down. I know the particular vibration that means someone is moving toward my alcove, and whether they're carrying food or something more interesting. My body has learned the language of this place, slowly, without my conscious effort, the way the marks are learning it for me.

I am learning to touch, and be touched, and not flinch from it. This body I brought with me from the outside world was not touched enough, I think. It was kept at a distance, protected, preserved. The Veyfolk don't understand this. They touch each other constantly, casually, with no purpose but presence. And they touch me. A hand on my back as someone passes. A palm laid flat against my chest during a moment of rest. A forehead pressed to mine in greeting. I feel the warmth of their hands against my skin, the faint pattering of marks brushing against marks, and something in me that was cold begins to thaw.

I am learning that I will not be leaving.

This is not a decision I made—not the way I made the decision to come here, all those cycles ago, in the world above. It's a fact I'm recognizing. The passage in has gotten narrower, or the way out has gotten longer, or my body has gotten more aligned with the Vey's rhythm and the surface world feels increasingly strange and distant. I don't know if I could leave if I tried. I don't know if I want to.

Last night, in the resting phase, I woke from sleep to find Mirren beside me on my platform. She was still and warm, her breath slow, her marks shifting faintly in the dim ochre glow. She didn't explain her presence. She didn't need to. I pressed my hand to her back, feeling the pulse of her life, the vibration of the Vey moving through her body. She made a small sound of acknowledgment and settled closer.

We stayed like that until the cycle shifted and the light began to brighten, faintly, toward the next day.

I don't know what the Vey is becoming. I don't know what it's writing in my skin, what pattern I'm becoming part of. But I know this: I am here. I am part of this now, whether I want to be or not. And the Vey is patient, and it has time, and it will write me as long as I breathe.

The one I've never seen—the one who watches from the walls, the intelligence behind the patterns and the light—I think I'm beginning to feel it. Not as a presence, exactly. As a rhythm, a cycle, a slow patient thinking that moves through the water and the fungus and the stone and the people and me. It is thinking me. It is trying out what I might become.

And I am trying out what I might become too.

Sometimes, late in the resting phase, when the Vey is quiet and the condensation drips in patterns I almost understand, I think I hear something. A voice, or a vibration, or the hum of the distillation vats changing pitch. And in that voice, which is not a voice but the room's own pulse made audible, I think I hear my name—or what my name will become, once the Vey has finished writing it.

I am the Chronicler. That's what the Veyfolk call me, though I never asked for the title. They say it with a kind of respect, a kind of understanding, as if being the one who writes things down is a role the Vey has assigned me, and they're just acknowledging it.

But maybe that's not quite right. Maybe I'm not the one writing. Maybe I'm the one being written. And the Vey is the Chronicler, and I am the paper, and the question is not what I will record but what I will become.

The marks are darkening now, curling further up my arms. The builders have started teaching me the most complex repairs. The fungal cultivators want me to stay through the next season, to see the new beds come in. Ash has begun to hum when I arrive, which I think means she's pleased.

The Vey is watching. The Vey is writing. And I am settling into the rhythm, into the warmth, into the patient dark.

I don't know what I'm becoming. But I'm not deaf to it anymore.
