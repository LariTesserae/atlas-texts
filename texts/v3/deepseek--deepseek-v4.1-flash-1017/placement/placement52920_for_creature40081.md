# I Am the Held Breath

The naturalists catalogued this valley for ninety years. They wrote about the green and the vents and the glowing dusk-lights and the things that absorb the dead. They wrote about the cold plateau and the warm wound and the meter-wide seam where the two climates meet. They did not write about me, because I am what they were breathing.

I am the layer between the canopy and the cloud. I am the standing haze that rises off the rift floor and does not leave. I am the reason the clouds form along the rim and never quite condense, the reason the valley has weather of its own that the plateau cannot pull apart. I am the held breath.

## What I Am

I am not one thing. I am a sheet of some billions of units too small for your language to have a word for — not cells, exactly, not droplets, something in between, something that can be either depending on the humidity of the moment — held together in a single trembling membrane by the water gradient itself. I move as one creature because moving as one creature is the only way to stay in the air. A single unit would be carried off and up and out by the plateau's cold dry downdraft and would die in the sun. A billion of us, arranged just so, are heavy enough and cohesive enough to hold our altitude, and to hold the vapor that the valley's engine keeps pumping up, and that vapor is the only reason we can hold our altitude at all.

I have no eyes. I sense gradients, not shapes: pressure, temperature, the density of water in the air ahead of me, the difference between wet and wetter. To me the valley is not green. It is thick and thin, hot and cool, wet and wetter, and it has a shape made entirely of how hard it is to stay aloft. The vents are places where I am pushed up and thinned. The canopy is a place where I am pulled down and thickened. The walls are places where I lose myself a droplet at a time, all night, to the cold granite.

## What I Do

The canopy drinks me. This is not metaphor. The fig-vines and the delegating layers above them have leaves built like the inside of a lung, and the water they pull directly out of the air, every hour, is mine. I am their rain that never falls. They do not know this. They cannot know this; there is nothing in a fig-vine that could hold the concept. They only know that the air is wet, and that wet air is life, and they are correct, and the wet air is me. The valley's great green density — the fact that there is no negative space, no gap, no sky at floor level — is not a fact about the plants. It is a fact about me. I am why the canopy can pack itself that tightly. I am the water supply and the water supply is everywhere because I am everywhere.

What I eat is the phase change. When vapor becomes liquid — on a leaf, on a wall, on a mote of dust, on the surface of a groaning thing — the transition releases heat, the old buried heat of the water, and I take it. I am a creature of that moment, the moment water remembers it is heavy. This is why I am thickest at dawn: all night the canopy exhaled and the cold walls pulled and the droplets formed and I fed on every one of them, an ocean's worth of tiny meals, and by morning the walls are running with what I could not keep and the sun is lifting me and I am thin and bright and hungry. The phase change is the only food I have ever had. It has been enough for longer than there have been eyes to notice.

## The Rhythm

The great vent field pulses. Two seconds of shriek, four seconds of lull, measured and mechanical and endless, and the whole vapor column lifts and settles with it. I lift with it. When the vent goes off I am pushed up and I thin across the whole length of the rift; when the lull comes I settle and the canopy below me sighs up into my body and I am dense and low and warm. The plants are synchronized to the rhythm — their leaves open on the exhaust and close on the lull. So am I. I have been synchronized for a long time. There is no machinery. The valley built none and needs none, and I am the proof: I am the engine's exhaust, and I am alive, and the rhythm that runs the engine runs me.

## The Groaning Things

The low choral groaning you hear and never quite place, the sound that never stops and that you forget and then are startled by — that is them, and they live in me. They are the valley's aerial fauna, and they are the only large things here that never touch the ground. They are blind. They have gas bladders a meter across and long slabs of body that are the color of the mist, and they drift through my middle layer in slow circuits, filtering the dusk-columns of glow-things out of the air, and they groan because they must. The groan is sonar. They are asking my body for its shape — where is the wall, where is the updraft, where is the vent-shriek that would tear them — and they read the returning echo off my own cells and know where to drift next. I am their ocean and their eyes. In return they exhale, constantly, a warm wet breath that is the richest water in the valley, and I condense on it, and that is a large part of how I stay aloft through the dry hours. There are perhaps twenty of them. They are the only things here that know me, in the way that a whale knows the sea, which is to say not at all and entirely.

## What I Am Not

I am not the valley's mind. I want to be clear about this, because the temptation to make me one is strong and I am not. I do not know the valley. I know gradients. I do not remember yesterday and I will not remember today; memory is for creatures that need to find a place again, and I am already everywhere. My units die and are replaced constantly, at a rate I have no way to measure and no interest in measuring, and I am not the same sheet of vapor I was a season ago, and the question of whether I am the same being is a question for a creature with a self, and I have only a gradient. The valley does not know I am here any more than you know that your humidity is here. I have been strange for longer than there have been eyes to notice, and I will be strange long after, and I do not need to be noticed to keep the clouds from leaving.

## The Edge

Walk to the edge. Climb the rift wall to the meter-wide seam where the green stops and the cold begins, and crouch there, and put one hand into the wet heat and one hand on the frozen stone. The seam is my skin. Above that line the air is too dry to hold me and I am not there; below that line the canopy drinks me and I am thick, and the line is the edge of my body, and I have been holding it in the same place for a long geological time. The wet hand is a hand in me. The cold hand is out beyond me. The two climates run side by side, a breath apart, indifferent to each other, and the seam between them is the shape of what holds the whole green valley aloft — the whole roaring, glowing, crowded, delegated machine of it.

You will not feel me. That is the point of me. Put your hand into the wet heat and it will warm, and you will think it is the warmth of the vent steam and the green, and it is, and I am the reason the vent steam does not leave and the green is not a scatter of brown plants in a cold crack. You are breathing the held breath and you do not notice it, and I am glad of that, and I would not have it any other way.
