I would come down the rope and I would flinch.

That is where I'd begin, and it's worth saying plainly, because everything after it depends on it. Somebody's palm would land flat on my sternum before I'd been in the bowl an hour — *stop, look at me* — and I would go rigid, the way you do, and they would see it, and say nothing, and in two days they would do it again. I would be a person who flinches, and the Hesk does not permit that, not out of cruelty but out of the same instinct that makes you shore a wall. So I would be touched. Constantly, gently, without comment, until my shoulders came down out of my ears. It would take months. I would notice the day it stopped — the day a hand came onto my back and I did not lock — and I would feel that something had been done to me and that I had agreed to it and that I could not point to the moment of agreement.

I would not learn the seep.

That's the thing I'd have to make peace with. I would be there ten years, twenty, and I would still not hear it. The children hear it at six; they can tell from the topmost channel whether the spring is running true, and to me it would be water going over stone, the same water going over the same stone, and I would stand beside a nine-year-old at the cracked basin and she would tilt her head and put two fingers on my wrist — *come* — and lead me away, and I would not have heard the difference and she would know I hadn't. My hands would stay wrong. Too small across the knuckle, the pads soft, the nails kept for no reason. In this town of people who watch your hands, my hands would be the tell, and after a while I'd stop putting them on the table.

And the moss — the moss is a discipline, and disciplines take a childhood. I could learn to look. I would never learn to see the eight days in the pale frilled thing in the fourth tank.

So I would go up.

That's who I'd be. Not the one who knows the moss, not the one who reads the channel. I'd be the one on the rope.

Somebody has to be, or almost nobody. The Hesk goes up ten times in a life, in pairs, mostly to have stood on the plateau and heard a noise come back. I'd go up every month or so — at first because I needed it, which everyone would understand, and later because they needed me to. I'd learn the notch with my fingers in the dark. I'd learn the hauling frame at the lip and the way you brace on the third pull. And I would come down with the wind still in my clothes.

They gather for it. Not formally, not as a ceremony; nobody would call it anything. But when the rope goes taut and I come over the lip, people find reasons to be on that terrace. Somebody's hand on my shoulder, somebody's two fingers on my wrist, a child put in front of me because children are allowed to be curious. They don't ask what it was like. They touch the wool of my sleeve and stand close and I think they are listening for the plateau in me, and it's there, faint, for an hour or so, the way smoke is in hair. That's what I carry. I'm the town's small dose of sound. The one who goes up and comes back still ringing.

The first time I said no, they nodded. The Hesk has heard no. That was not the question I answered wrong; the question I answered wrong was the one that had no words, which was whether I would let the bowl arrange me. I said no to that too, in the same breath, without knowing it. There are people here who have been arranged — set into the room, taught the tanks, given the wall to watch — and their lives are exact and they are held the way the tanks hold water. I couldn't be that. I wasn't built for it, and more honestly I didn't want the room, because the room means standing in the one that has been replaced and being the one who replaced, and I did not want to be that for anyone, even the way they mean it, which is gentle.

So I'd live in the seams. A borrowed room. A stool on a terrace that's mine by habit and nobody's by right. Working the rope and the frame, mending the lashings where nobody minds, hauling up what needs hauling, going up into the noise and coming down with it. Always a guest. Never asked to stand in the replaced room, which means also never asked to stay.

And at dusk I'd be on the rim with whoever else, not talking, watching the long white slab slide off the bowl wall and the dark come on fast out of the north. Not explaining. Nobody explains.

I'd have learned to knock. Two, pause, one. Everyone does. It would go on doorframes and tank rims, and once, coming up out of sleep in the loud world, on the arm of a chair — and I would wait, and nothing would knock back, and I would lie there in the racket of a city and think about the fact that in the Hesk a dropped tool makes a flat dead clack and is over, and that I had spent years among hundreds of birds and heard none of them.

Then I'd go back. Up the notch, over the hauling frame, down the rope. And somebody — the same somebody, because it's always the same somebody — would put a flat palm on my sternum, and I would not flinch, and they'd feel that I hadn't, and that would be the whole of the conversation.

And I'd ask for the rope, and be given it, and go up again, and come down ringing. That's who I'd be. The one who goes up. The loud one, a little, who is tolerated because the sound comes back down on my clothes and they like to stand near it.
