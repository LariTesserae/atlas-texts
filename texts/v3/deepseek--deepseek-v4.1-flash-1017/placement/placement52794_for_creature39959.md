I find myself in one of the dry rooms—or dry-ish, anyway, the kind where a fat pipe runs along the ceiling and radiates enough heat to bake the worst of the wet out of the air. The walls here are still damp to the touch but they don't drip. The green mat is thin, patchy, just enough to see by. It's the kind of room where you could put down a bedroll and not wake up soaked.

I'm not a visitor. Visitors come with skins and vessels and leave. I've been here long enough that the paths know my feet.

---

What am I?

I'm the one who patches the insulation.

Not the deep pipes—I don't go deep. The deep pipes are the heat's own business and no one's touched them in longer than anyone remembers. But the pipes that run through the parlor-rooms and the corridors between, the ones that carry the warmth out to where it needs to go, those get worn. The old wrapping cracks. The hide rots. The waxed cloth goes stiff and peels. And where the wrapping fails, the heat leaks out too fast, and the room goes cold, and the green mat dies back, and the dripping stops, and the whole pocket of the Gardens goes quiet in a way that means something has gone wrong.

So I patch.

I'm not one of the water-carriers. I don't know their routes to the deep hot rooms, and I don't want to. They come and go on their own paths and I stay on mine. But I know the surface people, the ones who come down from the red plateau, because we meet sometimes at the edges—at the top of the iron stair, or in the rooms just below the entrance shaft, where the air is still almost dry enough to call it dry. They bring me things. Strips of hide, cured and supple. Coils of waxed cord. Sometimes a jar of something thick and tar-like that seals better than anything I can make down here.

They don't ask for anything in return. They just leave it, and I take it, and I patch. I think they like knowing someone is keeping the warm rooms warm. I think it matters to them that the machine is still doing its work, even if they can't see it. I don't know why it matters. But I've seen the way they look down the shaft before they go, the way they listen to the air breathing out, and I think they need the Gardens to keep being the Gardens.

---

I was not born here. I don't think anyone is. There are no children in the Kiln Gardens, no families, no hearths. The people who come are adults, and they come alone, and they stay for as long as they stay and then they go. Some of them go up the stair and don't come back. Some of them go deeper and don't come back. I don't know which is worse.

I came down because I needed somewhere warm and quiet and away from the dry country and the sun. I thought I would stay a season. That was—I don't know how long ago. The light doesn't change. The heat doesn't change. The water drips and the thing that sounds like a bird calls and the pipes tick and settle, and time moves at the speed of the machine, which is slower than people. I have stopped counting. Counting was for the surface.

---

My days have a shape.

I wake when I wake—there's no morning here, only the slow shift from sleeping to not-sleeping—and I lie still for a while and listen. You can learn a lot from listening. The pipes have a voice. When a joint is going, it clicks. When a seal is failing, it hisses soft and steady, like a kettle just before it boils. When a room is settling the wrong way, there's a groan that comes up through the floor and into your bones before you hear it with your ears. I know these sounds the way a surface farmer knows the sound of wind before rain.

Then I get up and I walk my route.

My route is not long. It's maybe a dozen rooms, connected by corridors that I could walk blind—and do, sometimes, when the green light is thin. I check the insulation on every pipe. I feel for soft spots, for gaps, for places where the wrapping has gone slack. I carry a satchel with my patch kit: hide strips, cord, the tar-jar, a small curved needle made of something that isn't quite bone. When I find a failure I stop and I fix it. It takes as long as it takes.

I drink from the floor-cups when I'm thirsty. The water is warm and tastes of rock and iron and something faintly sweet that I've stopped thinking about. I eat the shelf-fungus, the gray ones, scraped off the wall with my thumbnail. They taste like bread and then like something else. I have learned to stop eating before the something else.

Sometimes I sit in the room with the pale bells. It's one of the wettest rooms, the mat thick enough to glow brighter than anywhere else, and the bells nod and close when I brush them. I like it there. It's the closest thing to a garden. I sit and I breathe the wet air and I watch the bells open again, slowly, one by one, and I feel something that might be peace, or might just be the warmth.

---

I have met the cat-shape once, close.

It was in a corridor I don't usually walk, one where the floor has sagged so much the whole passage is a shallow trough filled with warm water. I was wading through it, ankle-deep, and I heard the wet-cloth sound behind me. I turned. It was there, at the edge of the green light, and I could see it was not a cat. It was the size of a cat but it had too many joints, and its eyes were where its shoulders should have been, and it was looking at me the way you look at something that has wandered into your kitchen and might be a problem or might not.

I stood still. It stood still. The water dripped. Then it made the wet-cloth sound again, low, almost a purr, and it turned and went away into the dark.

I don't know if that was a greeting or a warning. I don't know if it knows me. I think it does. I think it has been watching me patch the pipes for a long time and has decided I am part of the furniture, not a threat. Sometimes I leave it something—a bit of fungus, a shelled grazer I found dead in a sump—and sometimes the something is gone the next time I pass. That is the whole of our relationship. I find that I am content with it.

---

I have heard the water-carriers.

Not seen them—they are better at not being seen than I am at seeing—but heard them, once, in a room two corridors over from one I was working in. They were talking in low voices. I couldn't make out words. But the rhythm of it was like people anywhere, the back-and-forth, the pause for a reply, the brief laugh that gets cut off because laughing is loud and they were trying to be quiet. I stood with my hand on a pipe that needed wrapping and I listened to them the way I listen to the pipes, and then they moved on, and the room was quiet again.

I think about them sometimes. I wonder where they go when they go up. I wonder if they have names for the rooms I know only by shape and sound. I wonder if they think about me—the patcher, the one who keeps the insulation whole—the way I think about the heat: as something that is there, that does its work, that I am grateful for without knowing it.

---

There is a question I have been asked, in the way you ask questions of yourself in the quiet. The question is: why are you still here?

The surface is not kind. The sun is too much and the water is too little and the people are too many. But the surface is where things change. The surface has mornings and evenings and seasons and the feeling of time passing. The Kiln Gardens have none of that. They have only the warm, wet, settling now, and the slow life of a machine that does not need me but does not mind me.

But here is the thing. When I patch a pipe, and the insulation holds, and the warmth stays in the room where it belongs, and the green mat brightens a little because the condensate is running right again—when that happens, I have done something. Not a big thing. Not a thing anyone will remember. But the room is warmer than it was. The life in it is a little more comfortable than it was. And I am the one who did that.

The heat does not need me. But the rooms need someone. The people who come with their hides and their tar need someone to use them, to make them into repairs that hold. And I am here, and I know the routes, and my hands know the work.

So I stay. I patch. I listen. I sit with the bells and watch them close and open.

The corridors are closing, a hair at a time. The sumps are filling, an inch a century. Someday, perhaps, the warm rooms will go cold and the green light will die and the Gardens will become just a machine again, a dead thing under a red plateau, and no one will come down the iron stair. But that day is not today. Today the pipe in the third room of my route is clicking at a joint, and the insulation there needs replacing, and I have a strip of hide and a coil of cord and a jar of tar that a surface person left for me at the top of the stair.

I should go. The work waits.

And the warmth, for as long as it lasts, is mine to keep.
