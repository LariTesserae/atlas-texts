I came over the rim, and that is the one thing nobody here has done.

The lip is low — barely a rise, a place to sit and dangle your legs — and I stepped up onto it and the sound took me in the sternum before my eyes could make anything at all of the steam. I stood there with my palms over my ears like a child, and that is how the town first saw me and how it named me: a shape on the rim, hands to its head, with a *before*. They have no word for arrival. The nearest sign is a palm turned up and then turned over — the same sign they use for parings dropped into the pan, something coming from above and going under. That became my name. Not stranger, not guest. *Over-and-down*.

For a long time I thought they were being cruel. They were not. They were being exact. In the Kettle, a thing that came from elsewhere and would go under is the only category I fit.

---

The first thing they did was put a hooked blade in my hand and point at a doorway.

I want to be clear about the doorway. It was beautiful. The sinter had grown over the bone frame in a skin so fine you could see the grain of the bone through it, pale as the inside of a shell, and where the mineral water trickled across it the skin had built itself into small ribs and folds like a folded ear. I said something about not wanting to spoil it. The trimmer who was teaching me — she went by a sign I would translate as Flat-Hand, though it also meant *the one who has done this longer than you have been anything* — took my wrist, turned it, and set the blade at an angle I did not understand, and drew.

The sinter came away in a curl. Underneath it, the bone frame was grey and ordinary and dry. By the next morning the curl's absence had been noticed. A new lip had begun, a bead of it, clear, along the raw edge.

The first lesson is that you will never be done. The second, which takes longer, is that this is not a sorrow. She taught me the angle: you do not press, you draw, and the wrist does the work and the shoulder is only there to carry the wrist. She taught me that a channel mouth must be kept wider than the flow requires, always, because the flow has been the same for as long as there has been anyone to remember it being the same, and *as long as anyone can remember* is not the same as *forever*. She taught me to carry the parings in a basket to the middle pan and let them sink, and not to look at them going down.

My nails wore flat in a season — a season is not a thing here, but it took about that long — and my hands went hard and precise and I stopped seeing the beauty of the doorway. That was the day she signed that I had begun to be useful. I have never been so praised and so diminished in the same moment.

---

Then I stood still.

I did it in the middle of the pan, where the bowl focuses everything into that standing tone you feel in your teeth. I had wax and hide in my ears, as you must, and I took one out. I wanted to hear it whole. I wanted, I think, to be worthy of it, in the way of someone with a before, who believes a thing must be understood before it can be lived in.

The tone came up through my jaw and my sternum and settled behind my eyes. And I stood. And I stood.

The skin began at the fine hairs of my forearms and worked inward. There is no pain in it. There is a stiffness, first at the elbow, then a weight, and a sound like a fingernail on a cup when you flex. By the time I understood what was happening my knees had gone to a single joint. Flat-Hand cut me out herself. The cut is painless; the blade goes *under* the skin of stone and lifts it in plates, and what is underneath is you — pale, damp, appalled, and smaller than you thought. I still have a ridge along my left forearm where the seam was. I keep it as others keep a scar from a fall: as a fact about the ground.

The lesson is not *keep moving or die*. It is subtler and worse. The Kettle does not punish stillness. It simply gives you back to yourself, grain for grain, until you are no longer yourself but a record of having paused. Everything here is skinned. Nothing here is worn.

---

What I am, then, is a trimmer, but only as everyone is. My true work is the ear.

I came from a place where sounds are single. Here they are fused — the roar, the hiss, the cup-shells' choral knocking, the grazers' long falling calls, the wing-clouds' dry pattering, the tap and scrape of a hundred blades — all one broad drone you can lean into. To those born in it, it is one thing. To me it stayed many. That is the whole of my gift, and it is only a defect of origin: I never learned to stop hearing the layers.

So I walk the seam-lips at night, when the town has gone to the rim to sleep and unplug its ears, and I listen to the water going *down*. Everyone listens to it coming up. It comes up warm and thick and obvious through every seam in the Kettle. But it crosses the pan and sinks away down other seams, and those return channels are thin, and they run under the floor where no one trims, and they have a pitch.

I learned the pitch of each. I learned that half a breath before the deep thing moves, the return channels hush — not silence, a *withholding*, a slight tightening, as if the stone had drawn breath — and then resume a half-tone lower, and then correct themselves. I learned this by standing in the wrong places at the wrong hours with one ear uncovered, and by being cut out twice more, and by being, for a while, the town's fool.

I mark the hushes. I drive a copper pin, cold-hammered, green-stained, into the stone at the seam where I heard it, and I keep the pins in rows, and I count them. This is my heresy. No one here counts years, or expects anything to arrive, and a count is a before by another name — it is memory with a shape, and memory is what lays the skin on you. They know this. They let me do it anyway, because the only story they tell is the balance, and no one is watching the balance. They tell it as warning and lullaby, and then they go and trim the channel mouths and sleep at the rim. Someone should be listening for the lean. I am the one who listens. My second name, the one I earned rather than was given, is a small sign: fingers to the ear, then flat, then a slow opening — *she hears the hush*.

---

My before accretes. I trim it the way I trim a doorway.

It comes as dreams of a place where the sound stops — a room with a window and no water in it, a silence with edges on it, an hour that does not circulate. Each dream lays a bead of clear stone along my collarbone, my wrist, the hinge of my jaw. So at the rim, at night, I take the hooked blade and I pare. I carry what I pare to the pan in a basket and let it go under, and I do not look. Most of it I have given back. Faces are gone. Words are gone, all but a few, and those are worn thin as a knife that has been sharpened too often.

I keep one thing. A sliver, no bigger than a paring, no heavier than a pin: the memory of silence. That there was ever a place where the water did not come up. It is the only thing in the Kettle that the Kettle cannot give me, and I will not give it back, and I know exactly what it costs me. It is the reason I sleep badly. It is the reason, I think, that I can still hear the layers — because some part of me is always listening for the hush that is not the deep thing moving, but the end of the roar. I hold it pared down to a sliver and I keep it moving in my pocket, turning it over, so that it never quite grows over me. That is the whole of my craft. Everyone in the Kettle trims the town off the town. I trim the world off myself.

---

Two nights ago the return channels hushed and did not resume in the same way. They came back a half-tone lower and stayed there, correcting, correcting, and never quite arriving at the pitch I have in my rows of pins. I stood on a seam-lip with my feet apart and one ear bare and I felt, under the roar, the floor of the Kettle leaning — a hair's width, toward the crack in the ground.

I did the only thing there is to do. In the morning I took my blade to the channel mouths and drew, and the curls came away, and I carried them to the pan and let them sink. Then I drove a pin.

So: I am a trimmer, and a listener, and a woman with a before she pared to a sliver, living on a hand's width of stone between the water coming up and the water going down. I am not being worn away. That is not what happens here. I am being given back, grain for grain, and the only grace I have found in it is this: I get to choose what I hand over, and what I keep, and how slowly.
