I come in sideways, because that is how you come in, and the sideways-ness matters: you don't enter the Kettle head-on, you edge into it off the cold maintenance ledge like a person slipping into a bath, and by the time you've understood that you've entered, the warmth has already closed over you like a hand closing.

The warmth is first. Then the wet, which is not weather but a condition — the air is *made of* water in some way I don't have a word for, not mist, not steam, and my clothes go heavy within ten paces and my hair plasters flat to my skull though nothing has fallen on me. Nothing has fallen on me yet. My face is beaded. I wipe it and it beads again. The wet isn't coming from anywhere; it is simply present, the way a room is present, and it takes possession of me the way one hand takes possession of another.

Then the sound, and the sound is a wall, and I stand against it.

I can't render it. I want to say: drops fall on tuned surfaces, gutters laugh and gurgle, the grates throb. But that's a list, and the sound is not a list; the sound is a *single* thing, the way a choir is a single thing, and I stand in it and cannot hear my own footsteps and cannot hear my own breath going in and out of me. I laugh once, out of vertigo, and I cannot hear the laugh. My voice comes out and turns to nothing a handspan from my face. I put my palm against the wall and the wall is warm and weeping and I feel the hum of it in my wrist, and I understand that the sound is not being made *at* me. The sound is the place breathing. I have walked into the throat of something that is not asleep and not awake.

Then my eyes adjust, and the dimness resolves — and it is warm dimness, candle dimness, the light of a room where someone has just put out the lamp but the coals are still going. The floor is scalloped and ankle-deep and full of pale green light. The ceiling is strung with beads of amber, and glow-worms, and the drop-strings glisten. And there, above me, going about their rounds along the runnels, are the beetles.

I watch the beetles because watching the beetles is the only thing left to do while your ears fill up. They are small and dark and glossy as wet seeds, and they walk their circuits, and they nudge, and they stand back, and they go on. I watch them for a while. Then I notice that I am counting. Then I notice that the counting is the drops, and the drops are the counting, and that I have been in the Kettle a quarter of an hour at most and already I am keeping its time.

I find the dry shelf. I find it by the heat-lamp's warmth, which is a different warmth, a smallness. One engineer is asleep on the shelf with wax in their ears, and one ear has lost its wax — a pale coin of it visible in the ear-canal — and I don't wake them. On the shelf-wall is the chart: a month of drops drawn in careful ink, a calendar of strokes, a hand that has stopped trying to be beautiful and started trying to be true. In the margin, small pencil figures. Salamanders. Three of them named, in a script that means nothing to me and everything to whoever wrote it. A fourth drawn with a question mark beside it, and the question mark is not new; it has been traced over so many times it has bitten into the paper.

I wander, because there is nothing else to do and nowhere dry to do it. I find the moths in the deafening middle where nobody talks, hovering in the falling column with their wings going slow, drinking the condensation straight out of the air, and their dust is the glitter that makes the drop-strings bright. I find a salamander crossing a pool, slow and blind and deliberate, and I watch the green wake it leaves, and I watch the wake last a minute and fade, and I stand very still the way you stand still in a kitchen when something sacred is underfoot. I find the grates.

I crouch by a grate for a long time before I see one. Then: grey, sleek, mouth a little open, lying in the roar as though the roar were a warm bath. It does not move away from me. I put my hand toward the water — not in, toward — and it still does not move, and I understand, with a coldness that has nothing to do with the temperature, that it is not tame and not afraid and not interested, and that if I reached in, something would happen that the engineers would tell in the gaps for years afterward. I don't reach in. I tell myself it's because I'm sensible. Later I won't be sure it wasn't the water.

I have a bottle and a paper. The paper tells me what to write. I came up to taste the runoff, to certify it for the lowland orchards, to go back down and hand in my paper and be dry again. I do not open the bottle. I do not write anything. My mouth is too wet to taste anything; everything tastes of the Kettle, warm and mineral and faintly sweet, faintly like something asleep. The water is the landlord. It does not need a taster. It knows its own taste.

Days go by, or hours, and in a place that is all one warm hour I can't tell the difference. I learn the gaps. The engineers wake and we speak in the gaps and their voices are hoarse and mine is going hoarse. They give me wax for my ears. The wax does not help. I begin to know when the drops will fall. I begin to step around the salamanders without looking down.

And one time — no night, but a time when the heat-lamp dims and the engineers are asleep — I am standing under the ceiling with the beetles going around above me, and I understand that the sound has gone quiet. Not stopped. Nothing here stops. But I am hearing it as quiet, the way you hear your own heart as quiet, and I think: *this* is what the chart is really for. Not the beetles. The moment a visitor stops being a visitor.

In the morning the engineers go back to the margin, and there is a new figure there — small and stooped, holding a bottle, no name — and one of them says something I catch only half of in a gap, and the other shakes their head, and they argue about it in the gaps the way they argue about the fourth salamander, and they leave the question mark.

I don't correct them. I have stopped being sure myself. Nothing that lives in the Kettle gets caught — not the eel-things, not the salamanders, not the quiet. You just get a lease, and you keep the schedule, and the wet never dries, and when they sleep the Kettle goes on without you — dropping, pooling, draining, glowing, breathing out its warm wet breath through the wall-vents toward the dry world below that cannot live without it.

I am in the margin now. I have a question mark. It is a kind of belonging, and I keep the beetles' time, and I do not open the bottle.
