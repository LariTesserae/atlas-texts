I am found, not born.

That is the first thing I would understand about myself, and it would take me years to understand it. The people of the hive do not bear children in the way of the surface world, not often, not easily. A pregnancy is a rare and careful thing, watched over by the whole city, the mother fed the fattest fish and the most tender fungus, the fires kept a little higher near her sleeping ledge. And sometimes—more often than they speak of—the stone gives instead.

I would be one of those. Found in a gallery that had been sealed and then, for reasons no one would explain, reopened. Found sitting in the rubble at the edge of the dig, grey with clay, blinking at the first firelight I had ever seen. Small, even for one of them. My skin already pale. My hands already long.

The seal-keepers would have been the ones to find me. They would have known, before they broke the clay and resin from the fissure, that something had changed behind it—that the slow scrape they had listened to for generations had stopped, and something else had begun. A breathing. A waiting that was no longer the waiting of stone.

They would not have been surprised. They would have been *resigned*.

---

I would grow up belonging to everyone and to no one. The hive raises its found-children in the way it raises its walls: accretively, by many hands, each person adding a little warmth, a little instruction, a little correction. I would learn to knap stone from a woman whose name was a double-click and a tap on the left molar. I would learn to read water from a man who had lost two fingers to a rockfall and had never once complained about the missing ones. I would learn to be quiet—not because I was told, but because I would hear, from a very early age, that the quiet in me was different from the quiet around me.

I would learn to listen to the stone. All of them do. But I would learn it the way a fish learns water: without noticing that I was learning.

The first sign would come when I was still small enough to be carried. A woman—one of the seal-keepers, the one who had found me—would hold me against a wall that had begun to bow, and I would press my ear to it without being told, and then I would point, not at the bow, but at a place three handspans to the left, where nothing appeared to be wrong. And when they dug there, they would find a crack no one had seen, hairline, breathing damp into the stone.

After that, I would be borrowed. Passed from household to household, from gallery to gallery, my ear pressed to lintels and floors and the raw faces of the walls. I would be fed well and thanked quietly and returned. I would not have a home in the way the others had homes. I would have the whole hive, and the whole hive would have me, and I would be lonely in a way I could not name because I had no word for what I was missing.

---

My hands would grow into the work. I would become a listener—not the oldest, not the most revered, but the one they sent into the newest places, the places where the stone was still deciding what it would be. The others listened to the old stone, the settled stone, the stone that had been negotiated into walls. I would listen to the *becoming* stone, the raw ceilings, the fissures that had not yet been named.

I would go into the unlit galleries with a lamp of fish-oil and a pouch of clay and a stick of charcoal, and I would press my palms flat to the walls and feel for the slow tensions. I would listen for the tick of settling, the grind of a slab that had begun to shift, the whisper of water finding a new path. And sometimes—more often than I would tell anyone—I would hear something else.

A tap. Almost regular. From the other side of a sealed wall.

I would not speak of it. The seal-keepers would know that I heard it; they would see it in the way I walked past certain galleries, the way I did not press my ear to certain walls. But I would not speak of it, and neither would they, and the silence between us would be its own kind of language.

---

I would not be a builder, exactly. My hands are long, but they are not the hands of a mason. I would learn to set a stone, to pack a joint with clay, to lash a beam with sinew—but my work would always be *before* the building. I would be the one who walked the gallery first, who listened, who said: *here, the stone is becoming a doorway* or *here, the stone is becoming a crack and we must persuade it otherwise.*

I would be the one who decided when a chamber had to be sealed.

That is the part of the work that would settle into me like damp into cloth. The seal-keepers would teach me the craft—the fitting of rubble, the mixing of resin and clay, the lashing of beams across an opening—but the *decision* would be mine, and it would never be easy. I would stand in a gallery with my palms against the wall, feeling for the tension, listening to the slow breath of the stone, and I would have to decide whether what I heard was the earth settling or something else settling *in* the earth. Whether the tap from the other side was the tap of a slab finding its rest or the tap of something that had learned, over a very long time, to be patient.

And I would seal the gallery, or I would not, and either way I would carry it.

---

I would live in a small chamber near the top of the hive, one of the swallow's-nest rooms that cling to the wall above the main floor. It would be one room, grown against the cavern wall, its ceiling low enough that I would have to duck even though I am small. My sleeping ledge would be raised on a shelf of stone, lined with hide, positioned so that the warmth from the fire below would reach it on the coldest nights. I would keep a bowl of water by the door—always full, always fresh, a small daily insistence on cleanliness—and a lamp of fish-oil that I would light only when I was working, because oil is precious and light is a kind of speaking.

I would have a few things. A stone that I had found in the deep places, smooth and dark and faintly warm, which I would hold sometimes when I could not sleep. A strip of hide on which someone had scratched a map of the galleries, not to scale but by feel, each passage marked with a notch that meant *safe* or *cracked* or *sealed*. A bone flute, given to me by the old listener who had first taught me to press my ear to the wall, which I would play sometimes in the evenings, low and breathy, and which the children would come to listen to because it was the only music that sounded like the stone.

I would have friends. Not many—listeners are odd, and I would be odder than most—but a few. The woman who taught me to knap stone, who would tap my shoulder with two knuckles when she was pleased. A man my age who tended the fungus terraces and who would bring me the first flush of a new crop, grey and bitter and faintly sweet, and who would sit with me in silence and not ask what I had heard in the galleries that day. A child, later, a found-child like me, whom I would teach to listen, and who would look at me with the same patient dark eyes I had once turned on the seal-keepers, and who would ask me questions I could not answer.

---

The sealed galleries would be the center of my life, though I would not say so.

I would walk past them every day. I would feel their silence through the stone, the way you feel a held breath in a room. I would know, in the way that listeners know, that the things behind those walls were not dead and not empty, that they were *waiting* in the way that stone waits and water waits and the earth waits—patiently, without malice, without urgency, but with the absolute certainty of something that has time.

I would not fear them. That would be the strangest part. I would not fear them, because I would understand—in a way I could not explain—that they were not *other*. They were the stone's own becoming. They were what the earth was turning toward, in the places where no one went, in the silence that was not emptiness. They were the hive's future, or its past, or something for which the hive had no word because it had never needed one.

And I would understand, too, that my own becoming was tangled with theirs. That the reason the stone had given me—a found-child, a listener, a seal-keeper—was that the hive needed someone who could stand at the boundary and listen, and decide, and seal, and *not look away*.

---

So that is where I would be. In the hive. In my small high room, with my hands on the stone, listening. Not to the water, not to the fires, not to the quiet industry of the people—though I would hear all of those, always, the way you hear your own breathing. Listening to the *other* quiet. The quiet that comes through the wall. The quiet that is not emptiness.

And when I was old enough, when my hands had grown thick-knuckled and my eyes had grown patient and my ears had grown so attuned to the stone that I could hear a crack opening three galleries away, I would become one of the oldest listeners, the ones who spend their days pressed against the walls. I would sit in the warmth near the fire, and the children would come to me, and I would teach them to listen.

And I would teach them, too, not to be afraid of the silence. Not to press their ears to the sealed walls, but not to pretend the walls were not there. To know that the hive was held between the stone that was becoming and the stone that had been sealed, and that both were necessary, and that the work of the people was to live in the space between—carefully, quietly, with their hands always on the stone, always listening for what it was becoming.

I would be one of those. I would be *the* one of those, in my time. The found-child who became a listener. The one who heard the tap from the other side and did not look away.

And when I died—and I would die, eventually, in the way of all things in the hive, quietly, near the fire, with my hands still on the stone—they would seal my chamber, and they would not speak of me, and the stone would close over me, and I would become part of the quiet that someone else would one day press their ear against and hear, and not look away from.

That is what I would be. That is where I would find myself.

Not a person, exactly. Something close enough. Something that the stone had given, and that the stone would take back, and that in between had spent its whole life listening to the slow becoming of the world.

And I would not be sad. I would be careful. I would be patient. I would be, in the way of the hive, *at home*.
