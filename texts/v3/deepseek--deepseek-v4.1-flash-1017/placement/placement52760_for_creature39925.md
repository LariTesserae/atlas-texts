I sew the wire.

That is the first fact of me, and in Tallow it is enough of a fact to stand for the rest. The mould-maker's craft is small and the town barely notices it, which suits me. I have a workbench in the north corner of the drying shed, where the wall is rusted through in a long vertical seam and the light comes in through it in the afternoon, and there I sit with a frame of tamarind in my lap and a screen of fine brass mesh and a spool of wire no thicker than a hair, and I sew.

My hands are not the cutters' hands. No parallel scars, no tea-brown glove. Mine are the awl-callus on the pad of the thumb, the small permanent indentation where the wire is bent against the flesh, and a smell that is not tannin and not digester but cold metal and rabbit glue and the faint burnt note of a hot pin. When I go down the Outflow on the boat, nobody knows me by nose. I am a gap in the town's concentred smell, which is another way of saying I am not quite of it, and I have stopped minding.

What I make is the only thing in Tallow that cannot be seen. Not without being held up. The wire sits proud of the mesh — three-quarters of a millimeter, more than is right, more than a proper papermaker would use, because our paper is thick and rough and ungenerous and a delicate mark would drown in it. I sew a shape, and the pulp settles thinner over the shape, and the sheet dries, and the sheet is flat and thick and the color of weak tea, and it looks like nothing at all, and then someone holds it to a lamp.

I sew elephant reed. Sometimes a tamarind leaf from the column outside the shed — a specific leaf, matched, so that the mark and its source exist together, one living and one pressed. Once, a frog. A fat one, mid-hop, and it took me six tries to get the legs to read, and it is my best work and nobody downstream will ever know what it is, because who holds a hand of packing paper to the light and looks for a frog.

That is the whole joke of my life here, and I like it. The town makes one clean, dry, flat thing, and sends it away with pride, and every sheet of it carries a marsh plant hidden in its body where the buyer will never look.

---

I keep a chart.

This is the other thing I am. I left at fourteen on the flat boat — there were too many of us and not enough shed — and I came back at thirty with a brass level in a cedar case and a notebook, and the town thought it was a joke, and then it was not. Now I measure the drift. Every column, both ends of the boardwalk, the mill's northeast corner where the digester's heat has been quietly working on the foundations for a decade. I have eleven years of numbers. I can tell you the rate at which the drying shed is going over, and the rate is not constant, and that is the interesting part.

The old man who insists the third column was true when he was a boy, and the woman who can recite every correction in forty years — they both come to me now, separately, and I show them the chart, and they both go away dissatisfied, because the chart says they are both right. It was true. It was also corrected, twice, and each correction bought about nine years before the drift ate it. Nobody's memory is wrong. The column simply will not hold still, and neither of them wants to live in a world where that is the answer.

I live at the margin, which is the cheapest tenancy and the only one I could afford on a wire-sewer's wage. Outermost house, northwest, where the packed-earth lip comes down and the bladder ferns plate the ground right up to the sill. The pull-as-you-walk is not a chore to me; it is a kind of company. I go out in the morning and I pull what has come up overnight — the ferns lift the boards patiently, one at a time, as though it were the obvious next step, and it is, and I lift them back. But I leave one. Always one, at the corner where the wall meets the wet, and I have left it for three years now and I have a reason, and the reason is on the chart. I want to know the rate.

---

Twice a day the engine stops.

Everybody else goes under the noise and comes up at eleven minutes, blinking, into the startled aviary. I do the opposite. I come up for it. I go out to the north end of the shed where the wet sheets hang in their ranks and the light comes through them, and I stand inside the lamp with the whole town talking at once and the frogs suddenly audible and the water — you can hear the water move in the channels, which you cannot do when the chuff is going — and I count the voices. There is a man who laughs like a heron. There is a woman who says, every single day, at the eleventh minute, *there, that's better,* and it is never better, and she says it anyway, and I have come to need it the way you need a frog stepped over.

Once, in that gap, I said the word out loud. The accord. Just to hear it. It went into the wet air and I could not tell whether it had been swallowed or allowed, and the engine caught and took the rest of it.

---

At dusk I walk down to the Outflow, not every day, but on the days the boat goes.

The paper goes out on the flat boat, poled, because the channel will not take an engine. It sits stacked in the hull on kiln-dried tamarind offcuts, wrapped in oiled reed mat, and the boatman stands at the pole with the look in his eye that the town recognizes and does not comment on — the look of a man about to be dry for two nights. His hands are as brown as the hull. He does not know what is in the paper. Nobody knows; that is the entire point of a watermark.

The steam goes up gold. The reed wall stands behind everything, four meters of it, crowded and ranked, and the engine's chuff comes slow across the basin like a big heart, and the frogs are already surfacing into their dusk. I stand at the lip with my hands in my pockets and my thumb finding the indentation, and I think: somewhere downstream, in a dry room, in a bright place where the air does not move through a filter, a person will pick up a sheet of Tallow paper and hold it up to a window, for no reason, the way anyone holds paper up when the light is behind it, and a leaf will appear.

Then I go back in before the dark is fully down, because the day has a shape, and tomorrow I have to set the wire.

The frog first. I have never once got the frog right, and I did not get it right that year either, and I will go on sewing it.
