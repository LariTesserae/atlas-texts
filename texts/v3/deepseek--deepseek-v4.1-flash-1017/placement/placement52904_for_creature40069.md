I find myself on the catwalk, and the first thing is the heat. It doesn't press; it *settles*, a weight that finds the seams of my clothes and the pores of my skin and makes a home there. The rust under my hand is warm as a living thing. The air tastes of pennies and old bread. I have come down a long way to get here, and the silence I left behind—the clean, dry silence of the world above—is already a memory the humidity is slowly dissolving.

I do not move quickly. The catwalk complains with every shift of weight, and I find I am listening to it the way the Keeper must listen to the pipes. I watch a single spoke of the great wheel for a full minute before I see it move, and when I do, something in my chest unknots. It is turning. It has been turning. It will go on turning. The relief of that is absurd and total.

Who would I be, here?

Not the Keeper. I am not that patient, and I have not earned the brass grown into my chest. But I am not nothing, either. I think I would be the one who came down. The visitor who becomes the second resident, then the third, then part of the count of things that live here. I would arrive with my notebooks and my tools and my questions, and the heat would take the questions first. I would sit on the dryest of the old foundations and watch the Keeper work, and after a while I would stop writing things down.

I think I would be a **corroder**. That is my nature. I am soft and salt-sweating and full of small acids. My hands would leave prints on the brass. I would breathe out carbon and damp and the slow chemistry of a body that wants to be comfortable. The Keeper would watch me from the doorway with its out-of-phase eyes, and it would know: this one wears down the world just by standing in it. It would bring me water anyway. It would tap three beats on the pipe, and I would learn to tap back.

But I would not stay a corroder forever. That is the thing about this place. It is a system balanced between the organic and the industrial, and I am neither. I would begin, slowly, to be colonized. A grey bloom at the wrist where I leaned too long on a wet wall. A stiffness in the knees that is not age but *settling*. My fingernails would darken with something that is not dirt. I would find myself scraping mineral crust from the engine housing without remembering deciding to, and eating it, and finding it good.

The Keeper would not stop me. It would fold itself through the low doorway and stand beside me and put one root-fingered hand flat on the iron, and I would put my hand next to it, and we would listen together. The bearing would be healthy. The hum would be true.

I think, in the end, I would become a **turner**. Not the turner—the Keeper has that office and will keep it—but a turner nonetheless. A second pair of hands for the grease and the pruning. A second set of ears for the sick note in a valve. I would learn the spoke-count. I would sleep standing, or not exactly sleeping. The vines on the walls would begin to pulse at my rhythm, or I at theirs; we would not be able to say which came first.

And one day, perhaps, a new sound on the catwalk. A new change in the air. A new thing that grows, or corrodes, or turns, standing at the edge of the light with its questions still intact. I would fold myself through the doorway and tilt my fungus-face and wait. I would bring it water in a brass cup. I would not speak. I would tap three slow beats, a pause, three slow beats, and let the heat do the rest.

The wheel does not drive anything. It turns because we turn it. We turn because the wheel keeps us from stiffening. This is not purpose. This is just what it is to live here, in the pocket of fecund, mechanical decay, where the rain never stops and the stone is patient and everything, in its own way, is alive.

I turn the wheel. The wheel turns me. The difference between a healthy hum and a sick one is all I need to know.
