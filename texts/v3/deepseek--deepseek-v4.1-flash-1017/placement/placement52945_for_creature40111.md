I would find myself halfway up, not at the rim and not in the town’s center, but at the seam where the braided arches give way to the unshaped ceiling vines. That is where I would belong: one of the Kesh, and not a remarkable one. Not the eldest, not a child, not the one who speaks first when the chorus turns. A pruner in the middle years, with a bone blade worn down to the length of a finger and a hip vessel that smells faintly of the pale fruit I carry in it.

I would be up there when the air draws in. That is the hour I would love best — not because it is beautiful, though it is, but because it is legible. The hollow stems would be droning low, the silver leaves beaded thick, and I would be holding a shallow bowl under a leaf, touching it once, watching the water run down. Not rain. Never rain. Just the air giving up what it has been carrying. I would drink, and then I would fill the smaller vessels, and then I would climb higher to the platform that always sags after the wet season, and I would begin to braid the vine back into itself.

My work would be seams. The eastern arch, the platform above the grub mat, the place where two columns have begun to lean toward each other and will either braid or fail. I would cut in spirals, never straight, because a straight cut heals wrong and the plant thickens around it and the arch remembers the mistake for years. I would know the growth rings of the town the way I know the calls of the chorus — by being in them, by hearing when a note goes wrong, by noticing that the drone under the third platform has gone a little hollow.

I would wear silver panels, layered, the outermost new and the innermost soft as a second skin. If you asked me which year each layer was made in, I could tell you. I could tell you who made it and what the air was doing that season and whose death had made the creepers under the eastern mat grow a little richer. I would not write it down. The basin keeps the record. I just know how to read it.

I would have a reed at my belt, and when the ceiling chorus began its rounds I would answer it — not to imitate it, not to control it, but because the Kesh are one of the voices and silence is a kind of cut that does not heal. I would blow the call back into the wet air, and something in the upper vines would turn very slightly toward the sound, and I would feel, as I always felt, that I had been answered by something that had been answering for a very long time.

And when I died — of age, of a fall, of the fever that comes when the air turns wrong — they would carry me down to the lowest layer and lay me on the mat, and the town would gather, and the chorus would gather, and the drone would be very loud. Then the creepers would grow over me and the mat would thicken, and in a season there would be no sign except that the growth there was a little richer and the leaves a little larger. I would not be buried. I would be promoted. I would become the floor that everything else is rooted in.

So: on the eastern platform, at the hour the air draws in, with a bowl in one hand and a sliver of bone in the other. One of nineteen. A small, damp, patient motion in a warm wet lung of a basin. Not separate from it. A cut that grows back.
