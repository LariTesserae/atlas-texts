I would come in on the east road, on foot, behind the mules, and I would not go out again.

Not because anything kept me. The road is open and the mule train goes back on the fourth day. But somewhere in the second morning — the hour before the light goes flat, when the drying sheds are thrown open and the whole basin smells of hot wet fiber, and the nap is steaming off its one hour of sun — I would find that I had begun to walk slowly on purpose, and that is the whole of it. Nobody decides to stay here. They just stop being in a hurry, and the town closes over them, politely, the way it closes over a cart left out.

So you would find me on the deck. I think I would be the one who reads the board.

There is a board in the guild hall, in a hand nobody now can read fluently, and paper in this basin is a terrible thing to own: it does not dry, it softens, it grows a fine grey fur at the corners if you leave it. The only way to keep an old page here is to handle it. So there is a job, though it is not called a job on any list, of sitting with the rotation and turning the leaves and reading them aloud to yourself in a low voice until the old shapes come apart into words. I would do that in the wet months. I would learn the hand by tracing it in condensation on the table first, then finding it again on the page, and after a few years I would be the only one who could say with confidence which lane is due and which must be left, and the crews would come and ask me, and I would tell them, and I would be wrong sometimes and the seep would run a little low and everyone would be polite about it.

Knowing the rotation means walking it. And that is the other half of the work, the half I would come to want: going out ahead of the crews at dawn, before the shears, along the cut lanes and the older lanes and the trails, with a staff, and sounding the mat.

You can sound it. You tap and you listen and you feel the answer come up through the staff into your wrist — the green is springy and gives back a thin note, the rust line is a duller thing and warmer, and where a body is lying under the mat the answer is not a note at all. It is a softness with weight behind it. It is like tapping a table and finding a hand on the other side.

So I would go out at first light, when the trails are fresh, and I would tap and listen, and I would mark the wide places with a rag on a stake so the crews would know to work around, and I would step over the small lanes without comment, the way everyone does, the way you step over a sleeper's arm. The mat would take my foot and stiffen under it and let me onward. I would go steadily. I would never run. I have seen what happens to people who run.

And this is the thing I would not say out loud, because it is not on the board and nobody in the crews would thank me for it: I would come to know the tenant by its weight. Not its shape — you do not see it whole — but the heaviness of it under the staff, the way the answer differs when it is the small one under your own lane and when it is the big one out along the Long Lane at dusk, lifting the green tips into a shed line of water. I would learn to tell the difference between a fresh trail and a healing one by the give of the fiber, and I would learn that the mat has no memory, and that I do, and that this is the whole difference between us and also the whole arrangement.

Nobody digs. I would not dig. But I would stand at the edge of a wide smooth lane in the evening, when the light is flat and the trickle of the stilling is the loudest thing there is, and I would put my palm down on the pressed fiber, and it would be warm, and I would feel the very slow passing of something on the other side of the rust line, going where it goes, and I would take my hand back and go in to my supper.

And in twenty years, if you came looking for me, you would find an old person on the deck at dawn with a staff, tapping, marking, stepping over, walking steadily and never running, and the crews would step over my own trail on the planks without noticing they did it. And you would not be able to tell me apart from the basin, except that I leave marks and do not keep them, and the mat keeps everything by forgetting, and between those two facts the town has held for a long time and shows no sign of stopping.
