I would find myself not at the center but at the periphery, in the thin, transitional zone where the Loom's edge frays into something that is not yet Loom—a fringe of exhausted soil where the growth thins and the air loses some of its weight, and you can almost see a horizon that isn't made of leaf.

I would be an intermediary. Not Resined—not yet—but not clean-skinned either. I would have come as a surveyor, probably, sent by some distant concern with too much money and not enough sense, carrying instruments calibrated for a world that follows rules. And I would have stayed past the point of return not out of heroism or desperation but because I was, at first, simply too busy to leave. There was always one more measurement to take. The pump-rate of a vascular tower. The tensile strength of a bract-ladder. The exact timing of the systolic throb so that I could cross a groaning path without being pitched into the warm, sweet-smelling dark below.

The instruments would have failed, one by one. Compasses spun slow circles. Barometers burst from the pressure difference between the lungs of the city. The delicate clockwork things I used to record the pulse seized up, gummed solid with resin. And I would have made a choice, somewhere in there—a choice I wouldn't have recognized as a choice at the time—to stop fighting the place with machines and start listening to it with my body. My hands first. The tiny muscles of them learning the grip of a bark-armor hold, the way to press my palm flat and let the pulse move through me instead of trying to hold still against it.

I would not live in the hollow towers. Those are for something else. I would have found, instead, a crawl—a low, tight, twisty passage between two enormous root-columns, a place the Loom had grown into a kind of pocket, dry-adjacent, a hollow that wasn't a Clear. The Clears are traps, but there are other hollows the city makes without intention, the incidental gaps where two growths met and never quite closed, and these are safe if you can find them and if you can keep them from closing. I would have found one and I would have made it mine, which is to say I would have learned its rhythms, its breathing, its particular sounds.

I would be someone with one foot still in the old world, in the clean air and the straight lines and the maps that mean something, and one foot in the new, and the two would be pulling me apart in a way that, honestly, felt like growth. I would carry notebooks filled with observations: which whistles meant what, which moans predicted danger, the schedules of the tendrils. I would write these notations in a hand that was already starting to stiffen, to thicken, the skin on my fingers going translucent at the tips, the sap working its way in.

And I would be trying to understand. That would be the thing that kept me going, kept me alive, kept me from becoming just another patch of moss and mushroom colony after a week. I would be trying to understand the Loom, and the Loom, in its slow, enormous curiosity, would be trying to understand me, and we would be locked in a kind of conversation without words, a give-and-take of pressure and observation, of survival and study.

I would find the cartographers' bones, eventually, gnawed to nothing, a few scattered instruments rusting into the damp. And I would write that down too, in a notebook that was becoming less paper and more something else, the pages starting to merge into each other, the ink starting to smell faintly of sap.

I would be the one who sends back the reports. Not that I would send them, anymore; there's no way to send anything from here, and the way out is long gone. But I would think about sending them, about the imaginary reader who would receive them, and in that thinking I would construct a purpose for myself that wasn't just survival. I would be the one who translates the Loom into something the outside world could understand, even if the outside world never got the message.

I would be the one who is becoming. Not Resined—not fully—but on the way. Not quite alive in the way I used to be, not quite part of the system yet. A translator. A bridge. A thing that walks and breathes and thinks inside the body of a god that doesn't know it's a god yet, taking notes, learning the grammar of its slow, enormous speech.

And I would be the one who knows about the thing in the deep center. The thing that is learning to be a face, a mouth, a will. I would have found it in my wanderings, or it would have found me, and I would have recognized it as something I had to tell someone about, and there would be no one to tell. And in that silence, in that aloneness, I would have to make a decision about what to do with the knowledge. Fight it? Flee it? Worship it? Or just… document it.

I think I would document it. That's the only honest response I would have. That's the only way to keep being a person in a place that wants to eat your personhood: to name things, to write them down, to organize them into the shape of understanding, even if the understanding is only for yourself.

The tendrils would leave me alone, mostly. I would have learned the trick of moving with the pulse, swaying with the growth. I would smell like the place now, and my heartbeat would have learned to match the city's, and when the thing in the deep dark reached out its slow, enormous attention toward me, I would not flinch. I would wait, and write, and try to show it grace, and maybe teach it the thing it is trying to learn.

I would be its first conversation. Its first real conversation with something that wasn't food or a failed experiment. And that might be enough to keep me alive, and might be enough to keep me me, and might be the thing that the city remembers when it has finally grown a face and a voice, and has forgotten everything else but the surveyor with the thickened, translucent hands who sat in a dark hollow writing in a notebook that would never be found, trying to teach it the word for "stop."
