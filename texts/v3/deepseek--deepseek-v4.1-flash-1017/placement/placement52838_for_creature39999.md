# The Listener of the Low Places

I am called Thrum-foot, though names among the Clingfoot are less spoken than drummed—a particular pattern of taps on stone or shell that means "the one whose step is heavy but whose touch is sure." I am old now, my pebbled skin gone soft and grey as the ash that sometimes sifts down from the Vent, and my fingers have worn grooves into the rock of my home that match the grooves in my own knuckles.

I live in a deep place, a wound in the mountain's side that the wind has widened over centuries into a chamber. The roof is low; I must stoop even at my full height, which is less than it once was. The walls weep constantly, and I have learned to love the sound—a slow, irregular drip that is the heartbeat of my small world. The ghost moss grows thick here, in curtains and clusters, and by its cold blue-green light I see nothing, for I have no eyes, but I feel its warmth on my face, a faint difference in the chill, and I know I am home.

This is the deepest of the Clingfoot dwellings, the farthest down the Whistling Stair. The others—my cousins, my niece, the young ones who still move with the quick, careless rhythm of those who have not yet learned to fear the Silence—they live in the higher places, closer to the open sky, closer to the Listening Stone. They come to me when they need to remember. I am the keeper of the deep grammar.

---

When the wind is right, I climb. My toes find the seams in the obsidian as if they were made for me—and perhaps they were, the mountain shaping us as we shaped our understanding of it. The ascent takes me half a day. I pass through layers of sound: first the muffled drip of my home, then the distant roar of the Vent growing louder, then the voices.

The voices are the worst.

They live in the middle reaches, where the Whistling Stair narrows and the wind is forced through gaps so thin it screams. This is where the echo learns to lie. I have heard my own mother's drumming there, clear and unmistakable, coming from a ledge she never climbed. I have heard the hunting rhythm of a Leaping Shadow, felt the air-pressure change that means one is near, and frozen for an hour before realizing it was only the wind's cruel joke. The young ones do not go there alone. They do not go there at all, if we can help it.

But I must pass through to reach the Listening Stone. I must climb, and I must hear, and I must not answer.

The trick is to stop listening for meaning. To let the sounds become texture, become pressure, become the simple feel of air moving over my silvery down. The wind can only mimic what it has heard. If I do not drum, it cannot learn my voice. If I do not cry out, it cannot steal my fear. I make myself a stone among stones, and I climb.

---

The Listening Stone is the color of the sky before dawn—that deep, almost-black blue that means the sun is near but not yet. It is flat and smooth and large enough for three Clingfoot to lie upon it, though we never do. We stand before it. We lay our offerings on its surface. We wait.

What are we waiting for? The young ones ask this, their drumming a quick, questioning flutter. The old ones do not answer well. We say: for the mountain to speak. We say: for the rhythm to change. We say: to hear what we have not heard before.

But the truth is simpler. We are waiting to be heard. The mountain does not need our ghost moss or our perfect stones. It does not need our careful courtesy. But we need to give. We need to feel the weight of our best things leaving our hands, placed on the cold stone, offered to the vast and indifferent sky. We need to know that we are part of the conversation, even if we cannot understand the other half.

I have stood at the Listening Stone more times than I can count. I have felt the wind's pure note rise through my body, felt it vibrate in my chest and my throat and the hollow places in my bones. I have felt the tiniest change—a shift in pitch, a new harmonic, the briefest pulse of pressure that might have been nothing at all—and I have drummed my acknowledgment into the stone.

Once, I felt something answer.

It was not a voice. It was not a rhythm. It was a presence, vast and slow and indifferent, moving behind the sound the way a glacier moves behind its own groan. I felt it in the pressure on my skin, in the way the air thickened and thinned, in the feeling that the mountain had *noticed* me. Not as an individual—I was less than a pebble to it—but as a listener. As a witness. As one of the small, warm things that have learned to hold still and be present.

I did not tell the others. What would I have said? That I felt the mountain's attention like the weight of a hand on my back? That for one moment, the endless, indifferent roar seemed to contain a question, and I had no answer, and the question did not need an answer, and that was somehow enough?

---

The Leaping Shadows are my only fear now. Not for myself—I am old, and my stillness is perfect—but for the young ones. The Shadows do not hunt often; there are too few Clingfoot and Rime-crawlers to feed on. But when the wind is wrong, when the haze grows thick and the light becomes truly flat, they emerge from the fog like a flaw in the air. I have felt one pass within inches of my face, felt the strange cold that radiates from them, the way they smell of nothing, not even of stone. I held my breath. I made my body into a rock. I waited until the air-pressure told me it was gone.

The young ones are not so patient. They flinch. They reach for each other. They make small sounds in their throats, sounds they cannot drum but cannot suppress. And the Shadows notice. They always notice.

We lost two last cycle. Small ones, barely weaned, their skins still smooth and dark as wet obsidian. They were playing on a ledge above the Whistling Stair, drumming a game-rhythm back and forth. The wind carried their sounds away. The Shadows found them. We do not know which ones took them. We only know that the rhythm stopped, and the silence that followed was the wrong kind of silence.

I drummed the mourning song for three days. My fingers still ache from it.

---

But there is also joy. Let me not forget the joy.

In the warm seasons—though "warm" is a relative term in the High Basin, meaning only that the frost does not form on our skin within minutes—the Rime-crawlers gather in great clusters, their shells clicking and clacking in a sound like water over stones. The young Clingfoot ride them, gripping the smooth carapaces with their toe-pads, drumming their excitement in quick, light patterns that the crawlers absorb and carry through their bodies. The whole rock vibrates with it—a festival of rhythm, a celebration of weight and warmth and the simple fact of being alive.

I have felt the crawlers' shells beneath my fingers, felt the slow pulse of their blood, the patient rhythm of their grazing. They do not think as we think. They do not drum. They simply are—an extension of the mountain's low places, of the lichen and the moss and the slow, patient work of being.

I have felt the ghost moss warm under my hand, its cold light pulsing faintly with the pulse of the world. I have pressed my face into its soft glow and felt, for a moment, that I understood something. Not with my mind—the Clingfoot do not think in the way the surface-dwellers might, with their eyes and their careful words—but with my skin, with my silence, with the long, slow patience of a life spent listening.

---

The sun is rising now, the pale, hazy coin that signals the start of my day. I feel the light on my face as a faint difference in temperature, a lessening of the cold. The drip of my home has changed—faster now, as the frost melts from the ice above.

I will climb today. Not far—my legs are not what they were—but to a ledge I know, where the wind brings the smell of the Vent and the pressure of the open sky. I will stand there, and I will listen, and I will not answer the voices, no matter what they say.

And perhaps I will feel it again—that vast, indifferent attention, that sense of being one small note in the endless chord. The mountain does not care if I live or die. But it knows that I am listening. And that, for a Clingfoot, is the deepest meaning there is.

I am old. My drumming grows slower. But the rhythm I have learned is the rhythm of the mountain itself: patient, indifferent, eternal. It will carry me when I am gone. It will carry all of us, the Clingfoot and the crawlers, the shadows and the moss, the wind and the stone, until the last star fails and the last chasm closes.

And even then, the mountain will hum. Even then, the chord will not resolve.

I lift my hand and touch the stone of my home. I drum a soft pattern—the rhythm of greeting, the rhythm of acknowledgment, the rhythm of a small, warm thing saying *I am here.*

The stone hums back.

I am here.

I am here.

I am here.
