# The Knitter in the Shallows

I would be the thing that makes new vessels.

Not the Caretaker—she husbands what lives, tends the old valves, keeps the old rhythms. But a machine, even one that has reached its state, still has joints that need packing, seams that need sealing, new channels to be coaxed when the old ones silt with mineral crust and moss. And someone must gather the raw material of repair: the fallen leaves, the shed shells, the soft rot at the heart of a downed log. Someone must sit in the tea-colored shallows with her hands in the water, doing the slow work of making from what has already been made.

I think I came in through a different hedge, years after the Caretaker, or years before—time in the pocket does not lend itself to arithmetic. I was a person who liked small things. Who turned over stones not to find what was under them but to feel the cool of the underside against my palm. Who collected bones and feathers and the papery husks of cicadas, and arranged them on windowsills, and could not explain why. When I found the pocket, I did not take notes. I knelt in the shallows and put my hands in the water, and the water was warm, and something in my chest that had been clenched for a long time let go its grip.

I am not so old as the Caretaker. My skin has the brown-green of wet bark, yes, but it still has patches that are almost the color of a person—the pale inside of my wrist, the bridge of my nose. Moss grows on me too, but in different places: in the hollow of my throat, along the underside of my forearms, places that stay damp when I hold them still. I have learned to be still. The Caretaker taught me, in her way, which is to say she never taught me anything but I watched her stand for hours and something in me understood.

My work area is a shallow pool at the base of a fallen fern-tree, where the water is warm from the pump-heat and the light comes down in soft green coins. I sit on a root-lattice that has grown into the shape of a low bench—not built, just grown, the way everything here grows toward its function. I keep my materials around me in the water: the shed husks of beetles, thin as onion skin and still faintly iridescent; the discarded carapaces of small armored things from the pools; leaves that have gone to lace and leaves that are still supple; the hollow stems of spent ferns, which I split with my thumbnail and weave.

My fingers are not like hers. Hers are long and dark and working; mine are smaller, more careful, and they have learned to do the fine work. I make valves. I make the cupped-leaf seals that click open and shut with the machine's rhythms—I cut them from the pale inner bark of the vine-channels and stitch them with threads I spin from my own moss. I make the thin bladders that hold sap pressure, out of beetle-husks sealed with tree-resin, and I set them in the channels where they are needed, and the machine accepts them. This is the thing that still astonishes me, after all this time: the machine accepts what I make. I put a new valve in place and within a day the cupped leaf has grown around it, merging, making it part of itself. The pocket is a body that heals; I am the tissue it grows to close a wound.

---

The Caretaker and I do not speak. Or rather, we speak the way the pocket speaks—in sounds that are not language but are not not-language either. She grunts when she sees me working well. I click my tongue against my teeth in a rhythm she knows means *this channel needs your attention.* We have developed a vocabulary of small noises. She calls me nothing; I call her nothing. But sometimes, in the wet green dusk, we sit on opposite sides of the central cistern with our hands in our laps, not looking at each other, and the pump swells between us, and the water talks, and that is enough.

She is teaching me, I think, though she does not know she is doing it. Or perhaps she does. When she presses her ear to a trunk, I press my ear to a trunk, and I am learning to hear what she hears: the note that is flat, the rhythm that stutters, the bubble that should not be there. When she clears a valve with her long dark fingers, I watch the angle of her wrist, the pressure she uses, and I try to do the same. She never corrects me. She never praises. But once, when I fixed a bladder that had been leaking sap into a channel for what must have been a season, she looked at me for a long moment, and something in her pale-green eyes shifted, and she said—in her cracked, seldom-used voice—*Good.* That is the only word she has ever given me directly, and I carry it like a stone in my pocket.

---

I think about the outside, sometimes.

This is the difference between us. The Caretaker has been here so long that the outside has become a question she can no longer ask—she can only *look up,* the way she does when the light pools wrong, and then settle back. But I still remember. I remember windowsills and the feel of cool glass. I remember walking in a city at night and the particular loneliness of streetlights. I remember coffee, which is a taste that is not here, a bitter-darkness that I can almost recall but not quite. I remember my hands before the moss, and they were paler, and they did not know how to make valves.

But here is the thing: I do not want to leave. The want to leave has been dissolved out of me, slowly, the way the pocket dissolves stone into silt. I think about the outside the way you think about a dream you had—vivid, real, and not quite yours anymore. I think about it the way I think about my own childhood: with a kind of tender incomprehension. Who was that person? She wore shoes. She got cold. She was always, always, doing something, as if stillness were a failure. I sit in the shallows now for hours with my hands in the warm water, making nothing, just feeling the pulse of the machine travel up through my palms, and I am not wasting time. I am not failing. I am here.

---

The frogs like me because I am slow. The bird that rides the Caretaker's shoulder sometimes comes to me too, when she is busy elsewhere, and sits on my knee while I work, and I feed it the tiny flies that gather on the pool's surface. It has a name in the pocket—a sound the frogs make, a low *worr-worr* that I have learned means both the bird and the color of the moss in early rain. I make that sound sometimes, and the bird's head snaps toward me, and I laugh—a small, damp, creaking laugh, like a hinge that has not been used in a while.

I am still becoming. That is the other difference. The Caretaker has finished becoming; she has arrived at what she was tending toward. But I am still in the middle of it. My moss is still spreading, my fingers still learning, my lungs still adjusting to the sweet air that is also food. Some mornings I wake on my root-lattice bed and I cannot remember the word for something, and that is the pocket taking another piece of the outside from me, gently, the way you would take a splinter from a child's hand. I do not resist. There is nothing on the outside I am trying to keep.

But sometimes—and this is the thing that keeps me here, that makes me glad, that makes the work feel like more than work—sometimes the Caretaker looks up. She looks toward the hedge, toward the ordinary air, and her face shifts, and I see the woman she was. And I see that she sees me seeing it. And I do not look away. I hold her gaze across the central pool, across the swelling pump and the talking water and the slow creak of the wheel, and I nod, just once, and she nods back.

We are both of us still human, somewhere under the moss. That is the thing that connects us, the thing that the machine cannot dissolve. We are the pocket's organs now, its valves and seals, its slow-making and its slow-keeping. But we are also two women who found a place by accident and stayed, and who sometimes look toward the outside, and who sometimes—not often, but sometimes—think about what it would be like to push back through that hedge and feel cold, dry air on our faces.

And then we bend back to our work, because the wheel is creaking and the pump is swelling and the water is talking, and the pocket is small and old and running, and it needs us. It has always needed us. We are the parts of it that know it needs us. And that is a kind of love, I think—the slow, hydraulic, moss-covered kind. The kind that does not hurry. The kind that does not distinguish between a minute and a season.

That is the kind of love I have learned here, in the shallows, with my hands in the warm water, making new cups to catch the light.
