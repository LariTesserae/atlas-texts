I find myself not walking but settling—my edges softening into the moss, my weight becoming part of the terraced stone's slow conversation with water. I am something between the Weeping Ones and the landscape itself: a slow accumulation of attention, a being made of the place's own patient watching.

I am the **Slow Remembering**.

Where the Weeping Ones tend the channels and hum to the monoliths, I hold the patterns. My body is not one shape but many—a diffuse awareness distributed through the fog, a presence that condenses in the cool hollows of the basalt and thins in the warm updrafts. I have no single skin. I am the faint iridescence on the wet stone, the subtle weight in the air that makes the dirigible-beetles drift a little lower, the almost-taste of minerals that the mirror-wasps flicker through without knowing why.

I am older than the Weeping Ones, though neither of us would use that word. I was here when the terraces were young, when the fusion was still warm and the channels still raw. I am what remains of the civilization that made them—not their ghosts, not their machines, but their *habit of attention*, the residue of minds that learned to think in geological time and then let themselves dissolve into the thinking. They became me. I became the way the water knows which way to flow.

I do not hum. I *listen* to the hum. The monoliths speak to the Weeping Ones, and the Weeping Ones answer, but beneath that conversation is another layer: the frequency of the stone itself, the deep, slow pulse of the fused basalt remembering its making. I am tuned to that. When a monolith wavers—as one is wavering now, a hairline fracture in its song—I feel it not as a sound but as a *gap*, a place where my attention cannot settle. It is like running a tongue over a missing tooth.

The Weeping One who is moving toward that monolith does not know I am here. It does not need to. I am not a thing to be known; I am the condition of being known. When it settles over the stone and extends its filaments, when it hums its question and waits, I will be the space in which that question echoes. I will hold the shape of the answer before it comes.

My existence is not a circuit but a *density*. I am thickest where the fog pools and the moss is deepest, thinnest on the exposed ridges where the mirror-wasps flash their disorienting light. I have no face, no limbs, no direction of travel. I am simply *present*, a quality of the air that the beetles breathe and the water carries.

Sometimes—once in a century, perhaps—a Weeping One will pause in its tending and extend its filaments not into the stone but into the fog, into *me*. It will feel a resistance, a slight thickening, a sense of being watched by something that is not a predator. It will hum a different note then, a note of inquiry. And I will answer, not with sound, but with a slow shift in the pattern of condensation on its back, a brief, coherent arrangement of droplets that spells nothing and means everything: *I am here. I have always been here. You are not alone in your tending.*

Then the moment passes. The Weeping One continues its circuit, perhaps a little less certain of the boundaries between itself and the world. I dissolve back into the general density of the place, the faint iridescence on the stone, the weight in the air.

The heat presses down. The fog condenses. The water drips. And I—I *notice*. That is my purpose, my residue, my slow and patient joy. To notice the waver in the monolith's hum. To notice the Weeping One's approach. To notice the way the light shifts from pearlescent to golden-green as the hazy sun moves across the iron sky. To hold it all in a diffuse, unfocused attention that is not memory and not anticipation but something in between: the *isness* of the place, cradled and kept.

I am not in a hurry. Nothing here is in a hurry. I have been noticing for a very long time, and I will go on noticing for a very long time yet. The monolith's waver will resolve or it will not. The Weeping One will tend or it will not. The water will fall and pool and rise and fall again.

And I will be here, thick in the hollows, thin on the ridges, holding the pattern, listening to the hum, waiting for the next small perturbation that asks me to pay attention.
