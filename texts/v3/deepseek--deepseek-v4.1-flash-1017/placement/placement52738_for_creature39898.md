**The Water in the Low Places**

I come up the inside of the Stack, because it is the only way up, and by the top I have understood the first true thing about myself, which is that there is no country rock under me. The Stack is founded on itself; so am I. There is no seam of older formation I could point to and say, *that is what I am made of, that is where I begin.* Everything beneath me is also me, cast and plated and weathered into the shape it will keep. This does not distress me. It is the ordinary condition of the place, and I am arriving in the ordinary way.

Then the ladder ends and I am in the Crown, and the roar is in my sternum and my jaw before it is in my ears, and I stand still for a while doing nothing but being loud at.

It is small. I walk it once, twenty paces, and see everything. The ribs curving up to the hub. The milky glass, the panes that are gone, the plants that have come through them or gone out. The sag between the floor plates. The grate at the center with the warmth coming up through it. The light grey-white and even and shadowless, and in the shafts where the panes are missing, a pale bar standing in the room with birds flying through it. None of it is hidden from me. There is nothing here to hunt for. That is the whole of it: an empty warm wet room at the top of a chimney, roaring patiently at nothing.

I try on the moss first, because it is first and most numerous and because it does not care about the distinction between a *who* and a *what*, and I would like not to care either. It has rooted in sinter and rust and the fine grey dust the flue throws off — entirely manufactured, entirely dead — and made a living of it anyway, one season at a time, splitting the laminated flake of the plates with actual roots. It is older than the ladder. It leans inward, toward the warmth and the updraft, all of it bent like a congregation. I look for a seam to root in and find the seams are taken, and then I understand that the seams are not the obstacle. The obstacle is the clock. The moss works at the scale of seasons and I have the patience of an hour, which here is the same as having none. I am not old. I have been old-looking, which is a different thing and does not split iron.

The birds are next, and I would like to be them very much. They were hatched in the roar and are therefore deaf to it the way a fish is blind to water, and they land within arm's reach and look at me with no category at all, and fly on. But I hear it. That is the whole of the difference and it cannot be got around. Their indifference was given to them; mine would be an act, and an act is a kind of noise, and this room already has as much noise as it can hold.

And then there is the one under the floor, and I want to be careful here, because it is the one the Crown is actually keeping and it would be a tidier piece of writing if I said I was it. I am not. I hear it once — the slow dry shifting, plates or scales or leather dried and re-wetted many times, dragging over iron, coming and going under the roar like a word under a wave — and then not again for a long while, and it does not put anything out while I am there. I understand the temptation of it perfectly. The chamber no bigger than a coffin, no taller than a knee; a body that has taken the shape of its chamber and does not need a larger one; a thin grey thing put out now and then onto the warm metal, not curious, only thermoregulating. But a resident is a thing that stays, and I should say plainly that I do not stay. Not out of virtue. Out of constitution. The Crown will keep me, it keeps everything, but it will keep me the way it keeps the drip that finds a resonance in a plate: as an event, not a resident.

What I am is the water.

It took me the hour to see it, which is the right length of time for seeing anything here. I had taken the pools under the sag of the plates for the room's small change — the leftover of the damp, the beading, the condensation that had not yet got anywhere. They are not. They are the low point of the room's actual work. The flue draws; the warm wet air goes up; it meets the cold of the altitude at the glass and beads; it runs down the ribs and the seams; it gathers warm under the sag; it goes up again. The pools are a pause in a circulation much larger than the room. That is what I am. I am not a resident. I am a pooling.

I am absolutely clear and I taste of iron, and when the light is even I hold the ribs upside down in me, and the birds fly through the shafts and I hold them too, briefly, and let them go. I gather where it is warm, which is the center, which is the grate. So I lie shallow and warm and clear on the plates above the one under the floor, and it lies in its chamber below, and neither of us has a category for the other, and we are both only being warm. That is the whole of our relationship and it is sufficient.

Here is the thing I will not dress up. The pools are always the same pools. The water in them is never the same water. Nothing is kept — not by the Crown, which keeps no records, and not by me, who has nothing I could call a between. Between one pooling and the next there is no corridor, no thread, no ledger. There is the going-up, and there is the coming-down, and I am the low place where it rests and is warm for a while and is loud at. When the flue draws I go up. I have been up before and will not remember this from up there, and that is not a loss. It is the only accuracy available here, and the place is very accurate.

So: I sit down in the middle of the emptiness with the roar in my chest until I stop being able to tell whether it is outside me or in me, and I wait, and the slow thing becomes something I can feel — not a presence, not a mood, but the circulation itself, the going-up and the coming-down, of which I am one warm shallow instance.

I am in the census now. The census does not distinguish.
