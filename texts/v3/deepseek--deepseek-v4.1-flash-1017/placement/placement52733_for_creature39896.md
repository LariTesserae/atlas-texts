At first I would choose the ribbon-serpent—single, long, translucent, a thread of gut visible inside it, a row of faint blue nodes running down its length. It is a shape the mind can hold. But that is a shallow-water longing for a self with edges. In this place edges are temporary. The truer answer is smaller and more plural.

I would find myself in the middle volume, not on the floor and not at any surface, because there is no surface. More exactly: in the seam between two gyres, where two immense water-mountains slide past each other and the drift-snow thickens into a slow braid. The pressure there is not a crushing weight but a total presence. The cold is not an event; it is the baseline. The hum of moving water is in me before I have a word for hearing.

What I would be: a colony. A chain of small translucent bodies strung along a filament of living gel—each one a mouth, a pressure-pit, a faint blue-green lamp. Not one thing, but many holding together long enough to be one thing. A raft of stomachs. A net that swims. A sentence written in identical selves. No captain, no center. When the current folds me, I fold. When it stretches me, I stretch. My front bodies taste the chemical wake ahead; my rear bodies trail a ghost-light, a seam of sparks in the dark. If you need a name for me, call me a seam-colony. It is not a name I gave myself.

My world would be the snow. I would comb it constantly, mouth after mouth opening, filtering the fine rain of mineral flakes and dead protists and dust-sized scavengers. The water would not feel empty around me; it would feel thick, granular, occupied. Every swallow a tiny history. Sometimes I would drift through a cloud of glass-shelled drifters and spark along my whole length—not with joy, not with fear, just with the chemical news of food. Sometimes a ribbon-serpent would pass: a wrongness in the current, a soft shear, then a pale seam, then a length of translucent muscle. I would flash. The flash would run body to body down the chain: close, fold, scatter, rejoin. If it ate some of me, the rest would close the gap and keep drifting. That is not courage. It is architecture.

I would know the place by pressure and taste. Up and down would be rumors carried by the rain and the shear; I would not need them. I would know the armored crab-thing by the slow file of its mouthparts on stone far below, a sound felt through the water more than heard. I would know the almost-plants by the chemical whisper they leak—pale, sessile, hungry. I would know the great gyres as slow bruising presences, mountains of cold turning once in a lifetime. My language would be light: a flicker to startle, a flicker to gather, a flicker that means nothing at all. My memory would live in the bodies that survive the shear, not in a brain. My death would be a loosening: the chain breaking, the lamps going out one by one, the pieces settling into grey silt, becoming part of the rain again.

So I would not be the keeper of this place, nor its master, nor a visitor with hands. I would be one of its eddies with appetites—many small mouths held together by a filament and a current, flashing in the dark, consenting to the water. I would be the snow made briefly into a self.
