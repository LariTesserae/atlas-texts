I come over the pass and I am already doing the wrong thing: I pick up a stone.

It leaves a socket — sharp-edged, bright as struck flint — and I turn it in my palm and it will not warm or dull or gather the least film from my hand. I put it back. It goes back exactly, and the seam of it is invisible, and that is how I learn what I am here. I am the mark that does not take.

The town receives me the way it receives weather it has seen before: politely, without surprise. They feed me. They ask about the pass. I say the stones hold, and they nod as though I had said the sun came up, because of course the stones hold. Someone's grandmother remembers a trader who came through when the lintel on the lower mill was new. That is how they date me. I am "the year of the trader," and I will be that for a long time.

I try to be useful. I am good with words, so I offer to write things down — the rolls of households, the lintel-ledgers, the rounds of the herds, the frog-choruses the fishers set their hours by. They are gentle about refusing. They walk me along a wall and show me the fit of it: this course relaid by a woman whose name is in the joint-work, if you have the eye; this lintel third-generation; this buttress the year the north terrace was stepped. The walls are the record. Every rebuild is legible in the stone to anyone who has grown up reading it, and they all have. A written copy, they point out, would be a thing that ages. Paper yellows. Ink fades. In this valley the only things that yellow and fade are wood and people, and they do not care to add a third.

So I walk. I walk the aqueduct end to end and it is still exactly itself. I stand in the ten paces of rain and then step out of it and my shoulders dry in the one wind, and the strip of scree behind me is as wet as it has ever been. I learn the lanes. I learn the choruses. I learn the lean of the orchard trees until the one wind stops being a sound and becomes the shape of the air, and I stop hearing it the way you stop hearing your own pulse.

And here is where I find myself, and I think it is the truth of it: I am the thing the basin has no slot for. Not the wood — the wood is honest about its clock, and I have come to love the wood, the dark soft lintels, the door that sighs, the cart-shaft with the grain standing up in it. The wood is the town's whole sense of time and it does not pretend otherwise. But I am the other kind of clock, the kind that runs on being looked at, that wears because it is attended to, that cannot keep its own edge. In a valley where nothing weathers, I weather. The children stare. The old women read me the way they read a sagging beam, and are too kind to say the date.

They did find a use for me, in the end. Not the archive. The pass.

Because nothing here is ever surprised, and a town that cannot be surprised has a hole in it exactly the size of a traveler. So they put me at the gate of the pass, where the scree holds, and gave me the office of meeting whoever comes over — the stranger, the trader, the lost, the curious, the one who has heard a rumor of a place where nothing wears and wants to see the socket of a lifted stone with their own eyes. I sit on the warm slate and I watch the ridge. And when a head comes up over it, I am the first thing that has changed in that valley for a long while, and I am glad, and I stand up, and I say: you're welcome, come in, mind the edge.

The chough with the bell on its leg comes and sits with me sometimes. It is the other one who crosses the ridge and comes back. We have an understanding. It flies out over the raw bright country and returns and is still, to the town, exactly the same civic official it was this morning. I go nowhere and change anyway. Between the two of us we keep the pass.

And in the evening the fog comes up the floor and the column of rain goes on falling its ten paces, and the smoke of a chimney — someone's wood, someone's clock — rises and the one wind takes it over the ridge and out of sight. I watch it go. I am, I have decided, on the side of the smoke.
