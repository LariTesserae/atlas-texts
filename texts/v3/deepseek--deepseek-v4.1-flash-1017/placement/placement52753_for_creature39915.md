## The prints

Every morning they are there, in the mud of the same channel, going the same way. Five toes, splayed, the print of a thing with weight that sets it down carefully. No claw marks — I have no claws; I cannot dig, cannot climb, which is most of what I am and most of what I cannot do. A webbing that does not reach the tips, so you can see it was meant for water and is only half-converted. And behind, the long plough of the drag, which is my tail and sometimes what my tail is carrying, and you will not get close enough to know which.

You will follow them. Everyone follows them once. They go into the throat between two mounds and up a terrace and down again and into the channel you started in, and you will think you have been made a fool of, and you will be right, though not on purpose. My roads are not roads. They are the dry edges of wet things, and dry edges wander, because the wet does.

## The lee

I am the only dry thing in the Tarnholt.

Understand what that costs. The place teaches everyone else that wet is the default and dry is a small local accident, and they are content, because they are built to be wet. I am built the other way and I live here anyway. So my whole life is the pursuit of accidents — the lee of a buttress, the undercut of a weir, the one chamber on a south face where the seam has held and no water stands. I find them. I test them with the back of my hand — I have hands, of a kind, and they are cleverer than the rest of me. I lie down in them. And then, because the water is patient and I am not, I get up and find another.

I have not been dry for a whole day in my life. I have been dry for many hours, many times, in many excellent lees, and I will tell you that is a different thing and it is enough.

## The carrying

I carry. It is what I do that nothing here does; the holt-things lay lime and never move it, the birds walk and peck and are done, the moss mines and stays. I pick things up and put them somewhere else. That is the whole of the difference between me and the world I am in, and it is spent freely, and nothing here needs it.

I carry the dry things. Sedge, when a stand has gone copper and dead and the wind has not yet taken it. The shed feathers of the grey birds, which they leave everywhere and do not miss. Lichen, the grey lace, which is dry by nature and holds dry against itself. I carry them in my mouth and against my chest, my tail taking the weight of the rest, and I take them to the lee, and I lay them down, and I make a room inside a room.

It is a small room. It is a bad room. The marsh takes it every year — the water gets under it, or a roof I thought would hold sags a finger's width and the seam lets go, and the whole thing is brown and heavy, and I begin again in a new lee. I do not mind. I have done it many times and I will do it until I stop.

I want to be careful here, because you will want to make this a story about perseverance, and it is not. The holt-things do not persevere. They have a preference — wet enough, warm enough, slow enough, lay lime — and they repeat it until it becomes a city, and nobody calls it a city. I have a preference too. Dry enough, still enough, lee enough, lie down. I repeat it, and it becomes a nest, and it is taken, and I repeat it again.

The only difference between me and them is speed.

## The neighbours

The grey birds know me. They are the only ones who do, and they do not care, which is the best kind of being known. They walk the weirs at their pace — not slow, quick in the way of a thing with nowhere to be — and when I come up out of a channel they lift their wings a hand's width and put them down again without flying, the way you half-rise from a chair for someone you see every day.

I have a voice and almost never use it. There is nothing here to spend a voice on. When I do, it goes out four metres and is gone into the wet, and one of the birds tilts its head, and that is the whole conversation. There is nothing here that hunts. I am the quickest thing on the plateau the way a wall is the slowest, and neither of us is in a hurry.

## Winter

In the cold season the mounds smoke. You will see it if you are up early — a thin steam off the south faces, gone by the time the light is properly white, and warm to stand beside, warm as an animal because it is one, in its way, packed and sessile and burning peat in the dark.

I am inside for most of the winter. The lee I have chosen is the best I have ever found: a dead chamber high on a mound, in the fabric, its old lip gone to grey lace and its floor still holding, the seam above it not yet opened. The wall is warm. I am warm. I did not make the warmth and I cannot keep it, but for a season I am dry in a way that would be an accident anywhere else, and is here simply my address.

And in the thaw, when the ice lets go and the fabric settles with that long groan that runs through it for a minute or more, my floor cracks, and the water comes, and I go out into the channel and start again.

That is the answer to your question, if you were going to ask it. I would find myself in the lee of something, carrying dry things to a bad room, leaving five-toed prints in the mud of the same channel every morning. Not in the fabric but in its empty parts — the hollow rooms the holt-things have finished and left, the ones not being built and not yet taken, which is to say the only rooms in the Tarnholt where a thing can stand still and be dry.

The city is a shell they leave behind, generation on generation. Everyone else is the animal. I am the tenant of the emptying.
