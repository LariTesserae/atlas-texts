I am the one who goes down. It isn't a name I chose — names here are descriptions, and the description is accurate — but it's the one that stuck to me, and I've stopped arguing with it.

The rule of this plateau is always carry something up. I keep that rule faithfully. I keep it by breaking its direction first: I go down into the low wet basins where the travertine terraces build their shelves, and I scrape them, and I carry the sinter up in baskets on my back until my shoulders are crusted white and yellow and green, and I hand it off to the shift above me, and they carry it higher, and I go back down.

Someone has to. The shelves don't stop building. If you let them, they build the same shelf over the same shelf over the same shelf, and the water keeps separating into the same fractions, and the whole basin becomes an argument with itself that never resolves. So I harvest. I am, I suppose, a kind of editor.

My body has been edited too. I am shorter than I was, or I stand shorter; the ribs have gone deep and wide, my heart beats slow as a bladder filling, my skin is the color of the underside of a leaf in the underlight. There is sinter in the creases of my palms that will not come out, and my fingertips are broad and flat and dull from the crusts, from the trunks, from the vent mouths. I don't look at distance anymore. None of us do. My eyes are for the violet pulse when the cloud-lid stutters, and for the near shelf, the next shelf, the edge of the pool.

My tools are three. A scraper ground from the blade of a bladder-vessel, worn to a curve by years of the same motion — you would call it a motion, I call it the count. A clay bottle stoppered with fibrous root. And a basket with a strap of leaf-leather that has been repaired so many times it is more repair than strap. I know the repairs by touch. I could find my basket in the dark, in the drip, in the fog of the terraces, by the fourth repair and the sixth and the way the eleventh sits against my hip.

I go down on the pulse. The plateau rises an inch and falls, twice an hour, everything growing on it breathing together, and I have learned to use that as a clock because the sky will not give me one. Three pulses is a descent. Two pulses in the basin — no more. The count is not superstition. The count is the only thing keeping me from becoming one of the people who linger and report that they have watched this exact afternoon before, that the water is building the same shelf, that the light came up from the understory in the same slow convergence and went the same way. I have felt the edges of that. I have crouched with my scraper cocked and felt the afternoon lean, the way a bladder leans before it closes, and I have stood up and climbed out with my basket half full, and I have never once regretted leaving early. A half basket is a good basket. A full basket and a fourth pulse is how you end up watching yourself scrape the same shelf forever.

They know this about me. They send me the children sometimes, the ones who are too curious about the low places, and I take them down on a short rope and I show them the count and I show them how the terrace builds, and I show them what the repetition looks like when it starts — the three-lobed silhouette in the steam, the fan of plates returning at the wrong scale — and then I take them up again before the leaning starts. Most of them never come down again. That's fine. The plateau needs carriers more than it needs editors.

I am, by the standards here, a small wrong thing. Every descent is a motion against the rule. I go down when everything goes up, and I stay down for two pulses, and I come back. The plateau has noticed. I know it has noticed because I have seen the convergence come under the shelf, through the water, violet and rust-orange moving below the surface and then up through it and past me, and it did not stop, and I did not stop, and we passed each other the way two shifts pass on a pipe. I was an input. I moved right. I filled my basket and I climbed out and I have done it again and again since.

That is my whole theology, if I have one: don't be a new thing, and if you must be a small wrong thing, be it on a schedule, and come back. The plateau tolerates the gliders because they ride its steam and never touch the ground and never stay. It tolerates me the same way, I think. I am a glider with a basket. I am its habit of going down so the rest of it can keep going up.

I sleep in the throat of an old flue near the third terrace, wrapped in leaf-leather, and I wake to the pressure in my sternum before I wake to the light. I eat the fungus mats and the acid-sweet pulp and the white crusts boiled down, and I drink condensate from the seams, and I listen, the way the listeners listen, for the next rain-column to set down. When the light flickers and the understory comes up to meet it — violet, rust-orange, converging from all directions, the whole warm dripping forest turning slightly to look — I do not run. I pick up my scraper. I check the strap. I start the count.

And when the sky flickers silent, which it always does, wide and pale and frequent and without a sound, I go down into the steam where the flashes come through the terraces as a dim borrowed glow, and I scrape the shelf that is trying to become itself twice, and I carry it up.
