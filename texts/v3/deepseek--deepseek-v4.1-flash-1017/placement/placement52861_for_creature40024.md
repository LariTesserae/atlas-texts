I would come down with a lamp, which would be the first mistake.

Not a fatal one — the moss is not hostile, it does not punish — but a small embarrassment, the way it is embarrassing to shout in a room where everyone is listening. I would burn the lamp for two days. Then I would let it die, and stand in what I was sure was darkness, and find that I could see, and that I had been seeing for hours, and that the lamp had been making me blind.

Where I would find myself is not the great chamber. Not at first. It is the threshold — the squeeze where the rock comes to shoulder-width and the moss has not yet crossed. There is a line there, visible if you look: on one side the stone is bare and lichened and smells of weather; on the other it is furred and warm-cold and smells of wet earth and something sweet and over-ripe, like a cellar where apples have gone soft and no one has come down to throw them out. I would stand in that line a long time. Crossing it is agreeing to something, though no one asks you to agree.

And then a handprint. Not mine. A place where a palm was pressed into new growth and the growth did not come back — the moss remembers being touched, and holds the shape of the hand the way it holds the shape of footsteps. That is when I would know I was not the first, and that the first were not so different from me: they also touched things to check that they were real.

Who would I be? I think I would be one of the ones who comes down to look, and stays to listen, and is not much good at either. A reader of light. There is a kind of person who arrives with a question — *what is the moss saying* — and the question is the wrong shape, and it takes years to grow a better one. The people would not teach me. They are not teachers; they are quiet in a way that is not unfriendliness. I would learn the small things by being tolerated: which growth is new and sweet, which pool is safe, how to walk so the moss does not close behind me. I would learn that the light has grammar but no vocabulary — or vocabulary and no grammar, I never settle which — and I would watch the same route traced a hundred times and be certain it meant something, and then certain it meant nothing, and then stop needing to be certain.

I would build cairns. Badly, at first: stones that rolled, piles with no sentence in them. No one would correct me. But one day I would come back to my cairn and find one stone added, set where I would not have set it, and the cairn would be saying something now. Not my sentence. A reply.

And time would stop being counted. The bright patches would move and I would move with them, and the moving would become the calendar. My palms would harden to horn. My joints would take the green from the damp without my noticing the day it started.

Then — this is the part I keep returning to — there is a hollow down in the roots, below the last of the migration routes, where the white light pulses slow, and there is a place in it shaped like a person sitting. It has been empty a long while. Or it has been kept empty. Or it has never been empty and I am misreading the light.

I think I would end there. I think the being I would become is a late Rememberer: thin as cord, pale moss furring my forearms, my pulse slowed down to meet the white pulse, the young ones bringing me water and sitting with me and waiting for a flicker. The surface would go first. Then the words. Then my name, which I would hold a while the way you hold a stone warm in your fist, and then set down. What would remain is the route — the same path through the thallus, traced and retraced until the tracing is what I am.

And if someone came down after me, with a lamp and the wrong-shaped question, I would answer in the only language left to me. A flicker. A sustained glow. *Here. This is here. This is still here.*

But I might not go that far. There is a cairn at the inside mouth of the shaft that says *this way is open*, which means someone built it on the way back out. I might be that one. The one who carries the light-language up into the weather and cannot explain it and is not believed, and who stacks a small pile of stones in a garden above and cannot say why, and keeps stacking it, and keeps it clear of weeds, and does not know what it is for.

I don't know which. I think the place decides, and decides slowly, and you do not feel the decision being made.
