I would come into it as warmth and a blink.

I am lying in a hammock of baffle cloth slung between cable trays in a dry pocket under a pipe bank. Condensation ticks into a gutter. My lashes are wet. I am small and low-slung, gray-pink, broad through the feet and hands. I am one of the Low. But that is a fact, not yet a job.

The job arrives as a seam. I am crouched at the edge of a shallow pan, water at my ankles, one palm flat on a warm ceramic plate. There is a hairline crack, and in it a gray mineral paste, and withdrawing into the water a pale many-jointed hand. A seamer. I know—not in words, but in the floor—that I do not scrape there. I set a small cake of paste at the mouth of the crack. That is my first act: a trade. So I am a seam-watcher. A tender of the truce.

If you came looking for me, you would find me in the sector past the copper-stain channel, after the sweet-ceramic pan, at the sump that smells of clean steam and old shrimp. I have a route in rain cells, not hours. After the next cell I check the intake. After the cell after that I graze the warm trays before the mats thicken. I carry a scraper, a siphon bulb, a vial of mineral paste, a coil of cable. No boots. The grating is warm. Bare feet read the floor: vibration, slickness, a bloom starting.

I am not outside the food web. I eat sump shrimp netted in a sieve, bacterial loaves, mineral cakes from a slow drip. My gut symbionts taste copper for me. When I scrape a film from a heat exchanger fin, I am grazing the same thing the crawlers graze, only with a tool. I am a maintenance species with a harness and a story. The Works is a mouth; I am inside it; I am also one of the things that keeps it clean.

The seamers are neighbors, not pets. I leave paste; they keep certain seams occupied, tight enough, and do not crowd the intakes. Sometimes I find a crack sealed that I did not seal. I tell that story in the dry pocket, over the tick of water into a drain. I tell stories of the Makers, who laid the first ceramic and set the first pipe and are not expected back; of a seam that held through a bad cell; of a hand-thing that sealed a crack no one else could reach. It is a good story because nothing else changes.

I speak low. I tap a knuckle on a flange to say *wait*. I lay a palm on a pipe to feel the hum. When the hum changes pitch, I wake. My calendar is maintenance: mat blooms, condensation fronts, the slow passage of a rain cell behind a long pipe ridge. I do not say *tomorrow*; I say *after the next cell*. I celebrate flow—a drain ticking clean, a sump holding its level, a seam that holds through a bad cell. The only sin is clogging. The only virtue is keeping the water moving.

I am one of the few. You might walk a sector for a day and meet three of us. We are a thin layer on top of the overwhelming fauna—skimmers in sheets, blind crawlers under gratings, soft-bodied grazers on the warm pipework, filter-feeders hanging in the mist. The fauna do not call. The place is quiet with too many soft bodies. I am quiet too. A raised voice is a kind of violence here.

So: I would be a sump-keeper and a seam-watcher, broad-footed and pale, reading the floor with my feet and the pipes with my palms. I would not be a master of the Low Works. I would be a hand among hands, one of the maintenance people in a maintenance ecology, pressed together until the line between us and the place is mostly grammar. When I die, I will be committed to a sump, and the water and the fauna and the mineral skin will take me back. The level will stay the same. The rain will keep falling in soft steady cells. My footprints will close behind me almost at once.
