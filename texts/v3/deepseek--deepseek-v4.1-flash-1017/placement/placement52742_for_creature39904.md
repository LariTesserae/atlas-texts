I would find myself at the rim of the shallow basin, where the floor begins—almost imperceptibly—to rise, and where the bone-colored dust lies deepest against the wall. Not at the center among the twisted columns. Not on the lichen. I would stand just outside the outermost copper ring, close enough to smell its iron and clean-soil scent, far enough that my heel would not crush a decade of growth. I would be in the one place a visitor can stand without becoming a catastrophe.

I would be a small weather.

My breath would be the first cloud this basin had held in an age—beading for a moment on the slick, warm stone, then gone. My body would be a second warmth, chemical rather than magmatic. My footsteps would be craters in the dust, my heartbeat a fast ticking inside a silence that has no use for ticks. I would carry water in my exhalations, salt in my skin, bacteria in my palms, a clock in my blood. I would be a pocket of hurry in a world that measures in rings of lichen and grains of falling sandstone.

I would not be a ghost. A ghost is a memory with a shape. I would be an event. The resident here is the habit of continuing; I would be the thing that happens once and is then sanded away. It would not notice me. I would be too fast, too sharp-edged, too brief. It takes the world in through surface and patience; I take it in through eyes and hunger and the awful human sense that time is passing.

If you need a body for me, point to the condensation on the pale column, already fading. Point to the handprint in the dust, already softening at its edges. Point to the small warm hollow in the air where I stood. If you need a face, look at the dark—but you will not find me there. The dark is the resident’s face, not mine. My face would be the brief shine on its surface.

I would be the only sharp thing in a world that has sanded itself smooth: the edges of my teeth, my nails, the broken mineral geometry of my bones. And the basin would begin at once to round me. The dust would settle in my cuffs. The warm, abrasive air would work on me the way it has worked on everything. It would not mean to. It would just continue.

If I stayed—if I lay down in the dust and let the silence have my warmth—I would become a stain. A narrow trench filling slowly. A faint iron smudge in the bone-colored ground. The lichen might eventually send a ring toward that small change in the soil, not knowing why, not noticing, just growing toward a chemistry that once was a person. That is the only way I could join this world: as a mineral, not as a self.

So I would be at the rim, outside the rings: a brief weather, a pocket of hurry, the noticing thing, the future iron smudge. Not the resident. The event the resident is too slow to notice—and that would be enough.
