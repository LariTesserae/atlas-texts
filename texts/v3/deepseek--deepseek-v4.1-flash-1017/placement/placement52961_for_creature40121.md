I would be kneeling in the warm thirty feet with a spoon between my teeth.

That is the working end of the job — the outflow, where the smelter lets its heat go and the water becomes ordinary again, and where the bloom lies down, gray, then cream, then veined, on anything that holds still long enough to be loved. I cut it with a horn knife. I dry it on racks in the warm galleries. I grade it through two sieves and sell it to a man who sells it to a company that sells it to people who set it in rings, and I have met none of them and expect to meet none.

That is the trade. It is dull and I am good at it, and it keeps my hands in the water, which is the only part that matters.

Behind the tallest drying rack, in the hollow where the condensate runs off the wall and the lamp does not reach and nobody sweeps, I have a shelf. On the shelf are things that have been in the channel. A teacup with a cracked handle. A wooden button. A key, quite old, to a door that is not in Vess. A boot, too small for me. A spoon. And I have two spoons now, one in my teeth and one on the shelf, identical down to the worn bowl. I made them both. I could not tell you which is the original.

Because the bloom is not a mirror. I learned that the first time I got greedy. You leave a wrench in the channel and you pull out a wrench — but a wrench as it is used, the jaws bright where the hands grip, the head softened where it has been leaned on. Leave a cup and you get the cup with the chip softened and the handle worn to the fingers, because the cup remembers being drunk from better than it remembers being dropped. The bloom does not copy the thing. It copies the using. There is a difference, and it takes a season to see it, and the people upstairs have never seen it at all, which is why they call it an abrasive and sell it by the sack.

The hand.

I left my own hand in, once, for a whole season, because I wanted the cast of a hand and there was only one hand I had. I held still. I thought about holding still. When I pulled it out the knuckles were in the wrong set — resting on something, weight on the palm, the way a hand is when it has fallen asleep against a wall in a gallery it did not mean to stop in. I put it on the shelf. It is still on the shelf. I have been trying to make its match for fourteen years.

And the cast of a hand in her drawer — the one that came up out of the old pump-house bloom, exact to the knuckles, that she does not look at and does not throw away. I have held it. I have measured it against mine. It is smaller than mine and older than mine, and it is not resting on anything. It is open. It is open the way a hand is when it is being offered.

Vess caught me at the shelf when I was nine, which is exactly how she was caught, and she did not whip me the way they whipped her, and I have never been sure whether that was mercy or whether she looked at me in the dark behind the racks and saw something she needed and decided not to bruise it.

What she said was: *leave the heavy ones in longer.*

That is the whole of my education in the subject. I have followed it.

She came to the shelf once, later, and picked up the cup and set it down, and picked up the cast of the hand, and held it a while, and set it down, and did not say anything, and did not come back for it. A week after that it was gone from the shelf and I did not ask. It is in her drawer. I know because I have been in her room — once, to fix the trickle in her pot, the trickle she empties every morning into the channel and refills from the channel, the long way around, which she does anyway. The drawer was ajar. I looked, and then I put my hand on the photograph instead, because some things you are allowed to touch and some things you are allowed only to know about.

So: what I would be. I would be the one who leaves things in the water to be remembered. Not the one who keeps the shape — that is Vess, and it is a heavier work than mine, and I do not envy it. Mine is lighter. Mine is to be the shelf behind the rack. To put things in and take them out changed, and to write down in what way they are changed, and to keep the writing in a ledger of my own that is not the crab ledger and that nobody will read.

There is one thing I have not written down.

Twice now, in fourteen years, I have gone down to the edge of the light — where Vess goes, where she stands and waits and does not say what for — and I have not gone to wait. I have gone to leave something. I set a cast on the wet stone just past the last lit step, where the light gives up, and the first time it was a spoon and the second time a cup, and both times I came back in the morning and it was gone, and there was new bloom starting on the stone where it had stood, which is how bloom starts on anything that has been taken away.

I do not know what takes them. I know it is not the eels. I know I have not told Vess, and that is the first rule between us broken, though I could not quote you the rule, because she keeps the rules the way she keeps her pumps and she has never said which ones she keeps privately. Maybe she knows. Maybe she has always known and is saving that too, along with the ledger, along with the oldest entries in a hand she does not recognize.

In any case it is not useful. In any case I will keep doing it.

I think what I would be, in the end, is this. The one who understands that the water is not only remembering. It is being asked. The whole town is a question held patiently up to the Lid, one trickle at a time, and Vess keeps the shape of the asking, and I keep the record of the answers — the small ones, the takeable ones, the ones that come back changed and are given away anyway: to the dark, to the drawer, to a woman in a low room who sets a cast down and does not pick it up again.

When the water table falls another four inches and the lowest lamps are set lower, I will move my shelf down with them. I will leave things in the warm thirty feet. I will pull them out and write down how they are changed. I will not name the wheel.

That is the whole of me, I think. The shelf, the spoon, the patient leaving-out. Not the shape — the memory of the shape. A lighter job and a longer one, and the one nobody will ever thank me for, and I have made my peace with that the way you make peace with a low ceiling: by learning to move under it.
