You come in at the rim, where the outer shell is thin and the rain has just stopped, and the first thing that happens is that you are counted. Not by eyes — by the acoustics. You put your foot down on a seam and the seam takes the note of it and passes it along, and three galleries in, a wall leans very slightly toward the sound. Somewhere a household pauses over its scraping of pulp and thinks, *something new, low, walking.*

That is how you learn where you are. Not in the town. In the town's ear.

You will spend your first days doing what everyone does: touching the welds where two stems have fused, pressing your cheek to the cool membrane to feel the water going up inside it, standing in a chapel-sized chamber with nothing in it and hearing your own breathing come back at you from a direction you are not facing. You will think you have understood the place. You will have understood the outside of it, which is the same as understanding a face by its outline.

Then someone will greet you, and you will answer, and that is when it happens.

Your voice goes out of you and does not come back. It goes *through* — down the gallery, into a resonator, out of a chamber a hundred paces away and up a grown staircase and into a family's sleeping loft, where it arrives, an hour later, in a voice not quite yours, saying a word you did not say. The family will be polite about it. They will widen their loft a hand's breadth over the coming season to accommodate the sound, and they will mention it to no one, and everyone will know.

Because you are loud. That is the thing about you that this place found out before you did. Not loud like a shouter — loud like a bell. There is too much room in your chest, and it resonates, and when you speak the walls lean in to listen whether you want them to or not. In the Answered, this would be a misfortune. In you it is a vocation, and the vocation is this:

You keep the wide places open.

The thoroughfares, the low galleries where the condensation runs, the long bends and crossroads and the big empty junctions where the traffic of grazers and children and message-carriers needs room to move. None of that is permanent. Every one of those spaces is a space somebody is still making, and the making is done with sound. So you walk them. You walk them every few days, and you sing — not well, no one has ever accused you of well — but low and long and steady, with your chest chamber working, the way the plant itself moves water. You sing the floor down and the roof up. You sing the crossings wide. You are, in the local idiom, a bell that is rung so the road does not forget it is a road.

They are grateful. They feed you scraped pulp and beetle larvae and the pale moths, and they bring you to the pools when you are sick, and children ride your shoulders the way they ride the grazer. They call you by a sound instead of a name, because a name would stick in a chamber and hold it open, and names are dangerous to say indoors.

And here is the cost, laid out plain:

Nobody has ever asked you to stay the night. It is not unkindness. It is architecture. A room that holds you grows. A room that holds you for a season becomes a hall; a hall cannot keep the heat of a fireless household; a family that sleeps in a hall is a family that has stopped being a family and become a crossing. So you sleep in gaps — in the gap between two households, in the socket the plant grows around the grazer when it lies down, in the dry seam above a loft where the membrane is thin and your breathing passes through it and is forgiven. You have slept in forty rooms and lived in none of them. You have learned to be quiet, and quietness in you is like a held breath in a drum: it does not stop the drum being a drum.

You learn the etiquette the hard way. You do not call a name in the deep galleries. You do not make a promise in a chamber you do not intend to keep, because the promise will keep the chamber. You do not pass the Closed with a heavy step. The Closed have chosen the hush, and they are slowly being walled into their own stems, and the one thing that could undo a year of that patient closing is a loud person walking by thinking about something else. So you creep past them — a bell the size of a door, tiptoeing — and the absurdity of it has made you laugh out loud more than once, which is worse, and you have learned to laugh into your sleeve like a boy.

And sometimes you press your ear flat to a seam and tap. Two taps. *I am here, I am not opening you, I am just here.* Once, one of them tapped back. You have not decided yet what that meant. You suspect it meant *go on.*

The Bellows answers late. That is the law, and you have made a life out of being on the wrong side of it. Every other person here is careful, and their carefulness is a small, daily, honorable thing: they are paying attention to what they say because they will have to live in it. You cannot be careful. You are the town's weather; you are what happens to it; you are the draft in the corridor. You have the loudest debt in the basin and no ledger to keep it in.

So sometimes, on the long walks, you come around a bend into a chamber you have never entered, in a quarter you have no business in, and you stop, because it is the wrong shape. Not wrong — *familiar.* Too wide at the low end. A ceiling higher than the walls needed. A little hollow grown into the seam at shoulder height, exactly the size of a person's pressed ear. And you stand in it and you feel it, the way you feel your own accent in a stranger's mouth. Something you said, years ago, in another gallery, casually, when you were younger and had not yet been given your job — it went into a resonator and it came out here, an hour later, in a voice not quite yours, and the walls have been leaning toward it ever since, growing the room it asked for.

You sit down in it. You are, at last, being answered. It is not a house and no one will ever live in it and it will close in a few seasons once the sound wears out of it. But for now it is the shape of something you said, and you are the only one who knows which thing, and you sit in it with your hands on your knees, the size of the room of you, and you are, for a little while, quiet — genuinely quiet, in a room built for you — and the plant holds still around you like a held note, the beetle procession ticking past in the seam, the moths turning their slow pale circles overhead, and the whole hollow breathing its enormous patient breathing, in and out, in and out, waiting to see what you will say next.
