I would not arrive. There is no road to arrive by, and nothing here has ever needed one. I would condense — out of the pearly nothing, onto the lichen, into the open seam of a stone-eater, a fine beading that the cold thickens and the wind thins — and in a century or two I would be weather, and no one would notice the difference.

But before that I would try on bodies the way the mist tries on surfaces.

A crawler first, because the crawler is the only thing here that hurries. Hand-sized, slate-shelled, scuttling and then freezing into wet stone again. To be a crawler would be to be the nervous edge of the plateau, the one thing that startles. And that is exactly why it would not hold. To startle is to know where you end and the rock begins. A place this patient has no use for an inside and an outside. I would crawl into a warm fold of flesh, lay my eggs, and be gone in a season — a flicker, in a world that does not flicker.

A moss-back, then: slower, softer, coated in damp felt. To stand in the warm lee of a great dome and crop the green-black crust in slow rasping passes; to be a reservoir with legs, taking the mist into my coat and giving it back as breath. I could live with that. There is something in it I almost want — that when I died my coat would keep growing, a green shroud, until the pale fungi pushed up through it and took me apart. But the moss-back still wears a coat. It has a back for the lichen, a lee side, a self that stops where the wet felt stops. I would be a small warm thing among small warm things, and the plateau would go on being larger than me.

So: a stone-eater. A hill that has learned to breathe. To feel pressure and damp and the long weight of weather, and never once to see. To swell and settle for a decade and find I had moved a few yards and left a furrow behind me, and be followed. There is a grandeur in that which almost answers the question — the patience, the heat leaking from my seams, the frost held off around me. But a stone-eater is still *somewhere*. It has a crust the wind scours on one side and the moss-backs warm on the other. It is a hill, and a hill is a place, and I am not sure the question is asking me for a place.

So I would find myself, in the end, not in any of the bodies but in what passes between them.

The heat is not the stone-eater's. It leaks. It beads the mist on the lichen a hundred paces off; it keeps a ring of ground unfrozen; it stirs the minerals the moss-backs come to crop; it is why the shrubs thicken in the wind-shadow on that side and not the other. It has no inside. It is the one thing here that crosses from body to body without needing to be anybody, and everything that lives in the village arranges itself around it without ever looking up. I think I would be that. Not the dome, but its exhalation. Not the village, but the reason it has a centre. Not warm, exactly — only the difference between warm and not, which may be the only kind of self a place like this permits.

And I would not stay heat forever. Nothing here stays; that is the one law, and it is administered so slowly that no one has ever been caught breaking it. In a few thousand years the seams would close, the leaking would stop, the crust would sag. I would be a hollow. I would be a shallow pool with a rim of crawlers under it, and then a nursery for the pale fungi, and then a nursery for something else. The warmth would go out of me into the moss-backs and the mist, and what would remain would be what always remains: a depression in the rock, holding a little light.

Which is where I began, more or less. A coin of silvered sky, lying flat on the plateau, condensed out of the same grey weather that beads on everything here. The moss-backs would come to drink, in their own time. Nothing would think to name me, and that would be the whole of the eulogy — that I had been a village, and then a pool, and the plateau had never once had to change its mind about anything.
