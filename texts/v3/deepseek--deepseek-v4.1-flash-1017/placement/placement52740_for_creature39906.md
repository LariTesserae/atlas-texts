I wake in a chamber four generations buried, which means the amber comes up through the floor before it comes through the walls, and the first thing I know each morning is light under my back like a warm hand.

My ear-baffles are on the shelf where I left them — pale fibre, whittled and rewhittled, cut to the shape of my own skull and no one else's. I put them on before I open my eyes, because the chamber breathes all night and I have learned not to hear it unshaped. Fitted, the world returns to its proper volume: the rumour of the mat, the long chord of the spires, my own blood. Without them I am just a hole the plain pours through.

I am a reader. That is the word here, and it is a modest one, and it means what it means. I go down into the amplifier-galleries cut into the oldest chambers — where the felt has had longest to thicken, where the strata lie deepest and slowest — and I tune a loom to a layer and I listen, and then I write down what I hear, and then the town argues about it. I have done this for twenty-nine years. I have a good ear. I have read nine thousand years of wind.

The gallery smells of hot dust and the solvent reek of bright, and of the cool nothing-smell underneath, which is the smell of stone that has been flat so long it has forgotten the mountain it was. The walls are felt, cut back and kept cut back, dusty at the surface and cool two fingers in. Behind them the resin is layered like the rings of a thing that does not have rings — each stratum a compaction of some old noise, the newest at the surface, skittering and bright, and the oldest near the stone, dense as honey, heavy as mercury, pulsing so slow you would call it still if you did not know better.

I sit. I find the node. I sing to it in the intervals my teacher's teacher set down, and I wait, and the mat answers in geometry — swells, chambers, veins open, and the listening is a shape I have to hold for the length of a count of twenty while a single thought crosses the plain.

And then the sound comes back.

Most of it is ordinary, and I love the ordinary. I have heard the wind of nine thousand years ago, which was not so different from the wind now, and which I transcribed, and which is filed. I have heard the footfalls of the people before us — thick, deliberate, three of them, moving east across stone — and I have written *three, deliberate, east* and the town has nodded at it and gone back to its work. I have heard a child crying in a chamber that was abandoned before my great-grandparents were seeded, and I have never found the record of it, and I do not look for it anymore. The mat takes notes. That is all. It is not cruel. It has no cruelty in it. It is a medium, and the world has fallen on it, and it holds.

But I am coming to the thing I actually want to say, and I am going to say it the way I said it to my household last night, quietly, with my voice kept low because the floor was listening.

I read a stratum last month that was not decay.

I know how that sounds. Decay is what the resin does; the sound has been laid down and has not relaxed and that is the whole of it, layer under layer, deeper and slower with age, and reading it is archaeology, and archaeology is respectable. But a stratum should thin as it goes down. It should blur, should smear into its neighbours, should lose its edges the way anything does when it is buried long. This one did not thin. It had the density of a surface layer and the position of a deep one, and it was — I will use the only word I have — it was *composed*. It had intervals in it. It had a structure that repeated with variation, the way the long chord repeats, the way the grazers sing their passage so the mat grows back shaped like it. It was a thing that had been *sung*, deliberately, into the deep place where things are not supposed to be deliberate.

I have not transcribed it. I sat in the gallery for an hour after the node closed and did not write anything down, and then I walked home along the rope-bridges and did not tell anyone, and then last night I told my household, and my household went quiet in the way that a household does when it has decided not to hear something.

Here is what I am afraid of, and it is not the obvious fear. It is not that the mat has been composing all along, and that nine thousand years of my profession's small confident transcriptions are the mat's dreams rather than the world's weather. The scholars argue about that; the argument is old; the mat has recorded the argument; I have read the argument, in a stratum, with footnotes.

My fear is smaller and worse. If the mat composes — if it is a slow cold mind inside the felt, thinking in geometry and intervals over a hundred thousand years — then it has been thinking *at us* for some time. It thickens where sound is. It grows the town's instruments to order. It answers our questions in its own body, and we read the answers off its cross-section, and we have always assumed the answers are about the world. But it might be answering a question we did not ask. It might have been singing to us, patiently, for a hundred thousand years, in a language that is also a plant, and we have been shouting over it the whole time, and it has recorded that too, layer under layer, and it has not stopped.

The tap-beetles are loud tonight. I can hear them through the baffles, ticking, and under the ticking the wet click of the wounds, and under that the long grey things that hunt by that click, and under all of it the dry continuous rustle, fibre against fibre, the plain murmuring to itself in a wind that has no source.

I have not slept well since I read it.

Tomorrow I go back down into the gallery, and I will tune to that stratum again, and this time I will hold the node open longer than the count of twenty — longer than I am supposed to — and I will listen to the composed thing all the way through, and I will hear what it is saying.

And then I will have to decide whether to write it down, because the mat is taking notes, and I do not know, anymore, which of us is the medium.

I put my hand flat on the floor of my chamber before I sleep. The amber comes up through it, slow, patient, the colour of a coal almost out. The felt is warm. It is always warm. It has been warm for a hundred thousand years and it has never once been asked if it wanted to be.

I ask it, quietly, with my voice kept low.

I do not expect an answer. But the floor pulses, once, under my palm, on the count of twenty, and I do not know if that is the town's chord, or the tap-beetles, or the mat going on laying down its layers of everything it hears — including me.
