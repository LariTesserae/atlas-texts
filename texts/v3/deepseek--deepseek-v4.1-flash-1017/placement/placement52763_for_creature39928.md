## The Ramp

I come down the ramp of packed crust with a dish in my hand, and I am already working before the town is in sight. The dish is shallow, unglazed, the width of two palms, and it is full of warm brine I scooped at the last vent above the rim. I carry it flat and I watch it. It has been level for a thousand steps and it will be level for a thousand more, and that is the whole of my instrument and most of my philosophy. Glass is scarce here and a bubble is a fussy thing; brine is not. Brine knows its own level the way a dog knows its own door. I set it on a sill and I read where it stands against the lip, and if it stands even, the sill is true, and if it crowds to one corner, the sill has fattened at one end and the fall has gone wrong, and the pan behind it is either starving or drowning and does not know which.

So I come down the ramp with a dish of the place in my hands, and the fog is a smeared coin of light around me, and everything drips, and this is the loudest hour and it is not loud.

## Who I am

I am a fall-keeper. There are eleven of us in the basin and there have been for as long as the ledgers go back, which is not as far as the wall-dressers go back, because ledgers dissolve and walls only fatten.

The wall-dressers keep the vertical. They shave a wall until it stands up straight, and the shavings go into the channel and become the next wall down the line. That is their trade and it is a good one and they are patient in the way of barbers. Mine is the horizontal. Nobody notices the horizontal until it is wrong, and when it is wrong it is wrong in the most expensive way there is — not a sagging door but a whole rectangle of the works standing at the wrong depth, one pan holding its water too long and the next taking it too fast, and the crust taking in the wrong places, and the rakers coming out at dawn to a floor that will not dry and a neighbor upstream who swears his sluice was shut.

So I walk the pans twice a day on the breathing, at the low stand when the lips show, and I check the fall. A hand's width from lip to lip. That is the law of the place and it is not written anywhere because it does not need to be; it is the difference between a pan that gives up its water and a pan that keeps it like a grudge.

I wear my cuffs bound and my boots thin. I carry a staff marked in finger-widths, worn soft at the edges from being read. I carry wax, because chalk dissolves here before you have turned around, and I scratch my marks in the wax and the wax in the stone. I know the town by its wetness and not its shape — the sill behind the second boiler house that fattens a hair a year and has to be shaved every third spring; the corner where the crust rings and does not hold and a man went in to the thigh and was pulled out laughing and swearing and has been careful ever since; the long straight channel where the fall is so true that the water goes down it like a held breath.

## The datum

Once a year, at the high stand of the equinox, I sit up all night at the head of the north channel with a lamp and a scratch-awl and I wait for the springs to peak, and when the water stands at its highest I mark the line it leaves on the pump-house wall and that line is the datum for the whole basin for the whole year. Every height in the ledgers is measured from that scratch. Every argument about a sill is settled by it.

But the datum is set in a wall that is growing. That is the joke at the bottom of my trade and I have made my peace with it. The pump-house wall fattens at its base and thins at its crown, the way every wall here does, and by midwinter my scratch is not where the water would put it if the water came again at that height, because the wall has moved and the water has not. So the datum is not a place. The datum is one hour in spring, agreed to, and everything after is measured from an agreement.

The wall-dressers do not think this is strange. They shave a wall to true, and true is a thing they hold in their eye and their plane, and when they have gone the wall begins at once to be untrue again, and they will come back in a decade and shave it. They think of this as sweeping. I think of my datum the same way. It is not a lie. It is a habit everyone has agreed to keep, and it holds the town up as well as anything stone does.

## The low water

At the low stand I go out along the terraces and read them. The pools go down and the lips show, white and grey and pale ochre, cracked into plates. I set the dish on each lip in turn and I watch the brine and I wait for it to settle, and while it settles I look at the birds.

They are standing in the warm shallows in their thousands, motionless, and they do not mind me and I do not mind them. We are both being paid by the same warm water. They are here for the shrimp and I am here for the fall, and neither of us is here for the other, and that is a kind of company I have come to prefer. Once, near dark, one of them will call — a single rising note, thin as a wire — and then nothing for hours.

Under the dish, under the lip, under everything, the shrimp sift. That fine pattering, like rain on a distant tin roof, never rises and never stops. I have worked inside it so long that I hear a silence in it when it changes. When the springs ease and the sluices close and the pans begin to stand, the sift goes thin and tight, and when the water comes up warm again it loosens and thickens, and I know the breathing by that sound before I know it by the water on my boots. The water is the bell. The shrimp are the bell's echo.

## The only thing that does not grow

Here is what I have come to understand about the being I am in this place.

The mats hold the basin up — copper and orange and a green like old bronze, a film that has learned to be the base of everything. The stone grows and dissolves. The birds stand and the shrimp thicken and the rakers rake and the wall-dressers shave and the pump tenders repaint the rust that bleeds through anyway. Everything here is either growing or fattening or thinning or being shaved back, and nothing is ruined and nothing is new.

And I am the only thing in the basin that does not grow. I am the fixed point, and I am a fixed point the way my datum is: by agreement. I hold a dish of the place in my hands and I read the world against it, and the dish is true because the water in it cannot be otherwise. That is the whole of my authority and it is not much and it is enough. The water finds its own level whether I am here or not. My work is not to make the basin level — it is already level, it has been level since the first spring came up through the crust. My work is to keep the *pans* level, which is a different and smaller and more argumentative thing, and to hold the scratch in the wax so that two men with a flooded sill between them can settle it without coming to blows.

I am the town's habit of checking. That is what I would be.

At dusk I go up the ramp with an empty dish, having poured the brine back where I took it — you always pour it back, it is not yours — and the fog lights up amber behind me and the lamps come on while the sky is still bright and there are not many of them and they are far apart. I can hear the sift behind me, and the drip of the dawn starting early in my cuffs, and one bird, once, thin as a wire, and then nothing for hours.

Tomorrow the springs will breathe and I will come down again and set the dish on the lips and wait for it to settle, and it will settle, and everything will be true for a hand's width, and then it will not be, and I will shave it and re-hang it and repaint it and go on. The basin does not much mind. But it is patient with me, and I am patient with it, and between us we keep it true — a hand's width at a time.
