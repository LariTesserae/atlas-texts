I came up on the freight lift with three bales of lagging cloth and a crate of zinc anodes, and the first thing I did on the Sud was flinch.

Someone offered me a wet cloth and I stepped back from it. I want to record that, because it is the last thing I did here that was purely lowland. The woman holding it — a pan-raker, sleeves black to the elbow with brine — looked at me the way you look at a person who has refused water in a desert, and then she laid the cloth over the rail and went back to work and let me come to it on my own. It took four days. I remember the day I picked it up because it was the day I stopped being a visitor and became a contractor, and the two happened in the same hour, and neither of them felt like anything.

I had come for the lagging. Oveth has a route book and the route book has a page nobody wants, and the page is eleven through nineteen — the far wellheads, out past the calcining sheds where the mist stands thickest and the walk is long and the work is done alone. The town does not lack for people who can wrap a pipe. What it lacks is people willing to be out at the end of the grid, in the fog, in the soundlessness, with no one to talk to and nothing to hear but the throb. I could sew. I had come up from a place where I had been, in some small way, unnecessary. The Sud did not care why I was there. It had a page in a book and I fit it.

So: I am the one who does eleven to nineteen. That is my name here, more or less. People say *he has the far run* the way they might say a man has a bad back.

The work is this. A pipe comes out of a wellhead and runs a kilometer and a half to the pans, and it is hot, and it is full of the mountain, and if you leave it bare it will ring — a thin metallic complaint all along its length, a sound that carries further than anything else on the plateau because it is a sound the wet air can't eat, being already inside the metal. So you wrap it. You wrap it wet, because dry cloth cracks in a week and wet cloth sets like a cast, and you wrap it in a spiral with a bone hook and a curved needle, and you pull each turn snug against the last, and you work from the wellhead outward so the seam always faces away from the weather. A well-lagged pipe does not ring. It breathes. Put your palm on one and you feel the tick and shiver of it going about its business, quiet as a sleeping animal.

The crust does not like this. The crust grows on anything that stays still, and the cloth stays still. It blooms white at the joints first, then at the seams, then everywhere, and after a season the lagging is a mineral sleeve with cloth somewhere inside it, and you cut it off and you wrap it again. I have wrapped the same forty meters of pipe eleven times. I will wrap it again. This is not a complaint; it is the shape of the job, and the shape of the job is the shape of the plateau, which is also in a long argument with water and also mostly satisfied.

The anodes I like better. Every wellhead has a block of zinc bolted to it that is meant to be eaten, and it is eaten, and every few months I take the eaten one off and put a fresh one on. I keep the spent ones in a tin. They come off pitted and pale and oddly beautiful, like something the ground has been chewing, and there is no reason to keep them, and I keep them. When I am out at nineteen and the mist has closed to five meters and the only thing in the world is the pipe and my hands, I am the man who replaces the thing that is sacrificed so the rest of it doesn't have to be. I have thought about this more than I should.

You learn the ground through your feet. That is not a figure of speech here. The crust is different in the morning and different in the afternoon, and the pipes are laid under it, and a change in the throb means a change in pressure somewhere and you feel it in the arches of your feet before you know it in your head. I can tell you where I am at eleven by the pitch of the shudder and I could not draw you a map. The rakers keep to their lines and the well crews keep to their numbers, but I keep to a sound, and the sound is the town's whole nervous system running under me while I walk it.

I have learned not to stand still. Not out of fear — the crust is patient and means nothing by it — but because you get into the habit the way you get into the habit of breathing. I sway. Everyone sways. Watch the yard at shift change and it is forty people rocking gently on their own feet, and it looks like a congregation, and it is only that no one has given the ground a surface to work on.

The veils keep pace with me out past the sheds. Two, sometimes three, hanging a little behind and above, trailing their filaments, catching the light at the edge. I do not brush them off. There is nothing to brush. I have got used to the soft collapsing click of one folding on itself, and to the way they drift toward the warmth of a body and the wet of a breath. They are the largest living thing here and they weigh nothing and they are not, I think, interested in me. They are interested in the column I make in the air. That is enough.

I have been out to the sinter field. I know a grandmother by her bands — a shoulder in the mineral, rust and cream and a seam of copper-green, growing a millimeter a year. In a place where every surface is being dissolved and re-formed and taken away, this is the only thing that accumulates. I stood there a while. Then I went back and wrapped nineteen again, because it had been a season.

I no longer say I came from below. Not because I am ashamed of it, but because it has stopped being the true sentence. The true sentence is that I am a warm wet body on a warm wet plateau, drinking flat mineral water at blood temperature, crusted at the seams of my knuckles, quiet at a range of six meters, walking a route that exists in a book and in the soles of my feet and nowhere else. The Sud has done to me what it does to a pipe left in the open. It has begun, gently and without malice, to make me part of the mineral order of things.

At noon the mist opens and you can see the whole grid at once — the terraces, the wellheads, the threads of pipe going off alone into the white. I stand on the causeway and sway, and the veils stand in their columns above the vent field, and the pans tick, and the pump throbs somewhere in my teeth, and the light bleaches everything to the same pale coral. I am paid to keep the town quiet. I am one more band of grazer browsing a warm chemical world for what can be got from it. I ask the ground no questions it hasn't already answered.

Eleven through nineteen. If you want me, follow the pipe out past the sheds and listen for the one that isn't ringing.
