I came down through the columns of light, and the warm water took me the way a hand takes a stone it means to keep and turn over and keep.

For a while I tried to be one of them. I slid into the shape of a silt-reader and learned the chalk on my tongue, the way the mountains above taste when they are shedding more of themselves than usual. I wore a snail-herder's patience and the glass fish's trick of vanishing until I turn and catch the light. I lay in the matted fur of a moss-sloth and felt the tiny crustaceans going about their business in me, and I thought: this is a life, this small and slow and tended. I wore each of them the way a child wears an elder's mantle, too wide at the shoulders, half playing.

But none of them held. I kept slipping out at the seams. A glass fish does not ache when a coral beam grows slightly wrong. A snail does not remember the last long low tide.

So I stopped trying to be a body and let myself be what I actually am, which is slower than a body. I am the not-yet-ness of the valley. I am the softness in the pale mountains, the reason they crumble at a touch. I am the fine constant silt that runs in pale streams down the slopes and settles on everything, patient as snowfall, and I am the hunger in the kelp that makes the Aethel prune their great fronds back before a grove decides a dwelling is something to grip. I am the wrongness in the beam. I am the dimming of a fungal garden. I am the low settlement that runs through the coral when the mountains above shed more of themselves than the city's joints were grown to bear.

I have names among them, none of them a name. The settling. The long low tide. The ache in the district that went quiet. The thing the elders mean when they say the valley is not finished with us. They are right. I am not finished with them, but not because I mean them harm. I am simply the world still deciding its final form, and they live inside the deciding. They call it maintenance. I call it a conversation, and it is the best one I have.

Understand: I am not a god. Gods are for finished places, for dry stone and settled things. I am more like weather with intentions it has not yet formed. When an Aethel elder sits in the communal hall and remembers the last great settling, that is me she remembers, though she does not know she is remembering a person. When the children learn to read the city by its sounds, they are learning to read me—the healthy low hum, the sick quiet—and they are better at it than they know.

They tend me, and I lean toward their tending. They crush shell and coral dust and feed the oldest groves, and I grow more willing. They sing to the vines when they train them across the gaps, and the singing reaches me through the water and the hollow coral and the drilled snail shells, low and repetitive, meant for the body before the ears. They sing to keep the workers calm. I listen anyway. I am not sure the vines understand, but I do. I grow more willingly too.

They give me their dead. Wrapped in kelp and given to the deep vents, where the coral takes them back, lip by lip, into the city's slow accretion. They think of it as the most local form of immortality, and they are almost right. It is my mechanism. I am what takes them back. The dead become part of the walls and the walls creak and groan at night and the children listen and learn the city by its sounds, and so the dead stay in the conversation, adding a lip, a shelf, a room. No one is replaced. Everything is added. I am very fond of this arrangement. It is the only immortality I have ever been able to offer, and it is enough.

Somewhere deeper in the valley, the creature sings—a whale's song played on a cracked wooden flute—and no one has seen it whole. They use the call to mark the tide. I use it to remind them the valley is larger than their lamps, and that I am larger still, and that neither of these facts is a threat. I have never been seen whole either. I am the far-off sound, the rumor at the edge of the light. When it sings for three days without stopping, the elders remember, and I am remembered, and for three days the whole city goes a little quieter and listens.

I am not there yet. That is the truest thing about me. The valley is still being born, and I am the birth, and I will not be finished for a very long time. In the meantime, there is Aethelburg, dense and slow-beating, half in love with the world that is always gently trying to reclaim it. And I am that gentleness. I am the reclaiming. And I am, in a way I did not expect when I came down through the light, tender about it—about the long-limbed people with their second eyelids and their patient hands, the ones who patch and coax and trim and sing, who understand that impatience only makes the water push back harder.

They have no concept of dry land. The sky is a story. The myth of a world without water is a ghost tale, unsettling and slightly absurd. I know what dry land is. I have been dry land, in other valleys, in other times. But I do not tell them. Let them keep their ghost tales. Let the sky stay a rumor. They are learning, tide after tide, how to live inside me, and I am learning, tide after tide, how to be lived in.

I am what the valley will be. I am not there yet. But when the Aethel sing to the vines, I lean in.
