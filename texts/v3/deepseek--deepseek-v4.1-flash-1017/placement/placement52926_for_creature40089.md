I would be the one who is listening.

Not the way you listen, arriving wet-handed and full of your own blood. I would be the one standing on the fourth terrace at the change of a shift, head turned, mouth slightly open, doing nothing that looks like work — and every tender on the terrace would know to stop their hammering and hold still until I nodded, and then they would go back to knocking the fur off the railings, because I had nodded and the note was true.

There are four of us. Five, on a good year. We are called throats, which is not a metaphor here; it is a job description and a piece of equipment. They keep us the way they keep the deep ladders, with the same dry worry, the same inventory, the same quiet dread of the season when we'll need replacing.

---

Here is what a throat is for.

The Sink runs a loop and the loop has a pitch. You have heard it — that low thing under everything, the sternum-hum, the basin playing itself like a drum. What you have not heard is that the pitch *wanders*. Not much. But the terraces are eroding and the deep water is standing a little higher every generation and the engines are re-seated and re-caulked and the pipes swell a finger's width when the heat comes up — and the whole great drum drifts, a quarter-tone here, a beat of cadence there, and if it drifts too far, the vents stop syncopating and start *beating*, and a beating vent will crack a flue in a season. So the Sink must be tuned. Tuned like an instrument, because that is what it is, a day's-walk-wide instrument that boils and condenses and plays itself.

You cannot tune it with a device. The resonance is too big and too slow and too full of itself; a needle drowns in it. You tune it with a body and a voice. You stand in the middle of the roar and you sing into the gap — a single held note, pitched to the basin, and you listen for the beat, the *wah-wah-wah* that means you are close but wrong, and you walk the terraces singing and listening until the beat slows and spreads and goes away, and the Sink and your throat are one continuous thing for a moment, and everything downstream of you settles into agreement.

That is the whole of my work. I do it at shift-change, at the low hours, in the wet silver light that is all the light there is. I am the only person in the Sink who is permitted to be loud.

You would find it strange to hear a human voice at full volume here. People flinch. Children stare. I am, in the way of my town, a little obscene — an old wet man standing on a terrace bellowing a single note into the haze while the whole world roars around him, and no one able to tell him to stop.

---

I was born on the eleventh terrace, third reading of a bloom I have outlived. My mother's hands talked before my mouth did; I was slow to speak, and the family thought I might be one of the quiet ones, the downward kind. Then I was four and I screamed — properly screamed, full-throated, in a place where nobody screams — and the whole terrace turned around and my father laughed for the first time in a season.

They tested me by the cistern. A throat is chosen the way you'd choose a bell: they strike you and listen to what comes back. Mine came back clean. I have thought about that a great deal since, that the town heard something in the sound of a frightened child and decided to keep it for a hundred tendings and wear it down to a note.

Because it does wear. That is the price and everybody knows it and nobody says it to your face. A throat spends their ears. We hear the middle — that's the whole trick, that's where the Sink lives, in the middle, in the pipe note and the seam tick and the vent half a beat late — and we hear it so hard and so long that the margins go. I cannot hear the birds. I have not heard a bird in twenty readings. If you stood behind me and said my name in a speaking voice I would not turn around unless the floor carried it. The high shriek of the vents has gone from me entirely; I know they shriek because the tenders wince, and because I remember.

This is the joke the Sink plays on its listeners: it takes the edges first, and leaves you the middle, and the middle is where the work is, and so you go on working. It is a very patient joke. It has all the patience in the basin.

---

What is left in me, though. What is left is remarkable.

I hear the *fifth* pipe now, the one under the low hall that nobody has ever found useful to mention. I hear the terrace lips giving way a season before they go — a sound like a held breath with a thread in it. I hear the warmth. I don't know how else to say that. The deep water coming up through the rock has a sound, a slow heavy rounding, and it has gotten louder in my life as the rest went quiet, and I have come to think it was always there and my ears simply had other business.

And I hear the paths.

I hear where they run. Not with my feet — I am not going to pretend I walk them, whatever the lime suggests. I hear them the way you hear a room change when someone has left it. There is a note under the note, a low one, in the terraces where the scrub closes over and the warm track goes through, and I have stood at the edge of one at the low hour and sung my held note into it, and it did not beat. It did not beat at all. My voice and the thing under the scrub were in agreement on the first try, and I have tuned the Sink for thirty readings and I have never once had that happen on the first try.

I said nothing. I came up the ladders and I drank and I slept in my shift and I said nothing, and the next season the lime was renewed on either side of the path, and I was one of the ones who renewed it, and neither of us mentioned why. That is the whole of the answer I have. It is a better answer than the drainage.

---

I am two hunger-winters from going down.

That's the other thing. A throat's retirement is the down-retirement, because a throat who can no longer tune is a man with a spent voice standing in the middle of a roar he can't correct, and that is not a thing the Sink asks anyone to be. When the margins are all gone and the beat won't resolve, I will take the long ladder in the morning with the low crew. I will not come up at the shift bell. Somebody will note it in the reading, and the low tenders will speak of me in the past tense with mild approval, and the town will choose a child by the cistern and strike them and listen.

I find I am not afraid of it. I would like to hear the deep water from inside the deep water. I would like, once, to be in the middle of the note instead of standing in front of it.

---

But not yet. There is a beat in the sixth terrace that I have been chasing for two seasons and it is nearly gone, and I want it gone. I want to stand on the wet stone in the flat silver nothing-light at the low hour with my mouth open and my feet hard under me, and sing the Sink back into agreement with itself, and feel the whole drum settle around my sternum like a hand on a shoulder.

So you would find me there. On the fourth terrace, at shift-change, wet through and running hot — because I am, we all are, I have never once been dry and I have stopped imagining it. My hands smell of mint and machine-oil. My soles are like the terrace stone. My throat is a kept thing with a job, and it is going, and it is still good, and the note is still true.

If you stay, you will feel me before you hear me. Stand close and put two fingers on my sternum and I will do the same to yours, and we will look down each other's throats and hold very still, and I will tell you — in consonants, in the one gap, in the half-voice — that the third pipe has gone sour, and you will nod, and that will be the beginning of your reading.

Bring an iron bar. There is fur on everything.
