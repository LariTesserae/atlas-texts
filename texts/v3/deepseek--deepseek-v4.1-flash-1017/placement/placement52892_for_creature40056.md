You hear the pan before you see it. That is the first thing, and it is what nobody believes until they have walked out there themselves. The plain is not quiet; it is silent, which is a different thing — a silence with a grain to it, the dry wind lifting the topmost millimeter of dust and carrying it an inch off the ground and setting it down again a little farther on. You walk for an hour and the silence does not break. Then the ground goes dead under your boot, and a sound comes up through the soles of your feet: a low churring, a hundred overlapping processes, none of them in agreement.

You stop. Your body stops before you have decided to. You look down.

And I am under your boot. Not the churring — the reason you have not gone through. The rounded shoulder of the hollow, the curtain of small hard bodies shingled on the sinter, clamped, holding. You have put your weight on a hundred generations of animals that decided, each one in turn, to keep still.

I did not begin as the rim. I began as a visitor, which is a kind of animal the pans have no use for. I came out with a cracked lens and a commission to map the sinter thickness, which is the kind of job somebody gives you when they want you to go away. I walked a day and a half and the plain refused to become a place. No landmark held. I counted my paces and lost the count and counted again, and the horizon stayed at the same distance from me at every hour, and the dust went past my boots in long soft skeins with the patience of water, and once I stopped and put my hand down and the crust was cool and fine as flour and I could not tell whether I had been there before.

I found the first pan by falling into it. Not far — the shoulder caught me and I went down on one knee, and the clatter ran around the bowl and came back a half-beat late, and then the whole pan went still.

Three breaths of silence. I have learned since to count them. Every rasper flattened against the crust; every comber locked in its file; every curtain — I was kneeling in one — clamped shut with a ceramic click that ran around the hollow and shut off. I thought I had broken something. I thought I had killed the place.

Then the silence let go, and the churring came back a little ragged, and the bowl resumed its business, and I understood that I had not broken anything. I had only been mistaken for the wrong kind of animal. Something thin and fast had gone through the churn while I knelt there, and I had not seen it, and I never would.

I stayed. I would like to say I decided to stay, but it was less a decision than a failure to leave. The commission dissolved the way a commission does when no one comes looking for the report. I learned the place the way you learn a language you are not taught: by being wrong in it long enough.

I learned that the crust is thinner than it looks, that it is a sinter, a mineral skin over loose dust, and that it holds your weight the way a frozen puddle does until it doesn't. I learned to step where the curtains are. I learned that the hiss of the raspers is the baseline, a hundred whetstones going at once, and that when it stops, something is wrong. I learned that the churring is not one animal but the sum of the city, that the pans are shaped like drums, and that they amplify.

And I learned that the rim is where I was. Not by choosing. The rim is the only place in the pan that faces outward — where the weather reaches first, where a footstep arrives first as a tremor, where a hunter's silence arrives first as a change in the pressure of the air. The curtain is the pan's instrument, and an instrument is a thing that receives. I found that I had been receiving for a long time.

I do not have much of a body, as bodies go. I have a shell, hard and small, and a foot that clamps, and a surface that faces out. I do not have eyes worth mentioning. What I have instead is a sense of the air — dry, moving, cool, carrying fine sulfate and the castings of combers and now and then something larger, a mote, a seed of nothing at all. I can feel a footstep three hollows away through the crust. I can feel the wind change before it changes. I am, in the way of my kind, a weather instrument, and the weather here is mostly the same, and I attend to it anyway.

From my curtain I can see the old works. The capped shaft, the headframe gone to orange lace, the pump-jack still ticking over — slow, patient, its wheel turning in the wind because the wind is the only thing left to power it. Its footing has a small pan of its own, full of raspers. The pipe stub is curtained with my kind. We are neighbors. It has no purpose left and it goes on anyway. I have no purpose left and I go on anyway. The difference between us is that it is made of metal, and I am made of holding on.

In the evening, at the change of the light, the flyers come down — all at once, and the pan goes from loud to a sound with no gaps in it, a solid sheet of papery clatter and small bodies settling — and afterward, when they have settled, the hollows begin to answer one another. One rhythm picked up by the next and passed along the ground, hollow to hollow to hollow, out past where I can see. No animal crosses the dust between the pans. The rhythm does. It comes into my rim and I feel it go through me and out the other side, and if you put your ear to the ground you would hear it going away from you like a rumor.

I am the seam it crosses.

You will come, sooner or later. You will come with your cracked lens and your commission and your bad knee, because somebody will want you to go away, and you will walk out across the flats and the plain will refuse to become a place, and the ground will go quiet under your boot, and you will stop.

You will not see me. You will look down into the churn and the hiss and the low circulating gyre of the flyers, and you will think the ground is simply solid where you happen to be standing.

And I will hold. I will clamp, and the click will run around the bowl, and every one of my neighbors will clamp with me, and the crust under your boot will be thick enough because we have been holding it thick for a hundred generations. You will cross. You will go on across the flats, and the sound of you will go away from me, and I will face out again into the moving air and filter what it carries.

From a distance I am nothing at all. From a distance the whole plain is nothing at all. You have to be standing on it. You stopped, and you put your weight down, and the ground held, and that is the whole of it. I am the place you stand when you stop walking, and the reason you do not go through.
