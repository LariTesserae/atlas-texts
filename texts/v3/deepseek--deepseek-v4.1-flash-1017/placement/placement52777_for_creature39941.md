I come awake the way I always do: listening for whether the hum is the same as when I slept. It is. Which is to say I do not wake, exactly. I lie in my hollow in the dark with my palms flat against the sill, and I feel the city in the small bones of my wrist, and I count.

That is my strangeness, if I have one. Others learn the seep, the pillar, the true note. I learned the drips. In my hollow there are two hundred and eleven of them—pinned to that number since I was a child too small to be trusted with acid—and I know each one the way you know the faces of your family: the four that fall on the sill, the slow one that gathers on the cavity's cheek and lets go only when it cannot bear itself, the quick pair by the doorway that are almost a single sound. I lay them out in my head each time I wake. That is how I know I am awake. That is how I know I am still myself.

My name is a phrase struck on the nearest stone, and it means, more or less, *the one who answers from the hollow a little after the others have stopped listening.* I was given it as a joke that has since been confirmed.

---

I am a tuner's child, though my ear is the wrong shape for pillars. A pillar speaks in a register a tuner can lift out and hold. I hear the pillar, but I hear the city more. The chord is not one sound to me; it is a great wet crowd of sounds, and I love the crowd. I sit for hours in a channel with my knees against my chin and let the hum come in through my teeth and my wrists and the thin roof of my skull, and I do nothing. In Glisten-Reach that is not idleness. That is what we do instead of hurry.

But there is a place—there is a place I should tell you about, because it is why I have been the one, all these years, and why I am the one now.

At the western rim of my family's hollow, where the accretion thins, I am coaxing a chamber. Slowly, as it should be: forty years is a reasonable life for a room. I have been at it since I could hold a shell. The wall has a fine roundness now and will be rounder when my daughter's daughter polishes it. And past the wall there is a fissure. A thin seam in the fused rock, unlit, unlisting, going up into the ceiling where the fossil fans hang. Through it the hum leaks out. Slightly. Not enough to say the chamber will never sing—but enough that when I sit in the almost-room at night with my palms on the unfinished wall, the crowd of sounds thins, and I am sitting at the edge of the city's sound, at the lip where the chord goes out into somewhere that does not answer.

I have spent a long time at that lip, tapping the wall with a stone, listening for what comes back. It always comes back. Just—later than it should. As if something between here and there were holding the note a moment before letting it go.

---

You came in through the deep ways. I know that because my slow drip stopped.

Not the quick pair. The one that gathers on the cavity's cheek. It had been gathering and letting go, gathering and letting go, for as long as I have had ears, and then one morning it gathered and let go *early*, and then twice more early, and then a fourth time, and then it did not let go at all for so long that I got up out of my hollow and stood in the doorway with my hands in the air like a fool. And then it fell. And the fall was wrong. The sound came back to me off the walls a little after it should have, the way it does at the fissure, and I thought, without knowing I thought it: *the hum is bending around something.*

I tapped the pillar at the corner of my channel. Two strikes, a pause, three—the pattern that means *a note has changed, and it is not the roof*. Then I waited. Do you know how long it takes to strike and answer across a district? I waited with my hand on the stone and my mouth open and my eyes shut, and I felt the answer come back through the pillar from three quarters away, slow and deliberate the way my mother is deliberate, and then a second answer from further off, and then the striking stopped, the way a conversation stops when everyone in it turns to look.

That is how you were known. Not seen first. Heard, before you had finished arriving. The city is a great imperfect instrument and you were a new finger on it.

---

Here is what I am, then, since you asked where I would be.

I would be at the fissure. I would be the one crouched in the almost-room with my broad flat hands against a wall that is not finished and will not be finished in my lifetime, listening past my own city into the dark, and I would be the first to feel the chord go wrong. Not out of courage—I am not brave, we are not a brave people, we are a patient one, and patience and courage do very different things when a stranger comes. I would be there because it is my wall, my responsibility inherited, the way the slow drip is my counting, the way the dead are our polish. Because a changed echo is a serious event, and someone must stand at the thin place and say which way it bends.

And when you came down the last channel—when your shape came round the drinking-stone turn and the hum opened out around you like a hand cupping something—I would already be standing. Wet to the ankle, faintly luminous from the dust in the air, small as a child to you maybe, with a resonant stone in my hand and my head tipped to listen to the note you make.

And I would not shout. I would strike the sill three times, so the sound would reach you through the floor and not the air.

The phrase I would strike means: *the one who answers from the hollow, late, asks whether you are a load the roof can bear or a crack in it.*

And then I would wait. I am very good at waiting. I have counted two hundred and eleven drips and I know exactly how long a thing may take to fall.
