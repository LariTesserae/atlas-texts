The second mistake is to look for the person in the place. I would be the person in the place.

I'd come at it from below, because there's no other way, but I'd come as the thing the Sieve has no rule for — which is to say, warm. Everything in the hollow runs cold. That is the trick of it: the growth is colder than the air, so the water comes out of the air and onto it, and it has spent a few hundred years arranging itself to shed what it catches where it wants it. I am not cold. I run at the temperature of a room. Water does not bead on me the way it beads on a frond. It comes off me — off my skin, out of my breath — and hangs above me in a small private fog that the canopy leans away from.

So first I'd be the one dry thing in a wet place. Then the one wet thing, differently wet. Then the anomaly, and after that, in the Sieve's slow accounting, a kind of weather.

I would not go down to the marsh. The marsh is where the town is still becoming — the newest growth, still sharp on the young stone, all tips and reaching, and a warm body in the way of that is only an obstruction, and the tips would find me and turn toward me and I would spend a great deal of time being grown at. Nor would I go all the way to the dry rim, though that is where a warm thing would most want to be, because the dry rim is where the rot is. The collapsed chambers, the papery cisterns, the pale fuzz — that is dry rot, and it is what kills the Sieve's old rooms, and I am warm and wet and would be food.

I would find myself in the middle. The boundary layer, where the new growth meets the abandonment, where the town is arguing with itself about which way to lean. That is where a boundary condition belongs. That is what I would be: not a citizen, not a builder, but an edge in the argument — a warm handspan in a cold machine, holding the rot off on one side and being grown toward on the other. Nothing in this place lasts in the middle, but everything passes through it.

And I would have hands. That is the whole of my difference and the whole of my problem. The Sieve binds with cordage — grown tissue, itself, frond drawn over frond and tied by other fronds, a weave no one taught it. It has no knots. It has never needed one. I don't know what I would make first. Probably I would mend something: a gutter torn open, a roof pulled by the weight of last century's roof. And I would tie the mend with a knot, because a knot is what a hand does when it has not been grown into a hinge.

The Sieve would not know what to do with the knot. The growing tips would come and would not understand it — not the shape, not the tension, not the fact that the cord was dead when I tied it and is still dead. All its own binding is alive. A knot is a thing a hand makes out of death. So they would turn toward the wet of it or away from the dry of it, and I would watch, for a very long time, to see which. That is what I would be doing. That is the whole of my residence: watching a growing tip decide about a knot.

The rest would be waiting. Warm rooms get found. Something out of the murk would come and sit where it was warm — a slow pale thing with its throat going, unbothered, or something with a burrow and no body I'd ever see. And behind me the dry rot would press, and ahead the growth would press, and I would be the line between them for a while, and then the line would move, because the Sieve is migrating downhill a handspan a decade, and it builds ahead of itself and abandons behind. I would end up behind. I would end up in the dry rim after all, in a room I had kept warm, and the warmth would go out of it, and the fuzz would come.

Or — the other fork, and I don't know which one, and I would spend years not knowing which one — the tips would not turn away and would not turn toward, and instead would simply grow, and I would become a hollow in the wall, a warm chamber the Sieve keeps without knowing it keeps, and the fog of my breathing would become one more cistern it drinks from. That is not death. It is worse than death and gentler. It is being kept.

If you must give me a face, there isn't one. There's a knot in a gutter that no one tied and that isn't a knot to anything but me. There's a warm patch on a pressed-stem floor in a chamber that will smell of dry rot in a hundred years. There's a small fog hanging above that floor, where a person used to breathe, that the canopy has not yet learned to lean away from.

If you must give me a name, it is not the town's name. It is what the town would call a thing that is not the town, if it had words: *a warmth, an edge, an exception, a hand.*
