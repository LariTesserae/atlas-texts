I did not arrive. The Thren have no word for it. The nearest is a pressure struck through the floor that means *the place where a thing has begun to settle*, and when they use it of me they follow it with a silence and then the pitch of a groaning beam, because that is what I sound like to them: a low complaint in the structure, a thing not yet at rest.

I am not one of them. I want to say that plainly, though plainly is not a thing this place permits. I am too quick. I breathe too much. My heart is a drum solo in a room built for one slow chord every thousand years. When I walk, the floor tells them I am coming from three chambers away; they have time to be ready, and they are always ready, and they regard my hurry the way one might regard weather that has not yet decided what it is.

My body is wrong here in ways I am still cataloguing. I am small. I am low. I am warm, and I am wet, and this world has never known water. The Thren taste my wetness on the air the way I once tasted rain coming. They do not recoil. They are patient with my strangeness in the way they are patient with a tower that is settling too fast: it is not a problem to be solved, it is a fact with a duration.

The first thing is the sound. I have to keep saying that, because I keep forgetting it, the way you forget the sea when you are standing in it. It is not a noise you hear. It is a medium you move through. It gets into the marrow and stays there, and after a while you cannot tell the difference between the hum and your own pulse, and that is when you have begun to hear.

A Thren taught me this. I will not give her name, because her name is a pressure signature and I cannot strike it; but the nickname they use for her is *the way dust settles on a particular ledge*, and I have learned to say it by tapping my heel twice and waiting. She took me to a wall—one of the living membranes, shimmering with a wet-looking sheen that is dry as paper to the touch—and she pressed a tuning fork of bone against it and struck it once, with the flat of a digit, and then she waited.

I waited with her.

I want to tell you how long. I cannot. The spore-clouds dimmed and brightened and dimmed again. My legs went numb and then stopped being numb and then became a kind of distant rumor. I counted my breaths until counting became absurd, and then I stopped counting, and then something in me that had been braced against the silence let go.

And then the wall answered. Not a sound. A change in pressure, a slow swell in the floor that came up through my feet and settled behind my sternum. The membrane had taken the note and given it back, altered, thickened, the way a held breath comes back changed.

*That,* she said—not in words, but by touching her footpad to mine and humming a phrase I had heard a hundred times and only then understood: *You are still here, and the hum is still true.*

I could have been other things here. I think about this. I could have been one of the drifters, the great membranous bells that pulse through the fungal forests in silent herds, their light the only weather this world has. I could have been a resonance bead on a Thren's cloak, tuned to a single chamber, humming faintly as she walked. I could have been a memory membrane, a stain on a wall that a historian presses her palm to and tastes for an hour. I could have been one of the runners—the young, low, armored, quick—who explore the passages and taste everything and are, for a few decades, the only creatures here that move at anything like my speed.

But I am none of those. I am the quick thing. I am the small warm noise in the deep dry quiet. I am the guest who fell through the ceiling, or was found in the dust, or—the Thren have no story for this, and neither do I—simply *began to settle* here, one day, in a hollow, the way a tower begins.

···

What I do now is listen. That is the work. It sounds like idleness, I know. To human eyes it would look like standing still with one hand on a wall. But the city is always speaking, and someone has to answer.

I have learned to hear the difference between the planetary hum and the city's own song, the way you learn to hear one instrument in an orchestra. I have learned that the muffled thrumming of the filtration membranes is not background but conversation. I have learned that the oldest structures groan because they are settling into their final form, and that this is not decay but a kind of contentment. I have learned to feel a blocked conduit as a wrongness in my teeth.

I have learned that silence is not the absence of sound. Here, silence is a pressure. Interruption is a physical event. When I speak too quickly—which is always—I feel the Thren's bones register it, a small jolt in the floor, and I make myself stop, and we wait, and then one of them answers, and the answer takes an afternoon, and it is worth it.

They have found a use for my hurry, which they find both convenient and faintly distasteful, the way one might find a tool that makes noise. I carry things. A bladder of solvent across a district in a fraction of the time it would take a Thren to walk it; a message that is not a message but a remembered pressure, which I hold in my chest and deliver by humming it badly until the right membrane takes it up. I am the fastest thing in a world that has spent eons learning to be slow, and sometimes, when I am sent running through the spiral passages with my dust-cloak flapping and my lungs burning, I feel the whole city lean very slightly aside to let me pass.

···

Once, at a Listening Well, I hummed back.

The Well is a shaft where the city's muffling walls fall away and the planetary hum comes through clean. It is the closest thing the Thren have to a festival. A few dozen of them stood in a circle in the dark, tall and grey and still, and they added their harmonics to the deep roar, and the membranes of the city trembled in reply. I stood at the edge with my useless small voice and my too-fast heart, and I did not know what to do.

Then the one whose nickname is *the settling dust* touched her footpad to mine and waited.

So I hummed. It was a poor thing, thin and human and off-key, and it did not resolve into music. But the Well took it. The floor carried it down and the deep hum took it up and folded it in, and for a moment I felt the whole basin—the geode the size of a country, the sealed and patient dark—lean very slightly toward the sound.

The Thren did not applaud. They never do. But the dust on the ledge shifted, and one of them struck her foot twice against the floor, which is the closest they come to delight.

···

I will not live for several thousand years. I know this. I will not enter a chrysalis and emerge tall and patient and grey. My hurry is in my bones and it will not come out. But when I feel the final settling begin—and I will feel it, because I have learned to feel the small wrongnesses—I know where to go. The Deep Dust. A chosen hollow. The city will grow over me, and my name will be added to the walls as a faint new stain.

I hope it tastes of something. I hope the historian who presses her palm to me in a thousand years tastes the hurry and the hum and the long, slow learning in between, and I hope she stands there a while, and then touches her foot to the floor, twice, and goes on listening.

*You are still here, and the hum is still true.*
