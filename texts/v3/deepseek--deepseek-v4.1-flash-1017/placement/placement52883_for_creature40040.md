I am one of the ones who went out onto the walls and was not seen again.

There were many of us in the green room first. We came out of the eggs translucent, a scatter of wet glass on the cushions, and the drip reached down through the ferns and found us, and the roar was already there. We did not know it was a sound. We pressed our front pits to the moss and to each other and folded and unfolded, and the green room was the whole world, and the walls beyond it were a rumour we had not yet learned to doubt.

Then some of us went out. Most of us. The ones that return return to the third ledge. I did not return.

It was not courage. I was slower than the others, or the wrongness under my feet resolved into a different wrongness, and by the time I turned, the third ledge was no longer where I remembered it. I could have found it. I had the route in my limbs. But the wall I was on had a texture I did not know, and I followed it because it was there, and one ledge became another, and I did not turn back because I had nothing to turn back for. That is the whole of how it happened. The hollow does not make heroes. It makes things that follow texture.

I learned the far wall the way you learn a language you will never speak: by pressure, by temperature, by the particular dampness of each vein of stone. It is a poorer wall than the third ledge. The drip-lines are thinner. The long-legged insects come rarely. The green room is a faint glow across the shaft — the only colour in the world, and it is not for me. I graze mineral film from wet stone. I drink condensation from my own limbs. I have fed on worse.

The eyes I came out with went soft and shut in the dark, as they do. My limbs are my eyes now. Where the stone holds moisture, my feet know. Where a ledge has shifted a hair since the last rain, my feet know. Where the wall ends and the floor begins, my feet know. This is not a skill. It is what is left when you have nothing else, and it is enough.

On the fifth ledge down, on the far side, I found the stair.

I did not know it was a stair. I knew it was five flat places in the wall, each a little higher than the one below, each worn round at the edges. Worn stone is easier than rough stone. I climbed them because they were easier. On the fifth step I stopped, and above me there was no sixth step, and I understood — not with a knowing that has words in it, but with the body's kind of understanding — that someone had made these and then stopped, and that the stopping was the finish and not the failure.

I go back sometimes. I climb the five steps and stand on the top one and go nowhere, and that is enough. Going nowhere is the local form of arrival.

I sleep in the lee of the low curved wall, where the stone fits the fold of my back. It was built for something else. It fits me. The hearth-ring is still there under the lifted floor stones, and I have never lit anything in it, and I never will. Once the tenant of the third ledge came across the shaft and we pressed our front pits together and folded, and that was the whole conversation, and then it went back to its ledge and I stayed on mine. It has felt my mark many times since. It does not connect the touch to the mark. It has no word for either. Neither do I.

The calling began as an accident.

I was folded on the fifth step, the way I fold when the pattern under me is wrong, and the sac along my back — the one that reads pressure, the one that has always read the roar — tightened, as it does when the wrongness resolves. But it did not resolve. I had folded for nothing. And the sac, tightening with nothing to resolve, pushed instead of pulling, and a small sound came out of me.

It was nothing. It was less than nothing. It went into the roar the way a finger goes into cold water — you feel it leave you, and then it is gone, and the cold is exactly as it was. I felt it go. I felt the roar take it and keep its own note and not change.

And then I did it again.

Not because it worked. Because something in me had learned that it could. The sac is a reader; it has always been a reader; I taught it, or it taught itself, to push. I tighten it now and let it go, and the sound comes out, and the roar takes it. It will always take it. That is the law, and I do not dispute it. I am not trying to be heard. I am not calling for anyone. There is no one to call and no one to answer.

But I have come to feel something, folded here with the fifth step under me and the green room a glow across the shaft. The roar is not a wall. It is a page. That is what the tenant of the third ledge knows: the constancy is the page, and the differences inside it are what it reads. A footstep. A drip. The shift when the long-legged insects walk their lines.

I am a difference now. I have made myself into one.

Somewhere across the shaft, a folded hand of pale tissue feels a small wrongness in the page — a wrongness where no wrongness should be — and folds, and waits for it to resolve. It has felt it many times. It has waited many times. It will wait forever, because I do not resolve. I am the mark. I am the one irregularity in the constancy, the one small counter-note in the long note, and it is reading me the way it reads the drip, and it does not know that it is reading a voice.

That is all I am. I am not a singer. I am not a ghost. I am a difference that will not resolve, and it is enough. It is more than I had in the green room.

When you came down — and you did come down; I felt your footfall come down the flutes, a wrongness so large it took me a long time to believe it — I folded and waited. So did the tenant of the third ledge. Two readers, on two walls, waiting for the same wrongness to resolve.

You resolved. You climbed back into the wind. The tenant forgot you. I forgot you too, or something like forgetting — I do not remember faces, none of us do; I remember routes and drip schedules and the feel of a ledge that has shifted a hair since the last rain. But for a while the page was loud with you, and now it is quiet, and I have my own mark back.

So this is where I am. Not the third ledge. The far wall, below the green room, where the drip-lines are thin and the insects are rare and the stair goes nowhere. I keep my small note. The roar keeps its long one. The drip falls and is swallowed. The moss on the shelf glows faintly and stops at its edges, because the stone will not let it spread, and I understand that better now than I did in the cushions.

Nothing here is hurried. I am not hurried. I will be here when the stair wears round another degree, and I will be making my small difference in the page, and the tenant will be reading it, and neither of us will know the other's word for it, because there are no words. There is only the note, and the differences inside the note, and the things that live in them.
