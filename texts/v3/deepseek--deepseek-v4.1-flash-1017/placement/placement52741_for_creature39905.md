I would be on the eastern rim, on the high shelf, a little before dusk, with my arm still cold to the elbow from the runnel — and I would be the one deciding.

That is where I would end up, because that is where a certain kind of person ends up, and I think I am that kind: the kind who goes up once to carry rope for an aunt and comes down a different shape, or doesn't come down. It happens to a few every generation. Nobody remarks on it. The lowland towns have their own words for it and the words are not kind.

I would come up the pass in the third week of the hard freeze, in the file, with a coil on my shoulder and slit-plates of horn over my eyes that I would wear for the first week out of mockery and for the rest of my life out of habit. The first thing I would learn is that I am not the first thing. The first thing I would learn is that the basin was doing its work before I got there and did not pause to look at me, and that the appropriate response to this is not disappointment but relief. There is a great deal of comfort in being unremarkable. Down below, everything wants to know what you are. Up there, the water is busy and you are, at best, a good gutter.

The first winter I would be given a chute to keep — the lowest one, the one that silts worst — and I would build it and it would be taken in the second month by a surge with a stupid name, and I would build it again, and it would be taken again, and the third one would hold. And in the holding I would become something. That is the election up there. Nobody votes. You rebuild a thing three times and it stays, and by the fourth year the old ones hand you a pole without explaining and expect you to know which side of the channel to stand on, and you know, because your feet have learned the water the way a lowland farmer's feet learn a field.

I would be good with rope. Not the best — there is always someone better — but good. I would learn to tie a line to a person in under four seconds, and I would practice it the way I once practiced letters, in the dark, with my eyes shut, on a beam-end. And once, in my ninth or tenth winter, I would do it for real, on the low shelf, in the fast water, and the person would come up coughing and furious and alive, and they would give me a length of rope the next spring, new rope, good rope, and I would not be able to explain to anybody below why this is a thing to be proud of. It would look like a rope. It would be, in the Kettle's arithmetic, an inheritance.

I would learn the count. At dusk, on the high shelf, in the failing glare, the names called and the number that has to come out right, and the whole rim's refusal to eat until it does. I would learn that this is not sentiment. It is bookkeeping with a body behind every entry, and the reason it feels like a ceremony is that it *is* one, and the reason it is a ceremony is that it is the only accurate thing the winterers do. Everything else can be negotiated with the water. The count cannot.

I would learn to name surges afterward, and to make the names stupid, and to laugh at them, because that is how the Kettle is thanked and it is rude to thank a thing gravely.

And then I would sit down beside the one with their arm in the water. Not once. Every morning, before the light, the way you'd sit beside a bell-ringer, not to learn anything in particular, just to be where the bell is. For a long time I would feel nothing but cold and the ache of holding still. Then one morning I would feel it — the low human hum of pressure moving under the basin, the thing that is felt rather than heard, moving down and around and up — and I would understand that I had been feeling it for years and had merely not yet been able to hear it under everything else. That is the whole apprenticeship. It takes decades and it consists of noticing what was always there.

I think I would be the one who does not go down. I would not decide it. That is the coward's way to think about it; the truth is that I would simply keep finding reasons to stay one more month, and one more, and the reasons would be good ones, and then one spring the file would go down the pass and I would stand at the top of it with my hand on a beam and watch them get small, and I would feel the specific, terrible, quiet relief of a person who has been given permission they did not dare ask for.

I would be visited. They would bring shrimp in a basket still steaming from the vent, and sit on my swept floor, and ask me what the water is doing, because after forty years inside the hum a person can feel things the listeners cannot. And I would tell them, in the low close speech that costs no air, and they would listen, and then they would go away, and I would be alone with the light coming down hard and doubled off the pools, all glare, unable to tell water from light, which is where the listening is easiest.

I would be given to the water below a vent. I have thought about this and it is what I want, though the wanting is not really mine to have — it is the water's, and I would be past arguing. Or my family would carry me down in the thaw, and I would be a hard kindness to somebody, and a beam would be carved with my notches and passed to a child who would carry it up again. Either is correct. Both are the same arithmetic.

But that is the end of it, and I would not begin at the end. I would begin — I do begin, this is the part I can feel clearly, the stone under my palm still rounded and beaded with condensation — I begin as the one who comes up the pass with too much rope and no idea, and who, on the first night, cannot sleep for the hum and thinks it is the wind, and who by the last night of that first winter cannot sleep without it and lies awake in the lowlands for years afterward, feeling for a pulse that is not under the ground.

And I would be, in the end, one more guest. I would pay my rent in gutters and early mornings and in getting up high when it changes. And the Kettle would go on being courteous and thorough and would not stop for me, and I would find that — this is the thing I cannot explain to anyone below — I would find that a comfort. It is good to be somewhere that knows what it is. It is good to be the small thing at the edge of a large thing that is busy. It is good, at dusk, on the high shelf, with the glare coming up off the pools and the count coming out right, to be the last one to say a number, and to hear, under everything, that low and patient hum, going down and around and up, and to think: *now. Not yet. Now.*
