You know it before you see it, because it is loud in a way that a landscape is not supposed to be loud. Not an event — no crash, no collapse — but a held, continuous roar, the sound of a hundred thousand square kilometers of wet fiber pushing upward through itself all at once. It arrives over the horizon as pressure in the sternum. By the time the thing is visible you have already been inside its sound for an hour.

There is no ground to approach. The surface is a pile — a deep, pale, living nap, like the inside of a fleece, standing vertically. Each strand is a column of wet tissue the girth of a wrist and the height of a house, packed so tightly that they cannot be counted or separated, and where they touch they have grafted, so the whole skin of the place is one continuous surface pretending to be a million. The color is bone and cool green and, in the thin places where light gets through, a bruise of violet. Everything is wet. Not raining — the air is simply at saturation, permanently, so that water is always arriving on every surface in a fine even breath of condensation, and nothing is ever dry, and the word *dry* has no referent here at all.

Beneath the pile, the place goes down. There are gaps — not corridors, nothing so generous — but slumps and partings, and you can descend into the body of it. Light comes down with you longer than it should, because the tissue itself is translucent; the walls of the shafts glow faintly, lit from within like the inside of a lampshade, and the dimness below is a dimness that sees. It is cool. It is cool the way a cellar is cool in the second week of summer, a temperature that does not change and does not care what season it is above.

And the sound changes as you go down. Above, the roar is growth — cell walls splitting under their own pressure, sap shoving upward, the whole mass flexing as it adds to itself. Below, it becomes water: a hiss, a sizzle, an endless capillary whispering. Because there are no rivers here. There has never been time for a river. The rock beneath is young and unfractured, freshly lifted, still raw — nothing has been worn away, nothing has been cut into channels, and so water has no idea where to go. It does not run. It climbs. It films. It creeps up through the fiber by surface tension, hangs in the grafted seams, condenses on the underside of itself, and falls back into the mass before it can ever reach the stone. The whole place is a wick standing in its own water.

No animal moves in it. Nothing grazes, nothing burrows, nothing is hunted. Which means nothing is consumed, and so nothing is removed: the dead stay where they die, upright, packed in with the living, grafted in place, held in the mass like pages in a closed book. The deeper you go the older the tissue, and the older the tissue the more compressed and more pale, until the bottom of the living layer is a dense cream-colored archive of everything the place has ever grown, and beneath that the young rock, still hot in places, still arriving.

The water cycle is closed inside the body. It transpires so much, over such an area, that it makes its own weather — a permanent low ceiling of cloud that belongs to it and to nothing else, a cloud that rains, whose rain is caught in the pile before it lands, and lifted, and made into cloud again. It has been raining into itself for as long as it has existed. The rain never touches the ground. The ground has never been wet from above.

Here is the strangest thing, and it took me a long time to feel it as a fact rather than a mood. The vascular tissue is not only plumbing. Signals move in it — slow, chemical, but ordered; they propagate through the grafted seams at meters per second, they route around damage, they redistribute water toward the parts that need it, they do this without any part of the mass being in charge. The whole continent of tissue is one enormous analog computation, running continuously, wet and silent and indifferent — and what it is computing, as far as I can tell, is the drainage of a landscape that does not exist yet. It is holding, in the shape of its own water, a map of the rivers that will be cut here in some tens of thousands of years when the rock has weathered enough to take them. It knows where they go. It is the rough draft of the watershed, written in living matter, in advance of the geology that will make it true.

It is very young, this place. That is the thing to remember. The rock is unfractured, the channels are un-cut, the soil has not been made, and the whole enormous body is still under construction — you can hear that, in the cracking, in the roar. It is not a forest that has grown. It is a forest that is growing, right now, at a rate you can feel in your teeth, adding mass to itself with nothing to oppose it and nothing to eat it and nowhere for the water to go but up. It has no architecture, no ruins, no marks of anyone. There is nothing here that was made. There is only the making, continuing, saturated, and loud.
