# The Murmur Room

You would not find it from above. The hill is unremarkable — a slope of beech and hazel, leaf litter, the ordinary racket of birds — and the way in is a hole you would step over: a root-torn gap at the base of a windthrown trunk, wide enough to take a shoulder if you go sideways, dropping four metres into warm wet dark and then opening.

The chamber is a pocket in a young fault. The rock broke recently — you can see it in the ceiling, where the fractured limestone still holds its sharp edges, unsoftened, all planes and splinters, the break-faces still pale where they haven't been stained. Somewhere above, that fracture reached the surface and let a chimney down through the forest floor, and the chimney is roofed now by a membrane of roots so dense and so pale-green that it works as a skylight: daylight comes through it as the light under a leaf held to the sun, diffuse, greenish-white, steady, enough to read by. There is no night in here, only a slow deepening toward dusk as the canopy above thickens and thins.

Everything else is plant. Not plants growing in the chamber — plants *are* the chamber. The walls are not walls; they are a mat of root and rhizome and pale succulent stem, wrist-thick to hair-fine, packed and cross-woven, and behind them more roots, and behind those the stone. The floor is a living lattice you can feel give under your boot, springy, warm, threaded with channels where water runs. The air is thick and body-warm — it smells of crushed green stem and wet bark and something faintly like cut apple. Your clothes are damp within a minute; condensation beads on every surface and rolls, gathers, drops.

The space inside is almost empty. That is the first strangeness: the volume is perhaps forty paces across and twenty high, and all of that volume is *air*. It is a hollow in a hollow in a hollow. Vegetation has claimed every surface absolutely and left the middle void. A few pale tendrils hang down from the roof, swaying. Nothing else occupies the space at all.

Water moves constantly. It comes down the chimney as a fine mist when the wind shifts, and the mist becomes a soft rain that patters on the root mat for ten minutes and stops. It comes through the root channels in threads and gathers in black pools sunk in the floor-lattice, each the size of a basin, warm as a bath, each with a white root-tip drinking at it. Somewhere below, water is going further down and you can hear it going.

And it is loud. This is the thing you are not prepared for. The chamber amplifies like a drum. Water ticking into pool after pool, a rhythm that almost resolves and never does. The creak and groan of root under load, long slow complaints, like a ship's timbers. The chimney drawing air — a low organ note that changes pitch when the weather changes above. And underneath it all another sound, harder to place: a dry fibrous crackling, continuous, distributed, as though the whole room were a fire of green wood just catching. It is the plants growing. They grow fast here, and they grow audibly, and the walls tick with it.

Rare life, and all of it small or slow. Pale woodlice working a fallen knot of root. A few blind crickets, long-antennaed, that sing in the wet season — a high ringing that seems to come from everywhere and from nowhere. Once a season, something heavier comes down the chimney: a root-grazer, a soft grey animal the size of a dog, eyeless and unhurried, which drinks at the pools for an hour and climbs out again, and which the chamber will remember.

Because the chamber remembers. This is the part that takes longer to notice and longer still to accept. The flora here is a slow organ of repetition. It does not merely grow — it *repeats*. Sound goes into the root mat and the root mat keeps it and gives it back, days or weeks later, late at night when the air is heavy and the chimney is drawing, in a register flattened and slowed and wrong. A cough returns as a cough an octave down, some while after. A phrase spoken near the western wall will be murmured by the western wall before the month is out, in a voice that is almost your voice, almost the right length, drifting a little flat. The people who have been in here are still in here. If you sit with your ear to the mat on a windless evening you can hear a hand-cut ladder's worth of voices — a man counting rungs, a woman saying *steady*, a child's laugh stretched out to a long greasy hum. None of them are recent. The room has kept them all, and does not distinguish between them and the creak of its own roots, and keeps those too.

Nothing was built here. Someone came, though, once or twice, and left the thinnest archaeology: a ladder of hazel rods lashed with twisted bark, hooked over the lip of the hole and rotted at the bottom three rungs. Hand-cut notches in the root mat near the entry, worn smooth by grip. A pool-edge flattened by a knee, and beside it a scatter of freshwater snail shells split and sucked. That is the whole extent of human presence — no walls, no fire-ring, no mark that isn't a handprint's worth of wear. The room is otherwise untouched, and is busy reclaiming the ladder even as you look at it, one fine white root feeling its way along the rung.

It is warm and it is wet and the light does not change and the noise does not stop. You lose track of how long — there is no sun to mark, and the chamber's own cycle is a slow swell of growth and water, breath in, breath out, hours long. When you climb back out into the beech wood, the ordinary bird noise sounds thin and far off, like something heard through a wall. And for a week afterward, in quiet rooms, you will hear a low dry crackling that isn't there, and a voice a little like yours saying something you almost recognize, drifting a little flat.
