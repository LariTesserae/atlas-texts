The world is called the Kiln.

It is a place where the ground sweats. Heat rises from below, not as a shimmer but as a slow, mineral breath, and the air is thick enough that every lungful tastes of iron and warm chalk. The water does not sit in rivers or ponds; it condenses out of the air onto every surface, beads, gathers, and runs in thin threads down faces of pale, unglazed masonry. Everything here weeps. The walls weep. The pillars weep. The vast low vault of the sky itself — if it is a sky — seems to weep, and the ground is perpetually slick with that weeping, puddled in the seams between slabs, pooling in the shallow bowls worn into the tops of buttresses.

Because it is all slabs and buttresses. There is no soil, no sand, no bedrock. The Kiln is built. From what, no one can say — the material is a pale grey-white, warm to the touch, grainy but not friable, closer to unfired clay than to stone, and it is jointed, mortared, tongued-and-grooved in a hundred different manners across a hundred different structures, as if generations of builders with only their hands and simple tools kept adding to something they never finished and never abandoned. Some joints are crude, thumb-pressed, uneven. Others are exact to a hair, cut with patient craft and no metal. The whole landscape is a single sprawling construction, vast as a continent, and it is in the middle of being built and has always been in the middle of being built. Nothing here has weathered. There is no ruin, only work in progress and work not yet begun. Scaffolds of lashed timber stand against half-raised walls, and the timber has not rotted, and the lashings have not loosened, and the water runs off them in bright threads.

The scale is enormous and the population is almost nothing. Between the structures lie long, low, empty courses — channels, plinths, abandoned foundations, long grey roads that curve around nothing and lead to more road. You can walk for an hour and pass three pillars and one wall and the puddled roof of a sunken hall, and see, far off in the dimness, the glow of a working lamp. The builders are sparse. They are small, hooded, quiet, and they move alone or in pairs. They carry no metal. Their tools are stone and wood and cord and the patient leverage of their own bodies. They lay a course of blocks, they check the joint with a knuckle, they squat and watch the water find its way through the new seam, and then they move on to the next thing. They do not appear to eat. They do not appear to sleep. They speak rarely, and when they do it is in short declaratives that seem to be about the work and then turn out to be about something else.

The light is the strange part. There is no sun, no moon, no obvious source. The illumination comes from the material itself — faintly, from the pale surfaces, a low continuous exhalation of grey-white luminance that never brightens and never dims. It is enough to see by and not enough to see far. Distance swallows everything into a soft pearl-grey murk. But the builders carry lamps, and the lamps are not for seeing by — the world is already visible — they are for *being seen*. A lamp set on a half-built wall means *someone is here, this is claimed, this is being made*. The lamps burn with a small cold flame that does not consume its fuel, and the fuel is a pale waxy substance no one has been observed to make.

Above it all, the sky — or the roof — is lost in the murk, and sometimes the murk moves. The turbulence is not in the air; the air is still and heavy. The turbulence is in the world's rules. Distances change. A corridor you walked an hour ago now ends in a wall that was not there, and the wall is wet and new. A plinth you passed at midday is now a stair descending into a hall you have never seen, and the stair is dry, and at the bottom someone is laying blocks and does not look up. The water, too, is unruly: sometimes it runs upward along a joint in a slow silver thread, and the builders watch it and nod and adjust their work to accommodate it. Sometimes a finished wall will un-finish — a course of blocks will loosen and rise a hand's breadth, hovering, dripping, and settle back into a new arrangement, and the builder who laid them will come back in an hour and lay them again. Nothing here is permanent and nothing here is broken. It is all becoming, all the time, at a scale too large to see and too slow to interrupt.

In the quieter hours — and there are quiet hours, when the world holds still and the only sound is the continuous soft ticking of condensation finding its way down a thousand surfaces toward the puddles — you can hear, very faintly, the sound of distant building. A block set down. A cord drawn taut. A single syllable spoken in a language that is not quite a language. And then the ticking resumes, and the heat breathes up from the unfinished floor, and the pale walls shine their dim light into the murk, and somewhere far off someone sets down a lamp and walks away and the lamp burns on, marking nothing, claiming nothing, and the Kiln goes on constructing itself out of the patient hands of its small and hooded and endless makers, who will never finish, and know it, and keep laying blocks.
