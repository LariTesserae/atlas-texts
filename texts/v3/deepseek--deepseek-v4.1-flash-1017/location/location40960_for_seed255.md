# The Drip-Fields of Ossory

You come into it through a throat of lashed hurdles and go down a ladder of pegged alder into a dark that is not empty. The first thing is the sound. It is not noise exactly — it is more like standing inside a struck instrument while it is still deciding what note it is. Water everywhere, but not as a river. As ten thousand separate arrivals. Each drip lands on a different surface, and each surface answers with a different voice: a flat tap off a slate shelf, a dull thud off felt, a bright coin-ring off a copper gutter-rim, a long sighing slide down a run of pitched planks. Multiply that by a valley and you have the floor of the world.

There is no sky. There has never been a sky here. What there is, instead, is a roof — or rather a hundred roofs, stepped and overlapping and rising in galleries toward the middle of the basin like the ribs of something that was built rather than grown. All of it is made. The floor you stand on is a deck of split boards laid crosswise over cribbing, and under the boards there is more deck, and under that, packed clay and rubble and driven piles, and if you pry up a board you find no soil, only the underside of the structure continuing down into dark. There is no natural substrate. Somewhere below the lowest cellar there is stone, but nobody has ever reached it to check.

The valley is low and shallow — you can walk its length in most of a day if you know the ways — but it is dense past any easy reckoning. The galleries stand close enough that you turn sideways to pass; the roof-combs come down low; planks cross over planks over planks, and between them hang the working parts of the place: gutters, sluices, cloths, screens, cisterns of stitched hide, funnels of hammered copper, cords and counterweights, ladders, catwalks of reed matting, thousands of small carved pegs holding ten thousand joints. Climb a stair and you find yourself in a room whose ceiling is another room's floor, and above that another, and the whole stack creaks and settles around you like a ship at anchor.

Everything is wet, or was wet, or is waiting to be wet. Water does not fall here in weather; it arrives as a slow inhabiting of the air — a fog that seeps in over the rim of the basin and condenses on the combs, and then begins, patiently, to run. It gathers in the gutters, and the gutters carry it down and inward, and at every junction someone has cut a lip or set a stone or tied a twist of cord so the water will go *there* and not *here*. Because *here* must stay wet and *there* must not. That is the first law. Green wood splits if it dries; a split peg lets go; a let-go joint drops a gallery; a dropped gallery takes the gutters with it and the whole district goes thirsty until it is rebuilt. So the builders wet the beams by hand in the dry spells, with wadded cloths and dippers, walking the catwalks in the dark with a lamp in one hand and a dripping rag in the other, anointing the frame like a priest.

It is always cool. Your breath goes out as a fog of its own and joins the general damp. Your hands go numb on the ladders and you learn to climb with your wrists. The wood is cold and slick and smells of tannin and lime and tar and the sweet rot that is not rot yet, and the air holds the smell of wet clay and old smoke and the faint mineral nothing of condensation.

Light is scarce and precious and made. There are oil lamps in niches, rushlights, a few panes of polished stone set where they will catch a flame from three galleries off. But most of the light is grown, not burned: the wet wood blooms with a thin luminous film, pale green and slow, that creeps along the underside of the joists and gathers in the joints and makes the whole ceiling a faint cloudy sky. It is not plant. It is not quite anything with a name — a slime, a smear, a patient something that eats the wet and gives back a glow. Where it thickens, it is bright enough to read a drip-tally by. It is everywhere, and it is the reason the place is not entirely black.

The few animals know the gutters. Pale beetles the length of a thumbnail, in colonies, walking the wet channels and eating the luminous film and being eaten, rarely, by a long blind thing that lives in the cisterns and is mostly a rumor. There are eels in the hide tanks, kept, or tolerated, or perhaps worshipped in a small domestic way. There are things that live in the cords and come out at the change of the wet. That is all. Nobody keeps beasts in the Drip-Fields. The Fields are for the water and the sound.

And the sound is the point. This is the thing you understand slowly, over days: the Fields are not a shelter that happens to be noisy. They are an instrument that happens to be inhabited. The builders are tuners. Every plank is tested before it is pegged — tapped with a knuckle, shaved at the edge, tapped again — and pegged only when it agrees with its neighbors. The galleries are pitched to carry certain notes down certain ways. The cisterns are sized for resonance. The copper funnels are hammered thin and hung where the drip will strike them. Walking the catwalks, you hear a tune that seems to come from everywhere and nowhere, and it is the fog, arriving, playing the roof-combs, running down into the tuned gutters, and the whole valley answering.

It is never finished. That is why the edges are raw. Half the eastern works are open ribs and fresh pale wood and piles of shavings and clay still waiting for daub; there are ladders leaning against nothing, scaffolds around a gallery not yet closed, joints cut and pegged and cut again, and the sound up there is ragged, out of tune, full of clacks and flats. The tuners work in the wet and the dark with simple tools — auger, adze, mallet, a worn copper blade, cord, bone pegs, a pot of tar — and they work by ear, and they are generations deep in a score nobody has ever heard whole. There is a plan. It exists, in tallies cut into beams, in drip-counts that are also a calendar, in the way one gallery answers another across the basin. But no one alive has walked the whole of it. The Fields extend out past the rim, down the long low valley, gallery past gallery, and the rumor of the far works comes back as a faint complicated music on the wet nights.

Stand on a high catwalk with the lamp shuttered and let your eyes go. The glow-films make a slow green weather under the roofs. The drips come down in their thousands, each on its own surface, and the whole dense dark world hums and clicks and rings and sighs around you, cool and wet and never still, being built, being tuned, being played.
