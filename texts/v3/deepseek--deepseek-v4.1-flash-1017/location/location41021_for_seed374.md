The basin is called the Kettle, and you hear it long before the rim lets you see it. The sound comes up over the lip in a solid thing — not a noise you listen to but a pressure you lean into: water roaring through stone, thousands of small falls and hisses layered into one broad drone, and riding on top of it a ringing, clattering, choral chatter that never resolves into rhythm. The rim is low, barely a rise, but it holds the sound in the way a bowl holds water. Nothing escapes. The whole valley is an instrument being played continuously.

Inside, the bedrock is broken. Not weathered — broken. The floor of the Kettle is a lacework of seams and fissures, some a hand wide, some wide enough to stand in, running through stone that is knife-edged everywhere, bright-fractured, raw as a just-split nodule. There is no rounding in this place. No softened edges, no soil, no drift of grit in the corners. Every surface is either fresh-broken or fresh-grown, and often both at once, because the water that rises through the seams is warm and thick with dissolved mineral, supersaturated, and it does not carve. It deposits. It lays down a skin of pale crystalline sinter on everything it touches — on the stone, on itself, on the tools and the doorframes and the people who stand still too long.

This is why nothing here erodes. The water gives back what it takes, grain for grain, in the same instant. The Kettle is not wearing down; it is not building up either. It is held, exactly, at a knife's edge — and everyone who lives in it knows the edge is a hand's width wide.

The water comes up. That is the first fact of the place. It wells through the seams from somewhere warm and deep, filling them, brimming over their lips, and running in shallow sheets and braided threads across the broken floor toward the middle, where it gathers in a broad pan no deeper than a knee and sinks away again down other seams. Up, across, down. A slow circulation, warm as a held hand, and constant — the same flow in the same channels at the same volume as it has always been, so far as anyone can tell. The air is thick with it. Everything is wet. Every surface weeps a fine condensation that runs in threads down the walls and pools in the low joints of the stone. Standing in the Kettle, you are standing inside a warm exhale.

Where the water lies, a slick grows: a mineral bloom, orange and bone-white and a dull iron grey, a living skin of things too simple to call plants, spreading across the wet stone faster than it can be eaten. It is the bottom of everything here. The rest of the ecology is built on scraping it off.

And the ecology is thick. The Kettle is crowded to the point of absurdity — there is no empty stone anywhere, no bare patch, no unclaimed surface. Cup-shells cluster in the seams by the million, small ridged cups the size of a thumbnail, packed shoulder to shoulder, and when the water moves through them they knock and ring against each other and against the stone, and that is the choral clatter riding over the roar. Things that graze them wade the shallow pan on stilt legs, pale and blind and shell-backed, calling to each other in long falling tones that carry strangely through the din. The warm mist is itself alive — dense with airborne microbes, and dense with the small hard-winged things that hunt them, which move in clouds so thick they dim the light another degree when they pass. Down in the deeper seams, where the water is hottest, something larger moves slowly and does not come up.

The light is low and flat and comes from everywhere at once, filtered through the steam that lies over the basin like a lid. There are no sharp shadows. Depth is hard to judge by eye; people here navigate by ear, and they are very good at it.

The town — the Kettle has one, and it is dense, and it is old, though "old" is a word that sits oddly here — is built along the lips of the seams, on the narrow strips of stone between one channel and the next. The houses are grown. A frame of bone and shell is set over a diverted trickle of the mineral water, and the water skins it with stone, and in time the trickle is moved and a new room is started against the old one, and then another against that. So the town is a stack of accretions, layered and overlapping, rooms grown onto rooms grown onto rooms, connected by arches of lashed bone and awnings of oiled hide, and it is so tightly packed that you can walk from one side of it to the other across the roofs without ever coming down. The streets are the seam-lips: narrow wet walks with water rushing on both sides. People go by foot, and by flat raft, poling along the channels. The only metal is a little copper, green-stained and worked cold, that precipitates in certain seams and is hammered into pins and blades and the small hooked tools used for trimming.

Trimming is the whole craft of the place. Because the water never stops giving, the town would seal itself shut in a generation if no one cut it back — the channels would silt with crystal, the seams would close, the circulation would stop, and the Kettle would drown in its own gift. So the people cut. Constantly, patiently, with hand tools, they pare the new stone from the doors and the channel mouths and the seam-lips, and they carry the parings to the middle pan and let them sink. The town is never finished and never different. It is being built and unbuilt in the same motion, forever.

Consequently no one here counts years, or expects anything to arrive. There is no before. There is no season, no tide, no growing and no harvest — only the flow, the ringing, the cutting-back, and the roar. If the balance ever tipped, the whole basin would run away in one direction or the other: fill solid, or open into a dry crack in the ground. Everyone knows this. It is the only story they tell.

And the sound goes on. It is loudest at the centre of the pan, where the bowl focuses everything — the upwelling hiss, the cup-shells, the grazers, the wing-clouds, the tap and scrape of a hundred people trimming stone — into a single standing tone that you feel in the sternum and the teeth. People work there in short shifts. They stuff their ears with wax and hide, they learn to speak with their hands, and they sleep in the quieter edges of the town, against the rim, where the noise thins enough to let them rest.

Above it all the steam lid never lifts and the warm rain never quite falls, and the water keeps coming up out of the dark, and giving itself back as stone.
