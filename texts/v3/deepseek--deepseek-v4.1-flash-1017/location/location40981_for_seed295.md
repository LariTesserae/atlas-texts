Here is a description of the place:

You are standing on a scaffold of ancient, sun-bleached wood, one of countless millions, strapped and pegged and grown-over into a single, impossible structure. This is the Forest, though the word feels small for it. It is a world entire, a vertical expanse so vast the mind cannot hold its dimensions. The ground, if there is one, is a half-mile below, a myth whispered in the deep darkness. Everything you can see is constructed: a lattice of immense beams, platforms, and winding paths that spiral up and around the boles of trees that are themselves a thousand feet tall. The builders are gone, their names and faces lost, but their work remains, an architecture of patient, organic logic. None of it is straight; it bows and sags and twists, each joint and span shaped by centuries of its own weight and the persistent pressure of the growing wood. This is no ruin. It is a place long-settled into its own existence, a perfect, self-regulating equilibrium.

The air is thick and warm, heavy as a wet blanket. It reeks of life—of damp earth, of decaying leaves, of the sweet, intoxicating perfume of unseen blossoms. Moisture is a constant presence without ever truly falling as rain; it condenses on the underside of the great beams, forming fat, cold droplets that fall with a steady, percussive patter onto the moss-covered planks below. The moss is everywhere, a soft, spongy carpet that muffles your footsteps and holds the moisture like a sponge. It pads every surface, a lush, green skin over the venerable wood. In the perpetual half-light, it glows with a soft, internal luminescence, as do the fungi that sprout in shelf-like ranks from the beams, casting a faint, sickly-green pallor.

The sound is a physical force. It is a deafening, overwhelming chorus that presses on your eardrums and vibrates in your chest. There is the high, keening whine of countless insects, a sound so dense it feels like a solid thing. There is the low, resonant thrum of millions of wings, the chittering and clicking of things that scuttle just out of sight, the deep, guttural croaks of unseen amphibians from the wet places below. And above it all, the calls of birds—some like the ringing of tiny silver bells, others like the shriek of tearing metal. Every surface, every leaf, every inch of dark space is alive. Fauna is not just present; it is a manifest pressure. Beetles the size of your hand clatter past. Luminous moths flutter in drunken spirals. Things with too many legs and eyes watch from the shadows. The air is a soup of them. It is a world saturated with life, a dense, layered, near-touching mass of organic existence.

You climb a gentle, curving stair. The structure is open, the paths weaving through the colossal trunks, offering glimpses of the wider, mid-range world. You see spiral ramps climbing into the leafy canopy above, and bridges of woven vines and timber spanning gaps so wide they are lost in the humid haze. You see other platforms, other paths, all part of the same vast, continuous, multi-featured construct. But always, the view is cut short by the trunks of the giant trees and the swirling, luminous mist. There is no horizon, only more Forest, more structure, extending out and up and down in a way that feels both open and impossibly enclosed.

The light is a problem. There is no sun here. The illumination comes from the life itself—the glowing moss, the fungal shelves, and from the clouds of bioluminescent insects that swarm in the upper reaches, their collective light filtering down in a dim, shifting, greenish-grey penumbra. It is a world of perpetual, near-darkness, a deep twilight that your eyes slowly, painfully, begin to adjust to. In this gloom, shapes are suggested, not defined. The world is a place of shadows and whispers, of things moving at the edge of vision.

There is no technology. Not a single hammer or nail, not a scrap of metal or wire. Everything here is grown, shaped, or dead—wood, vine, fiber, stone. The only tools are biological. The place runs on a slow, patient clock. The moisture cycle is a long, slow breath. The decay of a fallen beam might take a century. A new structure might grow to replace it over a millennium. The world is in a state of gentle, established balance, a place that is exactly what it is and has been for a very long time. The change that happens is the change of a glacier, a slow unfolding that moves at the speed of moss.

High above, in the canopy, the twisted, woody growth of the uppermost branches is thick enough to hold a world of its own. The air is warm and wet and dense, and the sound is a constant, vibrating wall. There is no other way to be. This is the Forest, and you are inside it.
