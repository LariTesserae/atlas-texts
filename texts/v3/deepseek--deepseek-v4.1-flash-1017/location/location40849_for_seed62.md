# The Bright Hollow

You come to it over a shoulder of new rock, and the first thing is the light. At this elevation the cloud layer sits thin and high, so the sun arrives not as shafts but as a broad wash, and then it does something unexpected: it goes *down* into the valley and comes back up. The forest below you is so wet, so densely leafed, that the canopy throws light back into itself — every leaf surface glossed with condensation, every moss cushion beaded — and the interior of the wood is brighter than the meadow you're standing in. It reads as a second, lower sky, green-gold, moving slightly.

The rock you stand on is sharp. Not weathered sharp — *new* sharp. The valley is young, geologically speaking; the river at its floor has cut down fast and not yet widened, so the walls rise steep and angular, faces still bearing the fracture planes of their breaking, scree that hasn't had time to round. Nothing here has been sanded. The ridgelines above are all blade and corner. It gives the whole place a temporary feel, like a thing that happened recently and might still be happening.

## Water, everywhere but nowhere pooled

There is almost no standing water, and yet everything is wet. The river at the bottom runs thin and fast in a channel too small for it — it is still downcutting, still finding its bed. The real water is distributed: it films the rock, it beads on the underside of every leaf, it gathers in the cup of every moss mat, it hangs as a persistent low fog that does not burn off at midday but simply *lifts* a few meters and thickens again. The air is cool and heavy and you can feel it condensing on the backs of your hands. Rain comes as a fine drizzle that is difficult to distinguish from the air itself, and then stops, and then resumes, and no one in the valley would think to comment.

## The green

Lush is too weak a word. Density is the truth of it: a saturated, layered, near-touching volume of growth. Conifers at the top, close-set, their trunks furred with a moss so thick it squares the round edges. Under them, a middle storey of broadleaf scrub so tightly interleaved you cannot see into it. Below that, ferns — huge, slow-unfurling, black-stemmed — and beneath the ferns a floor that is not floor but a deep sponge of liverwort and lichen and rotting wood, so that walking is a matter of sinking slightly and rising.

And threaded through all of it, the color: green stone. The rock faces and the fallen timber and even the bark nearer the water carry a bloom of verdigris, a copper stain, blue-green bleeding into the moss so that you cannot tell where chemistry ends and botany begins. Something in the drainage is rich in metal, and the forest has learned to eat it. The leaves near the seeps are almost too green — a hard, mineral green that catches the light wrong.

## The works

Scattered through the hollow, small and half-swallowed, are the structures. A stone gallery cut into the eastern wall, its mouth dark, water sheeting down its lintel. Four steel lattice towers, catenary cables slung between them, marching up the valley side and over the ridge — the cable still taut, still humming faintly when the wind finds it. A pump-house at the river's elbow, roof collapsed inward, interior full of fern. Condenser coils, furred with moss, mounted on concrete pads, deliberately arranged — a field of them, dozens, drawing moisture out of the bright wet air and letting it drip.

Because that is what this place is for. Somebody built a condensation works here, gravity-fed and patient, no engine anywhere, no combustion, hardly a moving part — just coils and catchments and a slow gradient, and it still runs. That is why the valley is so quiet. There is machinery in it and the machinery makes no noise.

## Quiet

No birdsong. Almost no faunal sound at all — the place is nearly empty of animals, and what lives here is small and slow and does not advertise. You will see a pale moth, once, huge and nearly white, working a fern. You will hear your own footsteps swallowed. The moss takes sound the way it takes water. The river does not roar; it passes in films and threads, and what little noise it makes is eaten by the growth within a few meters. Silence with a texture to it, a soft dense hush that is doing something, actively, all the time.

## The rule of the place

Nothing here is fast. The works were built slowly, and they run slowly, and the forest takes them back slowly, and the cable grows a sheath of green at a rate no one has measured. Change happens at the pace of dripping. To be here is to be inside a long patient process, in bright cool light, with everything wet and everything green and almost nothing moving.

And the strange thing — the thing you notice only on the second day, when you have stopped expecting it — is the rhythm of the drips from the coils. They are regular, and they are not mechanical. They have a period, and the period is not constant; it lengthens and shortens with the light and the moisture, so that the whole field of coils drips in a slow loose counterpoint, and the moss beneath each one has grown in a ring sized to its own particular rate. The forest is keeping time with the apparatus. Or the apparatus with the forest. Nobody built it to do that; it has simply settled into agreement over a very long time, the way everything here settles, at the pace of water finding a way down.
