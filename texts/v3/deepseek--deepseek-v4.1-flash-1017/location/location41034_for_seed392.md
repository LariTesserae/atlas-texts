The first thing you notice is the heat, and the second is the quiet. It is a thick, wet, oven-warm heat that hangs heavy as a blanket, the kind that beads on your skin and refuses to evaporate. And the quiet is deep, almost reverent, broken only by a sound like a slow, irregular heartbeat: a soft *plip... plip... plip* from somewhere deep in the greenery.

This is the Sunken Garden, or what the old maps call the Cistern Verge. It is a place that should not be. The ground is a shallow, sprawling bowl of reddish-brown concrete, perfectly smooth, but ridged like a washboard with narrow, parallel channels running for miles in gentle curves. It is a world of engineered terrain, a single poured surface that has been here so long it feels like a natural law. The elevation barely changes; the whole region lies low, a shallow basin catching the sky’s sweat.

Every one of the myriad drainage channels is a brimming rill of clear, warm water, flowing so slowly it seems more a suggestion of motion than actual current. From each of these channels, life has erupted. Not soil—there is no soil, no natural substrate anywhere. The lushness is a miracle of opportunism, a botanical insurgency. Thick, woolly mosses the color of jade carpet the concrete between the water-lines. From the moss, ferns uncurl in tight, architectural spirals, their fronds shiny and dripping. Pale, succulent stem-plants push up with determined force, their roots a fine white web plastered directly to the wet concrete. The air itself is thick with moisture, condensing on every surface, so the whole world glistens. It smells of wet stone, chlorophyll, and a faint, sweet perfume from a hanging vine with tiny purple bells that grows in explosive, pendulous clusters from the low ceiling.

For there is a ceiling. The basin is enclosed by a vast, low dome of the same reddish-brown concrete, supported by hundreds of thick, columnar pillars that rise at irregular intervals from the basin floor. The pillars are themselves overgrown, festooned with climbing plants and moss, turning them into verdant towers. The dome is perforated with thousands of small, circular skylights, and it is from these that the light falls. Not a harsh sun, but a brilliant, diffused radiance, like light through frosted glass, that fills the entire space with a bright, humid glow. There are no shadows here, only soft gradations of light. The dome is so low, and the scale so open, that the far side of the chamber disappears into a luminous, green-hazed distance.

The air is alive. Clouds of tiny, translucent insects dance in the light-shafts, their wings catching the glow like scattered dust. They are preyed upon by small, iridescent lizards that skitter with a soft clicking sound across the ridges and pillars, their scales mirroring the ambient light. But the most significant fauna are the architects of the sound. Somewhere, unseen behind curtains of ferns, lives the Plumb-bird. It is never seen, only heard, a reclusive creature that strikes a small, hollow mineral node in the concrete with a tiny, specialized beak. *Plip... plip... plip.* The sound is pure and bell-like, a lonely, steady metronome in the vast, silent warmth.

The rules of this world are simple and absolute. There is no wind. The air is perfectly still, which is why the heat feels so solid and the silence so profound. The water is not a torrent but a permanent, weeping presence always on the edge of your hearing. Nothing here is dead; the concrete is merely the armature for a patient, unstoppable taxonomy of plants. Everything is wet, warm, and growing.

But it is a strange, arrested growth. The world has a feeling of equilibrium, a frozen perfection. The ferns are not spreading; they are simply *there*. The water is not carving new channels; it has been in these channels forever. The Plumb-bird’s call is the same note it has always been. This is a world held in a state of perfect, humid stasis, a created Eden that has reached its final, stable form.

And yet, the weirdness. The more you look, the more you see that the patterns of the moss are not random, but form swirls and spirals that mimic fractals, repeating at every scale from the whole basin down to the tip of a single frond. The points of water dripping from the higher ferns seem to hesitate, just for a fraction of a second, before falling. The light from the skylights, when you stare at it, seems to pulse with a rhythm just slower than a heartbeat. The Plumb-bird’s *plip* has no echo; the sound falls dead in the thick air as if absorbed by a vast, soft entity.

It is a total, constructed thing. A terraformed landscape, a piece of planetary-scale engineering now completely reclaimed by a gentle, insidious biology. It is a greenhouse, a cathedral, an unfinished fountain, and a jungle, all poured from the same decaying, enduring recipe of concrete and warm water. The place is a held breath, a long, wet, quiet sigh of a world that has almost forgotten what it was supposed to be, and has become something else entirely.
