# The Steeping

You come in at the low end, where the roof has sagged until it nearly touches the lintel, and you go in stooped, sideways, one hand on the wet wood. The door is a wicker panel, unlatched, swollen; it moves like a held breath when you push it. Inside, the sound arrives before the sight does — a wall of it, water and voices, so loud that the first thing you do is lean forward and stop talking.

It is a small place. Thirty paces long, maybe, and half that across at its widest, and there is no point in it from which you can see all of it at once, because the middle is broken by baffles and beds and the roof comes down low over the far end. Yet it is not cramped. It is hollow, and the hollowness is the point: the pools sit far apart from one another, each one its own small weather, and between them are stretches of empty brick where nothing happens at all, wide as courtyards, and you cross them alone.

Everything here was made. There is no earth to find. You walk on brick — small brick, each one pressed in a thumbprint and laid in a basket-weave that has heaved and settled and heaved again for longer than anyone remembers — and where the brick has gone, there is plank, or puddled clay, or a hurdle of woven withies that gives a little under your weight. If you dig, you find more of the same, older, darker, the layers of a place that has been repaired over itself so many times that its floor is a kind of history. The bricks are worn round at the corners. The seams between them are filled with silt the color of strong tea.

The roof is a shallow vault of split plank and slate, carried on black alder posts set on stone pads, and it is not sound. Some bays have gone, and the light that comes through is dim and greenish and moves — it comes through in slats, in bars that shift with whatever the weather is doing above, so that the whole interior is lit like the inside of a lamp housing. Rain on the roof is a drumming you feel in your teeth. In the dry hours the light falls in long blades across the water and the water throws it back up onto the underside of the slats, wobbling.

Water is everywhere and nowhere deep. It never comes above a hand's depth. It enters at the high end through a clay lip where a channel has worn its own shape into the brick, and it goes out at the low end under a sill you can't see, and in between it moves slowly down through a whole sequence of small basins separated by clay walls — pans, each one lined and sealed by hand, each one holding water at a different stage of clearing. The first is cloudy with silt. The fourth is clear enough to see the bottom, and the bottom is brick. The water is cool, always, and smells of iron and cut reed and something faintly like a cellar. It seeps. It condenses on the underside of the planks and comes down in slow beads and drips, and the drips have their own rhythm, in some places faster than the frogs.

The frogs are why it's loud.

They are in every pan, in layers, at different depths and pitches — a low grunting from the big ones in the sump end, and above that a whole field of smaller voices, and in the corners the reed frogs making a sound like a wet finger drawn around a glass. They do not stop. And then, unpredictably, they do stop — the silence comes in a wave from the far end and rolls toward you and you are standing in a place with no sound at all except the water, and your skin crawls a little, and then the wave passes and the noise comes back up like a tide. Nobody has explained this to anyone's satisfaction.

There is more than frogs. Water beetles rowing in the clear pans. Caddis in their cases like little armored thumbs. Newts the length of a hand, hanging in the water with their legs out. A colony of small pale birds nests in the roof slats above the middle — they come and go through a gap in the slate and they are always talking, a dry chittering that sits on top of everything. In the black bays at the far end, where the roof is still sound and the light doesn't reach, bats. And in the last pan before the outflow there are eels, fat and old and slow, that came in by some route nobody has traced and that are, by long custom, not to be touched. They are the reason the place is called what it's called.

Almost nothing grows. What does grows in brick pockets, tended and then untended and then tended again: a willow at the high end, coppiced so viciously and so often that it is more a knuckle than a tree, and a fringe of rushes in three corners, and moss spread over every north-facing surface like a stain of green — and a film of duckweed on the still pans that is either a nuisance or a crop depending on who you ask. That is all. Sparse enough that each plant reads as an event. You notice the willow. You keep noticing it.

There is no metal here. The scoops are clay and wood, the shovels are wood, the buckets are staved and bound with withered withy, the pegs are pegs. Someone maintains this. The evidence is everywhere and the someone is not: fresh clay pressed into a failing lip, a new hurdle laid over a soft patch, rushes cut back, the wicker door re-hung. It has been maintained this way, by hand, with these tools or their ancestors of the same tools, for so long that the maintenance has become part of the erosion — the wear and the repair are the same slow process, and the place has settled into its own state, in balance, doing what it does. The water wants to go one way and the bricks want to heave another way and the frogs want a third, and all of it has arrived at an arrangement.

The rules are simple and nobody states them. Keep to the brick. Don't stand in the pans. Don't clear the reed from the outflow. Don't touch the eels. Don't try to find where the water comes from; it comes from the channel, and the channel comes from under the wall, and that is an answer. When the frogs go quiet in a wave, wait — don't move until it's passed. Speak low, and if you can't be heard, that's not a problem to be solved.

It is cool in here and it is always cool, and the air is wet enough that your hands go soft, and the light is dim and moving, and the sound is enormous, and you are standing in a space the size of a village hall with the population of a marsh. It is very empty. Between one pan and the next there is nothing at all — just brick, and the long dim blade of light, and the drip. Then the frogs come up again and the emptiness fills, and you understand the place a little better: it is not a ruin and it is not a garden and it is not quite a machine, though it was built to do a job and still does it. It is a made thing that has been left alone long enough to become itself, and it is loud with all the life that moved in when the makers stopped standing in the way.
