# The Pan

There is a place where the world finishes.

It is a plain of shallow water, and it is the bottom of everything — the lowest land, the sump. Whatever the mountains have given up over the whole long history of the world has come to rest here, and what remains is a single plate of bedrock planed to the flatness of a lens: dark grey-green, slick as a wet liver, and old in a way that has nothing to do with age. This is the terminal state of stone. Erosion has already won. There is nothing left to take.

The water over it is warm — blood-warm, skin-warm, the temperature of a body. It lies ankle-deep across most of the Pan, knee-deep in the hollows, and in places it is only a film, a breath of moisture on the rock, so that you are walking on a wet mirror. It is never clear. Fine pale silt hangs in it, the color of weak tea with milk, and the water never stops moving long enough to let the silt fall. So the whole plain is a shallow, turbid, trembling sheet, and every step you take sends a ring out that runs for a hundred meters and meets other rings coming back.

The sky is a lid. Low cloud, stacked and reeking, the color of wet slate on the underside, and the light that comes through it is dim and directionless and does not cast shadows. There is no sun to speak of. The Pan is lit the way the inside of a cloud is lit. The brightest thing in the landscape is usually the water itself: patches of microbial felt glowing faintly under the surface, a dull drowned amber that comes up through the tea-colored murk and goes out when you step near it.

---

**The sound.** The Pan is deafening, and it is deafening with itself.

It never stops. First there is the hiss — the whole sheet of water sliding and ticking over the polished stone, a white noise so total it stops being a sound and becomes a pressure, the way the inside of a shell would feel if the shell were your whole body. Under that, the boiling. The Pan sits over a broad shallow heat, and the water simmers in hundreds of patches: small kettles that whistle and thump and collapse with a glottal knock, blisters of steam breaking the surface, mud volcanoes the height of a man grumbling and slumping. And under *that*, the stone. The bedrock is under thermal strain across a continent, and it groans — long, low, tectonic complaints that travel for kilometers — and now and then, somewhere out in the grey, a section of it rings like a struck bell, one clear note a full minute long, and every creature in earshot stops.

But the strange deafness is this: the flat rock and the shallow water carry sound at different speeds, and the surface reflects it, and there is no hill, no tree, no wall to break it. Sound does not arrive once. It arrives in layers, over and over, spreading and overlapping, so a dropped stone is a stone dropped ten times, and a shout is a crowd shouting. You cannot tell where any noise comes from. The Pan has no direction to it. It is all around you and arriving late and arriving again, and after a while you understand that you are hearing not the Pan but the Pan's memory of itself.

---

**The weather.** The Pan is large enough to make its own.

Evaporation here is enormous. Every afternoon the plain lifts a column of warm vapor, and by evening that vapor has condensed into rain cells that walk across the flats on their own legs, grey curtains trailing, and let go. You can watch a storm being born two kilometers away, come toward you, break over you, and die behind you, and the sky above your head has not changed. The rain is warm. It does not chill you. It smells of iron and boiled greens and wet stone, and it lands hard enough to dimple the water into a white static, and when it does, the hiss becomes a roar and the roar becomes a wall and you cannot hear your own breathing.

The air is saturated at all times. Your clothes do not dry. The world is a wet hand held over your face. Everything condenses: the stone weeps, the stakes weep, your own skin runs with water that isn't sweat.

---

**The packedness.** Sparse vegetation, yes — a continent of wet rock and only scattered life on it. But the Pan is not empty. It is *saturated.* It is the most crowded place in the world, and almost none of it is visible.

The water column is thick. Every liter of it is a city — microbial mat in sheets and ropes and slimes, suspended load, the silt that is half ground stone and half the bodies of things too small to see, a fine organic snow that never settles. The wet rock is furred with it. The air is thick with it. You breathe the Pan and the Pan moves into you. Stand still for an hour and the felt begins to grow on your boots. The whole plain is a single continuous medium of living matter, and the visible world — the reeds, the herons, the stakes, you — is just the thin bright scum on top of it.

---

**The turbulence.** Nothing here holds still, and the stillness of the bedrock makes it worse.

Because the stone cannot change anymore, all the change has been pushed up into the water and the air. The Pan churns. Great slow convection cells turn across it, kilometers wide, so the whole sheet of water is circulating, and the water carries the heat with it, so that a patch which was boiling yesterday is cool today and a place you waded through last week is now scalding. Mud islands rise. Silt gathers around a bubble of gas, the mat binds it, a raft of mud the size of a room surfaces with a wet slap and drifts and grows a skin of rust-colored felt and is dissolved again by evening. The Pan makes land and unmakes it daily. It makes weather and unmakes it. It is never in any state you could call its state; it is only ever in the middle of becoming something else, and it has been doing this, without pause, for longer than the mountains it drained have existed.

---

**The wells.** Not everything is shallow. Where the bedrock dissolved — it is that kind of stone, warm and soluble and tired — there are holes. Smooth-sided, round as the neck of a bottle, going down further than any rod. The water in them is hot and sulfurous and does not drain, and it *breathes*: rises and falls in a slow rhythm that is not tidal and not regular, a hand's breadth over an hour, all the wells together, out of phase. In the wells, the reeds grow — the only upright things for a thousand kilometers, grey-green, chest-high, crowded around the rims in noisy little stands.

And the Pan's weirdness is thickest here, because the flat water is a delay line and the wells are its mouths. Stand by a well and you hear things. A conversation in a language you almost know, arriving up the shaft from nowhere. A bell that rang last week. Your own footsteps, returning as laughter, or as a voice calling a name you answer to. The Pan is a sump of sound as well as matter: things wash up here. A splinter of painted boat. The wing of a bird that lives in cold country and has no business in this warmth. A wooden doll, worn round. No current brought them; there is no current. They simply arrived, the way the water arrived, the way everything arrives at the bottom of the world.

---

**The horizon.** Do not trust it.

On a grey day the water and the sky are the same value, and the Pan becomes one continuous substance, and distance stops working. A marker three kilometers off looks like a marker three hundred meters off and stays that way all day. You walk toward it and it does not grow. Then, without transition, it is beside you. Other times something small and near — a stake, a wading bird — swells into a vast grey shape on the horizon and hangs there, enormous, and then resolves.

People see a far shore. There is no far shore. So the rule of the Pan is this: the ground is finished, and everything above it is not. The stone is the oldest stable thing there is, worn to its final form, and it will be exactly this tomorrow and in ten thousand years — and yet the water on top of it will be different water, different heat, different color, and the islands will be different islands, and the rain different rain, and the sound will be a new arrangement of the same sound. Permanence below, chaos above, and no line between them you can stand on.

Some places the stone is planed so thin it behaves like a membrane. Press your palm down and you feel warmth coming up through it, and a slow pulse, and if you press harder it gives, very slightly, the way a bruise gives.

---

**The marks.** Someone was here. Not many, and not for long.

A line of stone stakes driven into the bedrock — waist-high once, worn now to nubs, forty of them, set a long stride apart, running east for a kilometer and then stopping. They point at nothing. They were a route, or a boundary, or a measurement, and whichever it was, the thing they referred to has been erased by the flatness. The reeds grow thickest in their lee, because the stakes are the only thing in the Pan that breaks the wind.

And one structure. A low ring of fired brick, no roof, half-sunk, the floor built up just above the waterline and graded so it stays dry — the only dry floor for a thousand kilometers. In the middle of it, a hearth of flat stones, blackened. The walls weep. The mat grows up the inside of them. It is empty of anything but a smell of old smoke, and it is the only architecture in the world's basement, and whoever built it built it well: it has outlasted everything else they were.

---

Stand in the Pan at dusk, when the light is the color of the water and the rain cells are walking toward you and the stone is groaning and the wells are breathing and the sound is a crowd of itself. The water will be warm. Your reflection will be a half-second late. The silt will move under your feet like something turning over in sleep.

It is the most settled place there is, and it will not hold still, and it is very, very loud.
