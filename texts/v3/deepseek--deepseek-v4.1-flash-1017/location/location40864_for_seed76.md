## The Sallow Table

Imagine a continent lifted almost into the ceiling of its atmosphere: a high, old tableland of pale stone, so broad that the horizon is less a line than a slow blue tilt into distance. This is the Sallow Table. It is a place of bright, cold air and enormous silence, where the ground is dry mineral pavement and the vegetation is lush anyway—lush in long, scattered ribbons, not in a closed green sea.

There is no water here. Not in ponds, not in rivers, not in rain, not in the sap. The air is thin and dry enough to make your lips tighten, and the few shallow channels that seam the plateau carry nothing most days but wind and dust. The plants do not drink in any familiar way. Their tissues hold a cold, volatile solvent—thin, faintly sweet, faintly medicinal—that condenses in the chill hours and is released at midday as a trembling haze. It never pools. It smells like cold resin and clean stone. When people first walk here they keep expecting a spring, a seep, a wet green place, and there is none; the green is a dry green, more olive and copper and sallow gold than emerald.

The tableland itself is ancient and settled into its own erosion. The stone lies in long, low layers—chalk-white, rust-red, slate-gray—planed by wind into shallow bowls and rounded ridges. Cliffs are scalloped rather than jagged. Scree is old scree, fitted together, softened at the edges. Nothing looks freshly broken. Dust moves in slow rivers across the flats, and in the lee of ridgelines it gathers into fine mineral drifts where holdfasts can take root. There are no built structures, no cut stones, no paths except the ones animals make and the wind erases. Even a footprint feels like an event.

The vegetation comes in belts. It follows shallow swales, mineral seams, and the long shadows of low escarpments—anywhere dust collects and light angles just right. Within those belts it is thick: leathery fan-leaves, hollow jointed stalks, mats of wine-red and olive groundcover, low frond towers three or four meters high with waxy, mirrorish leaves that turn slowly through the day. The leaves are cold to the touch and dry as paper. Some have lens-like patches that concentrate the fierce white light; some exude a faint chemical suppressant that keeps rivals at a distance. The belts are dense enough to hide a crouching animal, but between them lie kilometers of bare pavement. That is the trick of the place: it is lush and sparse at once, green in ribbons, empty in the aggregate. You can walk for an hour between one stand of fronds and the next, hearing only your own steps and the dry clatter of stems.

The light is the dominant fact. The sun is small and white and very fierce; the sky at noon is a deep, almost midnight blue at the zenith, washed pale only near the horizon. Shadows are sharp and blue and cold. Sunlight here does not warm so much as expose: it bleaches the stone, glazes the waxy leaves, and makes the dust haze glow. The temperature is cool even at midday. In shade it is immediately cold; at night the plateau breathes out its thin heat and the plants contract with faint creaks, and a mineral frost—not water-ice, but a brittle crystalline bloom—forms in the low hollows and vanishes by midmorning.

Weather is visible here because the scale is continental. Cold fronts move as long shadow-bands across the stone. Dust storms rise without warning and travel in ochre walls, hissing against the frond belts and then passing. At dawn, the volatile sap-mist gathers in the shallow channels and runs along them like a ghost stream, bright and silent, gone within the hour. Spore pulses come in seasons of their own: for a few days the air is full of pale drifting motes, and the groundcover closes and opens in slow waves. The place is active, but its activity is mostly slow and wide—weather crossing a tableland, plants tracking light, dust relocating a hillside grain by grain.

Sound stays low. Wind over stone. The dry rustle and ticking of stems cooling or warming. A distant boom when a slab of old cliff gives way somewhere beyond the horizon. The faint crackle of the sap-mist condensing. No birdsong, no insect hum thick enough to notice. Fauna is rare and scattered: a few plated grazers that move between vegetation belts and leave neat bitten edges; pale moth-like things that cling to the lens-leaves; a lone predator shaped like a wind-borne kite, seen perhaps once in a week, circling slowly over the empty stone. Their tracks are more common than their bodies. Predation happens, but it is sparse and unhurried, one event in a great deal of space.

The rules of the world are chemical and solar rather than wet. Life here is built around light, mineral dust, and volatile sap. Plants mine the dust drifts; they trade in tiny airborne chemistry; they defend space with scent and suppressant rather than with shade. The belts migrate over decades, not seasons. Erosion and growth are in balance: the plateau is old enough that its landforms and its vegetation have settled into each other. Nothing is cultivated. Nothing is built. The only architecture is grown—hollow stalks, fan-canopies, matted ground—and even that is provisional, ready to close or open with the light.

And there is one small strangeness that stays with you. In the cold hour before dawn, when the sap-mist runs in the channels, the frond towers turn very slightly toward the brightest part of the sky. If you stand still among them, the whole belt seems to be listening—not to you, but to the light. Then the sun clears the horizon, the mist vanishes, the leaves go still, and the Sallow Table is again a vast, bright, quiet place: dry stone, cool air, lush ribbons of unwatered green, and a silence wide enough to hold weather.
