You hear it a day and a half before you see it — a low continuous roar that seems to come from the mountain's own throat, and people in the valleys below will tell you it is the sea, or a river in flood, or nothing, until you climb and the sound resolves into something no river makes: a note, held, with harmonics stacked on it. Then you come around a shoulder of rock and there is the cloud — a bright standing mass of it, luminous even at night, sitting on the shelf of the mountain at three thousand meters as if it had been nailed there. The cloud does not move. Nothing about it moves. What looks like weather from below is a lid.

The air outside the cloud is thin and cold and dry. You step in, and it is neither. It is blood-warm and saturated and heavy enough to lean on, and it smells of hot limestone and greenhouses and a thin thread of sulfur. The transition takes three steps. On one side, a world of blue distance and small wind. On the other, a greenhouse the size of a town.

And it is a town. That is the first wrongness: the thing on the shelf is organized. Terraces climb in flights, hundreds of them, each with a rounded lip and a brimming bowl. Between them run channels in every size, from the width of a wrist to the width of a road, carrying water in every direction, over and under and through. There are stairs — real stairs, worn into shallow bowls at the center of each tread — and arcades, and vaulted galleries, and chimneys that vent steam with a steady, unvarying pressure, and courtyards with a warm pool in the middle and a ring of stone benches around it. There are alleys so narrow you must turn sideways, and halls that open above you the height of a cathedral nave, ribbed and fluted and dripping from every rib. There are districts. You can feel them as you walk: here the vaults are low and crowded and everything is the color of bone; there the galleries are tall and the stone has gone rust-red and the air rings differently.

No one built it. That is the second wrongness, and it takes longer to notice, because the city is so legible as a city that you keep expecting to meet its masons. There are no masons. There is no metal here, no cut edge, no mark of a tool anywhere, no soot, no rope, no writing. The stones are not stacked; they are grown. The whole place is travertine and sinter and tufa — mineral that has been exhaled from hot water — and it is laid down by the living coat that covers almost every surface of it.

Call it the pelt. It is a skin of mats and films and bladders and fans, a few millimeters to a few hands thick, and it is the brightest thing here: sulfur yellow, rust orange, viridian, bone white, a magenta that hurts. From a distance the city looks like a heat map of itself. The pelt is what builds. It pulls carbonate out of the warm water and precipitates it as stone, and it grows toward the water and toward the light, and it dies on its downstream edge, and the stone stays where it died. So the city accretes in the direction of flow — slowly, steadily, in every channel and lip and vault at once — while the hottest water, the water coming straight off the vents, dissolves the same stone at the same rate. Growth and dissolution are exactly matched. The city is being built and eaten in the same instant, everywhere, forever, and the result is this: an architecture that has not changed in a thousand years is also, right now, under your hand, being made.

Stay still and watch a single terrace lip and you can see it. The color shifts — bloom, die, bloom — a slow shimmer like the surface of a boiling pot, if boiling were patient. Step back and the lip is stone, and it is the same stone it was this morning, and it will be the same stone it is tomorrow. Nothing here is the same matter twice. Everything here is the same shape.

The water never leaves. That is the engine of the whole place. It rains — constantly, warmly, in a fine fat drizzle that is more condensation than rain, and tastes faintly of chalk — and the rain runs down the terraces in the channels the city grew for it, and collects in the pools, and sinks, and hits the hot rock, and comes back up as steam, and the steam rises into the cloud, and the cloud rains. The same water, up and around and down, at the same rate, in the same channels, day and night. The vents do not surge. The pools do not rise or fall. The terrace lips spill in a rhythm so constant that you can use it as a clock and it will not drift.

And the sound. The sound is the thing you will remember, and it is deafening — a wall you walk into, that presses on the teeth and the sternum and the soles of the feet, that makes speech useless at arm's length, that you can feel in the stone through your palms. It is built of everything at once. The big basins boom, each at its own low pitch, and there are hundreds of them, so the bass line is thick and chordal. The small spills chatter and ring, tuned by their width. The steam hisses, and the deep vents roar. The pelt, growing and dying, makes a sound like sugar dissolving, a granular fizz, except that there are billions of it and it never stops, and the sum of all those tiny events is this: a constant. Nothing in it varies. There are no transients at all, and this is the strangest thing about it — a sound with no attack, no echo, no edges. It has no direction. You cannot tell where anything is by ear. You turn, and it is equally loud behind you and in front, and it is as loud in a narrow alley as in the great nave, and it does not stop when you cover your ears; it comes through your jaw.

There are no animals. No birds in the steam, no insects on the pelt, no fish in the warm pools, nothing that flies or crawls or calls. The only motion is water and steam and growth, and the only living things are rooted. They spread by runners and by fragments carried in the channels and by spores that go up with the steam and come down with the rain, and nothing eats them, and nothing is eaten. In a place this lush, this wet, this hot, the silence of the living world should be enormous; instead it is filled, floor to ceiling, with that single unchanging chord. The chord has no voices in it. That is why it does not sound like anything alive.

The rules hold everywhere, and they are simple, and they are absolute.

You cannot add. Anything you set down is colonized within a day and mineralized within three, and the shape of it is gone.

You cannot take away. Break a lip of a terrace and the water reroutes around the break, and the pelt grows into the wound, and the stone fills it, and in a season the break is a smooth place in the lip that has always been there.

You cannot leave a mark. Cut your name into the travertine and the water brings lime to the cut and the pelt brings its color, and the cut fills, and the sound closes over the difference. There is no history here. Nothing accumulates. The place is a shock absorber for event. The equilibrium is not passive; it is enforced, and it is total, and it does not care what it costs you.

And the shape — the shape persists. That is the only thing that persists. The matter is all in transit; the form is what stands still. A city of standing waves, made of water and mineral and light, holding a figure it has held since before anyone came to look at it, and will hold as long as the heat comes up and the cloud stays pinned to the shoulder of the mountain.

Walk out at dusk. The glare inside never really changes — the cloud is lit from below by the hot stone, and the day only warms it slightly — but at the rim the light goes gold and then rose and then the outside world goes blue and dark and enormous, and the shelf below you is still glowing, still roaring, still the same. Behind you the pelt blooms and dies in its millions, the terraces brim and spill, the steam climbs into the cloud and comes back down as warm rain. Nothing has happened. Nothing is about to. The note holds.

You can hear it from the valley, they say, on a still night — a low sound under the wind, like a sea where there is no sea, like a choir that has forgotten the words and will not stop singing them.
