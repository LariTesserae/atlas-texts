The world here is a drowned highland, a place where the sea did not so much invade as it did ascend, claiming a mountain plateau and remaking it in its own image. The water is the primary element, a vast, cold, and living presence. It is not the clear blue of a tropical sea but a deep, mineral green, thick with suspended life. The light that filters down from a surface far above is weak and diffuse, a perpetual, hazy twilight that paints the world in shades of jade and indigo.

The terrain is one of dramatic, submerged topography. This was once a rugged mountain range, and its peaks and valleys now form the geography of this aquatic world. Sheer cliffs of dark, weathered stone drop away into abyssal trenches, their faces etched with the slow, patient record of water's work. Broad, sloping plateaus are covered in a thick, soft ooze of sediment and decaying organic matter. The scale is regional, a landscape you could travel for days without ever seeing its like again, a endless, hilly seabed punctuated by the ghostly silhouettes of drowned mountains.

This is a lush place, but the greenery is not of the sort that sways in a wind. It is a forest of the deep, a thicket of kelp-analogues with broad, translucent fronds that drift in the slow, powerful currents. These plants are not green but a deep, oily bronze, and they grow in dense, scattered patches, clinging to the sides of cliffs and carpeting the more sheltered valleys. Their leaves are tough and leathery, designed to withstand the constant, powerful pull of the water.

The most active and defining feature of this landscape is the current. It is a ceaseless, powerful river-in-the-sea, a deep-water torrent that sweeps through the drowned valleys and over the plateaus. It is the sound of this world: a constant, low, thrumming roar that you feel in your bones more than you hear with your ears. It is the sound of immense force, of a river that has no banks, a wind that has no air. This current is the engine of the world, and everything here is shaped by it.

It is a place of strange, slow processes. Bubbles of gas, methane or something else, rise in intermittent streams from fissures in the rock, their silver spheres wobbling upwards in a hypnotic dance. The water itself is cold, a deep and penetrating chill that speaks of immense depths and sunless abysses. There is no animal life. The stillness of the fauna-less world is profound, broken only by the current's roar and the soft, organic sounds of the plant life. It is an ecology of plants and water and stone, a world that is active but silent in the way we understand it, a world of geological and botanical time.

Faint marks of a past presence are scattered across this landscape. They are not ruins in the traditional sense, but single, isolated structures of a simple, primitive design. Here, a ring of rough-hewn, basalt-like stones, half-buried in the ooze. There, a single, cylindrical tower of stone, its top sheared off, now home to a cluster of bronze kelp. They are weathered, settled into their environment, and speak of a people who lived here before the waters rose, a people who built with simple tools and a deep, perhaps fearful, understanding of the world. Now, their works are merely another feature of the terrain, worn smooth by the ceaseless current and colonized by the lush, dark vegetation.

The darkness is absolute in the deeps, a pressure and a presence all its own. But even in the perpetual twilight of the upper levels, it is a world of shadows and half-seen forms. The strange is the norm here. The way the kelp-fronds can seem to move with a purpose. The way the stone ruins feel less like markers of a past and more like anchors, holding something down. The way the current sometimes seems to whisper, a sound just on the edge of hearing, a low murmur that might be the voice of the water itself. The world feels old, patient, and entirely indifferent. It is a place that has been in-its-own-state for a very long time, and it will be so for a very long time to come.
