Here is a description of the landscape.

---

The place is a high, cold plateau of stone, vast and slow. It has no name, and nothing has ever needed one. The ground is not soil but a crust, a thin, dark green-black mat of lichen and frozen moss that cracks underfoot like old paint, revealing the grey, weathered bedrock beneath. The rock is ancient, scoured by an eternity of wind and ice, its surface smoothed into long, rolling waves and deep, shallow depressions. It feels settled, a landscape that has been in its own state for a very long time, and is in no hurry to be any other way.

Water is present, but it does not flow. It gathers in the depressions of the rock, forming shallow, cold pools that are mirrors of the dim, pearly sky. The air itself is a constant, heavy damp, a condensation that clings to every surface. It is not rain, but a fine, perpetual mist that beaded on the lichen, dripped from the edges of stones, and soaked into the thick, fibrous coats of the creatures that live here. When the wind thins, a profound silence settles over everything, broken only by the faint plink of water dripping from one stone to another, and the low, rhythmic breathing of the world itself.

That breathing comes from the fauna. They are everywhere, and they are the true inhabitants of this place. They are not animals that move through a landscape; they *are* the landscape. The most prominent are the "stone-eaters," colossal, dome-shaped creatures the size of hills, their bodies a symbiosis of flesh and rock, their slow, geological metabolism radiating a faint, internal warmth. From a distance, they are just more boulders, until one shifts its weight over the course of a decade, or you see the slow, rhythmic expansion of its flank. Around them, the ground is alive with smaller things: scuttling, many-legged "crawlers" the size of a hand, their shells the color of wet slate; and legions of "moss-backs," soft-bodied things that move with the speed of a growing plant, grazing on the lichen crust.

The vegetation is sparse but determined. It exists not as forests or grasses, but as small, tough patches: those lichen mats, tough, wired shrubs that grow in the wind-shadow of the great stone-eaters, and pale, tuberous fungi that push their heads through the damp moss. Nothing is taller than a person's knee. The air smells of wet stone, cold earth, and the faint, sweet-sour scent of the moss-backs' slow decay.

This is a world of slow gradients. A new pool might take a century to form; a great stone-eater might migrate a single mile in a thousand years, its path marked by the generations of moss-backs that follow it, feeding on the stirred-up minerals in its wake. The only light is the low, grey light of a perpetually overcast sky, a dimness that softens every edge and turns the pools into flat, silvered coins. It is a place built on a scale that dwarfs the individual, a continent of quiet, damp, and patient life, where the only industry is the immense, silent engine of biology itself.
