# The Bellows

A shallow bowl in the earth, a few hundred paces across, and every one of those paces is plant. Not a forest — forests have gaps and trunks and a floor you can see. This is a hollow filled to the brim with living matter, and the matter has organized itself the way a town organizes: there are thoroughfares, there are chambers, there are walls and roofs and awnings, and none of it was built. It grew, and then it kept growing into its own settled shape, and now the shape holds. The stems are enormous and hollow, the size of grain silos or small towers, and they lean into each other and fuse where they touch, so the whole basin is one continuous structure of pale green pipework and membrane and leaf, cross-braced and buttressed and worn smooth at every place a body has ever rubbed against it.

The scale is a trick. You can walk the whole thing in an hour. But you will not have seen it, because it is layered — the floor of the bowl is only the ground floor. The fused stems go up and branch and re-fuse, and there are levels, terraces, galleries, gap-lofts where the architecture opens into air and closes again, and a grown staircase of root-knots spiraling through it all. It reads as a town because it functions as one: traffic moves along the low galleries where the condensation runs, and the upper chambers are for things that want to be dry, and the deepest hollows are reservoirs of still, warm water.

And it is nearly empty. That is the strangest part. All this architecture and so much air inside it — vast hollow volumes, chambers the size of chapels with nothing in them but a warm draft and the smell of wet cellulose and a single beetle working a seam. The town is mostly void. The presences are scattered and the space between them is enormous and resonant, and the emptiness is not abandonment, it is *lung*. The place is breathing.

**The sound.** It never stops, and it is not background. The hollow stems are resonators — every one of them, tuned by length and thickness and the water level inside. Wind moves through the galleries and the whole basin hums and moans and then breaks into a roar. The fauna are inside the walls, not just around them: choral insects packed into the hollows by the thousand, a low continuous drone that rises and falls with the temperature, and something deeper — a great slow throb, felt in the sternum, that is the plant itself moving water and growing. It is deafening in the way a waterfall is deafening: you stop hearing it after a while, and then it stops, and you realize you have been shouting. When rain comes — and it comes often, brief and warm, condensing on the cool membranes and drumming the outer shell — the whole town becomes a drum and the drum becomes a choir.

**The weirdness** is in the acoustics, or what passes for acoustics here. Sound does not obey the room. Speak in one chamber and your voice emerges somewhere else — three galleries away, an hour later, in a voice not quite yours. The plant grows toward sound. This is the mechanism of the town: noise shapes it. A place that is loud gets thicker, gets buttressed, grows a ceiling; a place that goes quiet is slowly absorbed, its walls thickening inward until the chamber is a stem again. The inhabitants — and there are many, none of them tool-makers, none of them builders, all of them simply living here the way you live in a house that maintains itself — know this. They are careful what they say and where. Some of them sing to widen their rooms. Some of them have been silent for years, and the walls are closing on them, and they prefer it.

**Fauna** everywhere, in abundance, in a hollow that feels empty: moth-things the width of a hand, pale and slow; gliding things that run the upper galleries and never touch the floor; beetle-processions that clean the seams; a large soft grazer, low and wide, that crops the walls and keeps the thoroughfares from closing, and is itself cropped by nothing, and has no fear in it at all. The predators are the sound. Nothing hunts by sight here.

**Weather and water.** Warm. Always warm, the warmth of a compost heap's heart, the warmth of breath on glass. Damp without being wet: condensation beading on every surface, dripping in slow irregular taps that are part of the music, running in threads down the stems to the floor channels and the standing pools. Rain arrives without clouds you can see, from the saturated air itself. The pools are warm and green and full of small transparent things.

**The world's rules**, as best they can be told: nothing here is made, only grown; nothing is repaired, only thickened; nothing is thrown away, only absorbed. The place has been in balance so long that its erosion is a kind of polish — every edge rounded, every surface furred with moss, every junction blurred into a smooth organic weld. Time moves at the pace of the plant, which is faster than you would think and slower than you would like. And the single law that everyone here knows: *the Bellows listens, and it answers, and it answers late, and it answers in the shape of the walls.*
