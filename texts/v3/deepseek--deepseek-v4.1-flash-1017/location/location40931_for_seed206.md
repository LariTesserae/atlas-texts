Imagine a place you find by accident, not by navigation. There is no path to it that stays a path; you push through a hedge of something soft-leaved and wet, and the ground gives slightly underfoot, and then the air changes—warmer, heavier, tasting of green and iron and something sweet. You have stepped into a pocket.

It is small. That is the first thing your body knows, before your eyes catch up: the space is a room, not a landscape, though it has its own weather. Perhaps forty paces across. A bowl in the earth, or a hollow in the flank of a hill, sunk low enough that the water table lies close beneath everything. Step wrong and your boot sinks to the ankle in black mud that smells of old leaves and mineral. Step right and you stand on a lattice of roots so dense and springy it feels like a sprung floor, and beneath it the dark water breathes.

The green is total. This is not a place where plants grow; it is a place made *of* plant. Mosses sheathe every surface in pelts of emerald and rust. Ferns unroll from the walls in slow cascades. Above, the canopy closes entirely—broad leaves the size of serving platters, waxy and dripping, layered in ranks, so that daylight arrives not as sun but as a green liquid, filtered and diffused, that pools on the floor and makes everything look underwater. Nothing is bare. Even the air is full of drifting spores and small winged seeds, catching the light like slow snow. If you touch a trunk, your hand comes away green.

And it is wet, everywhere, all the time. Water beads on every leaf-tip; the air is so thick with humidity it condenses on your skin. Small streams thread down the bowl's sides, gathering in channels of exposed root, and where they meet the low center they form shallow, tea-colored pools. Between the pools, half-submerged, lies the machinery.

Because this place is worked. Not built—nothing here bears the hand of a carpenter or a mason—but *worked*, the way a spring is worked, the way a hive is worked. Somewhere in the center, half-swallowed by moss and roots, the ground itself functions. There are structures of root and living wood that have grown into the shapes of things that pump and turn: a great knotted ventricle of bark that swells and contracts, slowly, with a sound like a deep wet heartbeat, lifting water in a spiral of hollow vines. Sap moves under pressure here, and you can hear it, a continuous low gurgle and thrumming in the wood, a hydraulic hum that never stops. Valves of cupped leaves click open and shut. Somewhere a wooden wheel, strung with vine, turns forever in a channel of the stream, and its creaking is one voice in a chorus.

That chorus is the loudest thing about the place. It is never quiet. The water talks constantly—drip, trickle, splash, the soft boom of a root-cistern refilling. The pumps throb. The canopy rustles and patters with the weight of things moving through it. And the animals, which are everywhere, fill the rest: insects in clouds, beetle and fly and something with too many wings, droning and ticking and buzzing in overlapping strata; frogs that call in short percussive bursts, wood-on-wood; birds—small, dull-feathered, loud—that shriek and gossip in the middle layers; and below it all, occasionally, the deeper call of something larger moving slow through the ferns, patient, unhurried. Nothing here is silent long enough to feel alone, but nothing here is in a hurry either. Everything is exactly where it has been for a very long time.

The machine is old and settled. Its channels have worn into their beds; its roots have grown around their own older roots, thickening, weathering, sheathing themselves in moss and mineral crust. Nothing is under construction; nothing is failing. It has reached the state it was always tending toward, and now it simply *is*—a self-regulating engine of green, gurgling along at a pace that makes no distinction between a minute and a season. The water level does not rise or fall; the light does not brighten or dim; the pump swells and relaxes in its slow rhythm and does not speed or slow. You could leave and come back in a year and everything would be in its place, doing what it does, and the only difference would be the moss a shade thicker on the valves.

You feel, after a while, that you are inside something's lung. The warm damp air moves, faintly—drawn in, pushed out, drawn in—and the sound of it is the sound of breathing, if breathing were made of water and wood and a hundred small throats. It is industrial and it is alive, and it is very small, and it is entirely its own. You stepped into a pocket, and the pocket has been here all along, running.
