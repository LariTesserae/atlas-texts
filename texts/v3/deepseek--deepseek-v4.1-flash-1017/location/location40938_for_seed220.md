# The Sump at Vaur Rin

You come down through the throat, and the first thing that reaches you is not light — there is none — but a pressure on the skin, a warmth like standing near a sleeping animal. The air is 41 degrees and moves. It has a slow tidal respiratory rhythm, in for ninety seconds, out for ninety, driven by something deeper than you can see. Your ears pop. Then the sound arrives, and after that you stop thinking of it as arrival at all, because the sound was always already here, waiting for a body to notice it.

**The hollow.** The cavern is not vast in the way of cathedrals — it is vast in the way of a held breath. Roughly a kilometre and a half across at its widest, roof somewhere between forty and a hundred metres up depending on where the old collapses have brought it down. The floor is not flat. It undulates, terraced by centuries of mineral deposition and cut by channels. The whole volume is warm and close and *occupied*, and yet between the built things there are gaps of fifty, eighty, a hundred and twenty metres where nothing is — no structure, no growth, no animal, only the worn stone floor and the sound bouncing off it. You can stand in one of those gaps and feel the emptiness as a physical quality, like a room with the furniture taken out of it and the walls still warm from the people who left.

**Sound.** The place is loud. Not painfully, not with a single voice, but layered — a constant orchestral bed. The water is the bass: it does not run in a river but gathers and releases, seeping through the porous rock in a slow pulse, and every release point has its own note. Somewhere in the western wall there is a fissure that exhales every nineteen minutes with a low tone you feel in the sternum. The structures themselves sing — the ones still working. Their resonance is the reason the town is laid out as it is: each building tuned against its neighbours, the gaps between them calculated so the standing waves don't cancel. Move a wall two metres and the whole district changes key. The people here can hear a fault developing three streets away by the way the chord sours. Silence is not a comfort in Vaur Rin. Silence is a warning, and everyone knows it.

**Built.** Two hundred and some structures, clustered in three loose braids along the water-bearing seams, everything else empty stone. The town is dense where it is and nowhere where it isn't. The buildings are grown-and-assembled — you cannot call it either — masses of sintered mineral laid down layer by layer over generations by machines that are half printer, half coral. Walls have the grain of something that precipitated out of warm water, banded in cream and rust and a dull wet slate. Older strata sag and slump where the substrate beneath them has settled; nobody straightens them. The whole town leans. It has been leaning for four hundred years and it will keep leaning, and the leaning is load-bearing now; you could not correct it without bringing the roof down. Doors are irregular ovals. Ceilings bulge. The streets are worn into hollows a half-metre below the surrounding floor by nothing but feet and time.

Under the skins of the buildings, the technology. It is not hidden, exactly, but it is not displayed — it lives in the walls like a nervous system, threads of something that is not quite metal and not quite glass, warm to the touch, carrying resonance and current and a third thing the local engineers call *tide* and decline to explain further. It does things that the physics above ground does not permit: it stores the cavern's sound and gives it back on a delay, so a footstep in the eastern braid can arrive in the western braid eleven seconds later, faintly, as if someone were following. The machines don't have interfaces. They have dispositions. You ask them for heat and they give it to you if they like the way you ask. They are old enough to have opinions.

**Water.** It is everywhere and nowhere. No river; no pool you could bathe in. It comes as seep — as a sheen on the walls that makes the whole town faintly slick, as a fog that gathers in the low ground at the turning of the air-tide, as drips that fall on a rhythm so regular the residents set their clocks by particular ones. Certain corridors are wet year-round and hung with the only vegetation in the place: mats of a pale, threadlike growth, barely green, that feeds on the mineral film and the warmth and grows about a centimetre a month. It is not cultivated, but it is protected. You do not scrape the walls in Vaur Rin. The mats are the only thing in the cavern that makes oxygen, and everyone knows that too.

Drinking water comes from condensers — long cool fins run out into the empty gaps where the air moves, and the water beads and runs down into channels and is collected. The whole town drinks the breath of the cave. In lean cycles the fins are the most valuable objects in Vaur Rin and are guarded accordingly.

**Light.** Almost none. There is a faint something — a bioluminescent dust in the air-tide, or perhaps just the eye lying — that gives the empty gaps a suggestion of shape, the way a dark room has a suggestion of shape. The town itself makes its light. Lanterns of the same warm glassy thread, low and amber and insufficient, hung at corners and over doors. Between the braids, the darkness is total and the sound is the only map you have.

**Fauna.** Rare, and mostly small. Pale crawlers in the wall mats. A kind of long-legged thing, translucent, that comes out at the air-tide's turn and stands in the empty gaps and does not move for hours, and which the residents ignore entirely as a matter of etiquette. Once a season, something larger comes up from the deep seams — never seen clearly, only heard, a new low note in the bed and a pressure change and then it's gone. Nobody goes down to look.

**The rule of the place.** Vaur Rin runs on a single understanding: the cavern is a body and the town is a thing living in it, and the body is patient but not infinite. You do not dam the seeps. You do not silence a working structure. You do not go into the empty gaps alone at the turn of the tide. You keep the chord in tune. Do these things and the warmth holds, the water comes, the sound goes on being a comfort — and the place goes on being what it has always been: a small bright noisy warm dense knot of human making, settled deep into a cold dark quiet world that has not decided yet whether to keep it.
