# The Sill

You hear it before you see anything, and for a long while you think it is wind.

It isn't. It is water — a sheet of it, two fingers deep, sliding east across a plain of planed blue-gray stone the size of a county, and the stone is *pocked*. Ten thousand years of grit have drilled it with flutes and round holes and long grooves like the grain of a plank, and every one of those small voids takes the flow and turns it into tone. The plain does not babble. It roars, low and steady, a sound with no bottom to it, and the roar is made of a million small collisions that you can pick apart if you stand still long enough. Under the roar is a hiss. Under the hiss is a ringing, thin and mineral, coming up through your heels.

The horizon is a line. That's all it is — the water, the sky, and a seam between them, and the seam does not have a hill in it anywhere. This ground was a mountain once. It has been reduced, patiently, over a span of time that has no useful comparison, and now it is at rest. It is not eroding anymore, not really. It has arrived. The water crosses it the way a hand crosses a table.

The water is cool and clear and tastes faintly of chalk. It is not deep anywhere. You can walk the whole plain and never wet your knee; the deepest place is a pothole the width of a cart, drilled perfectly round and filled to its lip, and the water in it is a different color, green-black, and it is older than the water moving past it. The plain tilts so slightly you can't feel it, one hand's width of fall per mile, and that is enough. That is the whole engine.

Nothing grows here. Nothing has grown here in a very long time; there is no soil to hold the memory of growth. What looks at first like moss on the stone is a film of the stone itself, dissolving and re-setting, a mineral skin that thickens in the cool and thins when the wind picks up. But the plain is not empty. It is *loud with animals*.

They are hard things, most of them. Flat coiled grazers the size of a plate, shelled in silica, that clamp to the rock and rasp it — you can hear them at work, a sound like a whetstone, hundreds of them, and where they've been the stone is polished to a mirror. Things like glass thimbles that ride the current in loose rafts and knock together, ticking. In the potholes, clickers: blind, pale, communing in a code of snaps. And the boom-things, which are the reason the noise has weather in it — soft hollow animals, bloated with gas, that swell and contract and send out a low note you feel in your sternum rather than hear. When the water rises, the boom-things sing. When it falls, they stop. The plain's whole mood turns on two centimeters of depth.

Everyone here finds their way by sound, because the light does no work. The sky is a lid of even gray, and the sun through it is a smear, not a shape. There are no shadows to speak of; the water just gets a little brighter where it's shallow and a little duller where it's deep, and that difference is the entire map. Dusk here doesn't fall. It thickens, slowly, and thickens back. You sleep when you're tired and not before.

The animals have their own rules. Stand still in one place for an hour and you'll notice the pattern: there are columns of sound and there are pockets of nothing, and the pockets move. The plain is full of what the locals call *the Quiet* — a wader, tall and thin and jointed, that hunts by making no noise at all. It isn't silent because it's stealthy; it's silent because it's *wrong*, a hole in the acoustic world, and the grazers can't hear it coming because there is nothing to hear. Everything else on the Sill lives inside the roar, and the Quiet is the one thing that lives in the gap. You learn to watch for it by watching what stops rasping.

There are marks here. Not many. A line of cairns runs north for a mile and a half — piled fieldstone, hand-set, each one about the height of a knee, and the water has been wearing at their bases so long that they sit in their own little bowls of stone, sunk to the shoulder. A weir crosses a shallow braid of the flow: two wings of stacked rock, angled, with a gap in the middle, and the men who built it are beyond all reckoning because the rock is cut with hand-tools and the hand-tools are stone, and the whole structure has the settled, softened look of a thing that stopped being new before the language that named it existed. Half a mile on, a hut on stilts, roof caved, the posts still standing in their sockets, the water flowing between them. A channel cut through a rise in the bedrock, straight as a rule, silting up. That's all. Four or five things, scattered across a plain you could walk for a day. Whoever lived here was never many, and never stayed.

The people who come through now — a few families, following the shallow water, dragging sledges of bone and lashed hide — know the rules and will tell you them plainly, in the way you tell someone not to step off a cliff.

Sound is property. A family's stretch of plain is the stretch where the pattern of the noise is familiar; you can hear a stranger's footfall three hundred yards off and know it isn't yours. To be quiet is to be either dead or hunting, and no one will fault you for assuming the worse.

Water is depth, and depth is everything. Move with the wind at your back if you can. When the boom-things go silent, get off the low ground; the flow can find a new channel in an afternoon and the new channel will be exactly where you were standing.

Nothing grows, so nothing is replaced. Every thing you leave behind will be there. The cairns were a promise and the cairns are still keeping it.

And the noise — the noise is not a nuisance, it is the *weather*. On still days the roar comes apart into a thousand separable sounds, and you can hear the grazers two fields over and the clickers in a pothole you can't see, and people say the plain is talking. On windy days it all fuses into one long bellow and you cannot hear your own sledge behind you, and you walk with your hand on it. And in the hour before true dark, when the light smear goes down to almost nothing, the boom-things take the whole plain at once and hold a single note that goes on and on, and the stone under your feet gives it back a half-step lower, and you feel it in your teeth.

You cannot talk here. Not out loud, not usefully. Two people who need to say something press their jaw against the other's jaw and speak into the bone. It's an intimate way to argue.
