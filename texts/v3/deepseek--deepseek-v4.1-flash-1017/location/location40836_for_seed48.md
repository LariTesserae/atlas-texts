# The Basin of the Warm Shallows

You come down into it by a ramp of packed crust, and the basin opens below like a plate set out and forgotten. The rim is not high — the whole floor lies below the surrounding country, which is why the water is here and why the air is here and does not leave. As you drop, the air thickens and warms and takes on the smell of wet chalk, hot iron, and something faintly like clean laundry. This is the last of the wind. Below the rim there is almost none, because a bowl full of warm damp air is a bowl full of still air.

## Terrain

The floor is not flat. It is a staircase, and the steps are made of stone that the water made. Warm springs come up through the crust along a line of vents, and where the water has run and cooled and dropped its load, it has built terraces — low travertine dams, each one a hand's width to a man's height, each holding a shallow pool that spills over its lip to the next. The pools are milky, faintly blue-white at the edges, and every one of them is warm. Some are so warm you can only hold a hand in them; others are like a bath left standing an hour.

Between the terraces are ridges of dry crust, white and grey and pale ochre, cracked into plates that ring hollow underfoot. The crust is thin. In most places you can put your heel through it and find water in the hole before you've straightened up. This is the real fact of the place: the water table is essentially the surface. The basin is not a marsh with water in it — it is water with a crust on it, and the crust is the only ground there is.

The smell is alkaline and mineral, not rotten. Nothing here rots the way a swamp rots. Nothing is decomposing in the ordinary way, because there is nothing here to decompose that you would recognize.

## The stone that grows

The material of the whole basin is one substance in different moods. Fresh, it is soft enough to cut with a knife. Dried, it is a pale, porous, faintly ringing stone that takes a nail and holds a screw badly. It grows where water splashes and dissolves where rain falls. Every wall in the town is doing both at once, at different heights: fattening at its base, thinning at its crown, so that a wall is never quite the same shape twice in a decade.

This is why the town has wall-dressers. They walk the streets with long planes and shave the growing stone back to true. The shavings pile up white at the foot of the wall and are swept into the channels and carried away and become, eventually, the wall again somewhere downstream.

Doors stopped fitting centuries ago. Every door in the town is hung on hinges that have been re-set, re-drilled, re-lengthened. You can read a building's age by the number of filled hinge-holes in its jamb, like tree rings.

## The works

The town is spread wide and built low, and from inside it you cannot see its plan, though from the rim it is perfectly legible: long straight channels, a grid of shallow pans, pump houses at the nodes, boiler houses at the junctions, all of it laid out along the terraces like a hand along a spine.

The industry here is brine and it is old and slow and in good order. Pumps lift warm brine from the vents and run it down the channels and spread it into the pans, and the pans do the rest. Each rectangle is at a different stage. One is clear; the next is milky; the next has a crust you could walk on; the next is a pink slurry being raked; the next is dry, and men with flat shovels are taking up soda ash and salt and potash in white windrows, and the windrows go into sacks, and the sacks go onto a cart on a rail, and the rail goes to the shed at the rim, and that is the whole of the enterprise.

The machinery is iron and ceramic and steam and belt. Everything ferrous weeps rust — the basin is warm and wet and the rust never stops, so the pumps are painted every year and bleed orange anyway. Some of the buildings are working; many are not. Some have been deliberately flooded and are now just deep square pools with a wall around them, and the wall is being shaved back to true by nobody.

## The overwhelming quiet

The water is alive, and the life is not plants. It is film — iridescent mats laid on the stone in copper and orange and a green like old bronze, slick underfoot, thick as felt in the warmest runs. That is the base of everything.

Grazing the mat are the shrimp. They are the overwhelming fact of the basin. In the cool of the morning the water is clear and you see them as a faint suspension, a dust in the water. By afternoon the pools are pink with them, and by evening opaque, a soup that thickens against the light. They do not make a sound you could call a sound. What they make is a texture of noise — a fine sift, a pattering, like rain on a distant tin roof, that never rises and never stops. It is the loudest thing in the basin and it is quieter than your own breath.

Under them, in the sediment, worms and snails with shells of the local mineral. Above them, the flies, in columns over the warm vents. And above that, the birds.

The birds are why the horizon looks the way it does — a low grey-brown band that shivers. Stilts and avocets and a few heavier waders, standing in the warm shallows in their thousands, motionless, for hours. They nest on the dry ridges, on the crust, and their eggs are speckled exactly like the ground. They are almost entirely silent. Once, near dark, one will call — a single rising note, thin as a wire — and then nothing for hours. They come here for the shrimp, which is the same reason the town is here. In the harvest weeks the drying floors are raked into pink rows, and the birds stand at the fence and watch, and the men ignore them, and both parties are being paid by the same warm water.

## The rhythm

The springs breathe. It is a slow cycle, hours long, and everything in the basin is on it. The water rises through the vents and the sluices open and the pans fill and the channels run high and the shrimp rise with the water and the birds shift and feed and settle; then the springs ease, the sluices close, the pans stand, the crust begins to take, and the town is briefly a place of shallow mirrors. Twice a day, roughly. Nobody has to be told. The wall-dressers work the low water. The rakers work the dry pans. The pumps run against the breathing so the pans are never quite empty.

At dawn everything drips. The condensation is total — every rail, every rope, every iron thing, every bird's back beaded. For an hour the basin is a place of small water falling on itself, and that hour is the loudest of the day, and it is not loud.

Light comes through a lid. The sun is a smeared coin; some days it is not a coin at all, only a brightening. The fog sits in the bowl and lights up amber in the afternoon and goes grey at dusk, and the lamps in the town come on while the sky is still bright, and there are not many of them and they are far apart, so that at night the settlement is a scatter of dim points with long dark between, and the fog around each point is a soft sphere of itself.

## What the place asks

Walk carefully, because the crust rings hollow and sometimes it does not hold. Keep your cuffs out of the water; the salt will stiffen them and the soda will eat them. Drink from the town, not the ground. Your clothes will not dry — nothing dries here except in the pans, and the pans are engineered for it.

Nothing is ruined and nothing is new. The stone grows and the stone dissolves, and the town has been sitting at that balance long enough that it no longer fights it — it shaves, it re-hangs, it repaints. The water comes up warm and goes away as salt and shrimp and bird, and the whole basin is one process running at a slow even rate, and you are standing in it, and it does not much mind.
