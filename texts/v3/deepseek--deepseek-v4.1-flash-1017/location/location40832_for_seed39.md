# The Kettle

The first thing wrong with the mountain is that it has no rubble.

Every old mountain wears a skirt of its own debris — talus, scree, the fans of broken stone that mean the weather has been at it for a hundred thousand years. The Kettle has none. Its fractures are clean. Its faces are planes and edges that have never been rounded, and when you put your hand on them they cut. The mountain is not eroding. It is arriving. It is coming up out of the crust faster than the frost and the rain can take it apart, and so it has the look of something half-unpacked — surfaces that are still the inside of the rock, slick and glassy where they slipped past each other, unweathered, raw as a broken bone. The shape you see is not the shape it has; it's the shape it is about to have. Whole valleys are still deciding where to be.

It stands high enough that it makes its own weather, and the weather is mostly cloud. For hours every morning the summit is inside a cloud — not fog drifting past but a cloud that has come to sit, that the mountain has pulled up around itself and holds. Everything is wet. The rock weeps. Water stands in every hollow and runs in threads down every plane, and the threads join and part and vanish into cracks and come out again lower down, and some of them come out as steam.

Because the mountain is warm underneath. This is the second thing. The stone itself is warm — not hot, warm, the temperature of a body — because it is new rock and it has not finished cooling, because it is still being pushed up from below and the pushing makes heat. Put your palm flat on a fresh face in the cold air and it is like touching something asleep. And where the water gets down deep enough and comes back, it comes back hot, and it comes back carrying things: sulfur, salt, iron, a fine white bloom of minerals that crusts the lips of every crack it passes. The vents are not dramatic. They are hairline, most of them, seeping and hissing, and a few are the width of a wrist and breathe steadily, and one or two are big enough to stand in the steam of, if you wanted to, which you would not for long.

So the place works. That is the thing to understand about the Kettle: it is a works with no works in it. It takes in water, heats it, drives it through fissures, throws off the waste as steam, condenses it back on the cold stone above, and lays down its product in crusts and terraces and colored stains — ochre, sulfur-yellow, a deep rust-red where the iron comes out. It does this at scale, continuously, with no one tending it, and it has been doing it since before there was anyone to notice. The whole summit is a distillation column the size of a town, and it runs on pressure and heat and its own weight, and the only exhaust is the mist you are standing in.

Nothing here is built. There is no trail, no cairn, no stake, no mark of anybody's passage at all — and the mountain is not the kind of place that keeps a mark. If people have come up, they have left nothing, and the frost has taken even that. There is not so much as a fire ring. The only structures are the ones the water has made: sinter terraces stepping down a warm slope, shallow basins rimmed with salt, a cave of sorts where a vent has eaten a hollow into soft stone. They look designed. They are not.

---

Life is here in abundance, and almost all of it is in about one percent of the place.

The mountain is enormous and bare and cold, kilometers of wet grey stone with nothing on it, and then you come around a shoulder and there is a warm seam in the rock, and the seam is *full*. Flies the size of a grain of rice, in sheets, standing on the warm stone with their wings folded, so dense they look like a fur. Mites in the wet mineral mats, grazing the bacterial skin that the warm water grows — the mats are orange and white and they are the pasture, and everything else is either eating the mats or eating what eats the mats. A kind of squat, wingless cricket that lives its whole life within a meter of one vent and would die of cold in an hour if it walked away. Spiders that have learned the vents and web across them. And in the wet cracks where the steam condenses, small pale things that are almost certainly new to anyone who would care.

Between the seams, nothing. You can walk an hour across the summit and see no living thing at all except your own breath. That is the rhythm of the place: long emptiness, then a city. The life is punctate. It is a scatter of small hot towns on a cold empty continent, and each town has its own population, its own food chain, its own little economy of heat.

Above that, birds. Martins that nest on the warm faces and hunt the fly-sheets, and a heavier grey raptor — the only large animal that is regularly here — that has worked out that the colonies are a reliable meal and the updrafts off the warm slopes will hold it up without a wingbeat. It hangs. It watches. It drops.

And something with hooves comes up at night, when the cold is worst and the warm ground is worth the climb. You will not see it. You will find where it lay.

---

The sound of the Kettle is mostly not sound. It is quiet enough that a single drip is an event — you hear one, and then you wait, and then you hear another. Under that, if you stop moving, there is a hiss that is not quite a hiss, a low continuous venting that you feel in the stone through your boots more than you hear. The wind works the notches and makes a thin note in some of them and nothing in others. The fly-sheets have a sound, a dry papery rustle, if you get close. The rock cracks when it cools at night, one sharp report, and then nothing again for a long time. The raptor does not call.

The light is grey. That is the whole of it. The cloud comes and the light becomes flat and sourceless and the world loses its shadows and its depth and you cannot tell a near face from a far one. Twice, three times a day the cloud thins and the sun comes through as a white glare off the wet stone and everything is briefly, painfully bright, and then the cloud closes. There is no golden hour here. There is no warm light. The place is a wash of grey and black and the mineral colors, which are the only saturated things in it.

---

The rules, then, as best as they can be told:

**The warmth is the only wealth.** Everything that lives here lives near it or visits it. There is no other resource. Distance from a warm seam is the single measure of a thing's life — a meter is a home, ten meters is a journey, a hundred is a death.

**Nothing is finished.** The streams run one way this year and another way after the next rockfall. The terraces grow and then the vent shifts and they dry and a new terrace starts somewhere else. The mountain adds a few millimeters of height a year and takes a few away, and adds more than it takes, and so the whole thing is a process you are standing in the middle of rather than a place you are standing on. It has no climax. It has no old-growth. It is all young.

**Nothing is made, and nothing is kept.** There are no artifacts because there is no one to make them, and if there were, the wet and the frost and the mineral water would take them in a season. The mountain does not remember. It is not hostile; it simply has no surface for memory to stick to.

**And it is indifferent to scale.** The same processes that lay down a hand-sized terrace also run the whole summit, and you cannot tell from the shape of a thing whether it is a centimeter or a kilometer across until something breaks the pattern — a bird, your own boot. That is the vertigo of the Kettle. The place is empty and open and quiet, and it is running at full industrial tilt the whole time, and it will go on running exactly like this whether or not anyone ever stands in the steam again.
