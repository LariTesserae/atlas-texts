# The Sieve

Come at it from below and there is no town to see — only a shoulder of young mountain with its hide still on, grey-blue stone split into slabs that have not yet learned to be boulders. The fractures are clean. The glacial striations are still legible, grooves running one way like handwriting on a fresh page. Nothing here has been rounded off yet. The rock rings underfoot.

The hollow is a scoop in that shoulder, maybe two hundred paces across, rimmed by broken slabs, and it is colder than the ground around it. Cold air drains down off the ridge and pools there the way water would, sits, and does not leave. So the temperature drops at the lip of it, a step down, a change of air like entering a cellar. Your breath goes white and stays white.

And below the lip, everything is plant.

Not forest. Forest implies gaps, trunks with air between them, a floor you could kneel on. Here the growth has closed. The hollow is filled to its rim with living mass — a spongy root-felt for ground, and out of that felt a saturated thicket of pale stems, intergrown, crossed and re-crossed until there is no line of sight longer than a few paces. The colors are cold colors: bone-green, tallow, sulphur, old ivory, and in the deeper layers where growth has died standing, rust and oxblood and a wet dark brown. No leaves worth the name — fronds, straps, blades, spore-fans. Everything surfaces wet. The air is at its condensation point and the plants are colder than the air, so the water comes out on them: beaded, filmed, running in slow threads down every stem. It is always raining here, very slightly, from no cloud.

This is the whole of the town. There is nothing in it that is not grown.

The ground is a mat of root, and under the mat is not soil but older root, and under that, older still, going down four or five layers into a midden of its own dead. And throughout that accumulated mass — bored through it, hollowed out of it, roofed by it — are chambers. Galleries. Streets.

You can walk in it. That is the thing you understand after a while: this is a settlement, and it is small, and it is dense, and it is inhabited in the way a hive is inhabited. The corridors are the gaps the growth has left, some of them wide enough for two people, most of them not. The floors are intergrown stems laid flat and pressed by their own weight. The walls are root-walls, packed solid, sprouting. The roofs are woven — you can see the weave — fronds drawn over and bound by other fronds, cordage of grown tissue, tight enough to hold off the drip and let it run to the edges. Over each roof another roof, and above that the older roofs, dead, structural, gone soft with rot but still bearing. The town is stratigraphy. New growth on last century's growth on the century before. You can read the age of a wall the way you read a bank.

There are channels everywhere. That is the strange heart of the place, and once you see it you cannot unsee it: the whole town is a machine for catching water, and it has no parts that are not alive. Every surface faces the cold sky and gathers rime. Every frond is angled to shed what it gathers. The shed water runs into cupped hollows — cisterns, dozens of them, lined with a living skin that keeps them from leaking — and from the cisterns into gutters of plaited root, and down through hollow stems that run like pipework through the walls, and so to the chambers where water is wanted. The town drinks the weather. It has been arranged for this — deliberately, over a long time, by nothing with hands. No one tends it and it is tended.

At the low end the hollow opens into a marsh, standing water gone skinned with ice in the cold hours, cushions of moss holding it, frost heave shouldering the fresh stones up out of the wet in little rings. The marsh advances and retreats by the season, and where it advances the town grows into it, and where it retreats the town dies back, so the whole settlement is very slowly migrating downhill — creeping across the hollow a handspan a decade, building ahead of itself and abandoning behind. If you went down into the oldest chambers at the dry rim you would find them collapsed, hollow, hung with dry rot and the papery skins of old cisterns, and nothing living but a pale fuzz.

The light is poor. Overcast, and what gets through the canopy is blue-grey and flat and arrives late and leaves early. In the upper galleries you can see by it. Lower down it thins to a murk, and there the pale shelf-fungi take over, growing in brackets from the root-walls, giving off a weak green-white gleam that is just enough to keep you from walking into a wall. It is not bright. It is the light of a winter afternoon that never quite ends.

Sound is mostly water. Drip into cisterns, a hundred small irregular tones; the tick and creak of freezing in the moss; a slow sigh when the wind crosses the ridge and the whole canopy leans a little; sometimes a knock from deep in a stem where a column of water has lost its air. Now and then, rarely, something that is not water: a single low note from an animal you will not see, three times, and then an hour of dripping.

The animals are few and you will mostly find their leavings instead of them. A shed skin in a cistern. Gnaw-marks spiraling inside a dead stem. A burrow in the root-mat, warm, empty. Once, a slow pale amphibian the length of your hand, sitting in the water with its throat going, unbothered.

The rules of the place are simple and it keeps them. Nothing here is thrown away: the dead growth is the cistern lining and the floor and the soil and eventually the water. Nothing is built that was not grown, and nothing is grown that does not catch water, and the water goes where the channels send it, and the channels were made by the growth, and the growth is the town, and the town is one continuous thing that has been arguing with the cold for a few hundred years — young yet, still sharp, still finding its edges on the fresh stone.

It is small enough to cross in minutes. It will take you much longer than that, because it is packed to saturation and the way through is not straight, and because you will keep stopping to watch the water come out of the air.
