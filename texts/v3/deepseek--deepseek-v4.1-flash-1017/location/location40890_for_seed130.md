# The Sump

You come down into it by a stair that is no longer a stair. The treads have been rounded by centuries of water into a series of shallow bowls, each one holding a film of water an inch deep, each one spilling into the next with a sound like a struck glass the size of a room. The descent is gentle — the whole region is low, a wide dish rather than a valley — and by the fortieth bowl you have stopped noticing the individual spills, because they have joined the roar.

The roar is the first fact of the place. It is not a noise you hear so much as a pressure you stand inside. Somewhere out in the dark there are outflows, thousands of them, water dropping from lip to lip through a structure too large to see, and the whole thing has been tuned by its own erosion into a single resonant body. It comes up through the bowls of the stair, through the soles of your feet, through the bones of the jaw. You will shout once, out of animal habit, and the shout will be taken from your mouth and not returned. Speech is over. From here you communicate by touch, by the pressure of a hand on a shoulder, by the direction you lean.

The ground is not ground. Underfoot is a laminate — poured slag laid over sintered ash laid over something glassy and dark that rings faintly when struck — and it goes down further than anyone has cared to follow. There is no soil anywhere in the Sump. There is no bedrock in the sense of a thing that was always there. Everything you stand on was made, and then the water got into it, and then the centuries did their work. The edges are gone. Every corner is a curve. The surface has the soft, deliberate look of a stone in a river, except that the stone is a floor, and the river has been running for a very long time.

The water is a hand's width deep across most of the open ground, moving without hurry toward the low places, and it is cool — not cold, cool the way a cellar is cool, a temperature that has given up arguing. It carries a faint mineral slick that makes it feel heavier than water should. In the hollows it gathers into pools the size of courtyards, and the pools have their own architecture: scalloped rims, terraces of pale precipitate, small cones where a drip has been landing for so long that it has built the thing it lands on. You can read the age of a hollow by counting its terraces. Some of them have hundreds.

The structures stand far apart. That is the other fact of the Sump: the emptiness between things. A stack will rise out of the sheet-flow a kilometer from the next stack, and between them there is only water and the softened floor and the roar. No two are alike anymore. Some are chimneys whose mouths have sagged closed into lips. Some are the shells of basins, wide and low and filled to their brims with a still black water that never spills. Some are shapes you cannot name and do not need to — the housings of machines that stopped being recognizable before the language that named them stopped being spoken. The upper works are mostly open to the air now; the roofs went first.

What still runs, runs. There is motion everywhere in the Sump, but it is slow motion, the motion of things that have settled into their final form and see no reason to hurry: pumps that cycle once in the time it takes to walk a mile, valves that open and close on a thermal rhythm, a great vertical shaft in the middle distance that exhales a column of warm damp air and then inhales, exhales and inhales, and the sound of it is a low note under everything, a note you feel in the chest and not the ear. The place is active. It is not alive. These are different things, and the Sump makes the difference plain.

The air is saturated. You do not see fog; you are in fog, permanently, and it is warm against cool skin in a way that is almost a comfort. Condensation beads on every surface and runs in threads down the curves of the stacks, and where it runs it leaves a faint brightness, a scum of mineral that catches what light there is. That is how you find your way: not by seeing the structures, but by seeing the wet lines on them, the pale trails of water that has been going down the same path for longer than you can think about.

The light is almost nothing. There is no sun here, no sky worth the name — a ceiling of low vapour that never breaks and never fully darkens either, holding a dim grey-brown glow like the inside of a closed eye. What you can see, you see by that, and by the faint green-white shine that comes up out of certain pools, a luminescence in the precipitate itself, cold and even and utterly without flicker. It is not bright. It is the amount of light you would get from a single lamp left burning in a warehouse, and it is spread over miles. Your eyes will open wide and stay wide. You will learn to see by silhouette and by the shine of wet stone, and you will stop trusting edges.

The plants, if that is the word, keep to the seams. They grow where the sheet-flow slows and the mineral is rich — flat dark mats, furred and slightly damp, spreading across a joint in the floor or up the base of a stack in a shape that follows the water's memory rather than any plan of its own. They are sparse. You can walk twenty minutes and pass three of them. They are not green. Under the pools they go pale and translucent and put out fine threads that comb the current; above water they are nearly black and dry to the touch and smell of iron. Nothing eats them. Nothing grazes them. They simply spread, a finger's width a decade, and the water brings them what they need, and they are the only soft thing in the Sump.

There are no animals. No birds in the vapour, no insects on the mats, no fish in the black basins. The Sump is loud and moving and utterly without company. This is the strangeness of the place, and it takes days to name: the activity is all process, and none of it is life. The pumps cycle, the water climbs and falls, the mats spread, the drips build their cones — and nothing watches. Nothing is hiding. You are the only thing here that wants anything.

The water keeps a tide, though there is no moon to pull it. Twice a day the sheet-flow deepens and the roar rises by a note, and if you are standing in a low place the water will come to your knees and then to your thighs and then it will stop, and go back down, and leave a fresh bright line of mineral on everything it touched. The cycle is regular enough to keep time by. People who work the Sump say the tide is the only clock that matters, because the sound will not let you hear a watch and the dark will not let you read one.

Travel is by touch and memory. You go from stack to stack, and between them you keep your hand on a guide-line — a pipe, a rail, a ridge in the floor, anything that runs in a known direction — and you follow it until it ends, and then you stand still in the dark and the roar and you feel for the next. Distances lie. A kilometer here feels like three, and a hundred meters can feel like none at all, because the sound fills everything and the fog takes the rest. When you meet another person it is a shock, a hand closing on your arm out of the grey, and for a moment you will both just stand there, breathing, absurdly glad of a warm thing in all that cool constructed emptiness.

And under the whole Sump, faint as a pulse, there is the note of the shaft that breathes — in and out, in and out — and the water going over the bowls of the stair, and the drips building their cones, and the deep tuned roar of a place that was built, and used, and abandoned, and has spent a very long time becoming exactly what it now is.
