# The Keeper's Splinter

There is no water here. Not a pool, not a thread of runoff in a groove, not a bead of dew at dawn. The air is dry enough that after a minute of breathing it your mouth feels like paper, and the sky overhead is a hard, pale, cloudless blue with nothing in it that could ever fall. And yet the whole top of this place is green — packed, glossy, crowded green, layered three and four bodies deep, so dense that you only see the stone where something has cut it.

The place is small. You could walk it end to end in the time it takes to tell a story: an irregular raft of pale mineral, a few hundred paces long, lifted maybe forty meters above a plain of dry white dust that goes on in every direction with almost nothing in it. The raft's edges are cliffs, and the cliffs are new. You can see it at a glance — the fracture faces are still glassy, the strata still show their clean broken lips and the slicken-sheen where one layer slid off another, the talus at the foot is angular and has not yet been rounded by anything. Wind here is weak and patient and the stone has had no time to soften. The whole splinter came up recently, freshly shouldered out of the plain, and nothing has had the decades it would need to sand its corners down.

On top of it, though, is a town that looks very old.

## The water that is not loose

Understand the first rule and the rest follows: moisture exists here only inside living bodies. Nothing pools, nothing flows, nothing condenses. The organisms of this place have taken the whole world's water and locked it in tissue, and they will not give it back. Every plant you see is a vessel. The leaves are not leaves; they are bladders, thick-skinned, waxy, shut tight as a fist, and faintly translucent — hold your hand behind one and you see the green-gold shimmer of the fluid inside it, warm and slightly refractive, the only liquid in the world. Some are the size of a knuckle. Some are the size of a head. They crowd the stone in clusters and tiers, and they knock together in the weak wind with a papery, hollow sound, and they do not lose a drop.

So the green here is not the soft green of wet places. It is hard, glazed, varnished — olive and jade and a brassy yellow-green, with patches of rust-red and sulfur and wine-dark where the tissue is doing something else. The colors are saturated past what you would expect from a dry place, because all the fluid they need is already inside them, held at the temperature of blood. Put your palm on the flank of a big bladder at night and it is warm.

This is why the living things cluster. At dusk, when the temperature drops off the high stone, the whole summit radiates like an animal, and everything that cannot make its own heat comes in to be near it.

## The town

The architecture is grown. There is no cut timber here, no fired clay, no metal, nothing that was shaped by a hand holding a tool — and yet the summit is as organized as any settlement: chambers, galleries, buttressed walls, sloped aprons of cemented grit, terraces stepping down the leeward face, vents, stairs of thin protruding flanges, enclosed courtyards open to the sky.

Its builders are small and pale and eyeless and they move in slow tides, a colonial organism the size of a thumb-joint, working in the interstices of the bladder-plants. They feed on the plants' sealed fluid, and they excrete a cement — mineral grit bound in resin — and they lay it down at about a millimeter a year. A single chamber is a lifetime. A wall is several. They are not in a hurry because there is nothing here that rewards hurry: no flood to outrun, no season to beat, only the long accumulation of the dry and the warm.

The result is a town that looks like a reef that learned to build rooms. The floors are packed frass and grit, warm and slightly yielding underfoot, and they hold the shape of the bodies that have walked them so long the paths are worn into shallow grooves. The walls are laminated — you can count the years in them like tree rings, and in the older walls you find the mummified dead of earlier inhabitants pressed in as filler, desiccated to leather and then to stone-likeness, still faintly recognizable. Nothing rots here. With no free water there is nothing for rot to work in. The dead dry out and are re-used.

The infrastructure is all biological and all slow. There are fluid stores — sealed pods in the deep chambers, held in trust against a lean year. There are flues that carry warm dry air up from the sunlit faces and out through vents at the crest. There are dust-gardens: shallow trays of mineral powder where something grows that likes nothing at all. And there is a canopy. The highest tier of bladder-plants is spread into flat plates that track the sun; at midday they fold shut, one after another, down the whole summit, and the town goes dim and cool and quiet inside itself. At dusk they open again with a soft leathery sigh that runs across the roof of the place like something breathing out.

## Everything else that lives here

The fauna is abundant and small and packed in close. Things with plated backs the color of the dust. Things that live entirely inside the galleries and never come out. Things that hang from the underside of plates. A great many of them are there for the moisture and not the meat, and they have evolved to pierce rather than to chew: hollow spikes, awl-bodies, proboscises like drawn glass, a whole guild of small robbers whose entire way of life is to find a seam in a sealed body and open it. Some bladder-plants answer with armor — crusts of mineral, spines, false skins that bleed a useless dry sap. Some answer with decoys. A few have gone the other way and take the robbers instead, digesting them slowly in a closed cavity, not for the flesh but for the water in it.

And above and around all of it, the sound — or the lack of it. There is almost nothing to hear. Dry air carries poorly and this is a small high place in a still world. What you get instead is texture: the tick of small feet on grit, the creak andsettle of growing cement, the papery knock of bladders against each other, the faint sub-audible thrum of the colony working in the walls — felt in the soles of your feet more than heard. Nothing sings here. Nothing calls. The animals communicate by touch and by chemistry, and the quiet is not peaceful; it is a condition of the world, the way cold is a condition of a glacier. When a robber opens a bladder, the sound is a single soft pop, and then the fluid is gone into something's body and the world is as dry as before.

## Light and time

The light is ordinary and hard. High elevation, thin clean air, sun on white stone — at midday the exposed rock is bright enough to make you squint, and the shadows are sharp as cuts because everything casting them is sharp too. The warm comes on slowly through the morning and sits on the summit all afternoon, and the town folds up under it, and then in the evening the plates open and the place stirs and the warmth begins to seep back out of the walls into the cooling air, and the animals come in.

Nothing happens fast. The cement goes down a millimeter a year. The bladders swell and shrink on a cycle longer than a year, rationing what they hold. In a lean span the whole summit dims and pulls in, and the fluid stores in the deep chambers drop, and the colony slows almost to stopping, and the town waits — patiently, the way a thing waits that has nothing to do but outlast the dry. Then the pods fill again and the green comes back up glossy and the building resumes. The stone underneath is younger than the town on top of it: the splinter was punched up recently and carried its old crowded settlement with it, and the fresh raw cliffs are the new part, still glassy, still sharp, still unweathered, while the life on the summit goes on with a business it began long before the summit was this high.

Walk to the edge and the green stops within a hand's width of the drop. Beyond is the plain, white and featureless and nearly lifeless, going out to a haze. The boundary is exact and it is not gradual: on this side, everything is shut and full and warm and crowded and patient; on that side, nothing.
