The first thing is the smell — not of rot or bloom, but of warm stone and old water, the way a coast smells hours after the tide has gone out. **Vaeless** breathes slowly. The air is thick, wet-napped, the kind that beads on your forearms without falling; clouds hang low and grey-gold and never quite commit to rain, though they do, three or four times a day, in short soft passes that leave every surface sweating. The light beneath them is dim by definition — a filtered, underwater sort of noon that never gets brighter than a cathedral on a cloudy day, and never truly dark, because the city is full of its own pale glow.

The land is new. That's the strange thing to hold in your head. Vaeless sits in a basin only a few centuries out of the sea — the ground still rising, still remembering the water. The low ridges around the valley are sharp-jawed and unweathered, pale rock fresh enough that you can trace the old shorelines in it like rings in a cut tree. Nothing here has been rounded down yet. The basin shallows slope into a broad, soft floodplain where the streets begin, and the buildings seem to have been *set* into the young earth rather than built up from it, as though the city grew like a crystal does, predisposed. Everything is level, ground-hugging, low. You can see the whole of it from a low rise: a wide, quiet dish of a town, stone and glass and something glassier than glass, scattered like something spilled and left to settle.

**The built world.** The architecture is grown or printed or disgorged — the verbs don't quite work. Walls are pale, faintly translucent, veined with darker lines that look organic and are not; they carry light through themselves and dim it, so at night the city glows like a held lantern rather than burning like a torch. The streets are wide — far wider than a sparse population needs, generous with emptiness — and surfaced in something warm-grey that drinks the rain within minutes of it falling. Between the buildings, thick stands of trees and shrubs crowd in on every side, undomesticated, spilling over the walkways, and yet the city's conduits and infrastructure *bend around them* — the gutters, the walkways, the low transit rails all take quiet detours to spare a grove, a pond, a single old fig. No one seems to have fought this. The city was planned to be small in its footprint and large in its rooms, and to let the green come back where it could.

The **water** is the city's quiet signature. There is no river to speak of; the rain comes, and the land holds it in. Every tenth building has a cistern under it, open to the sky in a sunken courtyard the locals call a *well-court* — wide, knee-deep pools ringed with moss and reeds and a low hum of insects. In the morning, before the day's first rain, these are the brightest things in Vaeless, catching the grey-gold sky and returning it in pale coins. The water is warm and clean and smells faintly mineral. Children — the few of them — wade; the rest of the population does not swim; the courtyards are for sitting, for talking quietly, for the way the water holds the sky and gives you somewhere to put your eyes when the conversation runs out.

**The light and the hour.** Because the sun is dim and the day is long, the city runs on a soft, slow pulse. Behind the pale walls, the indefinite tech does most of the daily work, and the few people you meet walk with the unhurried patience of those who are not needed for the labour of the town. It is *quiet* — a careful quiet. The distant hum of the conduits is below hearing until you've been here an hour; the rain, when it comes, is the loudest thing in the city, drumming on the cistern courtyards and the flat pale roofs. The people, when they pass you, speak low, as though the buildings were asleep.

**The rules of the place.** The strange thing — the thing your body notices before your mind — is the **spatial patience** of the city. Doors open onto rooms larger than the skin of the building allows; corridors that should be three paces long go on for twelve; but nobody treats this as odd, and you shouldn't either. The buildings are not illusions. They are simply *bigger inside than out*, and the town's builders set that feature in centuries ago the way you'd fit a window. There is a low **contract of maintenance**: the conduits, the cisterns, the light-carrying walls are never mended, and never fail; nobody can explain the mechanism, and nobody tries. The city *keeps itself*. The inhabitants treat this the way a town might treat an old clock that has never needed winding — with a certain courtesy, a sort of politeness to the place they live in.

The people are **sparse** in this wide dish. You'll walk half a kilometre between encounters — the city is designed for a population three or four times its own, and the emptiness has a shape, a *presence* — the way an unoccupied chair is present in a room. It is not lonely. It is generous. There is room to think, and the pale walls make a soft bright halo around your own quiet.

**Ecology and the living.** The green has been let back — or was never pushed far out. Figs and olives and pale-barked trees of varieties you don't have a name for, thick shrubbery full of small unbothered birds, moss stitching the low ridges. The fauna are **rare and slow**: a few birds, some long-legged waders at the well-courts, something horned and calm that grazes the floodplain at the edges of town and is avoided by custom, not fear. The insects are the busiest thing here, and they don't bite. The **dynamic** pulse of the place is this: rain comes up in the afternoon, the courtyards fill, the green surges, the low ridges lose a little more of themselves into the basin, the city holds it all and steadies it. Early-formation, still becoming — the whole valley is *mid-sentence*.

**The sound** is what will stay with you. The rain's soft drumming. The faint mineral trickle of water into the cisterns. The low near-silence of the conduits. The birdsong, restrained. The way a voice carries further than it should here, through wide empty streets of pale stone — so that the quiet is not the absence of sound but the *room* the city has given sound to travel in.

And somewhere out on the floodplain, at the edge of the light, one of the verges collapses a little further toward the basin, softly, with a sound like a coin dropped in a well, and no one looks up.
