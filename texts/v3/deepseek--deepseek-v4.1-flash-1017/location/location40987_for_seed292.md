# The Harrows

You come to it from the north along the Fell, and you know you're close before you can see anything, because the water starts to argue.

The Fell is a cold current, and it comes down out of the higher latitudes at a walking pace, and it is the colour of unpolished tin. Then it meets the Counter — warm, slow, coming up from the south — and the two don't mix so much as collide, and everything above the Harrows turns into a zone of standing waves. This is the first thing to understand: this place is a mountain range drowned to its shoulders, and its shoulders are being hit by two oceans at once.

## The terrain

The Harrows were once enormous. Nobody can tell you how enormous, because the erosion here is finished. Not ongoing — finished. Every edge has been rounded off, every peak worn to a whaleback, every gully polished into a long smooth scoop. The rock is grey-green, and it has the texture of something handled for a very long time. You can lay your palm flat on a summit and feel nothing at all — no grain, no roughness, only a faint soapy slickness from the algae. There is no rubble anywhere. The rubble was removed by currents sometime before the first fish.

What is left is a range of domes, saddles, and kettles, and the domes are still mountains because they started out as something the size of a continent's spine and even near-flattened they rise three and four thousand metres off the eastern floor. That's the trick of the place: the shapes are gentle and the relief is monstrous. Between the domes the ground falls away into a trench with no visible other side, and the wall of that trench is the only steep thing left in the world — a smooth, cold, greenish drop that goes down past where the light gives out.

The highest backs come within a few metres of the surface. At slack water you can stand on them, waist-deep, in the wind, with the swell shouldering past you, and look down through clear water at a landscape going away beneath your feet in green and blue for as far as the water lets you see. It is like standing on the roof of a flooded cathedral.

## The water

The surface is never flat. The Fell and the Counter build their argument into a permanent field of standing waves over the central saddle — the Kettle — waves that don't travel anywhere, just stand and grind and hiss, three metres high, in the same place, all day, for as long as anyone has been counting. Around them the water opens whirlpools the width of a house and closes them again. The tide is not lunar. It goes with the currents, and slack water lasts eleven minutes, and everything that lives or works here schedules itself around those eleven minutes.

Then, twice a day, the bore. It arrives from the east as a single white line, a wall of water a metre and a half tall moving faster than a person can run, and it crosses the whole of the Kettle in a little under four minutes, and then it's gone, and the standing waves have changed their mind about where they were.

The rain here is not weather so much as atmosphere. Cold, fine, almost horizontal, coming up off the surface rather than down onto it. On the calm days — the eleven-minute ones — you get sun, and the whole green plateau of the Harrows lights up under it, and you can see the drowned saddles laid out like a map.

## The growth

It's lush in a way that surprises people who expect cold water to be barren. The saddles are forest. Pipe kelp — thick as a thigh at the holdfast, with gas-bladders the size of fists — grows up from thirty metres down and lays its canopy flat on the surface, and the canopy is continuous across whole valleys, so that from above the Harrows look like a field of brown-green leather that breathes. When the swell lifts it, the whole field lifts, and you hear it: a soft continuous clatter of bladders knocking together, tens of thousands of them, like a distant crowd.

Under the canopy it's a green-lit nave. Fronds go up past you. Fish hang in layers at different depths, each species holding its own band. Blood-moss — a deep red cushion algae — coats every upward-facing rock in a felt two centimetres thick, and where the domes break the surface it gives way to a slick olive mat of intertidal weed that stinks, richly, of iodine when the sun is on it.

And on the crests, in the seams and pockets where fresh water runs off at low tide, there are the only flowering things for a hundred kilometres: a small tough rosette plant, wind-pollinated, that grows in cushions no wider than a hand and has no business being here at all.

## The animals

Abundant, and loud, and unbothered. Fur seals haul out on the crowns in numbers that make the rock look shingled. The birds are the loudest thing after the water: auk-analogues that don't fly so much as fall with purpose, and gull-analogues that do fly and complain about it continuously, and a big slate-coloured skua that will take a fish out of your hand while you're holding it. Underwater the density is worse — shoals so thick they turn the water a different colour, a slow armoured bottom-feeder that grubs along the moss, and something large and grey that you mostly see the back of, once, from a distance, and then not again.

Everything here is either rock or animal. There is no soil. Nothing decays into ground; it decays into someone's meal. That is part of why the place feels so airless and so busy at the same time — no rot, no dust, no softness except the weed.

## What people left

Very little, and all of it still working, which is the unsettling part.

On the western crown there is the Pump — a squat brick and iron house with a wheel in the channel beneath it, built to pull power out of the current. It was abandoned a long time ago. The wheel is still turning. The iron has gone to a laminated orange rust, the brick is furred with moss, the roof is half gone, and the wheel still turns because the Fell still runs and no one ever came to take the brake off. It makes a low gear-groan, once every four seconds, that you can feel in your teeth from a hundred metres away.

Below the Kettle, at thirty metres, there's a drowned works — chimneys, a gantry, a row of pylons whose tops break the surface in a line like a fence across open water. The pylons are festooned with kelp and the cables are gone. Fish live in the chimneys. The whole thing has been so thoroughly taken over by the place that it reads less as ruin than as an unusually angular reef.

And scattered across the crowns, half a dozen shacks: tarred board, corrugated sheet, bolted down hard against the bore. Two or three are used. Whoever stays in them knows the eleven minutes and doesn't waste them.

## The rules of the world

Erosion finished here means nothing is loose. You cannot dig, because there's nothing to dig. You cannot build anything that relies on friction with the ground, because the ground is polished. Anything you leave standing gets scoured to a shape and stays that shape forever. A steel post becomes a soft grey finger. A shack becomes a smooth thing with no corners.

Below about twenty metres the noise stops. The standing waves are a surface phenomenon and the roar doesn't reach down. It goes cold, and quiet, and very blue, and the domes go on beneath you into the dark, and you can see for sixty metres in water that hasn't been disturbed.

And the light: normal light, but distributed wrongly. The surface takes everything. Under the canopy it's a green dusk all day. On the domes at slack water it is brutally bright — rock, weed, seals, rust, all bleached and glittering — and then the bore comes through and the whole plateau goes white, and then grey, and then the eleven minutes end, and it starts again.

Stand on a crown at slack water in the wind. The kelp clatters below you, the Pump groans, the bore is two minutes out and you can hear it coming as a hiss that becomes a roar. Everything here is old enough to be finished and busy enough to be brand new, and both of those things are the same thing, and the water goes on arguing with itself.
