# The Quill Field

You hear it before the last ridge gives way — and this is the first wrongness, because there is no wind. The air on the approach is dead still, cool and thin, and yet a sound comes up over the crest like the inside of a struck bell stretched out forever: a roar made of thousands of small falling waters, each one pitched, each one held.

Then you top the ridge and the field is below you, and it is white.

Not snow. The whole basin — a shallow high valley, three or four kilometres from lip to lip — is packed to the last centimetre with standing columns. They are mineral, cool to the touch and faintly translucent, something between quartz and old ice and the inside of a shell; they rise from a hand's breadth above the water to the height of two or three men, and they stand so close together that between most of them there is no gap you could get a knife into. They are not scattered. They are *set*, like teeth, like a comb, like a forest that stopped being a forest and decided to be architecture instead.

They are all wet.

The water is the second wrongness. It condenses out of the air — the air here is cold enough and heavy enough with moisture that it simply gives itself up — and it beads on every column, gathers, and runs. Not in sheets. In threads: one thread down each facet, always the same facet, always the same thread. The columns are fluted where this has been happening, scalloped, worn into waists and lips and little spiral grooves, and the carving is beautiful and finished. It is not going to get more beautiful. Nothing in this valley is going to get more finished than it already is.

Between the bases, the water stands in a continuous sheet, a palm deep, and it is exactly a palm deep everywhere, because the floor beneath it is a mosaic of shallow channels of a single depth, and the flow through them is so perfectly matched to what falls from above that the level does not rise or fall. You can watch it. You can watch it for an hour. It does not rise or fall.

The sound is that water, and it is enormous. Thousands of threads, thousands of drips into a shallow pan, and the aggregate of them is not noise but *tone* — a standing chord that never resolves, has no melody, and does not vary by so much as a hair between one moment and the next. Some of the columns are resonators; condensation finds them at their own pitches. Put your hand flat against one and you feel a hum in the mineral like a struck tuning fork that has forgotten how to stop. There are no echoes. Shout, and your voice goes out a few metres and is simply eaten. The field does not answer you and does not return you.

The light is pitiless. The sky is a hard milky white — high, thin, cloud that never breaks and never thickens — and the light comes down through it undimmed and strikes the wet columns and breaks into glare. Light climbs the facets, pools in the grooves, throws itself off the water sheet. There are shadows, technically, in the packed slots between the columns, but they are cool grey things, not dark, and there is nowhere in the valley to rest your eyes. Where spray lifts off a lip into the air, it hangs; a fixed rainbow stands in a dozen places, exactly where it always stands, and does not fade when you move.

Because the spray does not fall. That's the thing you notice third, and it takes a while, because at first you assume you're catching it mid-motion. The mist off the taller columns is a set piece. The droplets occupy their positions. You walk through them and they part around you and settle back and hang there again, in the same arrangement — and the cold comes off them onto your face and hands, and that's the only way you know they're real.

This is a place in equilibrium, and the equilibrium is total. Every quantity in it is conserved. The water that leaves the columns arrives at the water sheet; the water sheet gives it back to the air; the air gives it back to the columns. The mineral the water carries away is laid down again somewhere it can't be seen. The carving is finished. The chord is held. The air is cool and still and will be cool and still, and the light will be white, and the water will be a palm deep, and there will be no weather here, ever, in either direction. Time passes, in the sense that you get older while you stand in it. Nothing else does.

There is life, but you have to be patient with it. Pale flat things, the width of a coin and thinner, cling to the wet facets and move across them by a slow contraction, and they seem to be feeding on the mineral film the water leaves. You will see perhaps one every half hour, and you will learn to be glad of them. In the channels there are a few things like glass shrimp, blind, drifting, and they do not appear to go anywhere. That is the whole of the fauna. Not one green thing. Not one thread of anything that grows upward out of the water. The columns are the forest; the columns are all of it.

The rules, as far as you can work them out:

Everything here is either held or worn. Nothing is in between and nothing is becoming.

Sound does not travel past the lip of the valley. From the ridge behind you, the field is silent. You can see the fixed rainbows and the white glare and the hanging mist, and hear nothing at all, and then you step over and it is on you like a hand over your ears.

The water is never warm and never cold; it is always the temperature of the column it just left.

There is no path. The columns are packed too densely to walk among, and where a seam opens, it opens for six or eight metres and closes again, and the seams do not line up. You go around, or you don't go. People who come here tend to stand at the edge a long time and then leave without going in — not out of fear. There's simply nothing in there to reach. The place has no interior destination. It is all surface, and the surface is already exactly what it is.
