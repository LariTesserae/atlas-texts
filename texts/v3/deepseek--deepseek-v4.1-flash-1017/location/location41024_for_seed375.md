**The Sere Loom**

The place is a shallow, cold basin no wider than a hamlet, ringed by low scarps of pale mineral that look freshly broken. There is no water anywhere: no stream, no pool, no dew. The air is dry as a lamp and smells of chalk, cold iron, and a faint animal sweetness. The ground is a pavement of salt-glaze and gray ash, cracked into crisp polygons. The cracks are young and sharp; their edges have not been rounded by rain or sand. Underfoot the crust sometimes rings hollow.

Light is dim, the color of unpolished tin. Shadows are soft and long. Cool air pools in the basin each night and drains out slowly at dawn, and that temperature swing is the main weather. Sparse vegetation clings to fissures: wire-moss, salt-roses, low gray-green mats that metabolize mineral films and grow so slowly they seem like stains. No trees. No grass in any familiar sense. The basin is mostly bare, and the bareness is the point.

At its center lies the settlement: the Loom. It is a town in plan—lanes, plazas, vaults, chimney clusters, ramps, buried galleries—but it is not built with tools. No metal, no fire, no wheels. It is grown. Colonies of soft-bodied palp-weavers secrete a chitin-mineral composite, pearl-gray and bone-yellow and rust-orange, and they shape it by touch, by pressure, by the slow deposition of their own bodies. The city is layered: new shells grown over old, passages cut through earlier walls, floors raised on the compressed husks of previous generations. Its infrastructure is anatomical. Thermal chimneys pull cold air down into galleries; signal threads run through the crust and carry vibration; waste ducts and nursery bowls and molt vaults are all made of the same living stone. The lanes are not paved; they are licked smooth by the bellies of weavers.

It is urban and it is empty. The Loom was grown at settlement scale, but the density is almost nothing. Broad plazas lie between a few towers. Whole quarters are hollow shells, sealed and resonant. The living core is small: a handful of warm chambers, a few chimneys breathing, a scatter of occupied galleries. Most of the city is skeleton and conduit, waiting. The emptiness is not abandonment exactly; it is the normal condition. The weavers build for generations and occupy only what they need.

Fauna is rare. The palp-weavers themselves are seldom seen—pale, blind, many-limbed, moving through their own walls. Tick-mites graze fungal films in the galleries. A blind scarp-runner sometimes comes down from the rim to gnaw salt from the tower bases, and then the city’s defender organisms stir, and there is a brief, quiet predation. That is the ecosystem: slow, sparse, mostly hidden.

Sound is almost absent. No birds, no wind to speak of, no water. The place is silent enough that you hear your own pulse. But it is not dead. At night the salt crust ticks as it contracts. At dawn the chimneys exhale, and the Loom makes a low, felt thrum through the ground, more vibration than sound. Spore dust puffs from the towers in pale threads. Weavers repair a wall by eating the damaged edge and secreting a new one. Molts are shed, collected, reworked. The city breathes once a day, and its breath is dry.

The rules are biological. Warmth is currency; the deep galleries are inherited; ownership is marked by scent; damaged structures are not repaired by tools but digested and regrown. Nothing is wasted. The dead are incorporated. There is no free water, so the weavers run on sulfur and salt films and recycle what moisture is locked in their tissues. The Loom may be a city, or it may be one distributed organ that occasionally assembles a body. The weavers treat it as both. At the rim, the boundary is not a wall but a temperature: the cold air tide marks where the settlement’s warmth ends.

It is a small place, low and young and sharp-edged, dim under a tin sky. It is a city with almost no one in it, a biological infrastructure half alive, a quiet active nothing in the middle of a dry basin. You can cross it in a slow hour and hear only ticking, and the faint deep pulse of a town that is grown rather than made.
