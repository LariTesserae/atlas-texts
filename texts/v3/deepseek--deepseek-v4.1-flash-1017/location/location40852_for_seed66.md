# The Wet Terrace

You hear it before you see it, and what you hear is not one thing. It is a low continuous roar with strata in it, like a crowd in another room: the sheeting of water down ten thousand channels, the drone of the condenser-engines, and over all of it the animals — a chittering, whirring, clicking blanket of sound that never fully stops, only modulates from hour to hour as one cohort of voices hands off to the next.

The city sits on the shoulder of a high plateau, and the plateau is warm. Not hot — warm the way a bathroom is warm after a long shower, a warmth that comes with an atmosphere so saturated that the air itself feels like a material you push through. Daylight is ordinary here; the cloud cover breaks often enough that you get sun, and when the sun comes out the whole place steams. Mist lifts off every surface at once, moves a few meters, and settles again. You are never dry. Nothing here is ever dry.

## Water

Water is not weather here; it is geology. It arrives in three ways and stays for a long time in all of them.

It falls — brief hard showers that come on with almost no warning and end just as abruptly, leaving the stone black and glossy.

It condenses. This is the larger source. The plateau's air climbs its own slopes and cools just enough to give up its moisture onto anything with surface area, and the city is nothing but surface area. Every wall, every pipe, every cable and railing and stair has a bead of water on it. By night the condensation is heavy enough to run in threads and pool in the seams of the paving. The whole city is a distillation apparatus that was never designed as one and became one anyway.

And it seeps. The rock beneath is wet to a depth no one has sounded. It comes out of the hillside in ten thousand small wounds — not springs, just damp, weeping faces of stone that darken the walls below them and feed the channels cut into the streets. Every downhill line is a water line. The city's plan, in the degree to which it has a plan, is hydrological: gutters that become canals, canals that become cisterns, cisterns that become the reservoirs the engines drink from. Stairs are also spillways. Landings are also settling basins. Roofs are also catchments, ridged and glazed to shed into the same channels as everything else.

## Stone and Form

The plateau is old and has finished most of what it was going to do. The slopes are rounded, sagging into one another at angles that have stopped changing. Where the rock is exposed — and it is exposed everywhere, no green to cover it — you see fine banding, soft greys and rusts and chalky whites, and you see the marks of water working at it patiently for a very long time: flutes, hollows, undercuts, a whole vocabulary of shapes that read as softness but are actually just slowness. Nothing here is being carved fast. It was carved long ago, and now it mostly holds.

Where the city needed to cut, it cut — and those cuts are the only sharp edges for a hundred kilometers. You can read the city's age in them: the newest are still crisp, the oldest have rounded over and grown a skin of mineral deposit until they look natural.

## The Built Thing

The settlement is enormous. It is not a city in the sense of having an edge you could point to; it thins out gradually over tens of kilometers into scattered cisterns and lone pumping stations and finally into bare wet stone. At its heart it is dense and layered — buildings built onto buildings, galleries under galleries, a dozen generations of construction where each generation simply built on the waterproof shell of the last. Streets are narrow not from age but from crowding; there is very little unused volume anywhere.

Its materials are all mineral or grown. Fired clay, brick, cast and re-cast concrete, salt-glazed tile, sheets of corroded metal in green and orange, and — this is the distinctive one — shell. Great quantities of shell, ground and rebonded and set into mortar and paving and wall facing, so that whole districts have a faint pearly sheen under the water film and a texture like a thumbnail. The city is partly made of its own fauna. That is an old arrangement here and no one thinks it strange.

Industry runs on gradients. The condenser-engines are the big ones: vast ribbed halls of pipes and plates where the warm wet air is drawn through cold surfaces and the water runs off in sheets into the reservoirs, and the heat that is released is not wasted but used to drive the next stage, and the next. Steam is everywhere and steam is a building material. Factories here are loud, wet, hot, and full of moving water; they smell of hot metal and mineral silt and, faintly, of the sea that has not been here for a long time. Everything is powered by the fact that the plateau is high and the air below it is not.

## Fauna

There are no plants. Nothing here photosynthesizes; nothing is green. But the place is not sterile — it is the opposite. It is the busiest landscape you will ever stand in.

The base of the food web is a film. It grows on every wet stone face in the shade: a slick, faintly iridescent patina, millimeters thin, that pulls dissolved mineral out of the seeping water and fixes it into something edible. It is not a plant and does not pretend to be — it is closer to a stain that eats. But it is the pasture, and it is everywhere.

Grazing it are the shelled things: small, flat, slow, armored, with rasping mouths, moving up and down the damp walls in the millions, leaving clean trails through the film. They are the reason for the shell in the mortar. They are harvested — brushed off the walls into baskets, cracked, ground, fired.

Above them the density becomes overwhelming. There are siphon-creatures that hang in the mist on threads and drink it, filtering the water out of the air and letting the dry remainder go. There are swarms — membranous, winged, translucent, so numerous in the evening that they read as a change in the light rather than as animals; they are loud, and they are most of the sound. There are climbers in the pipework, long and jointed, that tap the warm conduits for heat and for the condensation that forms on them. There are things living inside the engines, in the warm wet dark of them, tolerated because they eat the film that would otherwise foul the plates. There are large slow animals in the canals — ponderous, shell-backed, half-submerged, more often heard than seen — that move up and down the water stairs on a schedule of their own and are considered, correctly, part of the infrastructure.

And there is the sound of all of it. It is not birdsong; nothing here sings. It is mechanical and choral at once — rasps, ticks, hums, the dry rattle of a million shells on stone, the rising whine of a swarm shifting as one, the low organ-notes of the big canal animals answering each other across districts. Add the water and the engines and the effect is of standing inside a working machine that is also alive: dense, layered, saturated with presences, never silent.

## The Rules of the World

The most important thing about this place is how slowly it changes. It looks frantic — the water, the noise, the sheer crowding of life — but nothing here is in a hurry. The terrain has reached its form. The city is not expanding; it is maintaining, repairing, re-glazing, re-shelling, replacing a wall here, a run of pipe there, at a pace so gradual that a district can be a century into a repair and no one finds that remarkable. The reservoirs fill and are drawn down on a cycle measured in seasons, not days. The animals respond to that cycle and to the daily pulse of the engines, and their swarms rise and fall by centimeters of water and degrees of warmth, not by events.

It is loud and it is slow. Those are not in conflict. The noise is a steady state — the sound of a system holding itself in place against a persistent inflow of water, one film-scrape and one condensation bead at a time. Nothing dramatic has happened here in a very long while. Nothing needs to.

One strangeness, and it is small: the film on the walls grows faster near the engines, and the shelled things follow it, and the harvest follows them, and the districts near the hot works are always marginally better-mortared than the districts far from them. The city is being built, in a very real sense, out of the warmth of its own machinery — accreted shell by shell, over centuries, in the wet warm dark, by a process nobody is managing and everybody depends on.

Stand on one of the high spillway stairs at dusk, when the swarms come up and the light goes silver through the mist, and the whole plateau below you is a single wet gleaming engine, roaring softly, breathing water, and — slowly, slowly — making more of itself.
