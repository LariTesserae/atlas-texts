# The Bellows

You come up through cloud for an hour and then the cloud stops, cut off clean as a table edge, and you are standing on the rim of a small stone bowl perhaps forty paces across. It is the size of a chapel. You can walk its perimeter in three minutes and you will not find a way down that isn't a scramble, and beyond the rim there is nothing but the white floor of the cloud and the thin cold air above it.

The bowl was not made. Nothing here was made. But it was not *grown*, either. The rock has surfaced recently — you can tell because every edge is still sharp, every fracture still bright, no rounding anywhere, as though the whole shelf shrugged up out of the mountain last week and the weather has not yet had a chance to touch it. The strata stand on end in thin vertical plates, and the plates are cut with channels. The channels run in parallel. They turn at right angles. They widen into chambers and narrow into throats, and water moves through them in a schedule.

That is the first thing you notice and the last thing you understand: the schedule. Water comes down the inside of the bowl in threads, and the threads arrive in pulses, and the pulses are regular, and the regularity is not the regularity of dripping. It is the regularity of something being *pumped*.

The bowl is wet everywhere. Not soaked — beaded. The stone sweats. Cool to the touch in the shadowed side, warm near the vents, and there are vents: low seams in the northern wall where a colorless steam slips out and up and is taken by the cold and made briefly visible and then not. The hiss of them is the floor of the sound here, an even breathing that never quickens. Over it, the ticking of water finding its levels. Under it, a slow clack, once every eleven or twelve heartbeats, from somewhere in the wall you can't get to — two stone surfaces meeting and parting, meeting and parting, with the patience of a thing that has no reason to stop.

It is loud. Not painfully. Loud the way a mill is loud when you are standing inside it and have stopped noticing.

Everything is green, and the green is the only thing here that looks like it belongs to the world you came from. Moss in deep cushions on every horizontal surface, furred and heavy with the condensation, dark where the water runs and pale where it doesn't. Ferns uncurl from the vertical joints — sharp stone, soft fronds, the contrast almost rude. Small round-leaved things you don't know the name of crowd the warm vents and lean away from the cold. The vegetation is thick but not continuous; it comes in islands with bare wet stone between them, and the bare stone is most of the bowl. The place is mostly emptiness. It is lush and it is empty at the same time and you have to stand in it a while before that stops feeling like a contradiction.

The light does not change. There is no sun here and no obvious source — the cloud itself seems to be lit from within, dimly, the color of old paper, and it gives everything a flat sourceless grey that never brightens and never dims. You will wait for evening. Evening does not come. The light is the same at the end of your visit as at the beginning, and if you stayed a week it would be the same at the end of the week, and this is a thing you know without being told.

The animals come. Not in herds — in ones and twos, and never the same species twice in a row. A beetle the length of your thumb, lacquered black, walking a channel edge with the fussy certainty of something that has done this before. A pale frog in the moss that does not jump when you move. A bird — small, brown, entirely ordinary — that lands on the rim, looks down into the bowl, and leaves without descending, as though it had come to check something and found it unchanged. Moths, in the dimness, though there is no night for them to be confused by. A thing like a centipede that crosses your boot and does not acknowledge it.

They all move on paths. You will see it if you watch long enough: the frog's route is the beetle's route, and the beetle's is the centipede's, and they do not collide because they are not there at the same time, and they are always there at the same time, and both of those sentences are true and you will not be able to say how.

Here is the rule of the place, and it took you the whole visit to feel it: nothing here is happening for the first time and nothing here is happening for the last time. The clack comes at its interval. The steam rises at its rate. The water finds its levels and the levels are the levels they were. The bowl is an equilibrium the way a held breath is an equilibrium. It has been exactly this for a very long time and it will be exactly this for a very long time and there is no gradient in it anywhere — no rust, no rot, no silting-up, no growth, no decay. The moss does not spread. The channels do not deepen. The stone does not round. It surfaced sharp and wet and it will stay sharp and wet, and the sharpness will never be touched.

And yet it runs. That is the thing you can't put down. There is no builder, no artifact, no scratched initial, no char, no slag — nothing here was ever held in a hand — and it runs anyway, this small wet engine of stone and steam and scheduled water, and the running is not a metaphor. Something in the arrangement of the plates and the channels and the vents is doing work. Heat moves. Water lifts. The clack is a stroke. The bowl is a machine the mountain assembled out of its own strata by some process that has no name in your language, and it has been running since before you came and will be running when the cloud takes you back.

The sound is wrong, at the end. That's what you'll remember. You'll be climbing out, hand over hand on the sharp fresh rock, and you'll hear the clack — and then, a half-second later, you'll hear the clack. The same one. Arriving twice, cause and effect traded places and neither of them willing to admit it. And the steam will be rising before the vent opens. And the frog will be in the moss before it arrives.

Nothing here is broken. That's the strangest part. It's not a place where reality has failed. It's a place where reality has settled into a different and equally workable arrangement, and you are the only thing in it that's out of order, dripping your minutes onto the stone, the one moving part in a room that has no use for one.

Above the rim, the cloud closes over you like a lid. Below, the hiss goes on, even, patient, unhurried, keeping time with nothing.
