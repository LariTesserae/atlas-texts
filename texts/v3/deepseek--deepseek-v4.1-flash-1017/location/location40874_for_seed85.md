# The Still Basin

You come to it from above, through a shaft in the rock, and the first thing that registers is not the dark or the cold but the fact that the water does not move. Not slowly. Not at all. There is no current to lean into, no drift to correct for, no sway of anything. You descend through water that behaves like a held breath. Your hands trail behind you and the water closes over the trail without a ripple, without a swirl, as though it has forgotten the habit of being disturbed.

The basin is small — a flooded cleft near the top of a young mountain, a raw fault-gash in black and grey stone, no wider than a cathedral nave and roofed by a lid of the same rock. The water fills it to the lid. There is no surface, no sky, no weather. Only the ceiling, close and dark, and beneath it this body of water that is cold enough to ache in the fingers and not cold enough to ice, and clear — impossibly clear, empty of silt, empty of plankton, empty of anything suspended at all. It is not murky. It is simply dark, and the darkness has a texture, a pressure, a weight on the eyes.

The rock is young and it has never been worn. The fault faces are knife-edged, the fractures fresh, the strata clean as a broken plate. In a normal sea the water would have rounded all of this in a few thousand years — would have polished the scarps and softened the corners and left a skin of silt in the hollows. Here there is no silt. There is no rounding. The stone is exactly as sharp as the day the basin filled, and it will be exactly as sharp forever, because nothing in this place is permitted to act on anything else. Erosion requires motion. Motion has been refused.

The city stands in tiers up the cleft's walls: pale, narrow, organized, small. It is built of a dense ceramic-mineral stuff, printed or grown in layers so fine the joints vanish, and it does not corrode, does not pit, does not accumulate a film of anything. Its towers stop well short of the lid, as if the builders were modest, or afraid of the ceiling. Threadlike bridges cross the gap between the walls. There are stairways cut into the stone, landings, sealed doors, shafts that go down into the mountain and come back up. Much of it is dark. Much of it is empty. Whole terraces stand unlit and unfurnished, waiting for a population that never arrived or has long since gone quiet. In the whole basin you might count forty lit windows, and they are scattered, and the space between them is enormous.

The light is cold and blue-white and comes only from the city itself — from a few lamps on long, slow cycles, and from the pale glow of the engines. There is no other light. Nothing down here produces its own: no weed, no algae, no biofilm on the stone. The surfaces are sterile. You could press your palm to a wall and the print would remain, undisturbed, for as long as the basin lasts, which is to say indefinitely.

Sound dies quickly. Not because the water is thick but because the same thing that swallows motion swallows noise; a shout travels a few meters and then is simply gone, absorbed, hushed into the general quiet. What you hear instead is the pressure in your ears, your own blood, your own breathing, and once every interval — you cannot say how long the interval is, because time here is not doing much either — a single low tone from somewhere in the city. The same note. The same duration. It has been the same note for as long as anyone has kept count, and no one has kept count.

Life is rare and slow. There are things that graze the stone — pale, blind, shelled, no bigger than your hand — and they move perhaps a centimeter a day, and there are not many of them. You might see three in the entire basin. They do not school and they do not flee. They are not part of any system you can identify, because there is no system here to be part of: nothing grows, nothing decays, nothing is consumed and nothing accumulates. They simply persist, as the city persists, in a place that has been taken out of time's hands.

The engines are what do it. They stand at the intersections of the tiers — pillars of something that is not quite matter, or not quite the matter you know, humming at a frequency you feel in the teeth rather than hear. Around them the water is stiller, if that is possible, and the cold is deeper, and the silence is more complete. They are not pumps or filters. They are sinks. They drink motion out of the water and put it somewhere else, and they have been doing it without pause or variation since before the lid went on, and they will keep doing it. Drop a stone and it falls straight down, no drift, no deflection, and settles without a puff of sediment. Exhale and the bubble does not rise. It hangs where it left you, a trembling sphere, and stays.

The rules of the place are simple and total. Nothing moves. Nothing changes. Nothing is worn and nothing is built and nothing is repaired, because nothing needs repair, because nothing degrades. The city's systems run in perfect equilibrium, self-maintaining, self-hushing. The few keepers who live here — tall, wrapped, slow, speaking little and mostly by touch — do not so much operate the basin as attend it. They walk the dark terraces on no schedule. They sit beside the engines. They discourage sound, and they discourage motion, and they will take your arm if you swim too briskly, not unkindly, and hold you still until you understand.

Because the water is holding something. This is the thing you begin to feel before you are told: the stillness is not incidental. The basin is a lens. Somewhere in that preternaturally clear and motionless volume there is an image, or a calculation, or a shape — the keepers disagree, or no longer know — and it is being kept sharp. Every ripple would blur it. Every current would smear it. Every rising bubble would drift through it and corrupt it. So the city was built, and the engines were set, and the water was forbidden to move, and the image has been held unblurred for a very long time, and will be held unblurred for a very long time yet.

What the image is, no one will show you. You may swim the length of the basin and look, and the water is perfectly clear, and you will see the sharp young rock and the pale tiers and the few cold lights and the three slow grazers and nothing else. But the keepers will watch you look, and they will be very still, and the tone will sound its same note, and the water will not move.
