The air here is the first thing you understand: it is not clear, but it is not heavy. It hangs in the low spaces between things, a warm, wet breath that smells of crushed green stems and the clean, mineral scent of wet stone. This is a place of perpetual, gentle condensation. The sky is a soft, luminous grey, a ceiling of cloud that filters the light into a diffuse, pearly glow, dimming the world to the quiet hum of a shadowed noon.

The landscape is a basin, a wide, shallow bowl of warm mud and new rock. The ground is soft underfoot, a spongy carpet of mosses and low, sprawling plants that release tiny puffs of moisture when you step. The "rock" is not ancient and weathered; it is a pale, porous stone, still warm from the earth's core, crumbling at the edges like fresh bread. Water is everywhere and nowhere. It does not gather in grand lakes but seeps and pools in the thousands of dimples and hollows of the newborn land. Each pocket is its own tiny, vibrant world, a mirror for the grey sky and a nursery for clouds of gnats that dance in silent, chaotic knots.

Through this soft, yielding terrain, the settlement has grown. It is not built, but *cultivated*. The people here—if you can call them that, and they are people of a sort—do not cut stone or fell trees. They encourage. Their homes are living structures, woven from the thick, rubbery vines that crawl across the basin floor. They guide the growth over generations, coaxing the plants into arches and domes and enclosed, nest-like rooms. The walls are layers of enormous, waxy leaves, overlapping like scales, shedding the constant dampness. The town is a cluster of these green, breathing mounds, scattered with deliberate sparseness across the basin floor, connected by paths of packed mud that wind between the seeping pools. There are no straight lines, no hard angles. It is an architecture of gentle curves and organic growth, a village that looks as if it has simply sprouted from the ground like a stand of strange, hollow trees.

The place is quiet. Not silent, but quiet. There is no wind to speak of, only the slow, heavy drift of the humid air. The dominant sound is water: a constant, low symphony of drips. A fat drop falling from a leaf into a pool with a clear *plink*. The soft, continuous trickle of a rill winding its way through the moss. The wet, sucking sound of a footfall. Against this, the sounds of life are subtle. The low, resonant croak of something large and amphibious, hidden in a seep. The rustle of countless small, soft-bodied creatures moving through the undergrowth. The air is thick with them, the fauna abundant but scattered. There are no great herds, but a proliferation of individual, hidden lives. Six-legged things that glide on wide, membranous wings. Creatures like soft, damp-furred slugs that graze on the fungi growing in the shadow of the homes. Slow, patient things that thrive in the warm wet. It is an ecology of the small and the hidden.

The rules of this world are the rules of the damp. Things do not burn here; they mold, they ferment, they rot. Stone does not crack from frost, it dissolves. The people do not conquer the land; they negotiate with it. Their tools are of shaped bone and hardened wood, but mostly they use their hands, and their patience. They are gardeners and herders of a sort, tending the vines that give them shelter and harvesting the pale, sweet fungi that grow in the dark corners of their homes. Their calendar is not marked by the sun, but by the slow pulse of the seeps and the blooming of the great, fleshy flowers that provide them with light. In a world with no fire, the faint, cool bioluminescence of these plants is the only lamp.

And the land itself is not finished. You can feel it. A subtle tremor now and then, a shift in the mud beneath your feet. A new spring bubbling up where there was none yesterday. The pale stone is still forming, the mud is still settling, the very shape of the basin is still being decided by the slow, patient work of the water and the warmth rising from below. This is a world caught in the act of becoming, a soft, warm, and quiet nursery of a landscape, and everything in it, from the people to the plants to the stone itself, is still learning what it is meant to be.
