# The Kettle

You reach it through a hole in a warm marsh, and the hole is the only part of it that is quiet.

The shaft goes down about thirty meters, and the walls of the shaft are not rock but a lattice — ceramic ribs the color of old teeth, woven with something that has since mineralized into a gray-green crust, so that the ladder is half architecture and half flowstone. The rungs are still true. Someone maintains them. There is no one here.

Then the shaft opens, and the sound arrives before the light does, though the light is considerable.

## The chamber

It is small as caves go — call it forty meters across, twenty high at the crown, a rounded bowl rather than a hall. The shape is entirely water's work: scalloped, fluted, every surface scooped into shallow concave pockets like the inside of a shell, the whole thing smoothed to the touch of a hand run along a wet animal. This took a long time. The chamber is not being made anymore; it is being *kept*. The water that carved it now runs through channels it has agreed upon, and the rock has grown accustomed.

The floor is a shallow basin, perhaps a hand's depth at the rim, deeper in the middle, and the water in it is *moving*. Not flowing in any direction you can name — churning, folding, lifting into columns and letting them fall, spiraling down into three or four sinks and welling back up somewhere else entirely. It goes fast. Faster than the gradient should allow.

And it is bright.

## The light and the noise are the same thing

Where the water shears against itself, it lights. Not a glow — a hard, white, noon-colored blaze that throws the scallops into relief and casts your shadow against the far wall, sharp-edged, wavering. Where the water slows, the light dims toward a pearly gray. Where it stops — in the lee of a stone, in the corners — it goes out entirely, and those corners are the only dark in the room.

So the chamber is a map of its own turbulence, and it roars. The sound is not the flat crash of a waterfall; it is layered. Underneath, a low organ tone that you feel in the sternum and in the fillings of your teeth, a standing wave the bowl was built to hold. Above that, the shear-hiss, continuous, like rain on canvas stretched tight. And over everything, the drips.

They fall from the ceiling constantly, hundreds of them, from the weeping rock — condensation, not seepage; the air is saturated and warm and the stone is cooler, so the ceiling sweats. But the drips are *timed*. They fall in figures. A run of seven, a pause, a run of three, a long silence, then a cascade that sounds like someone running down a stair. It is not random and it is not a code you could learn; it is more like a meter, the way a heart is a meter. You stop noticing it and then you notice you've been breathing to it.

## What the water does

The basin is a sorting. Water comes in from the marsh above, warm and brown with tannin and dissolved things, and it leaves by three sinks, and it does not leave the same. The chamber separates it — fractions, cut by shear and by fields you can feel as a faint tug on a watch-spring, a faint metallic taste at the back of the mouth. Nothing about the mechanism is visible. There are no machines. The mechanism *is* the water and the shape of the bowl and the old lattice in the walls, and the fact that it works is the only evidence that it is there at all.

The people who come down here — and they do come, occasionally, from the marsh villages — say the Kettle is a throat. What goes down it is swallowed and divided and passed on, and somewhere far below, in the dark under everything, something is drinking the fractions. They do not say this as a belief. They say it the way you'd say the mill grinds.

It has been running a long time. The conduits are gone — eaten, replaced by their own casts in flowstone, so the walls are studded with the ghosts of plumbing, blind sockets of translucent calcite where brass used to be. It still runs. It runs *because* it eroded; the wear and the work are the same process now, and the chamber is in a state it has settled into, the way a streambed is in a state. It will hold this shape for another thousand years and then it will change, and that will be fine too.

## The margins

Sparse, everything at the edges.

In the spray zones, on the shoulders of the scallops where the water only reaches as mist, there are mats of pale moss — a green so light it reads as gray — and threads of cress root trailing down from the shaft, and in the two still corners a skin of algae the color of rust. That is the whole of the vegetation. It survives on the mist and the light, and the light is not for it.

The animals are rarer. There is one kind. They look like limpets the size of a thumbnail, thick-shelled, blind, and they clamp to the scallops in the dry high pockets and wait. When the water quickens — when the room brightens and the organ tone climbs — they open, all at once, and feed on whatever the shear throws up against the stone. Then they close and wait again. You will see perhaps a dozen in a visit, scattered across a hundred square meters of wall, and you will hear them before you see them: a dry ticking, a thousand tiny lids, that finds its way into the gaps of the roar.

There is a story that one other thing lives here. Nobody has produced it.

## The built things

Scattered, small, and old in different ways.

Four alcoves are cut into the wall at shoulder height, evenly spaced, and each holds a socket of white ceramic and green-black brass, dead, cold, rimed with mineral. A walkway of sintered grate crosses the basin at the narrow end — still load-bearing, still level, and the water passes under it without touching. A single lamp-standard stands in the shallows near the eastern sink, and the standard is dark, because the chamber does not need it.

And on the dry shelf above the walkway, where the drips don't reach, someone has built a shrine: three stones stacked, a twist of wire, a chipped enamel cup holding a dried flower and four coins gone green. Recent. The coins are from a currency still in use.

## Rules

- **The light is the work.** When the Kettle blazes, it is doing what it is for. When it dims, it is resting or it is failing. It does not go fully dark.
- **Do not stand in the still corners.** The dark places are where the sorting does not reach, and things that fall into them are not returned at the rate you'd expect.
- **The drips keep time.** If the meter breaks — if the drips fall at random — leave. It has happened twice in living memory.
- **You may take water out. You may not put anything in.** The marsh villages are strict about this, and the strictness is not religious.

The air is warm and heavy and smells of wet limestone and iron and, faintly, of cut grass. You will be wet through in a minute. Your ears will ring for an hour after you climb out, and the ringing will be in the meter of the drips, and for a few days afterward you will find yourself tapping it out on tables without meaning to.
