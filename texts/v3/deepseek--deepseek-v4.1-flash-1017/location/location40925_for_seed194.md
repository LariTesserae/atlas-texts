# The Hearth-Knot

You will not see it. There is no light here — not night, not storm-darkness, but the plain fact of a world with no lamp in it, no star, no glow-worm, nothing that emits. The dark is not a condition of the place; it is the place's native medium, and everything that lives here lives without ever having needed the concept of a surface.

You find it by walking a long time over ground that is nearly flat and nearly featureless — bedrock planed down by ages of nothing much happening, dusted with a fine grey flour that is the milled remains of old fiber. The dust is soft and cold underfoot and gets into everything. There is no water anywhere; nothing damp, nothing that runs. The air is cool and still and smells faintly of tar and overripe yeast, and it grows stronger as you go, and then the low tone begins.

The tone is the first true fact of the place. It comes up through the soles of your feet before your ears take it: a slow, enormous, roughly thirty-cycle drone, felt in the sternum, in the sinuses, in the loose hinge of the jaw. It is loud. Not sharp — it is not a noise that hurts — but it fills the whole volume of the world, the way a cathedral fills with organ, and there is no distance in it, no echo telling you where it comes from. Then, riding on the drone, the other sounds: a tick and a creak, a long resinous groan that can last a full minute and sounds like a hull taking strain, a wet glassy crack, and far off, a thin whistling in a hollow that rises and falls over the course of an hour.

The ground rises a little — ten paces of gentle mound — and then falls away into a mouth. That is all the outside is: a low knot of grey fiber and dust, unlit, unremarkable, a thing you would walk past in a world that had eyes. The mouth is a lip of thick felted tissue, tall as your chest, worn round and smooth as a river stone by centuries of nothing but its own breathing.

Inside, the town.

It is small. You could walk its whole length in two hundred paces and cross it in sixty, and people did, once, for a very long time. But it is dense the way a wasp nest is dense — chambers stacked on chambers, galleries doubling back, floor above floor above floor, each layer grafted onto the last, so that the oldest rooms are deepest and lowest and most worn and the newest are hardly rooms at all, just swellings in the wall where someone was beginning to make one. The material of every wall, every floor, every stair is the same: plant. Dense pale fiber felted into a cork-like mass, seamed with darker cords that run like tendons, and slicked in the low places with a slow amber resin that is not water and does not evaporate. Nothing here is stone or metal or clay. The only mineral is grit in the dust.

And nothing here was cut and assembled. It was grown, and then tended — pruned, grafted, scored, bent, bound. You can feel the marks under your palm: old cut faces, healed over into pale scars; cords of twisted fiber still knotted around a limb that has swollen around the knot and half-swallowed it; a passage narrowed deliberately by a grown constriction, then widened again by a later hand; a floor deliberately leveled, then re-leveled, then abandoned to the slow heave of the living wood beneath it. The whole town is a graft, worked by hand tools — stone blades, bone wedges, fiber rope, fire-hardened points — over a span of generations so long that the earliest work and the bedrock are the same age, and the most recent work is older than any living memory.

There are almost no people here now. There is almost nothing here at all. That is the second true fact: it is empty. The chambers are huge and hollow relative to what they hold. In a room the size of a barn you will find one object — a worn stone wedge, a coil of cord, a hollow gourd-shaped growth — and then forty paces of nothing before the far wall, which you know is there only because the drone changes when you face it. The presences are few and far apart, and the space between them is vast and dark and resonant.

The town breathes. That is where the sound comes from, and it is the whole ecology in one motion. Beneath the basin the bedrock seeps a heavy gas — faintly sweet, faintly oily, heavier than air — and it pools in the low hollows at knee height, a slow lake of vapor you can feel as a coolness and a pressure and a taste. The plant draws it in through pores in the felted walls, and the gas moves through the hollow fiber channels of the structure in long slow tides, and the whole town swells and settles, swells and settles. One full breath takes about four hours. The drone is that breath, resonating in a hundred chambers tuned by their own growth; the whistle is a pinhole vent; the long groans are the older layers shifting against each other as they take the strain. The wet cracks are the fibers of a floor deciding, after a decade of deliberation, to give a millimeter.

Nothing else moves. There are no animals — no crawlers in the felt, no fliers in the gas, nothing that hunts. Nothing here has ever been hunted. The plant is the entire living mass, and it lives on rock and gas and the slow recollection of its own dead, and the dead are reabsorbed where they lie, a chamber at a time, over centuries. Resin creeps a centimeter a day and seals what it touches. Where a wall is broken, a pale scar tissue swells out and closes it, and the scar is warmer than the wall by a degree you can feel with a palm held still for a minute.

The rules of the place are simple, and they are the rules of something old that has stopped needing to hurry.

Everything grows toward closure. Doors are not doors; they are apertures that will be sealed within a lifetime, and the seal is not a failure of maintenance but the maintenance itself. A path walked every day stays open; a path not walked closes in thirty years. The town is a hand's width smaller in its openings than it was, every decade, and has been for a very long time.

The sound does not stop. There is no silence to measure it against. You will not hear a footstep here, but you will feel a footstep in the drone before you hear it — the chamber answers, and the answer tells you where you are.

The gas pools low. Lie down and you will breathe it; stand and you will not. Nothing here has any reason to lie down.

And the place is finishing. That is the deepest fact, deeper even than the dark: this is not a town that is being built or kept up or even ruined. It is a town in its final form, at the end of its long settling, and what is happening now is the last and slowest part — the built thing being taken back into the grown thing, chamber by chamber, scar by scar, decade by decade, until the whole careful humane knot of it is one more smooth grey mound on a plain that is already almost perfectly flat, breathing its four-hour breath in the dark, groaning quietly, and closing.

The name it had is worn off it. The hands that cut and tied and leveled it left their marks, and the marks are still there, softened, and will be there a while yet, and then they won't.
