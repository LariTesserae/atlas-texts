The first thing is the sound. It is not a noise you hear so much as a pressure you feel, a constant, grinding roar that vibrates in the marrow of your bones. It is the sound of the world growing. A low, subsonic thrum from the deep earth, a continuous, wet tearing of fibers, a high, crystalline chime of something breaking. It is the sound of a billion tons of plant matter slowly, patiently, crushing and consuming the works of a forgotten industry.

You are below the surface, in a vast cavern system that must be miles across. The "sky" is a ceiling of stone, lost in a dim, perpetual twilight. The light comes from the plants themselves. Great, bloated pods hang from the roof, pulsing with a sickly, bioluminescent green and purple light. Their glow is weak, swallowed by the immense dark, but enough to see by.

The air is cold, a deep, penetrating chill that has nothing to do with wind. It is the cold of a cellar, of deep water, of things that have never felt the sun. And it is damp. Water condenses on every surface, beading on the slick, fleshy surfaces of the plants and dripping with a constant, soft *plink-plink-plink* into shallow pools. The air tastes of wet soil, rust, and a sweet, cloying scent of nectar and decay.

The vegetation is everything. There is no bare stone that is not being pried apart by roots. The terrain is a landscape of plant-architecture. Thick, cable-like vines, some as big around as a person, snake from floor to ceiling, groaning with the slow, tectonic stress of holding the cavern's weight. There are vast, shelf-like fungi growing from the walls, their undersides a riot of gills that glow with a faint, amber light. The ground is a treacherous mat of spongy moss, slick with moisture, that gives way underfoot to reveal a labyrinth of smaller, paler roots. The scale is immense but broken up by this dense, organic clutter. You can see perhaps fifty feet before your view is swallowed by a thicket of pale, questing tendrils or a bulbous, breathing sac of a plant.

The sound has a rhythm, a slow, patient beat. You can feel it more than hear it: a deep, resonant thrum that pulses through the ground every minute or so. It is the sound of the great engines, still running. Scattered throughout this verdant chaos are the remains of the industry that came before. A massive, rusted gear, half-swallowed by a mound of moss, its teeth now just convenient anchors for vines. A great iron pipe, ruptured and bent, now gushing a torrent of warm, mineral-rich water that steams in the cold air and feeds a whole oasis of pale, feathery ferns. Here and there, the skeletal remains of a factory floor—a few girders, a collapsed gantry—are being actively disassembled by the plant life, the metal groaning as thick roots slowly, relentlessly pry it apart.

The beat is the heart of a great engine, still somewhere in the deep, its rhythmic thrum the pulse of this entire world. The plants seem to grow in time with it, their slow, patient expansion a direct response to the ancient, mechanical vibration. The dynamic is one of geological patience. Nothing happens quickly, except the constant, deafening roar of growth.

Animal life is rare. You might see a pale, many-legged thing scuttle across a root, or a cloud of tiny, iridescent flies hovering over a pool of stagnant water. They are fleeting presences in this place, dwarfed by the overwhelming, silent (and yet deafening) urgency of the plants. The only other things that move are the plants themselves, their slow, imperceptible creeping a constant reminder that you are the transient one here. This is a world of green and rust, of cold and damp, of a deafening, patient war between the organic and the mechanical, and the organic is winning.
