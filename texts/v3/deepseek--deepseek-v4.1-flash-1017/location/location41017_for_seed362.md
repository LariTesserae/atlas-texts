You come up over the last broken ground and the land does not fall away — it simply continues, and nothing interrupts it as far as the light carries. Low, flat, enormous. The sky is a cool milkglass white, and the ground beneath is pale, almost the same value, so the horizon reads as a seam rather than a line.

The ground is plant. All of it. Not a forest — more like a field that someone with a plan laid out and then left running for a very long time. Leaf-panels in dense overlapping ranks, each silvered and tilted to the light, near enough to touch and touching. Beneath them, stalks. Beneath those, a mat of root-cable and conduit woven so tightly it has become a floor — dark, compressed, hard as board. Cut it and it smells of cold iron, cut grass, and something clean and solvent, like a garage in winter.

Everything here is built. But the builders built with plant. No stone in sight, no soil either — just that board of old root going down further than you could dig, a hundred generations of itself pressed into a substrate. The towers standing up out of the plain are plant as well: trunks that swell into curved cooling shells, or narrow into stacks that vent. They are spaced, arranged, maintained. Some are young and pale. Some are patinated. A few have fallen, and the mat is taking them back, root-cable climbing the breach like a slow green wound closing.

The light is very bright and very cool. It comes through the milkglass without a visible sun, and it does not seem to fall on things so much as to come out of them — the panels glow faintly along their ridges, and the towers carry a rim of brightness even on the side away from the sky. Shadows exist, but they are shy. They pool only at the bases of things and do not stretch.

There is no water. None — not a pool, not a puddle, not a cloud's worth in the air. The atmosphere is thin, dry, cool against the skin. You do not sweat here, exactly, and your breath does not fog. The plants run on sap instead: volatile, pale, faintly sweet, moving under pressure through the conduits. All day the leaf-panels transpire it, and it hangs as a thin brilliance over the field, and by evening it condenses on the silvered surfaces in cold beads. At dawn the panels tilt and the beads run down into the stems and the sap goes back into the works. It evaporates clean, leaves no residue, and it is not water; touch it and it feels colder than it should, and it is gone from your finger before you can decide what it was.

The whole continent is a chemical works and a power station and a crop, and it has been one thing for so long that the three are no longer separable. The trunks circulate coolant. The stacks vent a hot dry breath. The leaf-panels are the solar intake, the electrolytic skin, the factory floor. Vast arrays of them turn, by the hour, in long waves across the plain, and the turning makes a sound like fine rain — except it isn't rain, and it isn't quite sound.

Because the place is silent. That is the first thing you notice after the brightness. The mat eats noise. The thin air does not carry it. There are no birds. When a stack vents, you feel it more than hear it — a soft, enormous exhalation, almost under the floor of hearing, and then a long silence that has a pressure to it. Sometimes a panel shifts in its socket and clicks. Once, far off, a tower settled and you heard the report of it arrive thin and late.

The venting towers make weather. Heat climbs off them in columns and the cool air comes in to replace it, so there are standing breezes here that run in fixed lanes, mile after mile, always in the same direction, and the panels along those lanes have been bent by them into a permanent lean. Between the lanes the air is still enough that the sap-vapor hangs like glass. You can walk from a lane into the stillness and feel the difference on your face, and then walk out again, and the wind is there waiting, doing what it has always done.

Life besides the plant is rare and small. There are things under the canopy — thumb-sized, translucent, jointed too many times — that move along fixed routes between certain towers, and never leave those routes. They are the pollinators, or the maintenance, or something the works needs that has no better name. You see one every hour or so if you look. The mat itself is the ecology; everything else is a detail it keeps for reasons of its own. There are places in the undercanopy where the crossings of root-cable are dense enough to be knots, and the knots are warm, and the faunules gather there and are still. You learn not to stand on the knots.

The place is old, and it has weather of its own now. The vented vapor has run for centuries in shallow grooves, cutting channels in the root-board the way water would. The towers carry patina. The fallen ones are half-swallowed. Nothing here is new and nothing is being repaired in any hurry. It eroded into itself a long time ago and settled into the shape it prefers, and the shape is holding.

And then there is the part you cannot file away. The panels track the light — of course they do — but they also track you, a fraction late, in a wave that follows your walking and then loses interest and returns to the sky. The brightness near you goes up very slightly when you pay attention to something, as though the place were leaning in. Walk an hour in a straight line and the same tower is ahead of you again, patinated on the same side, with the same fallen shell beside it, and the mat is warm under your boots, and the silence has a texture now, like a held breath in a large room. The sap-vapor smells faintly of something you were thinking about an hour ago.

The whole plain is a plant and a factory and a slow enormous nervous system, and it has been all three so long that it no longer distinguishes between them. It is not hostile. It is attentive. It runs its arrays and vents its heat and takes its sap back at dawn, and it is very bright and very quiet and very large, and it is thinking, in its way, about the small warm thing that has come walking out along its lanes.
