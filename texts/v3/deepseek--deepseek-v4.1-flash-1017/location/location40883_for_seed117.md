# The Weep-Bowl

You come into it downhill, always downhill, through a slot in the rock that narrows until you are walking sideways, and then it opens all at once into a bowl maybe three hundred paces across — small enough to cross in a few minutes, too dense to cross quickly.

The bowl is stepped. Not in a human way; in the way of a thing that has been doing one motion for a very long time. The floor is a stack of shallow basins, each a hand-span deep, each spilling into the next through a scalloped lip worn to glass-smoothness by centuries of water going over it. Travertine. The whole basin is limestone that the water has been building *up* even as it cuts down — a slow contradiction, terraces accreting at the rims while the channels between them deepen. It is not eroding toward some future shape. It is already in its shape, settled, the way an old fire is settled. The water goes the same way it has always gone.

And there is a great deal of water. It does not fall; it arrives. The air is at the temperature of breath on a cold window, and every surface in the bowl is beaded, furred with condensation, running. The walls weep. Not dramatically — a bead swells on the rock for a long time, hesitates, lets go, and the drip is the metronome of the whole place. Hundreds of them, out of phase, so the sound is not a rhythm but a texture: a fine continuous ticking and plashing, a quiet that is actually very busy, like rain heard through a wall. Sometimes a whole sheet of it releases at once from an upper ledge and clatters down the terraces, and then everything that lives here goes briefly still, and then resumes.

There are no plants. It takes a while to notice, because at a glance there is a great deal that looks like vegetation — pale fringes along the pool margins, tufted and fine, nodding in the drip. But they are sinter fingers, mineral straws grown outward where the splash lands, each one hollow, each one a fossil of a drip. And among them, where the water is warmer, the animals have built their own false thickets: tube-dwellers, thousands of them, each a little calcareous chimney the width of a pencil, clustered so densely along every seam and shelf that the rock is upholstered in them, and out of each chimney a fan of feathery arms is ceaselessly combing the water. They are not plants. They are far more interested in you than plants would be. Disturb the water and the nearest thicket furls, chimney by chimney, in a wave — a slow ripple of retraction that follows your hand around the pool.

Life is everywhere here, thick and layered and small. Under the terraces, in the wet dark of the overhangs, there are crustaceans the size of a thumbnail, blind, white, walking sideways over each other in slow carpeted crowds. Flatworms the color of milk. Snails grazing the mineral felt. Something larger, rarely seen, that leaves a track in the sediment like a rope being dragged. All of it cold-blooded and slow and extremely numerous. There is nothing here that flies. Nothing here that is bigger than a spread hand. But there is no surface you can put your palm on that is not already occupied.

Through all this, the machinery. It is scattered and it is old and it is not maintained, exactly, and it is not abandoned, exactly. A wheel of copper and iron, set in the main channel, turns in the falling water and has turned so long that the terraces have grown up around its lower rim and it now turns in a slot of its own making. A pump housing, half-swallowed by sinter, still shudders every few minutes. Lines of scaled pipe, verdigrised, run along the bowl wall and vanish into the rock, and come out again, and go in. Settling tanks the size of bath tubs, brimming, warm, their contents separated into bands of mineral silt. Somebody built all this to take something out of the water, or put something in, or lift it, or move it — the point is long lost. The machines do not need anyone. They are driven by the water they stand in and the temperature difference between the warm springs below and the cold condensing air above, and so they run on their own, in their own slow cycle, and the animals have learned them: the tube-dwellers crowd the warm outflow pipes, the blind crustaceans shelter under the housings where the water is still. The machines are part of the ecology now, another kind of shell.

The light does not change much. There is sky above the bowl, but it is a lid of low cloud that never breaks and never quite rains, only holds the water in the air until it gives it back to the rock. So the whole bowl sits in a grey-green dusk, and everything in it — terraces, pipes, animals — is the color of the underside of a leaf that doesn't exist: chalk, verdigris, pearl, the faintest pink where the warm water comes up. Colors do not come from the sun here. They come from the minerals and from the things that eat them.

And it is quiet, but it is not still. It is actively going: water arriving and departing, terraces growing a hair's breadth a decade, the wheel turning, the pump shuddering, the thickets combing and furling, the blind crowds shifting under the ledges, the drips ticking off time that this place does not otherwise keep. Stand in the middle of it, on a terrace worn smooth, and the strangest thing is not the machines or the false plants or the animals that think your hand is weather. It is that the whole bowl is a single organism of stone and water and small hungry things, and it has been breathing this way since before the machines, and it will go on breathing after them, and you are standing in its lung.
