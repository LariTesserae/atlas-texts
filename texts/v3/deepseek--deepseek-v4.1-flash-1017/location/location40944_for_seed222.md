# The Sweating Bench

You climb for most of a day and the air gets thinner but not colder, which is the first thing that's wrong. By the time you reach the rim the wind is steady and dry and the rock under your hands is warm, blood-warm, as though the mountain had been lying in the sun a long time and had decided to keep it.

Then you come over the edge and the country opens out.

## Terrain

It is a bench, not a peak — a broad high shelf of stone, tilted just enough that you can see the whole of it fall away in steps toward a hazy lower world. It runs out past any easy reading of it and folds into further shelves, further steps, a stack of them descending and ascending, so that the horizon is a series of ledges rather than a line.

The stone is the substance of the place. It has been weathered into its own settled vocabulary: flutes and gutters and shallow scalloped basins, all of them rounded, all of them old, worn into those shapes and then held there — not actively eroding now, just resting in the form the wind and the water and the long millennia left them. Nothing here is sharp. Everything is a curve worn smooth. Some basins are the size of a cupped hand; some you could lie down in. They step and terrace and repeat, and where they repeat they make patterns you catch yourself reading as design.

The stone is pale where the sun hits and a warmer grey-buff in the gullies, and it seems porous everywhere, like pumice or old coral, though it is heavier than that. It drinks sound. Stand in the open, in a wind you can feel on your face, and you hear almost nothing — the stone swallows the midrange, and what comes back to you is only the very low and the very high, so the wind arrives as a pressure rather than a noise.

## Water

There is no stream. There is no lake. The place is dry enough that your first hour there you think it's a desert.

It isn't. Water is a daily event, and it happens in the early morning.

The stone sweats. It begins before light, a bloom of condensation over every surface, thicker in the flutes and basins — the whole bench going dark and glossy at once, like a forehead. Where the basins are tilted, the dew finds the low lips and begins to run, a hundred small threads joining into a few dozen, the few dozen joining into four or five, and for perhaps an hour after first light there is running water on the bench: thin, fast, cold, tasting of mineral and stone-dust, gathering in the deeper scallops where it stands for a while and then is gone.

By midmorning the surface is dry to look at and still cool to touch. Under it, the rock is a sponge, holding the wet from the intermittent rains and the fog-days and whatever it draws up from below, and giving it back a little at a time. There are the pockets too — the tinajas, the ones deep enough to keep a lens of water through the dry weeks, each with its own small weather, its own film of green on the shaded side.

Standing water is rare enough here that the animals know every pocket of it individually. So do the structures.

## Light

The light is enormous. The sky is a hard blue-white, and the sun is not so much a disc as a presence that has been laid over the bench like a hand. Shadows are short and black and do not soften at the edges; the air is too thin and clean for that. There is a glare off the pale stone that gets into your eyes and stays there, so that even at midday you find yourself squinting into the scallops, looking for the darker wet places.

There are no clouds most days. On the days there are, they are high and thin and don't do much, and once or twice a season a wall of them comes up from the lower country and it rains — briefly, hard, warm, and then the bench steams and the flutes run all day and the whole place smells like a wet brick.

## Sound

Almost none. That's what stays with you.

The wind moves and you feel it pressing your clothes and there is, somewhere under hearing, the rush of it, but the bench eats the middle out of every sound. Birds — and there are birds, plenty — call in a register that arrives thin and far, as though they were calling from the next valley over when they are three strides from you. A loose stone falls and you hear the impact but not the clatter. Voices go dull at a dozen paces.

The animals seem to have taken this up as a habit rather than fought it. Nothing here sings. Things whistle, click, tap on stone, drum with a foot, breathe. The abundant life of the place is mostly quiet life, and the loudest thing on the bench most days is the hour of running water.

## Fauna

Abundant, and spread thin over the stone as a scatter of small individual lives rather than herds, and it arrives in a rush every morning with the water.

The dew-hour is the whole of the bench's economy. Out of the seams and the porous rock come the small things — flat, many-legged, grey as the stone, drinking from the threads where they cross a low lip. Out from under the scallops come the rounder ones, slow, with a soft wet look to them, who do not drink so much as sit in the basins and let the water come to them. There are things with shells like a thumbnail and things with no shell that leave a wet trail that dries before noon. Moths, or something moth-shaped, with wings that are pale until they are wet and then go translucent, so that in the drinking hour the air has a faint shifting glitter in it.

The birds are the large fauna and they are not very large. They come in on the morning wind from the lower shelves to work the wet lips, hopping in a rolling gait from basin to basin, and they are territorial about particular scallops in a way that suggests ownership is old and settled. Some of them are the color of the stone and some are the color of the wet stone, and when the bench dries at midmorning they go with it, out and down over the edge, and the bench is empty of them.

After that the place belongs to the slow things and to the predators, who are few and thin and work the shade-edges of the gullies in the heat of the day. And under everything, in the porous rock itself, there is a fauna of the interior — small, pale, patient things that live in the wet dark of the sponge and come to the surface only when it rains, and are the reason the stone is so thoroughly riddled, and are perhaps older than the bench.

## Vegetation

Sparse is the word, and the plants have made their accommodation. There is a mat, not a lawn, of tough grey-green stuff in the seams where the water runs longest, and it holds the seam against the wind and holds the dust and over years builds a little soil in the crack. There are succulents in the shade of the basin lips, thick and dull and full of water, some of them probably as old as the structures. There is a lichen that paints the north faces in slow orange circles, and a crust of something black that lives where the water stands and goes dormant when it doesn't.

Nothing here is tall. Nothing here casts a long shadow. The tallest plant on the bench is a shrub as high as your knee and it is a landmark; you can navigate by it.

## Built

There are structures, and they are scattered, and at first you take them for rocks.

They are small — posts and low walls and a few squat housings, no bigger than a person, set into the scallops as though they grew there. The material is the same pale stone and a dull grey metal that does not corrode and does not shine. There are no roads between them. There are faint marks in the stone — grooves where something ran, or was dragged, or was poured — and they are as weathered as the natural flutes and are worn into the same grammar of curves. These are partial remains. Most of what was here is under the surface now, or was taken, or was never more than the faint marks.

Some of them are still doing something.

## The Tech

You find this out on your second morning, when you notice that the dew on one particular post is heavier than the dew anywhere else, and that the water running down it is not running to the low ground but to a lip in the stone, and then into a scallop, and then along a groove, and then to a tinaja, where it stops.

The bench is an instrument. It was made into one a long time ago by somebody with a physics that is not yours, and it is mostly idle now, and it still works.

The flutes and scallops are not all natural. Some of them are, and some of them were cut to a different logic, and the erosion has since softened the difference so that you can only tell by the way the water behaves. The rock's porosity is not all geology. It holds water the way a charged thing holds charge, and lets it go on a cycle, and the cycle has a shape — it is not quite daily, it drifts, and the animals drift with it. The dew is not condensation. The dew is the bench exhaling.

The posts do something with the wet air. Stand close to one in the drinking hour and the hair on your arms lifts, and the water on the stone nearby beads and gathers and moves against the tilt, toward the post, against gravity, a slow crawl of droplets uphill. It is not dramatic. It is the quietest possible violation of what you thought was true. Then the hour passes and it stops and the post is just a grey post, warm, unremarkable, with a ring of slightly greener mat around its base.

There is a larger thing at the far end of the bench, where the shelves fold — a housing, half-buried, big enough to walk into if you crouched, and the interior is dry and warm and smells of nothing at all, and there is no dust inside it, and there never has been. The walls are the same porous stone and they are not stone. You can put your hand on it and feel a slow pulse that is not your own and is not quite regular.

Whatever the bench is for, it is not for you, and it does not appear to need you, and it has been in balance with the wind and the rain and the small grey animals for a very long time.

## Rules of the world

Water comes from the stone, on a cycle, and the cycle can be read. Everything alive here reads it.

Sound does not carry. You will not hear a thing coming. You will feel the wind, and you will see the water move, and you will learn to watch the birds, who go quiet and still before anything happens.

The pockets of water are owned. Not by law — there is no law here, no one to make one — but by long use, and the birds know the ownership, and the slow things in the basins know it, and if you drink from a tinaja you should drink from the windward lip and leave the rest. The bench keeps its accounts.

And the marks are older than the weathering, and the weathering has caught up to them, and that is the strangest thing about the place: not that there was high engineering here, but that it has been here so long it has settled into the landscape's own habits, has become one of the things the wind does, has gone quiet and patient and unremarkable, sweating its water every morning and giving it to whoever is there to drink.

You stay a few days. You learn the drinking hour. You find yourself standing very still in the enormous bright quiet, listening to almost nothing, watching the water climb a post against its own inclination, and you begin to suspect that the bench has not been abandoned at all — that idleness and long use look the same from the outside, and that you have arrived in the middle of a conversation that is being conducted very, very slowly, in water, in silence, in small increments of warm pale stone.
