# The Cold Anvil

The plateau is enormous. That is the first fact of it, the one that organizes all the others. It runs out in every direction until the running out becomes a kind of weather — a pale distance that the eye cannot finish. The sky above it is thin and hard and very high, and the light comes down through that thinness sharp-edged, so that shadows are precise and the far ridges look close enough to touch and are not.

The rock is young. It has not been rounded yet. The mountains along the plateau's rim are jagged, fresh-faced, still raw from whatever uplift raised them — sharp scarps, broken slabs, angles that catch the light and split it. Nothing here has had time to soften. The stone is grey and brown and sometimes a green-grey where lichen has crept in, but the lichen is thin, the moss is thin, the grass is thin and stiff and grows in tufts in the lee of rocks where the wind cannot reach it. Between the patches of living things the stone shows through everywhere, cold and clean and angular, and the whole plateau has the look of a place recently emerged and not yet settled into itself.

The cold is the second fact. It is not the cold of storms — the weather here is patient, not violent — but the cold of altitude and stone and thin air, a cold that lives in the ground and comes up through the feet and settles in the joints. Frost forms on the shaded sides of everything and stays there through the day. Breath is visible. Water, where it lies still, skins over at night and sometimes does not thaw. And yet —

And yet there is dampness. This is the strangeness at the heart of the place: a cold high country that is not dry. The moisture comes in pockets and intervals. Fog rolls up from the lower valleys in the mornings, thick and slow, and beads on the stone and the hide and the hair of things. Rain comes occasionally, not often, in long grey sessions that darken the rock and fill the channels. And everywhere beneath the surface there is water — meltwater from the high snows, seeping through the fissures of the young rock, emerging in springs that are small and cold and constant. The city is built around these springs. It has to be. They are the reason it is here at all.

The city is dense. That is the third fact, and it is the one that surprises. In all that vastness, the settlement is a knot — many knots, really, strung together across a cluster of ridges and hollows where the water gathers, so that to walk through it is to walk through a hundred small dense places connected by paths and stairs and narrow ways. The buildings are low and thick-walled, terraced into the slopes, leaning against one another for warmth and shelter. They are built of the local stone, split into slabs and stacked without mortar, and of bone — rib and horn and long leg-bone worked into lintels and pegs and door-frames — and of hide stretched over frames, and of wood that must have come from a long way down, because there are no trees here. The wood is precious and reused and worn smooth by hands. There is little metal; what there is, is small and old and mended. Most of the work is done with stone and bone and sinew and patience.

The construction is layered. New walls stand against old walls; upper storeys sit on lower ones; passages run between buildings and under them; roofs of hide and slab overlap and shed the rain into gutters and cisterns. Nothing is torn down. Things are added, patched, propped, built over. The city is an accumulation, a slow deposit of human care across generations, and it reads like geology — strata of dwelling, each layer answering the one beneath it. There are cisterns cut into the rock, lined with clay, holding the meltwater cold and clear. There are channels running along the paths to carry the rain. There are pools in the hollows, ringed with stone, where the water sits and reflects the hard pale sky. In the dry stretches between the springs, the ground is bare and dusty and cold, and the dampness is only a memory of fog.

The air is quiet. This is a hushed place. The wind is the main voice of it — a low continuous sound that comes across the plateau and through the gaps between the buildings and is always there, sometimes louder, sometimes almost gone. Beneath the wind there are smaller sounds: water moving in the channels, the creak of hide roofs, footsteps on stone, low voices, the sounds of animals. Because the fauna is abundant. That is the fourth fact, and it is the one that fills the place.

The animals are everywhere. Birds nest in every crevice of the stone — in the walls, under the eaves, in the fissures of the young mountains — and their droppings streak the rock white and grey. There are small furred things in the cracks and under the floors, and beetles in the damp hollows, and spiders in the corners of every room. In the cisterns there are pale fish, small and slow. Out on the plateau there are herds — heavy, shaggy, cold-adapted things that move across the vastness in long slow lines, grazing the thin grass, and the people of the city follow them and depend on them and have built their lives around their movements. There are predators too, lean and quiet, that come in the night and are respected. The abundance is not lushness; it is density within scarcity — many lives packed into the pockets where life can hold, the springs and the sheltered hollows and the crevices of the stone, while the open plateau between them is nearly empty.

The light is ordinary and exact. Day comes and goes in its cycle, and the sun crosses the thin sky and casts its sharp shadows and is gone, and the cold deepens, and the stars come out hard and close. There is nothing strange here. The world obeys its own rules: water runs downhill, stone is heavy, cold takes heat, living things need water and shelter and one another. The strangeness, if there is any, is only in the combination — the vastness and the density, the cold and the damp, the young sharp rock and the old slow city.

Time moves slowly. The city is not built in a season; it is built across generations, one wall at a time, one patch at a time. The herds move on their long cycles, and the springs run at their constant rate, and the frost forms and thaws and forms again. The rock erodes, but slowly — it is young yet, and the wearing-down has barely begun. The people here measure time in the movements of animals and the running of water and the slow accumulations of stone upon stone, and they are patient, because the place is patient, and because there is nothing here that rewards hurry. The cold anvil of the plateau holds them, and the springs feed them, and the wind speaks over them, and the vastness waits beyond the walls of the dense small city, pale and sharp and quiet, going on.
