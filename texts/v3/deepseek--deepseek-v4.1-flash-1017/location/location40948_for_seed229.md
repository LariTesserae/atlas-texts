# The Bell

High on the plateau, where the wind has scoured everything else down to a shine, there is a bowl in the rock about four hundred paces across. It is called the Bell, and it is older than it looks — the rim is worn round as a river stone, and the whole hollow has the settled, patient shape of a thing that finished becoming itself a long time ago.

There is no water here. Not a seep, not a puddle, not a bead of dew at the cold hour before the dim dawn. The air is dry enough to crack your lips in a minute, and nothing in the bowl has ever been wet. And yet the Bell is choked with growth — heavy, swollen, crowded growth, thick as a fever dream, so lush that in places you cannot see the ground.

## The ground

The floor is a hardpan of chalky dust and mineral crust, pale as a knuckle, faintly pink where the light hits it. It is not solid. Beneath a skin no thicker than a fingernail, the ground is hollow in places — the plateau's old gas pockets, sealed and empty — and when you walk, your steps boom. Not a thud: a note. The Bell is a drum with a floor of dust, and every footfall in the thin spots is answered somewhere under your feet by a low, resonant hum. The herders here walk on the balls of their feet without thinking about it, the way you'd tiptoe past a sleeping animal.

Around the rim, the erosion has been at work for a long time and has made its peace. Wind-carved shelves, undercut and then abandoned; ribs of harder stone standing out of the soft; dust drifted into long combs and flutes. Everything is polished to a dull sheen. Nothing is sharp.

## The dim

The light never gets higher than a late afternoon. The bowl sits under its own lid of dust: a whirlwind lives in it, a gyre that pours over the rim and spins and will not leave, and the gyre carries a permanent veil of powdered chalk and resin-dust that turns the sun to a brown-gold coin. Colors here are amber, rust, bone, and a bruised, dull purple where the growth is oldest. Shadows are long and soft. Nothing glitters except the resin.

## The growth

The plants are not green, and they are not wet. They are *voluminous* — that is the only honest word. A thicket looks at first like a heap of cushions, bolsters, bladders, hoses, hung and piled and braced against each other; and it is mostly air. The biggest of them, the bellows-plants, are hollow sacs taller than a person, skinned in a waxy ochre hide that creaks when the wind leans on it. Between them run cables of something like dry cordgrass, jointed and stiff, that clatter against each other in the gusting with a sound like a thousand small sticks. Lower down, mats of what the locals call lungmoss — a grey-green mat that inflates and deflates with the pressure changes of the gyre, slowly, all together, like the whole floor breathing.

They are lush the way a desert is vast: by commitment. Every leaf is a leather strap. Every surface is waxy, dusty, resinous. Nothing here evaporates, because there is nothing to evaporate, and so nothing here is soft.

## The sap

The water of this place is sap, and it is amber, and it is thick, and it smells of turpentine and burnt sugar. It lives in the plants and it moves through the animals and it comes back to the plants and it is very nearly a closed circle. You can tap a bellows-plant with a bone spile and draw a slow cup of it in an hour. It does not quench thirst, exactly. It coats. It burns going down. But the animals drink it, and the plants are fed by the animals, and that is the whole economy of the Bell.

The animals come for the sap, and they carry it in their bellies and on their bodies, and they leave it behind them in scat and shed shell and the long slow seep of their skin, and the plants take it up through a root-mat that lies just under the crust like a layer of felt. Nothing here rots. There is no mold, no damp decay, no smell of turned earth. The dead dry and are collected — by ants, by tenders, by the wind — and are packed into the root-mat and become growth. There is a great deal of death in the Bell, and it is all very tidy and very dry.

## The sound

It is never quiet. This is the loudest small place I have ever stood in.

The gyre roars at the rim, a steady rough hiss that never stops, and underneath it the cordgrass clatters and the bellows-plants creak and groan like a ship's rigging. And then there are the animals, and there are a great many animals. Swarms of hard-shelled chirpers go up in columns and come down and go up again, and the column of their wings is a physical pressure on the ear. There are drummers — flat, many-legged things the size of a hand, which find the thin places in the crust and beat on them, deliberately, with a rhythm that is not quite regular, and the ground takes the beat and hands it back up through your feet. There are gliding kites that ride the gyre in long ellipses; there are grey hopping herds that move through in a rush every so often, a hundred of them, and are gone; there are the carriers, slow and enormous and patient, walking cisterns of sap, with small animals riding them and drinking from the seams of their hide.

The Bell does not have a background sound. It has a hundred foregrounds, and they overlap.

## The rule

Here is the thing you have to understand about this place, the thing that makes it strange: **the plants grow toward noise.**

They do not turn to the light. There is hardly any light worth turning to. They turn — slowly, over hours, the bellows-plants swiveling on their root-braces like heads — toward whatever is loudest. A thicket is thickest where the swarms are thickest, where the drummers drum. Sound is fertility here. The sap flows toward the uproar, the animals follow the sap, the animals make the uproar, and the growth leans in to listen with its whole swollen body.

Which means that quiet is death. A grove that falls silent is a grove that stops being fed. Everyone who lives at the edge of the Bell knows this, and it is why the harps exist.

## The marks

There are a few structures. Not many — a handful, scattered, a long walk between each. Low shelters of woven cordgrass smeared with resin against the dust, half-sunk into the lee of the rim; a rack of drying racks; cairns of stacked crust-shards marking the safe paths over the hollow ground; a fire-pit of blackened stones, because resin burns, and burns hot, and the smell of it carries. There are rubbing-posts where the carriers come to be scratched, worn smooth and dark with handling. There are three harps along the western shelf: bone frames strung with sinew and gut and slivers of shell, braced across the gaps in the rock where the wind is forced to pass, and they make a constant low complaining music, a chord that never resolves, and the growth around them is the thickest in the Bell.

The tenders are few. You might see one — a figure on the rim in a resin-stiff coat, walking a path that drums, carrying a sack of spiles and a rattle. They tap; they do not cut. They keep the harps strung. They talk to the thickets, out loud, because the thickets are listening, and because a silent tender is a tender who has let a grove die. In the evening, when the gyre backs around and the note of the Bell changes, they sit at the fire-pit and drum on a flat stone, and the chirpers come down to see, and the bellows-plants turn their blind faces toward the noise and swell.

And when, once or twice a year, the wind falters and the Bell goes still — when the harps stop and the drummers stop and the whole bowl holds its breath — the tenders stop too, and stand very still in the dust, and wait, until the gyre takes the rim again and the roaring comes back and the place remembers how to be alive.
