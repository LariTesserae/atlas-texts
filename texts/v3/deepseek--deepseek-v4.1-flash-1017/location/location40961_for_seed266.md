# The Terraces of Orm

The world here is a single slow upheaval: a scarp of pale, sintered rock lifted three thousand meters above a basin so dry it has no name worth keeping. The basin floor is salt and dust and something granular between the two, a gray-white tartar that drifts in slow sheets and never pools. Nothing falls from the sky. The sky, when it is visible, is a thin warm bronze, and the light comes through it the way light comes through an old window—flattened, patient, without glare.

The escarpment is the country. It rises in terraces, each one a few hundred meters of wind-cut ledge above the next, stacked like the plates of a disassembled machine. The rock is layered: saltstone, ash-fused shale, and a hard vitreous crust the color of weak tea where the wind has polished it for a very long time. Erosion here is not spectacle. It is a settled craft. The terraces have been sanded into shapes that look almost intended—arches that hold, fins that narrow to a blade and keep standing, bowls scoured perfectly round. The wind does this over centuries with the patience of a clerk.

The wind is the weather. On a world this dry, the only atmosphere worth reporting is moving air, and the scarp makes its own: warm air climbs the face each day and spills over the top, and cold air comes down at night and pools in the bowls, and the whole thing breathes on a forty-hour cycle. During the climb, dust rises off the terraces and hangs in the updraft in long ropes, so the upper escarpment is always slightly veiled, and the light up there is grainy and gold. When the ropes thicken into a true storm, the settlements shutter and the cables go slack and everyone waits, because a dust-front at this scale takes two days to pass and there is no hurrying it.

On the terraces, strung out along the cliff like knots on a very long thread, are the towns. They are not dense. A town here is a hundred structures with kilometers of empty ledge between it and the next, and the emptiness is not a failure of the town—it is the point. The buildings are cut from the rock they stand on, or built of it in blocks, low and thick and flat-roofed, joined by stairs and terraced paths and, where the ledge gives out, by cable-lines and counterweighted lifts that groan up the face at a walking pace. The industrial works are everywhere and nowhere: a kiln the size of a temple standing alone on a shelf with nothing around it but scree; a refinery that is mostly a lattice of pipes and a slow-turning flywheel; a line of ore-cars climbing a switchback with no engine visible, pulled by a cable from above. The machinery runs on heat and gradient and the patient falling of weights, and it runs slowly, and it has been running a long time. There is the smell of hot metal and ozone and powdered stone. There is almost no noise—a low hum, the tick of cooling housings, the far cable singing in the wind, and long stretches of nothing at all.

Nothing green grows. The rock is colonized instead by a crust—thin, brittle, faintly iridescent mats that spread in the lee of stones and feed on the mineral dust and the warmth and the faint chemistry of the stone itself. It is not plant life; it is something older and slower, closer to a rumor of life. But it is the base of everything, and everything eats it. Fauna on Orm is abundant and small and strange. Drift-mites in the dust-ropes, translucent and patient. Stone-borers that leave the terraces pocked with neat round holes and are never seen. Grazers the size of a hand, plated in grit, that move across the crust-mats in slow herds and are in turn eaten by long pale things that live in the crevices and come out at night when the air pools cold. And above, riding the updraft in wide spirals all day, the drifters—enormous, membranous, almost weightless, with gas-bladders and trailing filaments, so that the upper escarpment is never quite empty of them. They cast a soft moving shadow over the terraces. They call rarely, and the call is low and carries a long way.

The people who live here keep the long rhythms. They work the kilns and the cable-yards, they mend the crust-mats where the ore-cars have cut them, they read the dust and know when to shutter. They built their towns to be outlived by nothing, and the towns are older than most of the machinery in them, and the machinery is being replaced a piece at a time by other machinery that is being replaced.

At evening the wind reverses and the rope of dust turns and begins its slide down the face, and the light goes the color of the crust, and for an hour the whole scarp is one warm low hum. Then it quiets, and the borers come out of their holes, and the drifters settle onto the high ledges to wait for morning.
