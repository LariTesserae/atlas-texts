# The Sump

You don't see it until you're almost falling in. The marsh is flat and cold and full of standing water, and then the ground simply stops — a lip of raw clay, a step, and below you a bowl perhaps ninety paces across, roofed over entirely. The place has one entrance and it is the sky, and the sky there is not sky but a ceiling of plants.

It is new. That's what the torn root-ends tell you, pale and unwethered, still sharp where they snapped. The collapse happened within living memory — the ground over a shallow void gave way and the marsh poured a little of itself in, and nothing has had time to soften since. The walls are sheer and freshly scarred, cut with the marks of their own falling, and there is no moss on the youngest faces, no lichen, no softened edge anywhere. Water beads out of the clay in strings and runs down. Nobody has decided yet what this place is going to look like; it is still becoming it.

The ceiling: the marsh's plant mass closed over the hole and did not stop. Roots come down in ropes, runners bridge the gap and thicken, pale leaves press flat against one another seeking the little light, tendrils hang and keep hanging. Over the years it has woven itself into a single mat, meters thick in places, and it is still growing down. Every so often something lets go — a fist of wet fiber, a whole shelf of leaf — and comes down into the water with a sound like a dropped coat. The people below cut it back. They have to. It would close the place entirely in a decade.

Almost no light gets through. What there is arrives as a green-brown murk, enough to make shapes, not enough to make colors. The real light is the animals, and there are so many animals.

The floor is water — a hand's depth over most of it, deeper in the old channels, a skin of standing cold that rises after rain and falls back slowly, leaving the mud glazed. It is never still. Everything is being walked on, swum through, hatched in, fed in. The overwhelming fact of the place is that it is full — full to the walls with small lives, and almost none of them make a sound. There are no choruses here. No birds, few frogs, nothing that calls. The overwhelming fauna of the Sump is a fauna of creeping and drifting and grazing, of things that have learned not to announce themselves in a dark closed bowl. You hear them as texture: a continuous fine ticking, the wet shift of bodies, the occasional soft plop no one looks up for.

Some of them are not animals exactly, or not only. In the ceiling mat live sessile things that are harvested like fruit — pale sacs, curled bodies rooted in the fiber, a soft gray flesh that opens when touched. Whether they are plant or animal is a question the Sump's people find funny and slightly rude. They cut them down with a bone hook and eat them. They are the staple.

The town is built out over the water on stilts. It is a real town — a couple of hundred people, a hundred-odd structures — but it is scattered, spread thin across the bowl, each house standing alone on its four or six poles with open water between it and the next. Nobody builds close. The place has room and the people use the room. Walkways of split sapling run between clusters and stop, deliberately, short of each other; you step from one to the next or you wade. The houses are woven and lashed: withies, hide, cord twisted from fiber, clay daubed into the gaps. There are ladders. There are hand-dug channels dredged through the mud to move the shallow water where they want it. There are weirs, small ones, stake-and-brush, that catch things. There is almost no metal — a few blades traded in from somewhere drier, worn to slivers and re-hafted many times. Everything else is bone, wood, clay, rope, hands.

Fire is possible but hated. A spark in the ceiling would take the whole bowl. So they light the dark with what they catch: jars and gourds of the drifting luminous things, kept alive on wet fiber, hung at doorways and along the walkways and carried on poles. It gives a low, uneven, breathing light, and it moves, and it dies, and is replaced. The town at night is a scatter of small wandering glows across a black bowl, and a great many things in the water and in the ceiling noticing them.

The rules of the place are mostly rules about timing. The water tells you when. It comes up after rain from the marsh above, filtering through the broken rim, and when it comes up so do the buried things, and the grazers follow, and the things that eat the grazers follow — and the whole population of the Sump moves in a slow pulse, up and down, in and out, on a schedule nobody wrote down but everybody knows. There are hours when you do not go on the water. There are channels you do not dredge. There are two weeks a year when the pale sacs in the ceiling open and release something into the air in numbers past counting, and everyone stays indoors with their lamps low and their mouths covered and waits for it to be over. Nobody has explained this to an outsider in a way an outsider understood.

The walls slump a little each year. Every few seasons a section comes down — you hear it first as a change in the dripping, then a long tearing, then a wet enormous thud and the water slapping the stilts — and there is a new raw place, and a new set of channels, and the town shifts a house or two and rebuilds the walkway somewhere else. Nothing here is finished. The place is too young to be finished.

And it is quiet. Always. People speak low out of habit rather than law. The loudest thing in the Sump is water: dripping off the walls, trickling through the weirs, lapping at the poles. Under that, the fine continuous ticking of an enormous number of small lives going about their business in the dark, in the wet, in a hole in the marsh that is still, after all these years, falling in.
