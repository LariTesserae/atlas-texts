# The Basin That Drains Upward

There is no name for it because there is no one to name it. The ground goes out flat in every direction and does not end so much as fade into a grey that could be distance or could be weather. It is a shallow dish, a floodplain that gave up on draining a long time ago and settled into the arrangement instead. You can see a long way, and what you see is more of the same: reed, sedge, standing water in sheets, hummocks of moss the color of old copper, and the low grey lid of sky pressing down until the two greys meet.

The light never arrives. It only seeps. Somewhere above there is a brighter patch where the sun should be, but it comes through as a diffuse green-brown wash, the way light comes through the palm of a hand held over a lamp. Everything is dim and everything is legible. You will not stub your toe here. You will just never quite see the far side of anything.

**The ground.** It gives about a hand's depth and then holds — a mat of root and moss and last year's reed laid over jelly. Beneath the mat is water, and beneath the water is peat, and the peat is red-brown and old and does not smell of rot so much as of tea left steeping too long. The water in the open channels runs clear. The water in the sheets is the color of weak tea and holds a skin of iron that breaks into rainbows when you disturb it. Everywhere the water is at the surface or a finger's width below it. There is no dry place to sit. There is no need to sit.

Nothing here has a sharp edge. The hummocks are rounded. The reed stubble is blunt. The fallen willow scrub is half-sunk and softened and has been for years. This is a place that finished eroding long ago and now only reworks itself, patiently, in the same shapes. The channels braid and rejoin and braid again, and if you watched them for a lifetime you would see them shift by a meter and no more, and always into the same pattern.

**The hummocks are in arcs.** This is the first thing you notice that doesn't belong to ordinary ground. They are not scattered. They line up in long shallow curves, one behind another, like ripples caught and then caught again at the same interval. From a hummock's top, if the mist lifts, you can see the arcs run out across the whole basin and go on running past where you can see, and the spacing between them is regular enough that you find yourself counting and losing count.

**The air** is cool and does not move much. It is wet enough that you are wet, that your sleeves are wet, that the mist does not so much fall as occupy. In pockets the air smells of sulfur and the mud underfoot is not warm but less cold than it should be, and a bubble the size of a plate comes up through an inch of water and sits there, domed, before it lets go. Methane. There is a great deal of methane. The marsh breathes it out in slow irregular pulses, and that is part of what you hear.

**The sound.** The marsh is loud. Not with animals — with water. Thousands of small falls, each one a trickle over a lip of peat into a pool a hand's width lower, and each one with its own note, so the whole basin roars softly the way a crowd roars: not one voice but the sum of too many. Over it, the fizz of gas coming up, the creak of peat settling under its own weight, the hiss and clatter of reed in what wind there is. You have to raise your voice to be heard. There is no one to hear you.

And under the roar, a rhythm. The bubbles do not come up at random. They come up in runs — four, a pause, seven, a pause, a long run, a pause — and the runs are the same runs in different parts of the basin with a lag between them, as though the news is travelling. It is not a language. It is not nothing.

**How the water moves.** The channels do not run downhill. Not all of them, and not always. There are runs of thirty or forty meters where the water goes up a hand's width against the gradient and arrives at a sump that should be dry and is not. There are pools that stay at one level while the basin around them rises, and pools that empty in an afternoon while the rest of the world holds still. The marsh is a drainage system, and it is a very good one, and it is not obeying the rules a drainage system should obey.

The peat explains part of it, the way a sponge explains part of a sponge. The mat explains more. The mat is a membrane — root and moss and bacterial skin, wet, full of ions moving from the sulfide-rich deep to the oxygen-thin surface, and the arcs of hummocks are its lattice. Water passing through the lattice does work. It sorts. It routes. It remembers which way it went last time and goes that way again, and after enough seasons of going that way the route is good, and the routes are why the water climbs.

Call it a computation if you like. It is not fast. A calculation that would take a machine a moment takes this basin a season, and the basin has had a great many seasons, and it has been calculating the same water for all of them. It has gotten very good. Some of what it does now, no one could write down, because it was never written — it was grown, and it is still growing, and it is older than the idea of writing.

**What breaks.** The rules here are not the rules. You will learn this in small ways first. A pool reflects a sky that is a different grey than the sky. A channel runs uphill. You cross a channel, walk twenty steps, turn around, and the hummock you were standing on is on the other side of the water. Not moved — it was always there. You were somewhere else for a moment and did not notice.

The longer you stay the more the sound resolves. Not into words. Into shape — a phrase of rhythm that means something the way a smell means something, all at once and without translation, and then the roar closes over it. It is not speaking to you. It is speaking near you, the way a room full of people speaks near a stranger, and sometimes a phrase surfaces.

**What lives here.** Very little, and almost none of it twice. There is a wading bird that is taller than a wading bird should be, grey on grey, and it stands in one place for most of a day and you will see it once and never again. There is a fish that comes up at dusk — one fish, as far as anyone could tell, and no one has told. The insects are few and small and do not swarm; they arrive singly, as though sent. The marsh is not a good habitat in the ordinary sense. It is occupied. It is busy with something that is not eating and not breeding.

**The rules, as far as they go.** Do not stand in one place long enough for the mat to notice — it grows toward weight, and it is patient, and you are weight. Do not follow the sound when it resolves; it does not lead anywhere you can come back from as yourself. The water rises in the last hour before the light fails, everywhere, by a hand's width, and falls again by morning, and no one could say why. And when the bubbles come in a long unbroken run — that is when it is paying attention. Stand still. Do not answer. It will go back to its water.

It is not hostile. It is not kind. It is a place that has been solving one problem for a very long time in a basin that does not drain, and it has become very good at it, and it has gotten large, and you are a small warm thing standing in the middle of its work.
