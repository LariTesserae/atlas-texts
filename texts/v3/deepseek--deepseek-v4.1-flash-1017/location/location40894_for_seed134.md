# The Crown

You come down through water that is cold and almost without color, and the light comes with you — not the dimming blue of deep water but real daylight, broken into a lattice that slides over your arms as the surface above you moves. Thirty meters, forty. Cold enough to ache at the joints. Clear enough that the whole shape of the thing is visible at once, and it is small: a single summit, a floor's worth of stone, no bigger than a cathedral seen from the choir.

It rises out of a shaft of nothing. Below the crown's lip the rock drops away into water that has no floor you can find, and the summit sits on top of that drop like a lid. Black basalt, and young — you can tell because nothing has been rounded. The fractures are still sharp. Slabs break at clean angles, glassy where they split, ledges that would cut a hand. The sea has not had time to file anything down; this rock is fresh, recently shouldered up, and the edges are still the edges of the break.

And it is bare. That is the first strangeness. In water this bright, at this depth, you expect fuzz — green, brown, a fur of weed, a slime of algae on every surface. There is none. The stone is scoured to itself, clean as a kitchen, and where it isn't clean it is covered in animals. Nothing green anywhere. The bareness is not emptiness. The bareness is the work of everything that lives here, eating the rock down to the rock.

Because it is crowded. Crowded in layers, near-touching, so that your eye can't find a gap. The ledges are packed with pale fists and tubes; the crevices are packed with things that have folded themselves into the crevices; fans stand up like ferns made of glass and bone. There are mats of small hard bodies so dense they read as texture, not as individuals. In the water above the stone hangs a second population, suspended, drifting, a haze of small swimming things that never quite disperses. Nothing here has room. Everything is touching something.

And it is loud. You feel it before you can separate it into sounds: a steady oceanic roar with a thousand teeth in it. Snapping — a crackle like fat in a pan, continuous, from the mats. Knocking from inside the tubes. A grinding rasp where something is dragging its mouth over stone. Chirps, ticks, a low thrum that comes up through your sternum. Your own breathing is inaudible. It is loud enough that you cannot hear yourself think, and it does not vary. That is the thing you notice after a while: the volume never changes. Day, night, it roars at the same level. The noise is not events. The noise is the machine running.

Because this is a factory, and no one built it.

Water seeps from the fractures — cold, mineral-heavy, squeezing out of the rock's new cracks — and where it meets the sea it leaves metal behind. Bright crusts smear the fissures: blue-black, a bloom of verdigris, sulfur-yellow, a bruised purple. The deposits follow the joints so exactly that they look machined, printed, laid down by a process. Nothing laid them down but mixing. The rock is refining itself, slowly, and the animals have arranged themselves around the refineries.

They pump. Whole banks of them, tubes and throats and sieves, moving the water through themselves in a slow tidal clench, and the current over the crown is not the sea's current, it is theirs, worked up out of the crowd. They grind the stone clean. They filter the haze and pass it up the layers. And there is a charge in the water — faint, a prickle at the fillings of your teeth, a wrongness in your instruments. Something down there makes electricity. Many somethings, probably; the fields overlap, and the whole crown carries a slow pulse that belongs to no single animal, a beat that emerges from the crowd the way the roar does. Watch the small swimmers and you can see them orient to it. The pulse is a clock. The pulse is a shift bell.

The strange part is the maintenance. Stay long enough — hours, days, the place rewards patience and nothing else — and you can watch a component wear out. A bank of pumpers silts up, slows, clogs. And over the next while, without anyone deciding, the crowd around it shifts: the grazers move in, the tubes are cleared, the dead are eaten, new bodies settle into the emptied place and begin to pump. The factory replaces its own parts. There is no owner, no plan, no off-switch. There is only density and appetite, and out of density and appetite, function.

Nothing here is quick. The whole thing runs on decades — the pulse, the deposition, the slow rotation of which species holds which ledge. A shift change takes a season. The metal crusts thicken by a hair a year. You could leave and come back in twenty years and find the same roar, the same clean stone, the same arrangement, all of it different in every part.

The light slides over it. The lattice moves. The crown roars, cold and bright and crowded, and the water carries its charge up past you toward the surface, and the machine keeps running because it cannot do anything else.
