# The Kettle

You come up the mountain through cloud, and the cloud is warm. That's the first wrong thing. Cloud should be cold at this height, and this one is not — it's a wet breath held against your face, and it smells faintly of iron and cut stone. The last hundred meters you climb by feel, hands on rock that is sharp in a way rock usually isn't: freshly broken, glassy at the edges, still ringing under the fingertips like the inside of a struck bell. Nothing has had time to round it off. This mountain is new.

Then the rim, and the drop.

The Kettle is not large. You could walk its width in a minute and a half, its length in three. It's a collapse scar, a shallow bowl punched into the summit maybe forty years ago by the look of the fractures — a piece of the mountain that simply let go and fell inward, and the wound is still open, still sharp-lipped, still steaming.

---

**Terrain.** The floor is a shelf of that same young stone, fractured into blades and tablets that tilt at argumentative angles, and between the blades the ground is a crust of pale sinter — mineral lace deposited by water that comes up hot, spreads thin, and leaves its burden behind. The sinter is soft underfoot in places and rings hollow in others, a thin shell over voids where the steam goes. The whole basin tilts very slightly toward the northwest, where a notch in the rim lets the overflow escape as a warm trickle down the outer face. That trickle has been running long enough to cut a channel. Not long. Years, not centuries. Everywhere the stone is bright, unweathered, raw — the erosion here is a child's erosion, all fresh edges and unfinished work.

**Water.** It is wet in a way that has nothing to do with rain. The air arrives already saturated — the cloud pours over the rim in slow curds and dissolves into the bowl — and the water does not so much fall as *settle*. It beads on every fracture, gathers in every seam. The hot ground makes the air above it restless; steam lifts off the sinter in ribbons, meets the cool of the cloud, condenses, and comes back down as a fine warm mist that never quite becomes drops. You are inside a weather. Your clothes are wet through in ten minutes and you will not notice until you leave, because the wetness is body temperature and the air is body temperature and the boundary between you and the place has gotten vague.

In the middle of the bowl, water does something water should not.

**The weave.** There are sixteen threads. I counted them twice. They hang in the air between the stone blades, and they are strung not downward but *across* — thin, taut, glassy filaments of water, each one perhaps a meter long, quivering, gathering light. They should fall. They do not fall. Surface tension alone will not hold a filament of that gauge over that span; the water would sag and break and be done with it. These hold, and they thicken, and every so often one of them *plucks* — a sound like a wet finger drawn around a glass rim, very quiet — and a bead of water travels along it, from one stone to the other, and releases.

Something is doing that. At the southwest edge, half-buried in the sinter, is the only made thing in the Kettle: a squat cylinder of a material that is not metal and not stone, dull as a river pebble, maybe the size of a hunched person, sitting in a collar of the mineral crust as though it grew there. There are no markings on it. There is no seam, no port, no control. It is warm to the touch — warmer than the ground, which is warm. It gives off a tone you feel in your teeth more than hear. And the threads run from its upper rim outward in a fan, sixteen of them, and the water in them moves according to no gradient you can measure — uphill as often as down, toward the cylinder as often as away.

Whatever this thing was for, it is still for it. It has been for it, patiently, in the dark and the steam, for longer than the basin has existed. The mountain came apart around it and it did not stop.

**Vegetation.** Lush, but not crowded — the Kettle does not do crowds. Each plant stands alone with a respectful gap around it, as though the place had agreed on a minimum distance. In the steam-ward crevices, mosses in colors moss should not be — a copper-green, a bruised violet — furring the stone in small precise cushions. Two ferns, old ones, their crowns a meter across, uncurling new fronds that are still wet and translucent. A scatter of small orchids with waxy leaves, growing out of pure sinter, which should not be possible. And in the lee of the tallest stone blade, one tree — no taller than four meters, crooked, its bark smooth and pale as a limb, its leaves narrow and held out flat as if to catch what the threads are saying. It is the only tree on the summit. It leans toward the weave. Everything green here leans toward the weave.

**Life.** You will probably see nothing that moves on its own. The fauna here is rare and small and knows you are coming. But if you sit still in the steam long enough, you may see one of the walkers: a pale thing the size of a thumbnail, more spider than crab or more crab than spider, that comes out of a crack and crosses to a thread and climbs onto it — walks *along* the filament of water without breaking it, its weight troubling the surface not at all, and drinks, and goes back. There are perhaps a dozen in the whole Kettle. They exist nowhere else. They are, as far as anyone can tell, made mostly of the same water they walk on.

**Sound.** Almost none. The hiss of steam, which you stop hearing. The tick and drip of the sinter. The occasional pluck of a thread. Your own breath, loud in your ears, which is the loudest thing in the basin and makes you want to breathe quietly. Twice an hour, if the wind is right, a low tone from the cylinder that is felt rather than heard and makes the threads shiver all at once, and then the water in them reverses, and the whole weave runs backward for a while, and the mosses brighten.

**Air.** Hot. Not the dry hot of a desert but a hot that is *full* — heavy, wet, mineral, faintly sulfurous under the iron. The light in it is ordinary daylight, but the steam scatters it fine, and everything in the Kettle looks lit from within rather than from above. At the wrong hour the whole bowl glows like the inside of a lamp.

**Rules.** Do not touch the threads. Not for fear of breaking them — you cannot break them; I tried, with a stick, and the stick came back wet and the thread was unbothered — but because they will find the water in your arm and pull, gently, and you will feel it, and you will want to stay. The place rewards stillness. That is the whole of its law: stay long enough, be quiet enough, and the water will start to arrange itself around you, and you will be one more thing in the basin that the weave leans toward. People have gone up to the Kettle and been found there days later, perfectly well, very wet, entirely unable to say what they had been doing.
