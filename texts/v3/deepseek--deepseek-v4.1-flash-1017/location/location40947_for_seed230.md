# Sarn

You come up onto it from below, through cloud, and the cloud does not so much lift as thin — the world going from white to grey to a soft granular brown, and then the first paving stones underfoot, wet and dark, and the sound of your own steps arriving before the town does.

Sarn sits on the shoulder of a mountain that has forgotten to keep rising. The plateau tilts gently north and then gives up, dropping away into a bowl of cloud that never entirely clears. Above the town the ridge goes bare and rubbly for a few hundred metres and then loses itself in the same white. There is no summit anyone goes to. There is no view, most days, except the view of the cloud moving, which it does slowly, like something breathing.

The stone is dark — slate and a granite that goes nearly black when it's wet, which is most mornings. Everything is wet in the way that stone in high, cool, still air is wet: not from rain but from the air itself, which is so full of water that it settles. Walls are dark to knee height and pale above, a tide-mark of damp. Handrails are cold and slick. Moss grows in the joints of the paving and in a band along the base of every wall, and nowhere else, because nothing else holds the water long enough.

Rain does come, but rarely and without violence — a fine grain that drifts rather than falls, that takes an hour to be noticed. The real water is the fog. It comes up the slope at dusk and leaves in the middle of the morning, and the town is built entirely around the fact of it. There are cisterns everywhere, sunk into the paving, their covers flush and iron-dark, and the streets are cut with shallow gutters that carry the condensation down and sideways into them. There are stone troughs at every corner, always brimming. There are roofs that are not roofs exactly but wide shallow bowls of slate, pitched so slightly they look flat, draining inward to a spout. The whole town is a hand held out.

---

It is small, and it is also, unmistakably, a town. There are streets — properly paved, cambered, laid out long ago and never altered — and there are stairs between the levels, broad ones, with no railing, worn into shallow dishes in the middle by feet that have been using them for a very long time. There are drains that run under the streets and emerge as arched mouths at the edges, and there is a wall around the whole of it, chest-high in places, and gates in the wall that stand open and have stood open so long that the hinges have rusted into the position of openness and could not now be closed if anyone wanted to.

But between the buildings there is a great deal of nothing. The town is not crowded; it is scattered, generously, over the slope. A house, and then thirty paces of wet paving and heath grass and a stone trough, and then another house. A terrace of four dwellings, and then a long empty stretch where a terrace of four dwellings used to be or was never built, and which is now a flat paved place with one bench. A granary standing alone on its stubby stone legs, its under-croft dark and dry and full of wind. A meeting house with a bell in a stone cot on the roof, the bell green and quiet. Emptiness is not the town's failure; it is the town's material. It has been built *with* the emptiness, the way a wall is built with the gaps between the stones.

The building is all hand-work and all old. Dry-stone courses fitted without mortar, or with a lime mortar so old it has gone the colour of the stone. Slate laid in diminishing courses. Timber that has weathered to a silver-grey so complete that new timber is deliberately left out in the weather for years before it is used, so it will not shame the rest. Turf on some roofs, thin and cropped. There is simple metal — hinges, nails, a few iron bands — but nothing that required a furnace bigger than a room. Nothing is painted. Nothing is decorated except by the marks of the tools that made it, and by lichen.

No one can tell you when the last new building went up. There is a story that a house on the north terrace was rebuilt within living memory, and the old people say it was rebuilt to be indistinguishable from what it replaced, and it is, and this is considered correct.

---

The vegetation is thin and low and does not try. Heath grass that stays cropped short by wind. Cushions of moss in the seeps. Lichen in three colours on every exposed stone, slow lichen, the kind that takes a century to make a circle the size of a plate. A few junipers, black and contorted, no taller than a person, growing where the wall breaks the wind. Nothing that could be called a tree. There is sedge in the boggy ground below the north gate, and a stand of it there has been cut for thatch, they say, since before anyone can say.

Animals are few and mostly kept. A dozen goats that know the town better than the children do and can be found on the cistern covers in the cold. A dog. Birds, occasionally — a chough on the bell cot, dark and loud, gone. In the cloud, sometimes, the sound of something passing that is never seen. Nobody looks up. It is not that kind of place.

---

What you notice, after a day, is the stillness — not of sound, because there is sound, but of *state*. The wind moves the grass and the fog and does not move the town. The gutters run and the cisterns fill and are drawn from and fill again, the same water, more or less, going around. Doors are opened and closed. Someone mends a hinge with an iron pin, slowly, over an afternoon, and then the hinge is mended, and that is the news.

The town is in equilibrium and knows it and is not troubled by it. Erosion here is not decay; it is the slow settlement of things into the shape they were always going to hold. The stairs are dished, the thresholds are scooped, the walls lean a degree inward and have leaned that degree for a hundred years and will not lean further. Everything is at the point of the curve where it flattens. Nothing needs doing that will not be done by hand, in time, by someone who has done it before, in the same way.

The rule of the world, if you want it stated, is this: **put it back as you found it.** Every tool returned to its hook. Every stone lifted out of a gutter set back in the same place. Nothing is thrown away, because nothing is far enough gone to throw away; there is a shed by the south gate full of bent nails and split slates and lengths of iron too short to be useful, sorted by size, in case. When someone dies their house is not emptied but closed, and left, and the roof is maintained, and after a while someone moves in.

---

Sounds, at night: water finding its way through stone. A shutter tapping, once, and then not again. The bell, which is rung at dawn and at dusk and which is slightly cracked and has been slightly cracked since before it was hung, so that it makes a flat, not unpleasant note that carries a long way into the cloud and comes back changed.

Mostly, though, quiet. The fog is a good conductor of footsteps and a bad conductor of everything else. You can hear someone coming from three streets away, and it will take them a while to arrive, and when they do they will nod, and go past, and the sound of them will go on being audible after they are gone, and then it will not be.

---

There is a place at the top of the town, on the flat paving where the emptiness is widest, where the cisterns are largest and oldest and their covers are worn smooth. From there you can see the whole of Sarn at once — not much, a scatter of dark roofs and dark walls across a tilted grey field, a bell, a bench, a hundred small spillings of water — and you can see that it fits the slope exactly, and that the slope was made for it, or the other way round, and that no one has needed to decide which for a very long time.
