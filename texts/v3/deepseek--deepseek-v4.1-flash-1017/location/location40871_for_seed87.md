# The Kettle

The first thing is the lid.

It is ice, and it is close — a ceiling of green-black glacier, thick as a cathedral's roof, sealed over a chamber in the rock beneath. It is not smooth. It sags and bulges, creased with pressure ridges, and down one side runs a long fracture, a seam of old bottle-glass green where the light gets through. That seam is the sky here. It is the only sky. It brightens and dims in slow tides as the glacier above shifts and thickens and thins, and nothing in the Kettle has ever seen it directly, only its glow, coming down blue-green and cold through forty feet of ice.

Below the ice is water to the walls. The chamber is small — a flooded pocket in the bedrock, fifty paces one way, thirty the other, roofed and sealed. You could walk it in a minute if there were anywhere to walk. There is no weather here but the melt, no horizon, no distance. The far wall is always at the edge of sight, and at the edge of sight it dissolves into dimness before it resolves. Everything is near. Everything is within reach or lost.

The water is barely above freezing, and it is heavy with it — dense, still, pressing. It does not move so much as lean. Cold like this holds its breath. And it is thick with life: the water is not clear but *occupied*, a slow blizzard of tiny things, copepods and larvae and drifting shells, so many that the light going through it is grainy, a snow-globe that never settles. They hang. They mill. The whole volume of the room is a single crowded street.

## The city

It sits on the floor and climbs the walls, and it is not built so much as *secreted*.

The Kettle's masons are colonial animals, soft-bodied and pale, each colony a fist of milky tubes no bigger than a hand. They filter the drifting snow out of the water and, from what they don't use, they extrude a cement — calcium and silica, milky at first, curing in the cold over weeks into a stone harder than the rock it stands on. A colony builds outward from itself, layer on layer, and the living tissue stays at the growing edge while everything behind it goes white and hard and dead. Every wall in the Kettle is alive on one face and stone on the other. The city is inside-out.

Generations have accumulated. The floor is a single mass now, a reef of it, and it has the logic of a reef: buttresses, alcoves, chimneys, courtyards, galleries bored through by other tenants, stairways of accidental stone. It looks planned. It is not planned. But it is not random either — and here is the thing you notice after a while, the thing that makes the back of your neck prickle: the towers are placed exactly under the drips.

Where the ice overhead fractures, meltwater comes down — cold, fresh, carrying dust and mineral and the fine ground flour of the glacier. That is the richest water in the room. So the colonies have built toward it. Each seep has a tower under it, and the towers lean inward to catch the fall, and the tallest of them stand almost close enough to touch the lid, their summits glistening where the melt runs over them. The skyline of the Kettle is a map of the cracks in its sky. If you could see the roof and the city at once you would see one shape drawn twice.

Concentric rings spread from the great seam — the oldest settlement, the deepest accretion, layer upon layer like stromatolites, a history you could read if you knew the language of strata. It is dense beyond anything built by hands: surface against surface, no gap unoccupied, every crevice claimed. Things live in the walls. Things live in the walls *of* the walls.

## The inhabitants

Sparse green — a fur of algae only in the seam's brightest reach, bacterial films grazing the stone, a few threadlike plants like hair combed upward. Not much. The Kettle doesn't run on light.

It runs on the fall. The drip-fed towers are the pantry, and everything that eats lives near them. Blind crustaceans the size of a thumbnail pick over the cement. Pale things like shrimp cling in the galleries. There are fish here, small and white and eyeless, that navigate by the pressure of the water, and there is a larger one — the Kettle's patient presence — shaped like a slow closing hand, which hangs in the wide gallery under the central tower and does not move for days, and then does. Nothing here chases. Everything here *arrives*.

And there are the borers. Worms and clams that chew the cement, that live inside the walls and honeycomb them, that leave the little galleries the shrimp nest in and open the courtyards to the water. They are the only erosion in the Kettle, and they cannot keep up. The masons outbuild them. The city thickens. It has been thickening for a very long time.

Nothing rots here. Nothing rusts, nothing burns, nothing dries. There is no fire in this world and no tool, only bodies and what bodies make. When a thing dies it does not decay so much as *settle*, and the cement closes over it, and it becomes floor. To die in the Kettle is to become architecture.

## The silence

The deep is loud in most places. Not here.

The Kettle is quiet the way a held breath is quiet. The water is too still and too heavy to carry much; the ice above swallows the glacier's grinding before it arrives. What is left is a floor of near-silence — the low press of cold, the faint tick of things settling, and now and then, rarely, the *plink* of a drip finding a tower, or a crack running through the ice overhead like a thought.

Because of this, almost nothing here speaks. There is no point. The creatures of the Kettle signal in light instead — dim pulses, pale flares, a slow conversation of glow that runs along the walls when something passes: a blush of blue where a shrimp is startled, a thread of green where the borers are working. At certain times, when a melt pulse comes down through the seam, the whole city answers it at once, and the towers light from base to summit in a soft rising wave, and for a minute the Kettle is a lit room with a green ceiling — then it fades, and the dark settles back, and the water goes on leaning against itself in the cold.

## The rules

Everything is close. There is no distance to travel and no horizon to travel toward; every street bends back into the one you came from. The roof is a sky that can be touched. The floor is a history that can be touched. Between them, in the dense cold water, a crowded city of blind things builds upward toward a leak, is slowly eaten from within, and outpaces its own decay — because here, for once, growth is faster than loss, and the world is not wearing away.

It is still being assembled. The rock of the walls is raw and sharp-edged and unweathered. Nothing here has had time to be old yet. The Kettle is not a ruin and not yet a finished thing: it is a construction site that is also a body, murmuring in light, under a lid of green ice, with the melt coming down.
