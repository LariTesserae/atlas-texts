The sound is the first thing. It is not a sound that arrives; it is a sound you are already inside. A vast, flattened churn, like a river the size of a sky, but there is no river here, not in the ordinary way. The sound is the place itself, a constant low grinding of water forced through stone, of vapor sighing through ten thousand narrow vents, of something like industry — rhythmic, percussive, stamping — but with no source you can point to. There is no factory. There is no *made* thing. And yet the world runs.

You stand on ground that is not quite solid. The surface is a pale, cold, mineral crust, slick with condensation, pocked and seam-riddled. Beneath it, just beneath your feet, water moves. Not a trickle, but a pressurized, urgent flow, threading through the crust like blood through a membrane. The crust is thin. Every step sends a dull hollowness up through your boots. In places the ground has collapsed — not recently, but long ago, in a time before time learned to keep records — leaving black, still pools sunk into the pale terrain. The pools are not empty. They breathe a slow mist. They are cold enough that your breath, too, becomes a puzzling white ghost. The air is always wet, always on the edge of frost. It does not snow. It does not rain. The water simply *is* — condensing on every cold surface, weeping from the seams in the crust, rising as fog from the sinkholes, then sublimating somewhere unseen to fall again as another kind of mist. The whole plain has been doing this forever. It has achieved the perfect, patient equilibrium of a machine left running for eons. It is eroded down to its wear-resistant bones.

The landscape is open — a plain, or the floor of some enormous subsided basin, stretching out to a distance where the fog swallows all lines. But the sense of openness is a lie. The place is packed. It is saturated. You cannot walk three meters without crossing a vent, a canal, a frost-domed pool, a cluster of the crawling things. The crawling things — *fauna*, if the word can stretch this far — are everywhere. There are no plants. There is no soil. There is only this cold, wet mineral world and its occupants, and the occupants are many.

They are not animals, or not only animals. Some are the size of a fist, jointed, scuttling on thin mineral limbs across the slick crust, their bodies plated in the same pale gray as the ground. When they die — and they do, constantly; the ground is littered with their molted shells — their bodies are absorbed back into the crust. You can see half-dissolved shapes in the stone, like insects in ancient amber. Others are larger, soft-bodied, pervasive, pillar-shaped creatures that anchor themselves around the vents and pulse slowly, filtering the steam. They are the color of rusted iron and they do not move from their chosen vent for their entire lives. They whistle faintly with the pressure of the vapor passing through their bodies. Their whistling is part of the soundscape, a high, eerily tonal note layered over the low grinding. And there are things that are not quite either — things that seem to be colonies, aggregations of small crawling parts that move with a single, sluggish purpose, like a spill of animate gravel.

The most unsettling presence is the one that has no body. It is embedded in the sound and the air. The industrial rhythm — the stamping, the pumping, the grinding — has an architecture to it, a syntax. The vents open and close in patterns. The pools drain and refill in sequences. The crawling things move in swarms that coincide with the vent cycles. The world is not just a landscape; it is a system, a vast, self-operating apparatus, and its operation is the only law. Cause and effect here do not obey the rules of a sane world. You can watch a creature step onto a pool's frozen surface and not break through, while a moment later a pebble dropped nearby fractures a foot of ice. You can hear a sound — a distant, bell-like ringing — and then, minutes later, find its source in a place you have already passed three times. The geography rearranges itself when the fog thickens. The sinkholes are in different positions. This is not because the ground has moved; it is because the ground has always been in every position, and your path through it only settles on one version of the landscape when you are looking.

You do not build shelter here. There is no wood, no clay, no stone that stays stone. And the world would not tolerate it anyway. The place has its own rhythm and you are a temporary irregularity in it. The creatures ignore you. The mist does not care. The grinding goes on, patient and eternal, and the dim light — the source of which is not the sky, which is only a low, even, gray-brown ceiling of fog, but seems to seep from the pools themselves, a faint phosphorescent coldness — makes every surface gleam wetly. You cannot see far. You do not need to. The landscape is the same in every direction: ground like a cracked scab, water moving under and over it, cold things crawling and pulsing and dying and becoming the ground again, the sound of an ancient, mindless industry that never started and never stops.

It is not a ruin. It is not abandoned. It is perfectly occupied — packed, saturated, full — with the only life that this place permits, and the only rule is that the process continues. You could stand still until your own body joined the crust. The place would not notice. It would only keep humming.
