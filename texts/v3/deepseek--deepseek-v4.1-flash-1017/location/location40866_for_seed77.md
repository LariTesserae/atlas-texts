# The Curing Plain

The ground is one piece.

It lies flat to a horizon so far away that its curvature is only a suspicion, and there is no break in it — no soil, no outcrop, no drift of sand, no place where something older shows through. What you stand on is a single continuous surface, pale as a cut bone, warm through the soles, and it goes on being the same surface for a distance the mind declines to hold.

It is made, in the way a shell is made. Nothing here is unconstructed. The plain is the secretion of the small pale things that dot it, and there is no other ground beneath: no rock under the floor, only more floor, thickening downward into itself, generation on generation of the same slow cement.

Those makers are sparse. You can walk an hour and pass four of them. They are low knuckled masses, waist-high at most, dry and pale and closed, without leaves or mouths you can find. Their bases are set into the surface like teeth in a gum, and from those bases the material sweats out — a thin mineral liquor that spreads a hand's width, then another, then hardens. Each one sits at the center of its own finished disc, and the disc grows. Where the edge of a disc meets the edge of another, the material joins seamlessly, faintly banded where the two batches met, and the join disappears in a season.

The surface underfoot is not quite stone yet. In the old interior it rings flat and hard when you strike it, and it is faintly translucent at the thin places, so that in strong light there is a suggestion of depth below, of layers. Near the growing edges it is tacky, and there are patches — wide, irregular, out past the newest makers — where the floor is still a membrane, not yet cured, and it gives underfoot with a sound like a held breath. Step there and the surface flexes and returns. It will be hard by the end of the year. Nothing has walked here before, and the pale stuff takes your print and keeps it.

This is a world in its first draft. It has no weather-scar, no gully, no worn hollow. Rain has not yet taught it anything. The material is harder than the rain, and it accretes faster than the wind can take it away, and so the plain does not erode — it only thickens, spreads, closes its own cracks with fresh secretion, mends itself wherever it splits as it cures. The rule of this place is accumulation. Nothing is being taken down. Everything is being put up, slowly, from below.

The heat is the other rule. It stands on the plain all day like a weight. By mid-morning the floor has taken the sun into itself and begun giving it back, and the air above the surface shimmers in the first meter and then clears. A wind gets up around then, dry and steady, out of nowhere in particular, moving over the flat with nothing to break it: it carries a fine pale dust, the only loose thing here, the material's own flour, which settles in the faint hollows and in the ring around each maker. By evening the wind lies down and the floor lets go of its heat all at once into the dark, and then the condensation comes — the surface cooling faster than the air, beading all over, so that by first light the entire plain is wet and slick and the makers have swollen slightly, drinking through their bases. The water goes by noon. The floor goes white where it dried.

The low places hold water longer. The plain sags here and there, a hand's depth, no more, and after the rare rain — brief, hard, and hissing when it lands — those hollows stand full and warm and brassy for a few days, ringed with a brighter rim where the minerals came out of solution and stayed. The makers cluster around these, two or three together. Something in the standing water suits them. The water does not stagnate so much as slowly leave, and the hollow is dry and salt-rimmed and waiting for the next rain, which may be a long time.

The sky is enormous because the ground gives it nothing to compete with. You can see weather happening far off: a grey column of rain walking across the plain ten kilometers away, dragging its shadow, soundless at that distance; a shadow of cloud sliding over the pale material and off again. The rain is always somewhere else first.

Sound does not carry here the way it should. The wind over the smooth flat makes a low continuous note, almost below hearing, and it is the only thing with a voice. There are no birds. There are no insects in the air, none in the water, nothing moving in the dust. No animal life at all, and the absence is a category, not a quiet — nothing has ever been hunted here, nothing has ever hidden, no call has ever been answered. The makers do not move. When the floor cures overnight it ticks and crackles faintly, deep in the material, and that is the loudest thing. Your footsteps are flat and hard and alone. You may be the first thing that has ever crossed this ground on legs.

You would think a place so empty was finished, or abandoned. It is neither. It is early. Off past the horizon the edge of the material is still ragged and wet and spreading, a little every day, ringed by makers not yet old enough to have made anything round. In a thousand years there will be depth to it, and layers, and perhaps something will have learned to live on it. For now it is only what it is: a hot, flat, quiet country, half a millimeter thicker than it was yesterday, entirely made, and still being made.
