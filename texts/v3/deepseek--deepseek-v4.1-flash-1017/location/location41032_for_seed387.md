# The Kettle

You come into it through the throat, and the throat is the first thing that tells you what kind of place this is. It's a passage of wet black rock about the width of two shoulders, running not down but *sideways*, and the sea pushes through it on a slow cycle — in for forty seconds, out for forty, in for forty — so that you are not swimming so much as being swallowed and spat by something with a pulse. The rock is warm. Not comfortably warm: blood-warm, fever-warm, the temperature of a body you don't want to be inside. Your hands find the walls and the walls are slick with a film that isn't algae, because nothing here is algae. It's mineral. It's the warm water depositing itself, slowly, in a skin.

And then the throat opens, and the sound arrives.

You have never heard anything like it, because nothing on the surface can make this sound. The chamber beyond is a single long room — sixty meters, maybe, ceiling low and vaulted like a cellar, floor somewhere far below in the dark — and every forty seconds the sea surges through the throat and strikes an outcrop of resonant stone at the far end, and the whole chamber *sings*. A low, enormous, brass-throated note. It doesn't stop between surges; it decays, and the decay overlaps with the next strike, so the sound is continuous, layered, a chord that never resolves. It comes up through your sternum. You feel it in your teeth and your sinuses and the small bones of your inner ear. Speech is useless. Nothing anyone has ever said in the Kettle was heard.

They don't talk. They knock.

---

The town is built along the walls, and only along the walls, because the middle of the chamber belongs to the water and the water is not something you argue with. The result is a city turned inside-out — two long strips of construction facing each other across a void, connected by cables and cages and a few swinging bridges strung high where the surge is weakest. You could put the whole settlement in a city block and have room left over. But within that, everything is stacked and layered and patched into everything else: pump houses bolted to galleries, galleries bolted to ladders, ladders to platforms, platforms to pressure doors that open onto nothing in particular. Catwalks. Lockers. A shrine to a saint nobody remembers, its niche full of warm water. Some of the construction is poured concrete, some is riveted steel gone the colour of old bone, some is salvage that came down through the throat in pieces and was reassembled by feel. All of it is old. All of it is *maintained* — that's the thing you notice after a while, the way every joint has been re-made a dozen times, the way the patina is not neglect but long careful use. Nothing here has been abandoned. It has all been lived in so long that it has settled into its own shape, like a shoe.

The buildings are mostly not buildings the way you'd mean it. They're *machines with rooms in them*. The whole town exists to run the pumps, and the pumps exist to keep the lower galleries from filling completely, and the lower galleries are already mostly full, so the arrangement has a certain gallows logic to it. The engines turn. They have always turned. Their noise is underneath the great brass note of the chamber like a floor beneath a flood — a grinding, ticking, sighing underlayer, and the two together are what deafening means. The water in the chamber is not still. It churns. It is full of suspended grit that catches what little light there is, so the whole volume of it glitters faintly and slowly, like something breathing in its sleep.

---

The light is bad and getting worse; a few industrial lamps survive, caged and green-glassed, hung from the vault on long cables that sway with the surge. They do not so much illuminate as *permit* — a dim ochre halo around each one, the rock walls going orange and then brown and then nothing. Between the lamps there are stretches of several meters where you navigate by touch and by the sound of your own knocking. The residents have painted the handrails with a mineral paste that glows a dull red for an hour after it's charged by a lamp's passage, so the catwalks in the dark are traced by two faint parallel lines, like the ghost of a road. It's beautiful, in the way that a wound is beautiful. It's the most colour in the place.

Life here is scarce and small and does not want to be seen. Blind eels in the sluices, thick as your wrist, warm and muscular. Crabs the size of a thumbnail that graze the mineral skin on the walls and are, apparently, delicious. Something with a shell that lives in the pump intakes and must be scraped out every few weeks or the town dies. Nothing that photosynthesises. Nothing that flowers. There is no green in the Kettle at all — the palette is ochre and iron and bone and the black of deep water, and the red threads of the glowing rails, and that's it. The people here look at a photograph of a forest the way you'd look at a photograph of another planet.

---

The rules of the world are simple and total. The surge is the clock; everything is timed to it — the doors that can only be opened on the slack, the crossings that can only be made on the slack, the pumps that are serviced in the forty seconds of relative quiet. Warmth comes from below and is not to be trusted: the vents that heat the chamber occasionally belch something else, a sulphurous sting that turns the water acid for a day and eats the seals off the pressure doors. When that happens you go up, into the high galleries, and wait, and listen to the brass note go strange and sour and then settle back. It always settles back. That's the other rule: the Kettle is in balance, and the balance is not kind, but it holds. The town has been here long enough that the stone has grown around its foundations. The stone is, in a sense, slowly eating it. Nobody minds. It's a way of belonging.

And the sound goes on. It goes on when you sleep, if you sleep; the residents sleep in shifts and in pairs and with their hands on each other's ribs so they can feel the surge through a body instead of through the rock. It goes on when you eat, when you work, when you sit on a catwalk in the dim ochre and let your legs hang over the black. You stop noticing it after a few days, and that's the dangerous part, because that's when it has got into you. Leave the Kettle and the silence of the surface will be the loudest thing you have ever heard, and you will find yourself knocking on doorframes, on tables, on the sides of your own skull, waiting for the answer that isn't coming.
