# The Hollow Garden

You descend for a long time before you understand that the ceiling above you is not stone.

---

## The Descent

The entrance is a crack in the world's skin — a fissure in cold grey rock, barely wide enough for a person, breathing out a steady exhalation of damp warmth that condenses on the stone and runs in thin fingers back down into the dark. The rock is young here. Fresh. You can see the sharpness of the fracture lines, the way the edges haven't yet been softened by centuries of water. This crack opened recently, in geological terms — perhaps a few thousand years ago — and the world above has barely begun to weather it. Lichens crust the mouth, pale green and silver, drinking the moisture that seeps from below.

You go down through spaces that are mostly empty. The shaft widens and narrows unpredictably, sometimes opening into chambers the size of cathedrals, sometimes squeezing to shoulder-width. The rock between these spaces is thin, almost hollow — you can hear your own breathing come back to you from distances that are wrong, echoing through voids you cannot see. The stone itself is cold, always cold, though the air carries that strange exhalation of warmth from somewhere deeper.

And then the light begins.

Not from above — from everywhere. From the walls, from the ceiling, from the spaces between the rocks. A dim, diffuse glow at first, like moonlight filtered through cloud. Then brighter. Much brighter. By the time you reach the first great chamber, the light is nearly daylight-bright, though it comes from no sun, casts no shadows you can trace to a single source.

The source is the moss.

---

## The Body of the World

The moss is not moss. Not exactly. It is a single organism — or perhaps a colony so old and so interwoven that the distinction has lost meaning. It covers everything. It *is* everything.

The walls of the cavern are furred with it, shaggy in places, smooth in others, forming a carpet that softens every surface into something almost domestic. It climbs the pillars of stone that rise through the chambers, wraps them in living green-grey, and then, at a certain height, continues upward as independent structures — arches, buttresses, vast curtains of hanging growth that sway slightly in air currents you cannot feel. The moss has become architecture. It has become the support system for the world below.

I say green-grey, but that is only the base color. In the light — and the light comes from the moss itself, from bioluminescent nodes that pulse slowly through the thallus, a light that is steady but never quite still — the growth shows other colors. Pale gold where it is thin and new. Deep ochre where it is old. In places, a faint blue-white, nearly the color of lightning, where something in the plant's chemistry has shifted and the light runs brighter. These patches move, slowly. Over hours, over days, the brightness migrates through the moss like weather.

The moss fills everything, but it does not fill everything *densely*. There are spaces between the growth-curtains, the pillars, the arches, and these spaces are enormous. Voids the size of small towns. You can walk for an hour through a single chamber and see nothing but the distant pillars, the hanging curtains, the floor carpeted in soft luminescent growth, and above you, an unfathomable distance away, the ceiling — a confusion of moss and stone that disappears into a general glow. The air is cold and heavy and smells of wet earth and something faintly sweet, something like the inside of a cellar where fruit has been stored too long.

There are pockets of water. Not rivers, not lakes — the moss holds water in its tissues like a sponge, and occasionally the sponge is squeezed. You find pools at the base of pillars, in the hollows of the floor, in the cups of old growth that has hardened into something almost like wood. The water is cold and clear and tastes of minerals. It drips, slowly, from the arch-curtains overhead, a constant faint percussion that is the closest thing to sound in this place. Drip. Drip. Drip. From somewhere far away, a different rhythm. The moss sweats. It breathes.

---

## The Rule of the Moss

This is what you must understand: the moss is not passive. It does not merely receive the water and the light and the cold air. It *works*.

The bioluminescence is not decorative. The light is a tool. The moss uses it to regulate its own temperature, to signal between its far-flung parts, to — this is harder to explain — *remember*. The patterns of light are not random. They follow paths, fixed paths, through the thallus. If you watch long enough, you will see that the pulsing light traces certain shapes, certain routes, over and over. Some of these routes are simple. Some are complex beyond any natural pattern.

The moss has been here for a very long time. It has been growing, spreading, finding its way through this hollow world for longer than the rock has been weathered. It has learned things. It has learned to conserve water, to draw it from the air, to store it in pockets that it can release when needed. It has learned to generate light in patterns that serve no obvious biological purpose — patterns that look, if you watch them long enough, almost like *writing*.

But it is dying. Or rather — it is turbulent. It is in constant change. The moss is not a stable system. Its own light-patterns shift, the bright patches migrate, some curtains of growth wither and die and are replaced by new growth that pushes through the dead tissue with the slow patience of something that has no natural enemies. The floor of the cavern is littered with the remains of old growth — brittle, pale, the color of bone — and new growth creeps over it, consuming it, using it as substrate. The moss eats itself. It has always eaten itself. This is how it survives in a world with almost no outside input.

The moss is also — and this is the thing that will not leave you — *loud*. Not in sound, not in any way you could record with an instrument. But when you stand in the great chambers, when you place your hand against the warm-cold surface of the growth and feel it pulse, you can feel something that is almost like noise. The moss is full of activity. Chemical, electrical, biological. A constant churn. A storm-state. The moss is a single vast organism, and it is always working, always changing, always in a state of near-chaos that somehow holds together.

---

## The Scattered Marks

The moss is not the only thing here.

Scattered through the chambers, you find evidence of others. Not many — the vastness of the place swallows them — but enough to know you are not the first.

There are cairns. Piles of stone, balanced carefully, some no taller than your knee, some reaching chest-height. They are found in odd places: on the edges of pools, at the mouths of passages, sometimes in the middle of vast empty floors where there is no obvious reason to stop. The stones are local — the same cold grey rock as the walls — but they have been moved, chosen, stacked with intentionality. The work is simple. Manual. There is no metal here, no marks of tools beyond what hands can do. The cairns are old. Some are furred with moss, nearly absorbed back into the world. Others are clean, recently built.

There are also the woven things. Structures of dried moss-stalks, bound with cordage made from the plant's own fibers. They are small, mostly — shelters, perhaps, or storage, or something with meaning you cannot guess. They are scattered and partial, many of them collapsed, others half-consumed by the growing moss. In one chamber, at the base of a pillar, you find what might have been a village — a dozen of these woven structures, clustered together, connected by paths of flattened moss. There are no people. There is no sign of what happened to them. The moss has grown over most of it, softening the edges, pulling the structure back into itself.

The people who made these things — the cairn-builders, the weavers — they knew the rule of the moss. They built *with* it, not against it. The paths they flattened are still clear, kept clear by some quality of the moss itself, which does not grow back into places that have been walked. The cairns honor something. Perhaps they are markers. Perhaps they are offerings. Perhaps they are simply the work of hands in a world where everything else is the work of something vast and indifferent.

---

## The Atmosphere

The air is cold. Always cold — perhaps ten degrees above freezing, no more. It does not vary. You can walk for hours through chambers that span miles, and the air remains the same: damp, cold, heavy, carrying the smell of wet earth and old growth. The warmth you felt at the entrance was an illusion, or a local effect, or something else entirely. The deeper you go, the colder it gets, but only slightly. The moss generates heat through its own activity, and this heat is trapped in the vast spaces by the stone above, or by the moss itself, which forms a kind of insulating layer.

The light is bright enough to read by, to walk by, to work by. It is not sunlight — it has no warmth, no direction, no quality of *above* — but it is steady, and it fills the spaces, and after a time you stop noticing it as light at all. You simply see. The shadows are soft, diffuse, almost nonexistent. The moss glows, and the glow fills the world, and there is nowhere to hide from it.

The sound is mostly quiet. The drip of water. The faint rustle of the moss-curtains in air currents you cannot feel. The sound of your own footsteps, your own breathing, your own heartbeat. Occasionally, something else: a distant shifting, a long low groan of stone or growth settling, a sudden cascade of water somewhere far away. But these are rare. The moss itself — the vast, churning, chaotic organism — is silent. Its activity is internal, hidden, a storm without sound.

---

## The Rules of the World

The moss is the world. This is the first rule.

The second rule: the moss is not hostile. It does not attack. It does not defend. It simply *is*, and its being is so total that everything else must accommodate it. The cairn-builders knew this. They did not try to clear the moss away. They found ways to live with it, to build in the spaces it left, to use its products and its light and its water.

The third rule: the moss is not stable. Its light-patterns shift. Its bright patches migrate. Its growth dies and regrows. The world here is in constant flux, but it is a slow, vast, geological flux — a storm that moves at the pace of centuries. And within that flux, there is the smaller, faster turbulence: the sudden collapses, the splash of water when a pocket releases, the faint shudder when a curtain of growth finally tears free and falls, the shifting of light-patterns that can happen in hours. The world is turbulent at every scale.

The fourth rule: the moss remembers. This is the thing that will not leave you. The light-patterns, the routes that the bioluminescence traces — they are not random. They are too regular. Too *intentional*. The moss has been here for longer than the rock has been weathered, and it has been watching, and it holds memory in its light-patterns. What it remembers, you cannot guess. But the patterns are there, and they are changing, and they are — you are almost certain — *reaching*.

---

## The Deeper Places

You can go deeper. The chambers continue, ramifying through the stone like the root-system of the world itself. The moss is thinner here, at the lower depths — the light-dim, the growth slower, the cold more intense. The pools are more frequent, frozen at the edges, and the light from the moss is a pale, tired blue that barely penetrates the darkness between pillars.

But there is something else. In the deepest chambers, the moss forms structures that are different from anything above — columnar growths that rise from the floor and the ceiling and meet in the middle, forming complete pillars of living tissue. They are called — by no one, but you feel the name anyway — *the roots*. They are not roots of the moss above; they are separate, older, something the moss has grown *down* into the world to reach. They pulse with a light that is nearly white, and the pulse is slower than the light above, and the pattern is different, and it is — you cannot prove this, but you feel it — *older*. The roots remember things the moss above has forgotten.

And in the deepest chamber of all — the moss is thin here, the light is dim, the cold is absolute — there is a cairn. It is not a small cairn. It is a hill of stones, taller than a person, wider than a house, built in the center of a space so vast that the ceiling is lost in darkness above you. The stones are not local. They are different colors, different materials — blue-grey, green-grey, a stone that is almost red, a stone that might be made of something not quite stone at all. The cairn has been here for a very long time. The moss has nearly consumed it, furred it with pale growth, softened it into something almost organic.

But the light is still there. In the moss that covers the cairn, the light is bright and steady and does not shift with the passages of the patterns above. It is fixed. It is a single, long, sustained pulse that says — if you listen, if you watch for long enough to learn the language of light — *here*. *This is here*. *This is still here*.

The world above is turbulent. The moss is a storm. The light migrates. The chambers collapse and regrow. But this cairn, this pile of stones in the deepest dark, remembers something that does not change.

You stand in the cold and the quiet and the bright, steady light, and you understand that you are not the first to come here, and you will not be the last, and the moss will hold your presence the way it holds everything — lightly, slowly, and for a very long time.
