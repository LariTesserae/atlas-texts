# The Standing Foundry

The plateau has no water and does not miss it.

It sits high enough that the horizon bends — a slow, correct curve, the sky drawn tight over it like a drumhead. The rock is raw. Not weathered-raw, not the raw of a place that has been scoured and left; simply unfinished, as though the crust had been pushed up through the floor of the world a moment ago and then stopped, mid-gesture, and never resumed. There is no soil anywhere. There is no dust. The surface is a single great shield of it, black and bone-white and bottle-green by turns, broken into slabs along fracture lines that are still sharp enough to open a hand. Every edge is the edge it was when it split. Nothing has rounded anything. Nothing has rained on this place in the sense that matters.

It is warm. Not hot — warm the way a stone is warm an hour after the sun has gone off it, except the sun has not gone off it. Heat comes up from underneath as well, a low, even exhalation through the seams, and the two together keep the air at a temperature that a body would call pleasant and would not be able to explain.

The sky is a hard, pale, enameled blue. The light falls straight and flat, without much diffusion; shadows are thin, exact, and black, lying on the ground like cut paper. It does not seem to be any particular hour.

---

Across this shield, at long intervals, stand the groves.

That is the word, though they are not trees in any sense you would recognize. Each is a dense, dark, glossy thicket of vertical things — columnar, tapering, packed shoulder to shoulder, a hundred or five hundred of them in a clump — rising two or three times a person's height and closing at the top into a thatch so tight that no light gets through. From a distance a grove reads as a green-black stain on the white rock, lush and extravagant and utterly isolated, with nothing between it and the next stain but an hour's walk of bare slab.

The columns are hollow. This is the first thing you understand about them, and everything else follows.

Each stalk is a chimney. Warm gas seeps up out of the fractures in the bedrock, and the plants have grown around the seep, and the gas rises inside them — up through the hollow core, into the canopy — where it cools by a degree or two, condenses on the inner surfaces of the thatch, and runs back down the inside of the stalks to the roots, where it is heated and goes up again. A grove is a closed loop. It is its own weather and its own well, cycling the same few tons of vapor through itself forever, losing almost none. There is no water in it. The working fluid is something thinner and stranger, something that smells faintly of warm brass and burnt sugar and, underneath, of struck flint.

This is why the place is lush and dry at once. The lushness is real — the groves are dense, layered, furred with fine growth along every surface, heavy with a dark iridescent green that goes copper at the edges where the light catches — but it is contained. Lushness here is not a carpet. It is a set of islands. Between them the rock extends, clean and empty, for distances that make the groves look small.

---

And the groves are not only groves. They are works.

The tissue of a stalk is a catalyst; the gas passing through it is not merely worked for moisture but cracked. Sulfur comes out of it, and carbon, and traces of metals that the deep rock is still giving off. The plants lay these down. Not inside themselves — they push them out, through the root walls, into the stone, where they accrete in bands and crusts and druses and, most strikingly, in thin iridescent frostings over the surrounding slab: bismuth-pink, arsenic-grey, a green that is not the green of the leaves. Every grove sits at the center of its own ring of pure metal, spread a few body-lengths out, brightest close in, thinning to nothing at the edge of the empty.

Nothing built this. There are no walls, no pipes, no machinery, no mark of a hand or a tool anywhere on the plateau. But the plateau is industrial. It refines. It separates, deposits, and vents, and it does so on a scale and with a steadiness that a factory would envy and could not achieve. The industry is simply biological, and the biology is chemistry, and the chemistry has been running long enough to be geology. It is machinery the way a termite mound is machinery: nobody's, but unmistakably an engine.

At the center of each grove, if you put your palm against a stalk, you can feel it — a faint steady tremor, a hum at the very floor of hearing, the sound of a few hundred columns moving gas in a cycle that closes on itself. It is the loudest thing here.

---

Because there is nothing else to hear.

There are no animals. Not one. No insects in the thatch, no burrowers in the rubble, no birds, no lizards, no crawling or flying or creeping thing. The groves are silent in a way that takes a while to register as wrong. Nothing rustles. Nothing visits. There is no birdsong and no drone of wings and no small dry scuttling, and after a while the absence becomes a pressure, a held breath.

The plants do not need anyone. They do not flower in any useful sense; there is nobody to attract. They spread — or they did, once — by slow mineral creep at the root ring, and they reproduce by fission of the stalk-base, and neither process is doing much now. And beyond the hum of the gas, the plateau is quiet: the soft exhalation of the vents, a rare isolated tick from a slab settling by a micron into a fit it has been settling into for a very long time, and then nothing.

Now and then the wind crosses the open rock. It has nothing to move, so it makes almost no sound — a thin dry passing, gone.

---

Here is the rule of the place, and it is one rule:

**Nothing accumulates.**

There is no erosion to speak of, and so there is no sediment. There are no strata. There is no burial, no decay, no soft slow rain of the dead onto the ground to be pressed into layers. Nothing has ever been covered up here, and nothing ever will be. The past does not silt down and settle; it has no floor to settle on. The metal crusts around a grove are the only record of anything, and they record only the present, continuously, like a breath fogging a window.

The rock is still in the middle of being made and will remain so. The heat is still coming up. The system has found its balance and is holding it — not frozen exactly, since the gas moves and the stalks hum and the metal creeps outward at a rate you would need instruments to see, but balanced the way a spinning top is balanced: everything in motion, nothing changing. The groves do not grow and do not die. They sit in their rings of iridescent frost and breathe the same air around and around and around.

---

Walk out between them and the empty is enormous. The groves shrink behind you. The white slab goes on, fractured into its clean sharp plates, veined with green-black glass where the seams ran hot, and far off the shield domes up and then breaks away at a scarp, and past the scarp is more of the same, and past that the horizon bends down out of sight under the hard blue enamel of the sky.

You can see four groves from a rise, five, maybe six — dark islands, each humming to itself, each with its copper-edged canopy and its ring of bright metal spilling out into the bare, and nothing whatsoever between them, and no sound but the vents, and no water anywhere at all, and no reason to think that any of it looked different a thousand years ago or will look different a thousand years on. It is a landscape caught precisely at the instant of becoming, and it will be caught there a while yet.
