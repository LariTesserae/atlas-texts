# The Kettle

You come down a stair that was built for men in boots and is now a stair for nobody. It turns twice inside a concrete shaft and ends in water — not deep, ankle-deep, standing in the last step and going no further. The stairwell is the only straight line left in the place. Everything below it has stopped agreeing with itself.

The room is small. You can stand in the middle and touch, with a wide throw, three of its four walls. It was a pump-house once: a low vaulted chamber the size of a village chapel, built to house one machine and the men who watched it. Then the ground beneath it let go — not a collapse, more a settling, the way a sleeper sinks into a mattress — and the chamber dropped nine feet into the limestone and cracked along its crown. It has been forty years, maybe fifty, which in stone is nothing at all. The break edges are still sharp enough to cut a hand. The paint on the wall — a municipal green, two tones, a stripe at shoulder height — is still visibly green where the damp hasn't reached. This is a young place. It has the raw, bright, unfinished look of something that has not yet been worn into itself.

**Terrain.** The floor is a mosaic of slabs, each one tilted its own few degrees, stepping down toward a sump at the north end where the ground gave way most. The slabs are concrete poured in sections, and the joins between them have opened into seams a finger wide, and out of the seams the under-place breathes. Warm. Steady. A smell of wet limestone and hot iron and something faintly like bread. Between the slabs the void goes down further than the sump does — you can drop a bolt and count a long time. The walls are brick below, concrete above, and the brick has begun to bloom at the joints with a fine white fur of salts, drawn up out of the rock and left behind as the moisture leaves. Little mineral gardens. Little ghost orchards of gypsum.

**Water.** It does not rain here; it condenses. The ceiling is cooler than the air by enough that water beads on it constantly and falls in a slow irregular percussion, so that the whole room is a place of small wet impacts. The sump at the north end is the only true pool, three feet across, and it is warm — blood warm, a little more — because the heat comes up through it. It is not stagnant. A thread of flow enters at one side and leaves at the other, going down into the dark along a channel it has cut in fifty years, which is faster than water should cut limestone here, and suggests the water is not only water. The air holds itself at a temperature that would be a fever in a person. Your clothes go heavy with it in a quarter hour. Nothing dries in the Kettle. A match struck will not stay lit for long.

**Vegetation.** And yet it is a garden. That is the thing that stops you at the bottom of the stairs and keeps you there. Lush is the wrong word only because it suggests abundance and this is not abundant — it is *thick* in pockets and bare in between, a scatter of green islands on a floor of pale stone, with three or four paces of emptiness between each. Along the south wall, where the drip is heaviest, a curtain of ferns has taken the whole corner, hanging in tiers, their fronds a color that is not quite green — the green has been starved of light and gone pale, almost yellow at the tips, almost white at the root, like things grown in a cellar. There is moss in every seam, deep enough to press a thumb into. There are pale runners crossing open floor, sending up shoots at intervals. And in the sump, submerged, a mat of something fibrous and slow that breathes bubbles.

It is lush the way a wound is lush. Nothing here flowers, and nothing here is pollinated, because there is nothing here to do it. Not an insect. Not a fly on the water. The plants do not seed — they divide, they run, they break and root and run again. Every green thing in the room is a piece of some other green thing in the room. Some of them, the older islands under the drip, may be a single organism spread over thirty feet of broken floor, one continuous self with bare concrete in the middle of it. That is the rule of the Kettle, and it is the rule of most places that get sealed: nothing comes in, nothing goes out, and what lives learns to live without the other kind.

**Built things.** The machine is still here and still going. It occupies the east end — a pump, horizontally opposed, flywheel taller than a man, bolted to a plinth that has not moved though the floor around it has. Someone has removed the guard. Someone has also, at some point, hung a coat on the guard-rail bracket. The flywheel turns. It turns so slowly that you have to watch a full minute to be sure, and then you cannot unsee it — a hand's width of rim, in and out of the light, in and out. Something in the pump has found a way to keep cycling water from the sump back into the sump, and the machine runs on that, and has been running on that, unwatched, since long before the paint stopped mattering. Its housing is warm. The plants nearest it are the largest.

There are other marks. A steel ladder bolted to the wall going up four rungs and ending at nothing. A clipboard, weathered to a curl. A tin cup. Graffiti by the stair, initials and a year. The evidence is scattered, sparse, partial — a fistful of human moments left in a room that has otherwise stopped being about people.

**Light.** Almost none. A single fracture in the vault lets down a seam of daylight no wider than a knife, and it moves — across the floor, across the ferns, across the flywheel, following the hour above. On an overcast day it is grey and you see only shapes. But there is another light, low and constant: the sump glows. Faintly. An orange-brown underlight that comes up out of the water and paints the ceiling above it, and makes the ferns on that side look lit from beneath, like something on a stage. The water is warm and the glow is warm and the two are probably the same fact.

**Sound.** The drip. The flywheel's low tick at the top of each revolution, once every eleven seconds. A slow churn from the sump. Under everything, too low to hear, a pressure you feel in the teeth — the under-place breathing. Nothing else. No birds because there are no birds. It is the quietest green place you will ever stand in, and the quiet is not peaceful, exactly; it is the quiet of a room where a machine is working on something you don't understand, patiently, at a rate you cannot perceive, and will not finish today.

**The rules, as well as I can tell them.** Down here a thing survives by closing its loop. The pump closes its loop. The plants close theirs. The water closes its. Nothing in the Kettle needs anything from outside except heat and the seam of grey daylight, and the heat comes from below and the light comes from above and both arrive without being asked. The cycles are long. The fern curtain took decades and will take decades more to reach the far wall. The sump channel gains, by my guess, a millimeter a year. If you came back in a lifetime you would find the green islands a pace wider and the fracture in the vault a knuckle wider and the paint stripe a shade more gone — and the flywheel turning, and the drip falling, and no one there.
