The place is a high, cold shoulder of a continent, a land of stone and slow water. It is a country that has settled into its final form. The mountains here are not the sharp, young peaks of a rising world, but the deeply worn knuckles of an ancient range, smoothed by an eternity of wind and frost. Their slopes are long, gentle, and covered not in scree but in a dense, soft mat of vegetation—a lushness born of cold persistence, not warmth. The scale is immense; from any high point, the horizon is a vast, hazy curve, and you feel the planet's breadth in the weight of the air.

The sky is a vast, pale bowl, its light normal and diffuse, but the scale is so overwhelming that the concept of "normal" feels quaint. Weather is a local, personal thing; you can watch a rain squall, a thin grey curtain, walk across a valley miles away, while you stand in a pocket of damp, condensation-beaded stillness. The air is always heavy with moisture, not as a thick humidity, but as a cold, clinging presence. It settles on your skin, on the stones, on the leaves. The ground is a patchwork of textures: ancient, flattened bedrock carved into sinuous, shallow channels, and vast, soft terrains of moss and low-lying shrubs that hold water like a sponge.

This is a world of water that does not rush. It seeps. The rivers are not roaring torrents but silver threads winding through the ancient valleys, their courses dictated by the deep, settled topography. Some are wide and so slow they seem to be barely moving, their surfaces a polished, dark mirror reflecting the pale sky. They are called the Lichen Rivers, for the thick, bioluminescent mats that cling to the rocks in their beds. These mats, a soft, pulsing blue-green, provide the only real color in the landscape, a subtle, living glow that becomes apparent in the long, grey dusks.

The vegetation is the dominant feature. It is not a forest of tall trees, but a dense, low canopy of shrubs, ferns, and immense, fleshy-leafed plants that thrive in the cold damp. They are a deep, saturated green, almost black in the shadows. This lushness is layered over the stone in a way that speaks of immense age. The plants seem to have grown into the rock itself, their roots a fine, inescapable network. There are no animals. The silence is profound, broken only by the whisper of wind through the fleshy leaves, the gentle sound of water seeping, and the occasional, startling crack of a rock splitting from frost. This is a world without birdsong, without the rustle of small creatures, and the absence is a presence in itself.

Into this vast, quiet, and empty place, humanity has fit itself. There is one settlement, a town called Hart's Fold. It is built not *on* the land, but *into* it. Its structures are of stone and turf, low and rounded, their roofs blending seamlessly with the hillsides. They are built along the courses of the seeping rivers, using the old, worn channels as streets. The architecture is a careful negotiation with the earth: a house is as likely to be a excavated cavern as a built-up structure of hand-stacked stone. There is a definite infrastructure, a network of these waterways and stone-paved paths that connect the town's functions, but it is a quiet, integrated thing. There are no towers, no great monuments—only the persistent, low-grade organization of a people who have learned to live in the seams of a giant.

The technology is simple, of hand-tools and manual craft. Metal is rare, scavenged and reworked for only the most essential purposes. The people of Hart's Fold are master weavers of the strong, fibrous plants and patient carvers of the ancient, soft stone. Their lives are a slow, rhythmic cycle of tending the glowing lichen-rivers, harvesting the fleshy leaves, and maintaining their water-channels against the constant, gentle creep of the moss.

But the world here is not stable. The ancient, settled nature of the land is a physical state, but the *rules* of that state are frayed. This is the primary truth of the place: reality is thin. The past is not a memory but a present, physical layer. You can be walking along a stone path and suddenly be walking on the same path a thousand years ago, the stone fresh and sharp, the air smelling of different woodsmoke. A person might speak, and their words hang in the air as visible, colored shapes for a moment before dissolving. You might find a small, perfectly flat and polished stone that, when held, shows you a clear image of the valley as it was when the glaciers retreated. These are not grand, spectacular events; they are a constant, quiet background noise of instability. A tool might be a hand-axe in the morning and a simple iron blade by evening. The people of Hart's Fold have learned to navigate this. Their "common sense" is a set of rules for a world with no fixed rules: don't stare too long at the water, always carry a piece of worked stone, and if the path ahead looks too new, wait for a moment for it to settle back into age.

The dynamic nature of this world is not one of predator and prey, but of process. It is the active process of reality sorting itself out, of the ancient past bleeding through the worn-out present. It is the weather of a vast planet, the slow biological pace of the plants, and the constant, geological-scale erosion that grinds everything back to a final, quiet flatness. It is a place of immense, quiet age that is constantly, subtly, re-inventing its own history. It is a landscape of profound solitude, where the emptiness is filled not with life, but with the immense, quiet pressure of time itself.
