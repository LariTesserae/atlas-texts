# The Sinter Bowl

**I.**

High on the shoulder of a mountain that has no name anybody remembers, there is a shallow bowl in the rock about forty paces across. It is not a crater, though it looks like one; the rock was not thrown out, it was eaten away, slowly, by something in the air or something in the stone, and the rim that remains is scalloped and pale and worn to a soft edge, like the lip of a cup that has been drunk from for a thousand years.

The bowl is dry. Not dry in the way a desert is dry — a desert still has the idea of water in it, the memory of rain, the shape of old rivers. This place has no such memory. Nothing here has ever been wet. The floor is a crust of mineral sinter, white and gray and faintly greasy to the touch, laid down in thin sheets that flake and re-form, flake and re-form, so the surface is always somewhere between powder and plate. Beneath it the rock is dark and dense and warm. Everything here is warm. The heat does not come from above — the sun, if there is one, is a rumor on this mountain — it comes from below, from a seam in the stone, a fault hairline that breathes.

And the air is thin. So high that breathing is work, and the work is a kind of company: you hear your own blood. The air smells of hot flint and something faintly like burnt hair, and it is so dry that it does not fog, does not carry, does not forgive. Sounds die fast in it. A shout goes three paces and is swallowed.

**II.**

The bowl is full.

Not with anything you would call a population at first — with cobbles. That is what they look like in the near-dark: a packed field of flat, rounded, gray-brown stones, fitted together shoulder to shoulder, so tightly that the sinter between them is barely visible, so tightly that to walk you would have to step on them. They are packed and layered, a second skin over the floor of the bowl, thicker at the center, thinning toward the rim, and they are not stones.

Each is about the size of a hand, some larger, a few the size of a head. They are leathery, dry, warm to the touch, and they pulse — a slow, deep, tidal pulse that travels through the whole mass, one body answering the next, until the entire bowl is breathing like a single animal. They have no eyes. They have no eyes because there is nothing to see. Their upper surfaces are smooth and dun and slightly concave, a dish that catches the heat from below; their undersides are a soft mouth, a wet-dark slit that opens against the sinter and grazes, and the grazing is why the floor never settles into a single form.

They are silent. That is the strangest thing about them and the thing that takes longest to accept. A place this crowded — packed until there is no room between presences, saturated, a bowl brimming with bodies — should roar. Instead there is a low continuous susurrus, dry husks shifting against dry husks, a sound like a river heard from a great distance through stone, and under it the pulse, and over it nothing. No calls. Once, twice in a long night, some creature at the edge lifts its whole body in a slow wave and makes a sound — a thin, swallowed click, almost apologetic — and the mass ripples in answer, and then the quiet returns. They communicate through contact and warmth. A voice here is a waste of heat.

**III.**

The heat breathes. About every hour and a half, the seam beneath the bowl pulses, and the sinter goes hotter, and the packed mass presses inward — the stones slide against one another with a long grinding sigh, the whole field contracting toward the center, layering itself, clustering over the warmest crack. When the pulse fades, the mass thins again, opens, drifts back out toward the edges, and a few — always a few — peel away from the rim and go down the mountain, out of the bowl, into the dark, walking on their soft undersides in a slow deliberate way. Others come up from below to replace them. The exchange is constant. The bowl is not a home; it is a hearth, and the creatures are the sort of thing that comes to a hearth at night and leaves again before morning.

They drink nothing. There is nothing to drink. They take what they need from the mineral crust they graze — hydrogen bound in stone, water that is not water, moisture that the sinter releases only under the slow mill of their mouths. Their bodies are almost perfectly dry. Handle one and your palms come away dusted with a fine pale powder, and the powder is warm, and it smells faintly of the same burnt-flint heat that the air smells of.

Nothing grows here. There is no moss, no lichen, no seed in the wind. Where the sinter is oldest, at the very center, there is a faint efflorescence — pale crystalline blooms, branching like frost or like the veins of a leaf — and whether it is alive is a question the place does not answer. It resists being asked. It is a pattern that grows and dies and grows, and it may be mineral, and it may be pre-life, and it may be the bowl's only other citizen. It is beautiful and it is not comforting.

**IV.**

There are marks of making. Scattered, faint, old enough to be almost natural.

Around part of the rim, a low wall of dry-stone — flat pieces of the sinter stacked without mortar, in a ring that does not close. The fit is good; whoever stacked them knew the material. Two cairns, waist-high, of the same pale stone, one near the wall and one on the opposite side of the bowl, both worn down by wind and by the long habit of the creatures pressing past them. And along the rim's inner face, at intervals, small hollows scooped into the crust, each about the size of a hand, each exactly the size of the creatures that live here, and each worn smooth — by hands, or by something with no hands at all.

The wall does not keep the mass in. It never did. The creatures cross it, and their passage is why the stones are rounded. Someone built these things, or something did, and no one built anything since. There is no tool here, no metal, no fire that anyone struck. It is a place people knew, once, and marked, and stopped coming to, and forgot — except the ones who come out of the dark, up the mountain, every night, to lie in the warmth and pulse with the others and say nothing.

**V.**

The light is nearly none. There is no moon worth having, no sky to speak of — the rim of the bowl and the dust of the high air cut it down to a faint ashen line at the lip, and even that comes and goes. What there is comes from the seam: a deep, dull red glow that bleeds up through the sinter in one place near the center, too faint to read by, just enough to tell the pale crust from the dark rock and the stones from the dust and to silver the edges of the packed mass as it breathes.

The rules of the place are small and total. Heat is the only wealth; it comes from below and it does not last. Density is safety — the center is the warmest and the most crowded and the most still. Water is nothing; there is none, there has never been any, and a body that arrives here carrying water will keep it to itself and lose it slowly and be gone. And the bowl is always in motion, always exchanging, always pulsing, though nothing ever seems to happen. It has been like this for a very long time. The wall is worn to softness. The hollows are smooth. The sinter lays itself down and flakes away in the same slow rhythm, and the mountain holds the bowl, and the bowl holds the heat, and the heat holds the packed and pulsing dark — a hearth with no fire to see, a congregation with no god, a quiet that goes on and on.
