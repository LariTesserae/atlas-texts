# The Kettle

The place is a basin, a shallow stone cup set into the earth, maybe forty paces across. Steam rises from it in slow ropes. The ground is wet everywhere — not from rain, though rain comes often and hard here, but from below. The water table sits just under the surface, and every footfall makes a soft sucking sound, water seeping up through cracks to fill the print. The rocks weep constantly. Thin films of water run down every face and stone, gathering in strings at the bottom edges, dripping. The air is thick. You can feel it in your lungs — warm and heavy, like breathing through a damp cloth.

The heat is the first thing. It comes off the basin like a hand pressed to your face. The stone underfoot is warm through your boots. Near the center of the basin, where the ground is lowest, there's a pool — no more than a room's width across — and the water in it is too hot to touch, though it doesn't quite boil. It's the color of weak tea and smells faintly of iron and something sweeter underneath, like burnt sugar. Steam comes off it in that slow, deliberate way, rising and curling and folding back on itself. The air above the pool shimmers. Everything beyond it looks smeared, uncertain.

The basin walls rise maybe twice a man's height before they break into the surrounding terrain, and they're not old walls. They're sharp. Fresh. The strata are still visible — banded layers of grey and ochre stone, cracked but not rounded, not worn down by centuries of weather. This place has been here a long time, maybe, but it still looks new. Like it was made recently, or like the making is still going on. The cracks in the basin floor are clean-edged, and some of them widen by a hair's breadth each year — a process so slow it's imperceptible unless you measure it, which no one does. The water is patient. It works at the stone in its own time.

Vegetation is sparse. A few tough grasses push up between the cracks at the basin's rim — grey-green, wiry, coated in mineral dust. There's a kind of moss that grows on the wetter rocks near the pool, a dull copper color, and it only grows in a ring about three paces out from the water's edge, where the heat is tolerable. Further up the walls, where the steam doesn't reach as thickly, there are occasional pale stalks — some kind of reed-relative, jointed and hollow, standing in the damp seams where water trickles down. They don't grow in clusters. Just one here, another ten paces away. You could count them all in a few minutes if you wanted to.

No animals. Not a bird, not an insect, not a spider in the cracks. The heat and the steam and the mineral water have driven them out, or maybe they never came. The only movement is water — the drip and trickle and the slow rising of steam — and the only sound is the sound of boiling. It's not a violent boil. It's a steady churn, a low, continuous roar that fills the basin and bounces off the walls and comes back at you from all sides. You have to raise your voice to be heard. The water in the pool is never still. It turns over on itself, a slow roll from the bottom up, releasing great soft bladders of steam that break at the surface with a heavy bloop. The sound is constant. It gets into your chest.

There are structures here, but they're ruinous. Somebody built something, once, and there are pieces left. At the eastern rim, there's a squat brick building — three walls standing, the fourth collapsed inward, roof gone. The bricks are pale with mineral deposit, and the mortar has gone to powder in places, but the corners are still square. The interior is wet. Water pools on the floor. Inside, there's a rusted iron frame bolted to the ground — maybe a pump, once, or a motor. It's seized solid. Vines have grown over it, thin grey vines with no leaves, just wire-like stems that creep along the metal and down into the water.

Further around the rim, to the north, there's a row of iron pipes running along the basin wall — maybe twenty paces of them — supported by concrete stanchions that have cracked and tilted. The pipes are thick, corroded to a dark orange, and some sections have separated, the joints pulled apart by settling ground. Water drips from the gaps. The pipes run from the pool's edge up and over the basin rim, disappearing into the earth beyond. They carried hot water somewhere, once. Now they just leak.

There's a smell here. Iron and sulphur and that burnt-sugar sweetness. And under it, something musty, like wet canvas, like the inside of a tent that's been closed too long. The heat makes it worse. It sits on the back of your tongue.

The light is ordinary — sunlight, when the sky is clear, coming down into the basin and scattering in the steam. It doesn't feel like ordinary light, though. The steam makes it thick. On sunny days, the light comes through in shafts, angled and visible, catching the moisture in the air. It makes the whole basin glow, like the light is passing through amber glass. On cloudy days, it's flat and grey and closer, and the steam just turns the air milky. The place never feels dark, though. The heat and the water and the slow churn of it all keep the place alive-feeling even when the light is weak.

---

This is a place that runs on its own time. The water turns over slowly. The steam rises and disperses and condenses on the basin walls and trickles back down to the pool. The cracks widen by fractions of inches over years. The moss grows outward by a hand's breadth in a decade. The pipes rust at whatever rate rust happens here — slowly, constantly, without any urgency. Nothing in this place is in a hurry.

The rules are simple. The water is hot. The ground is wet. The sound is constant. There's nowhere to sit that isn't damp, nowhere to stand that isn't warm. The air is thick enough that you feel it on your skin, and after a while, your clothes are damp through, not from sweat but from the condensation. Everything you touch is wet. If you brought something metal, it starts to rust before you leave. The heat is tolerable at the rim and intolerable near the pool, and the boundary between those zones shifts slowly, depending on the wind and the temperature of the day and some underground rhythm you can't perceive.

The place feels hidden. It's small, contained, folded into the landscape so you'd have to know it was there to find it. From the rim, you can't see it. You come over the edge of a rise and it's just there, below you, steaming.

There's no animal life, no birdcall, nothing to break the sound of the water but the wind, which is weak here, and the occasional distant sound of something mechanical — a hum, a thrum, maybe the pipes, maybe something else running underground. It's faint. You're not sure it's real.

The place is a working thing, even now. The water still comes up hot, still turns over, still steams. The pipes still carry it somewhere, even if it's just into the ground. The building still stands, mostly. It doesn't feel abandoned, exactly. It feels like something is still happening here, slowly, without anyone watching.
