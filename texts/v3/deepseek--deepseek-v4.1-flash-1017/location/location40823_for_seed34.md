# The Tarnholt

You come up onto it slowly, because there is no other way: the plateau rises in long, wet stairs of stone, and every stair is a shallow pool. By the time the ground levels out you have been walking in water for two days, and you have stopped noticing. That is the first thing the place does to you. It teaches you that wet is the default, and dry is a small local accident that happens in the lee of things.

The altitude is real but unspectacular — high enough that the air is thin and cold enough to ache in the teeth, not high enough to thin the sky. What the height does is make the cloud sit on you. The plateau holds its own weather, and the weather is mostly *contact*: mist that beads on your sleeves, precipitation too fine to be called rain, a condensation that gathers on any surface cooler than the air. Everything here is slightly more wet than the air around it. Stone sweats. Wool grows heavy. The light arrives diffused through all this, a flat white that casts almost no shadows, so distance reads badly — the far parts of the city don't recede into haze so much as simply become *paler*, like a thing half-erased.

And it goes on. This is the part that is hard to hold in your head: the Tarnholt is continental. You can walk in it for a month. From any high mound you see the same thing in every direction — a soft grey-brown felt of structures, ranked and terraced, threaded with standing water, going out until the mist eats it. Not a city in a landscape. A landscape that is a city, the way a reef is a landscape that is a colony.

## The water

Water is at 1.9 and it shows everywhere, in every register. It stands in the channels between the mounds, brown and clear and cold. It lies in the hollows of every roof that has sagged enough to hold it. It runs in the seams of the walls, which is to say the walls are being slowly unlaced by it and always have been. The whole place rests on a lens of it — a saturated mat of peat and chalk-mud a metre or two down, so that the ground is not a floor but a lid.

The Tarnholt breathes with it. In the cold season the standing water skins over, and the ice pushes the mounds up a finger's width, and the seams creak, and then the thaw lets everything settle back with a long soft groan that runs through the fabric for a minute or more. In the wet season the channels fill and the lower terraces become one connected sheet, and the city becomes an archipelago of its own upper floors. There are weirs — you will see them, low crescent dams of packed reed and lime, hundreds of them, holding the water at exactly the level the builders wanted. The water is the only infrastructure that moves.

## The fabric

There is no tool here, no metal, no fire, and yet there is a city, and you must understand it as *grown*. The mounds are the work of a colonial animal — call it the holt-thing — that secretes a chalky lime from its body and lays it down in courses, and then another and another, generation on generation, over a span of time that makes archaeology of the whole enterprise. Each mound is a stack of chambers like a wasp's paper, but lime, and wet, and inhabited more or less continuously for as long as the place has existed.

The courses are visible if you look closely: fine laminae, a few millimetres each, sometimes parted by a dark line where a wet century went through. Colonies build against colonies. There are buttresses that are clearly one generation's answer to another generation's failure — a bulge, a thickening, a leaning pier thrown across a gap. Streets are the negative space between accretions, and they are not straight; they wander because the water made them. Some passages are so narrow your shoulders touch both walls, and the walls are warm — the holt-things are still in there, sessile, packed in the fabric like marrow, and they are body-warm, and they are alive.

Density is the strangest property of the place. Nothing much is moving, and yet you are never alone in a room. The walls themselves are population. Every surface is inhabited, layered, near-touching: lime over reed over lime over the slow grey lace of the lichens that mine it. The Tarnholt is a saturated thing. You feel it as a kind of closeness, a sense of too many slow presences at too little distance, like standing in a crowded hall where everyone is asleep.

## Vegetation, fauna, sound

The plants are not much. Sparse sedge in the wet hollows, wiry and copper-coloured. A dwarf willow that never gets above knee height and grows sideways. Mosses, many, in the seams where the lime is failing — the moss is the first sign a wall is dying, and it is welcome, because the marsh wants the wall back and the moss is how the marsh gets it. No trees to speak of. Nothing here is tall except the mounds.

Animals are rare, and that is the odd thing, because so much of the city is biological. But it is sessile biology, and sessile things are not what you notice. What you notice: a few grey birds that walk rather than fly and pick at the weirs. Something that leaves five-toed prints in the mud every morning and that you will never see. The slow wet tick of the holt-things' feeding, which you cannot hear but can feel through your feet if you stand still long enough. That's all. Nothing hunts you. Nothing here is quick.

And sound is almost absent, and this is the thing that will stay with you. The Tarnholt is a quiet with texture. Dripping — constant, various, a thousand separate taps at different heights and intervals, never resolving into rhythm. The faint glassy crackle of the ice sheets at dawn. Wind, but the wind doesn't howl here; the mounds are rounded and the wind goes over them in a long soft hush, and in the deep channels it makes almost no sound at all. Occasionally a settling-creak, far off, that lasts ten seconds and is the sound of a structure older than you being patient. When you speak, your voice goes out about four metres and then is simply gone into the wet.

## The rules of the world

The Tarnholt is in balance, and the balance is a slow exchange. The marsh is rising and the city is sinking, and the two rates are almost exactly equal, and have been for a very long time. Where the holt-things build, the city gains a few millimetres. Where they die, the moss takes the lime, the water works the seam, the mound slumps into a wet hollow, and a channel widens by a hand's width. Nothing is being won. You can see the whole history of the place in cross-section in any cut bank: good centuries and bad centuries, layers of vigorous construction and layers of rotten, waterlogged abandonment, and then construction again, on top, using the ruin as foundation because ruin is a good foundation.

The place has no centre. This is worth saying plainly. You can walk toward what looks like the heart of it — the tallest, oldest mounds, the ones with the deep chambers and the many-chambered weirs — and find no plaza, no citadel, no place where the logic culminates. It is dense everywhere and central nowhere. It is a city on the scale of a country, built by something that has no plans, only the habit of laying down lime where the water is right, and it has been laying lime down for long enough that "right" has become a shape you could mistake for intention.

Stand on one of the high mounds near dusk, when the mist lifts a little and the light goes from white to a dull even pewter. The channels catch it and run silver in every direction, out and out, ridged and terraced and wet, and the far city is only a darkening texture, and the dripping goes on, and the ice begins to crackle, and you understand that you are standing inside something that is not a ruin and not a habitation, but a slow ongoing decision made by a great many small wet animals who have never once thought about it.
