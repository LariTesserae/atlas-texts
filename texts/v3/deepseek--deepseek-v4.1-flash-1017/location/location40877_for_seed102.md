You find it by the warmth first. There is a crack in the side of a hill, low and narrow and easy to walk past, and out of it comes air that is several degrees warmer than the day. Not damp-warm. There is no damp anywhere on the rock around the opening — no moss, no dark seep, nothing that suggests the earth is breathing water out. The air is dry-warm, like the breath of something asleep, and it comes steadily, without draft, without pulse. Put your palm near the crack and you feel nothing moving it. It is simply there, and has been there a long time.

Then, if you put your ear close, the sound. You hear it through the rock before you hear it through the air — a low held note, glassy, as if someone has pressed a wet finger around the rim of an enormous glass and never stopped.

You go in. It is a squeeze, and then it opens, and you are standing in the chamber.

---

It is not a cave, exactly. It is a fracture that stopped.

The rock split here — recently, in geological terms, so recently that the break is still bright. The far wall is a single clean plane of it, pale and sharp, unweathered, with edges that would cut you if you leaned wrong. The ceiling is a slab that did not fall. The floor is rubble that has not had time to round. Every surface is new. Nothing here has been smoothed by centuries of anything. A geologist would call this a young place, an infant place, a place that came into being and then — this is the part that matters — did not continue.

The chamber is small. Twenty paces one way, perhaps eight the other, the ceiling low enough that a tall person stoops. It is the kind of space you could fill with a house, or a chapel, and no more. Above it, presumably, is a hill and a sky and a moving world. None of that reaches down here. The air is still. There are no draughts, no seepages, no drip-line, no pool, no film of moisture on the stone. You will notice, after a while, that your mouth is dry, and that you are the only wet thing in the room.

Because it is extremely warm, and evenly so. There is no cool corner. The wall is warm, the floor is warm, the air is warm and the same warm everywhere, as though the room were the inside of a body. You can walk its whole perimeter with your hand out and find no gradient, no place where the heat comes from. It simply is. The heat has no source you can point to, and it has no direction, and it does not vary.

The light is dim. It comes from the room itself — a faint luminosity in the tips of the growth, and a paler sheen along the fresh faces of the fracture, where the broken rock glows the way a struck mineral sometimes glows, faintly, with a light that is almost a color and almost a memory of a color. You can see. You cannot see well. Everything is amber and bone and gray, and the shadows are soft because there is nowhere for a hard shadow to be cast from.

---

The growth is what makes you stop.

It is lush. There is no other word, and the word is a lie, because nothing here is wet. It covers perhaps a third of the floor and climbs the walls in patches, and it is dense — layered, deep, soft to the touch, a pile of small pale cups and fronds and curled ribbons that look like they should be dripping. They are not. Touch one and it is dry as paper. Press it and it gives, warm and resilient, like the pad of a thumb. It smells of nothing much — a faint mineral smell, a faint smell like clean stone after sun.

It should not be able to live. There is no water in this room, not a drop, not a film, not a vapour. There is no sun. And yet it is thick and thriving and in bloom, every cup open, every ribbon unfurled. The trick, as far as you can tell, is the rock itself. The fracture is fresh, and fresh rock is a larder — it is full of things that have not yet been locked away, and the growth is feeding on the broken faces directly, the way lichen feeds on bare stone, only faster, only hungrier. It has eaten its way out from the crack in a bloom that must have happened in a hurry and then — stopped. Exactly at the point where the supply met the demand. The whole field of it sits at the precise size the rock can keep alive, and it does not grow past that, and it does not shrink.

And it takes back its dead. There is no rot here, no mould, no crumble. Anything that dies in this room is absorbed — drawn down into the mat and taken apart and turned back into cups and ribbons, over a period of time so even and so slow that you cannot see it happening. The growth is not merely alive. It is a ledger, kept perfectly.

---

The sound is loud. Not loud like a waterfall. Loud like standing inside a bell that is being struck, continuously, by something patient.

It has layers, and once you have been in the room long enough to stop being frightened of it, you can pick them apart. There is the low note underneath, the one you heard through the rock: a sustained tone that seems to live in the fracture itself. The far wall is held open by stress — you can feel it, the tension in the new stone — and it rings the way a glass rings when it is pressed almost to breaking and held there. It has been almost breaking for a very long time. It will go on almost breaking.

Over that, the chorus. The room is full of animals. They are small and hard-shelled and many-legged, and they are everywhere — scattered across the growth, hanging from the ceiling in loose clusters, moving over the rubble in slow unhurried files. They are amber and rust and bone-white. Most of them have plates on their backs that they can rub or drum, and they do, and the small closed space with its hard fresh walls does not absorb the sound so much as hold it, so that a hundred small noises become one broad roar.

The roar has a beat. Listen long enough and you will find it: a phrase, a figure, a sequence of ticks and chimes and drones that comes around and comes around. The animals keep time with one another. They are not random. They are a clock, and the clock is wound to a period you could time with your pulse if your pulse were steady, and every pass through the loop is identical to the last.

You can shout in here and the sound will swallow it. You can shout again and hear the same shout swallowed in the same way.

---

Here is the thing you will not notice for an hour, and then will not be able to un-notice.

Nothing changes.

The animals are abundant — the floor is busy with them, the growth is busy with them — and yet their number does not move. One dies. You can watch it happen: a shell goes still, tips over, is quietly surrounded and taken down into the mat. And elsewhere, at the same moment, in a place you were not looking, another one comes up out of the growth, fully formed, and takes its place in the file. The ledger balances. The count holds. The chorus does not waver by a single note.

The growth does not spread. The rubble does not shift. The warm air does not stir. The note in the wall does not rise or fall. You could come back in a season and find the same cups open, the same files moving, the same phrase coming around. You could come back in a lifetime.

And you are not the first to come.

There are marks. They are sparse and small and they are scattered as though whoever made them did not stay long or did not stay in one place. A ring of stones on a clear patch of floor, set as though around a fire that was never lit. A low wall, three courses high, of stacked rubble, doing nothing, holding nothing back. A scatter of knapped flakes, and two blades, and a bone awl — simple things, hand-work, the tools of people who worked stone and bone and had no metal. There is a black rosette of soot on the ceiling above the stone ring, from a torch, and the soot is as black as the day it was made. There is a handprint pressed into a patch of soft pale crust on the near wall, small, five fingers spread, and the crust has hardened around it and kept it perfectly — every whorl, every crease of the palm.

The tools have not rusted or rotted, because there is nothing here to rust or rot them. The soot has not faded. The print has not weathered. Someone came down through the crack, and stayed a little while, and left these things, and this room has not touched them since. It has simply held them, the way it holds everything, in the exact shape they were in when the room took them.

---

You stand in the warm dim and the wall of sound, among the pale cups and the moving files and the handprint, and you become aware of the one thing in the chamber that is not in equilibrium.

You. You are breathing out moisture. You are shedding heat. You are carrying time in with you, in your lungs and your skin and your boots, and the room is not built to take it. The room is a closed circuit that has been closed so long it has forgotten how to be open. It is lush and loud and full of life, and it is completely, utterly still.

Listen: the note, the phrase, the loop.

It will be the same when you leave. It will be the same whether you leave or not.
