# The Throat

High on the mountain's shoulder, where the air is thin enough that every breath feels borrowed, the rock has split. Not a canyon — nothing so generous — but a fissure, a slot perhaps twenty paces long and two or three wide at its widest, plunging down through the mountain's skin into warmth. From outside it is invisible until you are nearly upon it: a black seam in pale granite, exhaling. The wind over the summit is dry and cold and constant; the fissure breathes back something else entirely, a slow warm tide of air that smells of wet stone and ammonia and green things rotting sweetly.

You would notice the sound before the smell, and the warmth before either.

## Terrain

The walls are water-carved — fluted, scalloped, polished into soft curves by centuries of seep and drip. This is not violent erosion; it is the patient kind, the kind that has found its balance and holds it. Every surface is either slick or furred. The rock sweats. Condensation beads on the high walls and gathers into threads that run down the flutes and fall, and the falling has worn small cups into the ledges, and the cups overflow into each other, and the whole fissure is a quiet machine of water moving down. Along the lip, where the light gets in, the stone is stained in long vertical streaks — white, rust, a green so dark it reads as black.

The floor is not trustworthy. It is a layered thing: wet gravel, then guano compacted over decades, then bone, then more guano, a soft sour mat that gives underfoot and holds the shape of your boot. Potholes near the bottom hold standing water — small, warm, milky with minerals, never quite drying, never quite flowing. It has rained recently; the walls are beaded and the pools are high.

At the base, where the fissure narrows to a crack too small for a body, the warm air comes from below. There is no visible vent, no steam plume, nothing so dramatic — just a steady exhalation from the dark, blood-warm, mineral-heavy, that makes the whole slot ten degrees kinder than the mountain around it. In winter, they say, the snow at the lip melts in a ring. The warmth is the reason for everything else here.

## The living density

This is a nursery and a roost and a larder, all stacked in the same twenty paces.

The swifts own the upper walls. Their nests are glued to the stone in clusters, mud and saliva and feather, hundreds of them, and the birds come and go through the slot of sky above like thrown stones, screaming — a sound that in the open would be thin but in this stone throat becomes a tearing, delighted shriek that fills the whole space and leaves no room for anything else. They never stop. Even at night a few stir and chatter.

Below them, bats. Not many species — two, maybe three — but many bodies. They hang in the fluted hollows in rows, furred pendants, and when the light fails they drop and the air is thick with them, a soft leathery clatter, a clicking that is almost subaudible but you feel it in your teeth. Their guano feeds everything below, and everything below is beetles and mites and millipedes and pale things with too many legs, working the mat in shifts. Spiders web the narrow places where the walls pinch. A frog sits in each pool — the same species, one per pool, as though assigned — and they call at dusk in a chorus that starts with one and spreads and stops all at once, and the silence after is startling.

At the lip, in the thin vegetation, there is more: gray tussocks of grass, a few cushions of moss, lichen crusts in orange and pale green, a fern that has no business at this elevation, thriving on the warmth. Sparse, yes — nothing here is lush — but every pocket of soil holds someone. A small bird I cannot name nests in a crack in the lip and scolds anything that comes near. There are droppings on every ledge. The whole place is marked, claimed, occupied.

## Sound and dark

The darkness is not total at midday; a blade of sky lies along the top of the fissure, and the light that reaches the walls is a gray-green twilight at best. But step three paces in and the walls close overhead and it is night at noon. Then the sound becomes the map. The drip chorus — dozens of separate threads, each with its own pitch and rhythm, some pattering, some a single clear note every few seconds, a slow irregular music that never repeats. The hiss of the warm air. The swifts above. The bat chatter. The water finding its way. And underneath it all, so low you might mistake it for your own pulse, a hum from the crack at the bottom — resonant, harmonic, the mountain's throat humming a note. It is probably just air moving through a constriction. It probably is.

## Rules of the world

Nothing here is made. There are no fire scars, no cairns, no flakes of stone, no handholds worn by other hands. The only builders are the larvae in the pools, who case themselves in grains of sand, and the swifts, and the bats do not build at all. Whatever lives here lives by the place's terms: it is warm, it is wet, it is loud, and it is small. The outside is cold and dry and enormous, and so the fissure is a trap and a refuge at once. Creatures come in and do not leave. The floor tells you so.

The cycles are slow. The drip builds travertine a hair's width a year. The pools rise and fall over seasons, not hours. The guano deepens; the fissure widens imperceptibly; the swifts return; the bats return; the frogs are always there, one per pool, patient as stones. It has been in this state a long time and will be for a long time yet. You are the fastest thing that has ever happened here, and the place will not notice you leave.
