# The Throat

You come up the outside of the mountain for two days and then you stop coming up, because the mountain stops being a mountain and becomes a wall with a crack in it, and the crack is where the city is.

It is not a city you would see from any distance. It is a fissure no wider than a room in most places, running vertically through four hundred meters of rock that is younger than most trees — fresh, black, glassy, still settling, its faces unsoftened by any weather that has had time to do the softening. The fracture planes are so new they ring when you tap them. Nothing has grown here. Nothing has died here. The rock simply broke, recently, along lines of least resistance, and the break has not yet learned to be anything but a break.

Inside it is hot. The heat does not come from the sun — the sun is a rumor, a knife-edge of white far above where the crack pinches almost shut. The heat comes from below, from the mass of the mountain itself, which is still expensively warm from its own making. It rises up the fissure the way heat does, and pools in the upper galleries, and makes the air dry and thin and faintly sulfurous near the bottom and almost comfortable near the top. Everything you touch is warm. Some things are too warm to keep touching.

And yet it is damp. This is the trick of the place, the thing that makes it habitable. On a cycle of roughly nine hours, the mountain exhales: the whole crack draws a slow column of vapor up from the wet dark at its base, and the vapor climbs into the cooler upper rock, and it condenses. It beads on the glassy faces, gathers, runs in thin braided threads down the fracture lines, and pools in the basins that the builders carved to catch it. Then the cycle turns, the mountain inhales, the air goes dry and still, and everything that condensed begins slowly to evaporate again. The people here — the people who were here — called the wet half of the cycle the climb and the dry half the draw. Water is a tide that comes and goes twice a day and never touches the sea.

The city is a splint. That is the first thing to understand about it, and the last.

The fissure wants to close. The mountain is young and heavy and still relaxing into itself, and every century the crack narrows by a hand's width, and given another few thousand years it would seal itself shut with no one to notice. Someone noticed. The built thing here is essentially a lattice of long ceramic pins and grown-glass struts, wedged and rooted into both faces, holding the crack open at the width it was when they arrived — a promise of a width, maintained by machinery that no longer needs anyone to maintain it. The pins are warm. They tick. They are under enormous load and they are also, in some way that current engineering cannot fully account for, converting that load: the mountain's patient settling, its slow grind of self-compression, is harvested. The lattice eats the mountain's patience and pays out light — a dim amber, the color of a coal nearly out — and pumping, and the small constant work of keeping the galleries dry during the draw and full during the climb.

There is no one here now. The galleries are empty of people and were emptied a long time ago, in a way that left everything tidy. There are maybe forty rooms in the whole Throat, scattered up and down the fissure, and between any two of them is a long climb of cable and rung and ledge, and between all of them is mostly nothing: hot air, warm rock, the long vertical hollow of the crack, a darkness that is not hostile, just dark. The density of the place is almost zero. It is a city the way a spine is a body.

The rooms themselves are cut-and-grown — half quarried from the glassy rock, half extruded from it by processes the builders used and did not leave instructions for. The walls have the sheen of obsidian and the give of a fired clay. Where the lattice meets the wall, there is a fine tracery of mineral growth, salt and sulfate and something like frost that is not frost, blooming in the seep basins where the climb leaves water standing. It is not life. It organizes itself anyway: it grows in whorls, in fans, in thin dendritic feathers that break and re-form with the cycle. If you sit in one of the empty rooms for the length of a climb you can watch it decide.

The light is amber and it is not enough. The lattice glows along its struts, brightest at the pins where the load is greatest, so that the Throat at night — and it is always night, or near enough, the sky a hard cold blade far overhead — looks like a mineshaft lit by the weight of the mountain. In the lower galleries there is another light: a dull red breathing from the rock itself, up through the crack from the deep warm, so faint you have to be still to see it. Standing between the two is the whole of the place. Amber above. Ember below. A column of hot damp dark with a few small rooms strung through it. The scale is intimate and the space is vast, and both are true at once, and that is the paradox of the Throat: it is a pocket you can hold in your mind and a void you could fall up into forever.

The sound is almost nothing, and everything that is there is mineral. The tick of the pins under load. The drip of the climb, which is not chaotic — it has a rhythm, a syncopation against the rock, and after a day you can hear where in the cycle you are just from the dripping. A long low groan every nine hours when the mountain turns over, so low you feel it in the sternum before the ears. Underneath all of it, the vent hush: the faint continuous hiss of hot air moving up a narrow place, which is the sound of the whole world breathing.

There are no animals. There is no moss, no lichen, no weed in the seep basins, no bird, no insect. The whorls in the basins are chemistry. The feathers of salt are chemistry. Nothing here eats anything. And yet the place is not still — it condenses and evaporates, it expands and contracts, it groans and ticks and glows and holds itself open against a mountain that would very much like to close. It has a schedule and a metabolism and no life at all, and the strangest thing about the Throat is how quickly that stops being strange.

The rule of the world here, the one the builders wrote into the lattice and the one anyone staying must learn: mind the draw. During the climb the galleries are cool enough and the air is wet enough and the cable is dry enough to climb. During the draw the rock sweats its heat back out and the air thins and the pins run hottest and the upper galleries become a place where the heat pools and does not move, and the trapped vapor scalds, and the mountain's settling is audible, and the lattice groans like something with a grievance. Nobody climbs during the draw. Nobody has climbed during the draw for a long time, and the empty rooms are all still tidy, and the splint is still holding, and the water still comes twice a day on a nine-hour tide, and the Throat keeps its schedule whether or not anyone is there to keep it.
