# The Sump

You come to it from above, through water that thickens as it warms. The surface is a long way back — twelve fathoms, maybe more — and somewhere around the halfway point the color changes from a bright green-gold to something closer to old brass, and the pressure settles onto your shoulders like a hand, and then the bottom of the world comes up out of the murk: a grid of lights.

That's the first thing. Not the buildings, not the silt plain, not the ring of broken concrete that walls the district off from the open floor. The lights. Small square windows, dozens of them, a few hundred, set into roofs and walls at every angle, each one a warm sulfurous yellow, each one with a faint stir of movement behind the glass. Air inside. Water outside. Lamps burning in both.

They call it the Sump, and it is small. A person can swim its length in four minutes if they know the currents. It is a single district of a city that no longer has other districts — a borough of tenements and foundries and pumping-houses that was built on a warm shelf of rock and stayed when the sea came up over it, because the sea coming up over it turned out to be survivable. The seawall still rings it, half-drowned, a horseshoe of slumped concrete and rebar, and beyond the wall there is nothing but the slow silt plain and whatever moves out there, which is not much and not often.

Inside the wall, there is no room. That is the thing that strikes you. Every cubic fathom of the Sump is doing work. The buildings lean on each other across the drowned streets — five storeys, six, brick and iron and green ceramic tile, roofs at what used to be third-floor height and are now the local ground. Catwalks run between them, and catwalks over the catwalks, and ladders, and pipe runs, and strung cables gone slack with weed, and awnings of rusted sheet metal furred with growth. Chimneys push up toward the surface in clusters of four and five, some of them capped with iron bonnets that vent a slow wobble of warmer water upward, so that above the district there is a fuzz of heat-shimmer standing in the water like a forest of ghost trees.

It is warm. Blood-warm, bath-warm, warmer in the lee of the outfalls. There is a hot seam under the shelf that nobody has ever seen and everybody has a story about, and it keeps the basin at a temperature that lets people swim in their shirtsleeves and lets the weeds grow like a rumour. The water tastes of iron and something faintly sweet-sour, sulfur and old brick, and it holds a haze — not silt exactly, a fine suspension that never quite settles, so that every light has a halo and every distance has a bloom.

The growth is everywhere the current lets it be. Eelgrass in the streets, thick as a carpet, waist-high on the swimmers who cross it. Red algae matting the hot outfalls in sheets you can peel off in your hand. Kelp on the cables, hanging in slow curtains that move one way and then, hours later, the other. The lee faces of every wall are forest — weed and sponge and things like pale fingers that close when you touch them. It is lush and it is shaggy and it has been lush for a long time, and it has stopped being an invasion and become the local condition. The buildings are not being eaten. They are being worn, and the wearing has its own settled look, like a stone in a river.

Sound is the strangest absence. Underwater is quiet anyway, but the Sump is quieter than it should be. What you hear is your own blood, and the long grinding hum of the pumps, which is less a sound than a pressure in the sternum, a slow pulse at maybe eleven beats a minute. The pumps are the reason the district is alive. They are old — older than anyone's memory of them, driven off the heat seam and the tidal head both, and they move the water through the district in a patient circuit so that nothing stagnates and nothing silts up faster than it can be cleared. When a pump throws a bearing, the whole quarter goes quiet and still and everyone feels it, and someone goes down with a wrench. There are nine of them. There have always been nine.

And they are maintained, because the Sump is inhabited. That is the second thing you notice, after the lights: the lights have people behind them. The rooms are dry. Air, held in by tar and pitch and the simple stubbornness of well-built walls, pressurized just enough to keep the water out and breathe in. You come down a drowned street between tall windows full of yellow light, and behind each one there is a room with a stove and a table and a shelf of jars, and a person, and the water on the glass makes them look like they're inside a lantern.

The streets are water and the houses are air, and the people move between them through hatches and vestibules and short flooded corridors, and they are unhurried in a way that takes a visitor a while to understand. They can hold their breath for six minutes. Not all of them, not the children, but most adults, and they do not think this is remarkable; they think it is like being able to carry a heavy thing. Down here the days are the tide and the pumps, and the work is the work, and the warmth gets into you and slows you down to its own pace: you find yourself sitting on a catwalk for an hour watching a curtain of kelp swing, and it seems like a reasonable use of an hour.

Animals are rare. There are the eels that come to the outfalls and lie in the hot water in knots, and there is something large and plated that grazes the eelgrass at the district's edge and is seen perhaps twice a year, and there are the little translucent things in the cisterns that nobody has decided are animals at all. That is most of it. The Sump is a place of plants and machinery and people, and the plants are winning the census.

Above, the light comes down weak and green and gets weaker with depth, and by the time it reaches the roofs it is only enough to tell noon from evening. Mostly the district lights itself. At night — if night means anything down here, and the people say it does, they keep hours — the windows go warmer and dimmer as lamps are turned down, and the glow of the whole place shrinks to a scatter of coals in a very dark room, and the pumps go on beating, and the kelp goes on swinging, and the silt comes down in its fine slow snow and is carried away again, fathom by fathom, year by year.

It has been in balance for a long time. The silt falls; the pumps push. The weed grows; the people cut it back from the intakes. The walls weather and the tar is renewed. Nothing here is new and nothing here is falling down, and if you stayed a decade you would not see the district change, only the seasons of it: the warm months when the algae blooms red and the water goes cloudy and sweet, and the cool months when it clears and you can see from the seawall to the far chimneys, and the whole drowned borough stands up in the brass-colored water like a thing remembered rather than seen.
