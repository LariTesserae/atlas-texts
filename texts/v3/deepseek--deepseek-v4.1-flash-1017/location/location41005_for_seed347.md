The town does not sit beside the water; it grew inside it. This is a place where the water table climbed through the earth and simply stopped, establishing a permanent, glassy ceiling just below the surface. The settlement—if it can be called that—is a lattice of woven roots and carved, living wood that hangs downward from the saturated soil of the world above, or grows upward from the flooded floor of the basin. It is a vertical place, a submerged cliff-face of habitation.

The scale is that of a valley, a great flooded sinkhole perhaps a mile across, its walls and floor packed with a life that has learned to be dense. There is no empty space. Every ledge and protruding knot of root is claimed. The architecture is entirely biological, an act of cultivation rather than construction. The walls of a home are the braided, patient growth of a dozen different plant species, guided over decades to form a watertight, insulated shell. Light is not a resource to be wasted. It filters down from the world above in a murky, green-gold diffused glow, constant and soft. Here and there, clusters of luminous fungi or pale, flat worms cling to the underside of root systems, providing a local, quiet light for a doorway or a path.

Water is the medium of everything. It is not a rushing current but a deep, heavy presence that presses against you. The water near the surface is cold and fresh, but walk—or rather, drift—downward, and it becomes a heavier, saltier brine, warmed slightly by the planet's slow heart. The two layers do not mix easily; they slide past one another in a constant, silent negotiation.

Sound is subdued, swallowed by the thick, saturated air and the sheer density of the biological matter. The place is not silent, but its sounds are close and personal. The slow, rhythmic *tock-tock-tock* of a wood-boring grub echoes through a dozen homes. The rustle of a colony of skittering shrimp moving through a root channel sounds like a whisper in the next room. The water itself is a sound-dampener, though you can hear the occasional, distant *plink* of a water-fat seed pod dropping from a high branch into a pool far below, or the low, resonant groan of a great structural root settling under its own immense weight.

Life is packed into every available niche. The ecology is a study in cooperation and close-quarters competition. The primary builders are a species of communal, semi-sessile crustaceans that secrete a calcite-like cement, fusing the plant roots into unbreakable joints. In turn, the plants provide them with sugars and a safe harbor from predators. The air bladders of a particular vine are harvested for flotation devices and storage pouches. Nothing is wasted. A fallen leaf is consumed by a dozen different organisms in sequence. Predators are slow, ambush hunters: a translucent, tentacled creature that mimics the texture of a common root, or a predatory flatworm that glides silently across the ceiling of a home to snatch an unguarded pupa.

The rules of this world are rules of density and damp. The primary law is the Agreement: no structure may grow so large that it collapses and damages its neighbors. Disputes are settled by a council of elder weavers who can read the structural stress in the roots. The greatest crime is to introduce a blight or a boring insect into the communal lattice. Privacy is a function of sound and light, not of walls. A family's home is distinguished by the soft blue glow of its particular fungus-lamps or the specific, contained hum of the beetles they cultivate for food. The people themselves are adapted to the pressure: slow-moving, patient, with large, dark eyes and hands that are both strong and incredibly delicate, capable of weaving the fine, living threads of a new addition to a home.

The place is young. The sinkhole itself is a recent geological event, perhaps only a few thousand years old, and the town within it is still in its adolescence. New ledges are constantly being discovered and settled on the sheer walls. The water level is slowly, imperceptibly rising, and the lowest dwellings are already being abandoned, their woven walls becoming artificial reefs for a new generation of inhabitants. The entire town is in a state of constant, quiet, dynamic adjustment—an active negotiation between the relentless water, the growing roots, and the dense, teeming, patient life that calls it home.
