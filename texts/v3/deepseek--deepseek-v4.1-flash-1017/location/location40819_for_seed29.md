**The Flue**

High on the shoulder of a dry massif, where the air is thin enough to sting the throat, there is a seam in the rock that is not quite a cave mouth — a shoulder-wide crack breathing out heat. The whole landscape is what lies behind it: one chamber, low-ceilinged, kidney-shaped, and entirely self-contained. You could cross it in forty paces. You will not need to. Everything in it is within reach.

The rock is new. That is the first thing you notice, before anything else. Nothing here has been rounded. The walls split and broke and simply stopped — plates of dark igneous stone standing at hard angles, their faces glassy where they parted, the edges still sharp enough to open a glove. The floor is less a floor than a jam of fallen slabs and fresh scree, none of it settled, none of it worn. No water has smoothed it, no wind has sanded it, no frost has wedged it apart. A fault opened this room and then thought better of it. It is a place paused in the middle of being made.

Everything is sharp, and everything is coated. Sulfur yellows the seams. Ochre and rust bleed from the iron. White salt crusts bloom in rings where vapor weeps out of the stone and leaves its chemistry behind. In the deepest corner a fissure exhales without pause, hot enough that the air above it shivers and the far wall swims — and around that mouth the minerals have built themselves into lips and ledges and small strange flanges, accretion laid down millimeter by patient millimeter across decades. The place is a foundry that assembled itself. The fissure is its furnace door. A shaft in the ceiling pulls the hot air up and out, and cool air crawls in low along the floor, and that slow convection is the engine of the whole room — it turns over once every few hours, a breath you can feel cross your ankles and then your face. There are no pipes and no rivets and nothing anyone built. But it works. It runs. It has been running, unattended, the entire time.

There is no water. Not a pool, not a drip, not a film on the stone. The dryness is total and it presses on the skin like a hand. What moisture exists is locked inside the salt crusts, or held in the bodies of the things that live here.

And they are everywhere. This is the density of the place: there is no bare stone left to put a palm on. Every surface is a moving rind of small hard-bodied animals, pale as the salt they graze, blind, shelled, packed shoulder to shoulder in their thousands, working the mineral crust down to the rock and then waiting, with a patience that looks like geology, for it to bloom again. Larger things move among them — segmented, hand-long, soot-black, scraping over the slabs on stiff legs. Something in the walls knocks, three times, then stops for a minute, then three times again. Near the hot fissure the animals cluster so thickly that the stone looks furred. They do not rot when they die, not here; the air is too dry and too sterile, so the floor is littered with husks and shells and desiccated husks of husks, and the living walk over the dead and the dead do not go away. The room is saturated. There is no space between presences. Everything is touching something.

It is loud. That takes getting used to. The vent roars — not a roar, quite, more a steady forced hiss, the sound of a great bellows held open — and the hard walls throw it back and forth until it fills the chamber completely. Under it, the seething: a sound like rain on stone, except it is ten thousand feet. A tick and a click and a rasp. Now and then a slab shifts somewhere with a low boom that rolls around the walls and dies. The room is small enough that sound has nowhere to escape; it compounds. You feel the noise in your teeth.

And the light is almost nothing. Dull red at the fissure mouth, deep and banked, the color of a coal seen through ash. Faint sheen wherever a shell catches it. The dark is not absolute, but it is close, and your eyes never fully adjust; you learn the room by ear and by heat instead. The hot side and the cold side. The near wall and the far. The vent's slow pulse — brighter, dimmer, a hand's width of glow breathing in and out over hours.

The rules of the place are simple and they do not bend. Heat comes from below and leaves through the roof. Air follows the heat. The animals follow the air. Nothing grows here that is not mineral or animal; there is no green, no softness, nothing that photosynthesizes, nothing that expects the sun. Moisture is the only currency and there is almost none of it. Time runs slow — the crusts thicken across a lifetime, the vent drifts a finger's width in a century, the swarms bloom and crash on a cycle longer than a season — but the room is never quiet while it waits. Loud and patient at once. That is the whole character of it.

Outside, the massif is bare and high and cold at night. Inside, the crack in the rock breathes out, steady as a sleeping animal, and the small blind things go on scraping the salt, and the stone goes on being sharp, and the room goes on being made.
