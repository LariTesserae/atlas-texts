# The Accretion

You come down through a fissure that is still widening — you can feel it widening, a slow tick of stone somewhere behind your ribs — and the air changes from cool dry to warm wet with a mineral tang, like the breath of something with a fever. Then the fissure opens and the country is under you, and it is enormous.

**Terrain.** This is a buried world, but not a dead one. It's a honeycomb of galleries and chambers stacked on chambers, welded together by shafts and chimneys and squeezed passages, extending out past any horizon you can see — a region the size of a valley system, folded into rock and lit from within. The floor beneath your feet is not old. It is *forming*. The stone here is raw, angular, unfinished; edges are sharp because nothing has had time to round them. Water comes through the ceiling in a warm drizzle, and where it lands it *leaves something behind*. Sinter crusts. Candy-colored flowstone. Columns that were not there a season ago and will be welded shut in a season more. The walls are thicker than they were. The passages are narrower. You are standing inside a geology that is mid-sentence.

Nothing here has eroded. Nothing here has been worn down. It has all been *added*. The whole country is a construction site with no builders visible, and the masons are water and heat and time, working fast.

**Water.** Not a lake, not a river — moisture everywhere, distributed, airborne. Condensation on every surface, beading and running. Warm rain from the roof at irregular intervals, sometimes fine mist, sometimes a hiss and a spatter. Pools stand in the low bowls of the floor, each one ringed with a pale rind of new mineral. Little streams cut channels that silt themselves shut within months and find new routes. It is damp the way a mouth is damp. You will never be dry here and you will never quite be wet.

**Light.** You expected blackness and got a dusk. Light seeps down through hundreds of fissures and pinholes from a surface nobody here has seen, and it arrives diffused, ambered, strained through stone — the color of light through a hand held over a lamp. And the place makes its own: the fresh sinter glows faintly while it sets, a dull rose that fades as it hardens, so the newest stone is the brightest and the ceilings look like cooling embers. Water carries a suspended luminosity too, so every drip is a brief streak of green-gold. You can see. You can see *too much*.

**Fauna — this is the overwhelming fact of the place.** It is not a cave with some animals in it. It is an animal world that happens to be made of stone. Life here is thick, layered, near-touching, crowded into every volume — not plants, nothing green, nothing that roots, but a density of animals that would be called a reef if a reef had no coral.

They are mostly small and mostly invertebrate and mostly *many*. Grazers the size of a thumbnail scrape the fresh sinter for the salts in it, and they travel in sheets, and the sheets move like weather. Filter-feeders — pale, feathery, sessile things — colonize the damp ceilings and open and close with the rhythm of the drips. Burrowers work the silt of the pools, and their castings build low mounds that themselves become new terrain. There are things with too many legs that run in columns along the walls and stop all at once, as if a switch were thrown. There are armored, crawling scavengers that follow the columns and eat what the columns drop.

And there are the large ones, the reason the small ones form columns. Something the length of your arm, blind, warm-bodied, that hunts by feeling the water for the disturbance of other movement — you will hear it before you see it, a wet drag and a click. Something colonial and enormous that occupies whole chambers: a single organism spread across a hundred square meters of ceiling, half animal and half mineral, that shivers when the stone beneath it shifts, and from which the smaller fauna hang like fruit. It has no name in any language you know. It has been growing since before the fissure you came through was open.

**Built.** Scattered, small, and easy to miss, which is a mistake. Hand-dug alcoves cut into the soft new stone, their mouths daubed with a mud-and-fiber paste that hardens as it dries — the paste is a local trick, the same chemistry as the walls. Screens of woven sinew and splintered rock, set to catch the drifting grazers. Bone and horn tools, knapped stone, a little cold-hammered metal traded up from somewhere above and prized to the point of being repaired forever rather than replaced. Smoke-stains. Caches. Ladders of lashed wood that lead up into chimneys and are gone by the next season because the chimney sealed itself. The settlements are seasonal and movable, because the country will not hold still: a chamber that was a good place to live five years ago is now a column and a wall and a crawl. The people come back to it anyway, out of stubbornness or memory, and dig it open again.

**Sound.** Loud, and never the same twice. The percussion of the drips is constant and layered — a thousand small impacts at a thousand pitches, so dense it becomes a wash. Running water somewhere, always, changing direction. The creak and tick of stone setting, which sounds like a ship in a swell. The fauna: a hum from the sessile things, clicks and rasps and a chittering that swells when the columns move, and then — every so often — a *surge*, everything vocal at once, the whole chamber rising to a shriek and falling back, though nothing you can see has changed. The large blind hunter makes almost no sound at all, which is why the silence around it is noticeable.

**The rules of the world.** Things grow here; they don't wear away. Nothing is finished. What is open today closes. What is sealed today may split open in a tremor and let you through. The stone is warm because heat comes up from below and will not be argued with. Water always carries something with it and always leaves something behind — so trust no surface you touched last season, and no passage you remember, and no tool you set down and did not bring with you. The animals are the calendar: when the grazer sheets thin and the filter-feeders close up tight, the chamber is about to do something, and you should be elsewhere. And the ceiling organism — nobody says its name indoors, and nobody harms it, and everyone who lives here for long has, at some point, put a hand against it and felt it answer, slowly, the way a wall would answer if a wall were patient and awake.

**Turbulence.** It is not a quiet country. Tremors pass through on no schedule; new chimneys tear themselves open with a crack you feel in your teeth; a pool that was still in the morning is a ring of dry rind by nightfall because the water found a different route down. Swarms surge and drain like tides with no moon to call them. The whole region is an anthill the size of a province, and it is not building toward anything. It is just *happening*, continuously, in the warm wet dark, and you are inside it and it does not care.
