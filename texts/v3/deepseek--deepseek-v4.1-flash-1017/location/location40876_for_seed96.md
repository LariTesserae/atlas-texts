# The Laid Country

The change comes over the last kilometer of the climb. Gravel, then smaller gravel, then a leveling — and then the ground is flags. There is no threshold, no gate, no wall to announce the edge. You find the first joint under your boot before you have noticed that the ground has been laid.

The flags are pale, grey-white, close-set. Every one is a little different in size, and every one is fitted to its neighbors with a joint no wider than a knife blade. The joints are filled with a fine pale silt that is not mortar and does not need to be: it holds water the way a wick does. Step on a joint and a thin line of water rises around your sole and sinks back. It has not rained here in days. The whole surface is like that — wet without weather.

## The shape of the country

You are in a high shallow basin, ringed by low ridges that are more like shoulders than mountains. The air is thin and cool and bright, the sky a hard white-blue, the sun strong enough to make you squint and the mornings cold enough to show your breath. The wind lives up on the rims and only visits the floor.

The scale of it is a country, not a building. Follow a gutter and you can walk two days and still be walking on laid stone. The masonry climbs over the shoulder of the ridge and you can see it going down the far side in terraces before the weather closes in and takes it away from you.

And it is all stone. Course on course, ledge on ledge, every horizontal cut into a shallow channel, every overhanging edge undercut with a drip-groove so the water that gathers on top is thrown clear and caught again a meter below. The basin is a single continuous instrument for moving small amounts of water a short distance downward, and it is playing, constantly, at a volume just under the threshold of attention.

Somebody — and this is the thing you keep turning over — cut a groove in the underside of nearly every projecting stone for no reason but to guide a thread of water. There are thousands upon thousands of these stones. The patience in it is not human patience. Or it is human patience, but spent so long ago, and so continuously since, that it has become something else.

## The water

The water arrives two ways. Some of it seeps out of the ridge, cold and clean, but most of it condenses: the stone is cold, the night air is not, and by dawn every surface is beaded. The grooves catch the beads. The beads become threads. The threads run to ledges, the ledges run to gutters, the gutters run to basins, and the basins are overfull — every one of them brimming and spilling into the next below, a chain of small pourings that crosses the whole basin in a hundred thousand quiet steps.

The cisterns are not cisterns in the way you expect. They are shallow, wide, fitted into the paving's own level, and so numerous that the ground is more water than stone in places — a lace of standing water an inch deep, each sheet a slightly different color of pale.

The water is so clear it is nearly invisible. You see it only by the movement it makes and the light it throws. It has no taste at all. It is very cold.

## The absence

Nothing grows here, and it takes longer than it should to notice how wrong that is.

There is no moss at the seeps. No lichen on the ridge faces, no green in any joint, no film on the standing water, no flies finding it. The stone has been wet for — you cannot say how long, but long — and it is as clean as a washed plate. The water runs and nothing drinks. There are no birds to hear, no insects to break the surface, no spiders in the angles between courses. Sound does not stop at a hush; it stops at a cleanliness, as if the whole basin had been scrubbed and then left.

It is not dead, exactly. Dead things leave a smell and a disorder. This is more like a place that has been kept sterile on purpose, and kept sterile very well.

## The packing

You cannot walk a straight line anywhere in it.

The basin floor is not open. It is a low forest of stubs and pillars and half-walls and stacked blocks, all of them coursed, all of them dressed, rising to the knee or the chest or the shoulder and stopping there without any sign of having been broken off. Between them are lanes just wide enough to turn in. Beyond the lanes are slots — narrow vertical gaps into blackness — and if you put your ear to one you hear water, and then, behind the water, a sound like the inside of a bell stuffed with cloth: more stone, filling the volume below you, course on course, going down further than you can see.

The interiors are not rooms. They are filled. Whatever this place is, it is more solid than hollow, and the hollows it does have are so tight that a person is not really the intended occupant of them. You do not go into the Laid Country. You go between its teeth.

## The making

Across days of walking you will not find one fallen block. No spall, no chip, no rubble, no drift of grit in a corner, no weed-crack, no frost-shattered edge. Every arris is sharp enough to cut your palm. Every corner is crisp. You could believe the whole basin was finished yesterday, and you would be half right.

In the lee of the stubs, out of the wind, there are stacks of fresh stone. Cut, dressed, waiting. Not arranged as a mason arranges stone for a day's work — no scatter of chips, no dust, no tools, no marking cord, no mallet — just blocks, stacked with great care, in a place where nothing at all is happening.

Because everything here is only ever added. You will walk for two days and never find a single place where a stone was taken out. Every repair is a shoring. Every failure is buttressed and left in place under the new work. Grievances are remembered in masonry. This is why the basin is packed to saturation: nothing has ever been removed from it, not once, and things that only accumulate, accumulate without mercy.

And the tool marks. Every dressed face bears the same family of scars — pecked, battered, ground — the marks of stone working stone, of hand tools without a single metal edge anywhere in the country. No one has found a chisel. No one has found a blade. The blocks are the only evidence that hands were ever here, and the hands have left nothing else: no image, no figure, no mark that means anything. Only fit. Only the astonishing, obsessive correctness of one stone against another.

## Sound

The place is quiet in the way a house is quiet at night, when you can hear it being a house.

Under everything there is a low continuous susurrus — not a roar, not even a murmur you could point to, just the sum of ten thousand small pourings and drippings, arriving at slightly different rhythms, never resolving. Once in a long while a stone ticks somewhere, settling into a fit. The wind comes over the rim and gets broken up by the stubs and reaches you in pieces. Your footsteps are absorbed; the wet joints do not even squeak.

Drop a pebble and the basin answers strangely — the sound goes out across the stone and comes back from nowhere in particular, from the whole ring of the ridges at once, as if you had knocked on the floor of a very large, very full room.

## The rule

By the second day it has become an obvious thing, the way the obvious takes time.

The water is not weather here. The water is the tenant. It condenses, it is caught, it is routed, it is spilled, and it has been routed so carefully, in such a fine-grained way, for so long, that the country has grown around its routing — the way a shell grows around a channel. The masonry follows the water's logic absolutely: not a wall without a drip-groove, not a level without a fall, not a basin without an overflow. Not one drop ever leaves the basin by an unplanned route.

And the rule of the Laid Country, the rule you feel in your knees after two days of walking on it, is this: nothing is taken away. Nothing grows. Nothing decays. Nothing arrives except by fitting into what is already there, and what is already there was made, and is being made, slowly, by something with an inhuman tolerance for the long duration — a course a season, a groove a year, patient as condensation, indifferent as a wick.

You leave the way you came, and the edge of it is just as unmarked going out as it was coming in. Behind you the water goes on stepping down, basin to basin, bright and colorless and unheard, in a country that is entirely made and has no one in it, and is very slowly not finished.
