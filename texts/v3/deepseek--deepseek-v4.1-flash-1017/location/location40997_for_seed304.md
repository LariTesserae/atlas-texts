# Skelf

The land here is younger than most of the people standing on it.

You come to it from higher ground and it announces itself first as a change in the sound underfoot: the crisp knock of dry earth giving way to a soft, closed, absorbent tread, as if you had stepped onto the lid of something. Then the smell — not rot exactly, but the smell of a cellar opened, of clay and cold water and cut stems.

Skelf sits on a nose of new ground pushed out into a broad, shallow basin where a river loses its nerve. The river comes down fast off the hills carrying grit and sand and the flayed material of somewhere upstream, and when it hits the flat it drops what it's carrying. It has been dropping it for maybe sixty years, maybe a hundred. The result is a delta the shape of a hand, low and level, its edges still raw, its channels cut sharp and unweathered, banks that slump in clean wet slabs because nothing has had time to soften them. This is not old country. There are no rounded hills here, no patient meanders. The water cuts a new channel in a night and the old channel silts up in a season. The land is a thing being made in front of you, and Skelf is built on the making of it.

## The town

Skelf is small — you can walk it in ten minutes and be surprised it took that long — and it is packed to the edges. There is no sprawl because there is no room to sprawl: the firm ground is a narrow lens, and everything that wants to stand up has crowded onto it. Houses lean shoulder to shoulder, sharing walls, sharing chimney stacks, sharing the same corner post. Lanes are the width of a loaded barrow and no wider; between the buildings there are gaps you can't call alleys, just the spaces a person can turn around in. Roofs almost touch. From above the town looks less like a plan than a knot pulled tight.

It is built of what the river brings and what the marsh grows. Alder and willow poles, coppiced and cut small, lashed with split withy and hide. Walls daubed with the local clay, which is grey and slick and shrinks as it dries, so every wall is faintly cracked and every crack is a home for something small. Reed thatch, deep and combed, replaced in patches rather than in whole roofs. Floors of packed earth and hurdle. Bone for pins and toggles, antler for levers, a little bog iron worked at one forge and used sparingly — a knife blade, a hinge, a nail that gets pulled and reused and pulled again. Nothing here is made new if it can be made over. The town is a long argument with decay, conducted with hand tools.

And it is layered, because the ground is rising under it. The delta is still accreting, and Skelf is being very slowly buried from below. Each generation's floor is the previous generation's ceiling. You go down a step into most doorways; in some houses you go down three. Older rooms lie beneath the living ones, dark and damp and sometimes still in use as cellars, their beams furred with white mould, their walls showing the fingerprints of people who pressed the clay flat a lifetime ago. When someone digs a new privy they find a hearth. When someone digs a hearth they find a floor, and a wall, and sometimes a doorway with the door still in it, swollen shut.

## Water

Water is not an event here. It is a condition.

The water table is a hand's depth below the surface in most of the town. Dig anywhere and the hole fills. The wells are wide and shallow and shored with timber that has to be replaced as it rots, and the water in them is cold and tastes of clay and iron. The ground weeps. Cellar walls run with condensation, and the weeping is steady enough that people leave a lip of stone at the base of a wall to guide it, a small gutter-trench, a channel that takes it out to the lane. Mornings the whole town is beaded. Rope swells and tightens overnight. Leather goes soft and green. Bread must be eaten or it grows a fur. There is a constant, quiet, domestic war against moisture, and moisture always has the better supply lines.

Rain arrives often and leaves slowly. After it, the lanes are standing water over hard clay, and the whole delta shines, and the new-cut channel edges show their raw cross-section — bands of sand and silt and darker organic seams like a slice of cake. The river shifts its load. Somewhere out in the basin a new bar is building, smooth and pale, and in a few years there will be willow on it, and in a few more someone will be arguing about whether it counts as Skelf ground.

## Sound, light, air

It is a quiet place, but not silent, because the water is always doing something — going over a lip, gurgling in a shored trench, ticking into a well. The reeds move when the wind finds them and the sound is a dry papery hiss that stops as soon as the wind does. Voices carry oddly: the wet ground and the close walls deaden them at a distance and then return them, so you hear a conversation three houses over more clearly than the one in the next room.

The light is ordinary and honest. Cool, most days — a low grey ceiling of cloud with the sun a pale disc behind it, and the wet ground throwing that light back up so everything is lit slightly from below. On clear days the light is hard and clear and the puddles go full of sky. At dusk the thatch holds the last of it, orange along the ridges, and the smoke from the chimneys lies down in the lanes instead of rising, because the air is heavy with water and has no interest in lifting anything.

## Living things

There is not much green, and the green there is, is small and tough and low. Reeds and sedge at the margins. Moss on every north face and every rotting beam. A few willows pollarded to knuckle, kept as a crop. Nettles in the wet corners where the middens are. Nothing here grows tall except the people's need for poles.

Animals are few and mostly not wild. Ducks, kept for eggs and let loose on the channels. A few goats on the drier hummocks. Eels in the wells, sometimes, and the children are sent to deal with them. Out in the basin, herons, patient and grey, and the occasional thing in the water that people don't name, that leaves a wake in a channel too shallow for anything large.

## The rules of the place

Things sink here, and things come back.

Drop a tool in the marsh and it will return to you, years later, black and waterlogged and harder than it was, dredged up by a neighbour who recognised the handle. The ground takes and the ground gives. This is a common enough occurrence that Skelf has a custom about it: found property waits a year and a day, and if it is not claimed it belongs to the finder. There is a shed by the well where found things wait — a hatchet, a boot, a carved spoon, a child's wooden horse — and everyone in town has walked past it and looked to see if something of theirs has come back.

And the town keeps lifting itself. The ground rises a thumb's width in a good year, and the buildings must be raised to match, jacked on green poles and shimmed with flat stones, the sills cut out and replaced, the doorways retrimmed. The town has a trade for this, a small guild of levellers who can tell you which corner of your house is sinking slower and which is already gone. They are busy. There is always a house half-jacked, standing on its props with its underfloor open to the daylight, and someone's grandmother in it, refusing to leave while the work is done.

That is Skelf: a small, wet, crowded, patient place, sixty years old and still forming, built of the river's leavings on the river's leavings, lifted an inch every few years by people who know exactly what the ground will do next and keep building on it anyway. The water is near. The land is new. Nothing here has finished settling — not the banks, not the town, not the ground itself — and everyone who lives here has made a life out of that being true.
