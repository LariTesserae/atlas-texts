The way in is a seam, not a door. It opens in the wall of a deeper passage where the rock has split along an old bedding plane, and the gap is narrow enough that a person has to turn sideways and let the breath of it come over their face before they commit. The breath is warm. Warmer than the passage behind. It carries water — not the smell of water exactly, but the smell of hot stone that has been wet a long time, and under that something faintly alkaline, like the air over a kettle left to cool.

The passage rock is cool and ordinary, gray, dry in its cracks. Two meters in, the walls begin to bead. Another meter, and there is water standing in the low spots of the floor, warm to the touch, and the seam widens without warning into a room.

It is small. A flattened pocket in the rock, four meters across at its widest, two high at the crown, its floor a shallow basin tipped slightly to one side so that the water gathers in a palm-deep pool along the far wall. Everything about it is fresh. The walls are fracture surfaces, still angular, still sharp — the edges where two planes meet could open a hand, and the rock has not had time to round a single one of them. There are no stalactites, no flowstone, no rounded scallops of the kind that take centuries. The chamber was opened, and then the water found it, and that is all the history it has.

And it is lit. Not by any opening to the sky — there is none, the ceiling is unbroken stone — but by the walls themselves. Every surface carries a film, less than a millimeter thick, pale as the inside of a shell, and that film gives off light. It is a cold, even, greenish-white light, and there is enough of it that the room reads like a small white kitchen at evening: no true shadows, everything legible, the far corner dimmer only because the film there is thinner. Where a chip of the wall has flaked away you can see the film in cross-section, banded, pale and paler, hundreds of fine layers laid down like the rings of something that grew.

The film is wet. All of it. Water condenses on the film, gathers into threads, runs down in slow channels, hangs from the ceiling in droplets that swell for a long time and then let go with a sound like a single struck note, the pitch shifting a little hour by hour as the air thickens and thins. The air is saturated — hot, perhaps forty degrees above what a body likes, heavy with vapor, so that the pool steams continuously and the mist sits in the upper third of the room and moves in slow rolls when anything disturbs it. There is no telling the temperature of the walls by looking; they are all the same pale glow. Touch one and it is blood-warm. Touch the water and it is warmer.

The water is the interesting part, if you watch it. Close to the film, it does not behave like water. It holds shape — not frozen, but arranged, layered, a few molecules deep and then a few hundred, ordered against the mineral surface the way a crystal is ordered, and the ordering is what the film grows from. Scrape the film away with a fingernail and the bare rock beneath it is dull, and the water that touches it goes briefly slack and ordinary, and then within minutes the layers re-form out of the hot saturated air and the light comes back in the scraped place, paler at first, then full. The room repairs itself the way a cut lip does. It is doing, without anything being built, what a laboratory would need a clean room and a power supply and a decade to attempt.

Nothing here is constructed. There are no tools, no marks, no paths worn by feet. But the place is not random either. It is a pocket where a steep gradient — heat climbing from the rock below, water seeping from the fracture above, mineral dissolving from both — is being spent on making order, and the order is very precise, and it is entirely an accident of chemistry.

Almost nothing lives here, and what does is barely recognizable as alive. On the walls, six or seven of them, are flat pale things the size of a palm, wet, soft, without eyes or limbs or anything you would call a head. They cling to the film and creep across it at a pace you cannot see, only infer: leave and come back and one has moved a hand's width. Where they have passed, the film is gone and the wall is dark, and behind them the film returns, brighter for a while than the untouched surface around it. When one is touched it contracts and goes still and exudes a thin clear fluid that smells of the pool. That is the whole of their behavior, as far as it can be observed. In the water, drifting on the convection, are motes — small, colorless, more than dust, less than anything with a name; they gather where the grazers have been.

The sound of the room is the sound of water finding its way out of the air: the drip, the hiss of the pool, and very occasionally, somewhere in the wall, a sharp tick as a fresh fracture opens a hair's width and a new surface begins, that hour, to film over. Under the tick there is sometimes a softer sound, a faint wet adhesion, as one of the grazers moves.

The pocket will not last. Already, at the waterline, mineral is precipitating out of the hot pool in a thin crust, and the crust is spreading, and in some number of decades the fracture will seal itself and the pool will go still and the film will starve and dim and go out, and the room will be a dry angular void in the rock with a faint banded residue on the walls, and no one will know it was ever bright. For now it is new, and the water is still coming in, and the light is on.
