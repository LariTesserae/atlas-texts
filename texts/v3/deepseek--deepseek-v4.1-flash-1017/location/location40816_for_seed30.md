# The Cup

High on the shoulder of a mountain that has no name anyone agrees on, there is a bowl in the rock about forty paces across. It is not a crater and not a sinkhole; it is more like a cup that the mountain is still in the act of throwing. The rim is uneven, unfinished, soft-edged in places where the stone has the consistency of pressed clay. You can put your hand against it and feel it warm and slightly tacky, and if you leave your palm there a minute and pull it away, there will be a faint pale bloom on your skin where something has precipitated out of the air onto you.

This is the whole of the place. A small room of stone at the top of the world, and everything else happens inside it.

## The wet

The air is the first fact. It is warm — bathwater warm, blood warm — and it is so saturated that the difference between air and rain is mostly a matter of emphasis. The mountain's interior is a chimney; heat comes up through cracks from somewhere below and carries moisture with it, and that moisture hits the cooler evening air at the rim and lets go. The result is a standing weather: a slow, continuous precipitation that is not quite rain. It falls in fat intermittent beads. It gathers on every upward-facing surface and runs. The stone is never dry. Nothing here is ever dry. The whole cup sweats.

At the bottom, a hand's depth of water sits in the lowest hollow, and it is not still — it is being fed and drained at the same time, so it turns slowly, a lazy millwheel of a pool, and the surface never quite settles into a reflection. Around its edge the ground is a soft apron of mineral mud, and it is warm there, and it moves slightly underfoot, settling.

## The rock, still becoming

Nothing has been worn away here. That is the strangest thing about the place and the thing it takes longest to notice. Erosion is a story about time passing, and this place is not yet old enough to have one. Instead the opposite is happening: the stone is *arriving*. Every surface is accreting. The rim is thicker this year than it was last year. The pool's edge creeps inward a finger's width each season, and the water pushes back, and the two of them negotiate.

The material is banded and pearly and in places translucent, so that when the low light catches the lip of the cup it glows a little from within, faintly amber, like a nail held up to a candle. There are places where the mineral has grown in thin hollow spars that chime when the wind crosses them, and those spars are the closest thing to old stone in the whole basin — a few decades, perhaps, and already they are being buried by the newer layers coming up around their feet.

## The scaffold

But there is something here that was not precipitated by weather, or not by weather alone. Spanning the upper part of the cup, from rim to rim, is a lattice — a loose net of pale threads, each one no thicker than a hair, that crosses the open air in a pattern that is not quite regular and not quite random. It is alive in the sense that it grows, and it is a machine in the sense that it computes. You can tell because the threads brighten in sequence, a slow pulse traveling from one anchor to the next and back, and the pattern of the pulse is not a repeating one. It is doing something. It has been doing it, undisturbed, for as long as anyone has climbed up here to check.

The lattice draws from the water. Where its threads touch the pool it pulls mineral out and extends itself, and where the mineral builds up too heavily on a thread, the thread sheds the crust in a small glittering fall, and the crust becomes part of the rim, and the rim becomes the mountain. So the machine and the geology are the same process, and it is not clear which one is the byproduct of the other.

## The built things

Scattered among the rocks at the rim are a handful of human objects, and they are all partial. A coil of cable that ends in nothing. A tripod whose feet have been grown over by stone, so that it looks planted rather than set down. A stack of flat plates, half-absorbed, their surfaces still faintly legible as diagrams if you tilt them to the light. There was never a building here. There was never more than an outpost: someone came up, set a few instruments at the edge of the lattice, and left, or was absorbed into the long slow work of watching. The instruments are still watching. Two of them are still powered, and nobody knows by what, and they make a sound like a tuning fork struck very far away.

## The animals

The place is loud with animals, and they are the reason it is loud, and they are here for the water. The cup is a well at the top of a dry mountain, and everything that can climb has come to drink.

There are the mist-feeders: small, soft, translucent things the size of a thumb, that cling to the underside of the lattice and open themselves to the falling beads, and when they are full they glow the same amber as the stone. There are grazers on the mineral mud, hard-shelled and slow, that scrape the fresh accretion and leave clean tracks that fill in behind them within a day. There are the climbers, furred and quick, that come up the outside of the mountain in the dark and drink from the pool and go down again, and their claws on the unfinished stone make a sound like someone knocking politely at a door, over and over.

And there are the things that eat the climbers, which are large and do not come often, and when they come everything else goes quiet for a moment before the noise resumes doubled.

## The sound

The sound is constant and layered near the top of hearing — the hiss of endless condensation, the chime of the spars, the far tuning-fork hum of the instruments, the chorus of the mist-feeders, which is not song but a dense crackling like a fire of tiny dry sticks. Under it all, the mountain itself moves warm air through its cracks with a low breath that rises and falls on a slow cycle you can only perceive if you stay long enough to stop hearing it.

It is not the loud of a city. It is the loud of a very small space filled completely with activity. There is no silence in the cup. There is only the brief attentiveness when the big thing comes, and then the noise closes over again.

## The light

Light arrives late and leaves early, and even at its best it is a dim, suspended glow — the sun filtered through the standing mist into a diffuse amber that casts no shadows and makes distance hard to judge. You cannot tell how far across the cup is by looking. You can only walk it. Most of the time the light is the light of the lattice and the mist-feeders and the luminous rim, and the world is a small warm cave at the top of the sky, and outside it, somewhere below, is a colder and drier and much larger world that you have briefly climbed out of.

## The rules

Do not break the threads. They will regrow, but slowly, and the pattern they regrow into will not be the same pattern, and something will have been lost that no one can name.

Do not take the water away in a vessel. The pool does not want to be a pool anywhere else.

Everything here is being built. If you stay, you will be built into it too — not violently, just slowly, a little mineral at a time, the way the tripod was. The regulars at the rim do not think of this as a danger. They think of it as the reason to come.
