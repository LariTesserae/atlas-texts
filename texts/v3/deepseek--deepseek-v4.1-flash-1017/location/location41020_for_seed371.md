High on the mountain, where the ridge breaks into a clutter of fresh-fractured stone, there is a slot in the rock that breathes out cold mist. You hear it before you reach it: a knocking, a hiss, a low grinding groan that comes and goes on a rhythm of about forty seconds. The gravel underfoot is trembling. The air coming out of the slot smells of wet iron and crushed green.

The slot opens into a room. It is not large — thirty paces across at its widest, roofed by a wedge of stone that has fallen and jammed, and above that roof, a mat of vegetation so thick it seals the sky out entirely. There is no light in here to speak of. A knife-blade of grey comes through one seam near the top and is swallowed almost immediately by the mist, and what it touches is not much: wet black rock, and the pale undersides of leaves. Everything else must be learned by hand and ear.

The rock is young. It has not been rounded yet. The walls are all fracture — sharp cornices, splintered fins, edges that would cut you — and the floor is a rubble of chips that have not had time to become sand. This is a place the mountain made recently and is still arguing with. Nothing has settled. Every surface is slick with condensation, and the condensation is what makes the room possible: it runs down the fresh stone, gathers in a hundred small caught basins, and feeds the green.

And the green is everywhere. It does not grow here so much as it packs itself in. Moss in slabs an inch thick, furred and dripping; creepers that have found every crack and filled it; broad soft leaves layered one against another so closely you cannot put a hand between them; hollow stalks, pale and jointed, standing in clusters like the pipes of an organ, each one half full of water. The plants here are pale — cream, bone, a green so deep it reads as black — because there is no sun to reach for. They have grown toward the water instead. They are wet, all of them, and their surfaces are hairy or waxy or cupped to catch the fog as it moves. Nothing in this room is dry. Nothing in this room is still.

Because the water does not flow here — it pulses. Somewhere above, in fissures too narrow to enter, melt and rain gather and press. Pressure builds. And then, on that forty-second rhythm, something gives way, and a slug of water is forced through the stone and released into the room: through a fist-width vent in the north wall, down a chute behind the jammed boulder, out of a dozen small holes at once. The room fills with mist and noise. The hollow stalks take the surge and shriek — genuinely shriek, tuned to different pitches by their length and how much water they hold, so that the blowout arrives as a chord. The bladder-plants along the floor snap shut and pop open. The loose rubble on the floor lifts and resettles. Then the pressure drops, the vents suck air, everything gulps, and there is a long minute of dripping and ticking before the build begins again.

It sounds like a factory. That is the plainest thing to say about it. The knock of water against a stone throat is a stamping mill; the vent-hiss is a steam line; the groan of the jammed boulder shifting a hair in its seat is a press under load. There are even bellows — the whole mat of vegetation overhead moves as a single membrane when the pressure changes, drawing air in and pushing it out through the seam, so that the room exhales and the leaves rub and the sound comes from everywhere at once. It is loud. You cannot talk over it comfortably. It is the noise of scaled production, of something being made in quantity by mechanism, and there is no worker anywhere in it. No animal lives here — not a fly in the mist, not a beetle in the rubble, not a bird on the roof. The room is entirely plant and stone and water, and it runs itself.

The turbulence is the strangest part. The room is not stable; it is in the middle of becoming something else. Because the stone is young and the edges are sharp and the water keeps forcing, small collapses happen constantly — a fin sheds, a channel silts and cuts a new one, a basin fills and overtops and abandons the plant that was drinking from it. In one place a whole shelf of moss has been undercut and hangs, still growing, over a hole that was solid last month. The vegetation surges into every new opening and is drowned out of every old one. Nothing here is old. The grove is probably younger than a person. It will be choked or washed out or buried within a few decades, and a different room will be open somewhere else in the same fracture, and it will fill with pale leaves and begin to knock.

There is a rule to it, and the rule is: everything is on the water's schedule. The blowout comes, the plants open and close, the air moves, the boulder settles, the mist rises and condenses and runs down, and then the build begins again. Nothing in the room is exempt. Stand in it long enough and you can feel your own breath fall into the forty-second rhythm without meaning to.

Outside, the mountain is cold and grey and quiet, and the mist pouring out of the slot goes down the slope and thins. Ten paces from the entrance you can barely hear it — the din shuts off like a door closing. A hundred paces away there is nothing at all: bare young stone, wind, the far drop. The room keeps its noise to itself, packed in tight under the fallen wedge, making its pale green and its water hammering in the dark, entirely uninterested in whether anyone ever finds the way in.
