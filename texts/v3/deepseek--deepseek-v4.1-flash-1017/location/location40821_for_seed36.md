# The Vault of Unspent Rain

You come down through stone that has not decided what it is yet. The throat is narrow and warm to the touch, and it sweats. Your palms come away slick with a film that is not quite water and not quite the stone's own substance — the walls here are young, still annealing, still faintly plastic under the pressure of a fingertip. There are no cracks in them. There are no chips, no scuff marks, no dust in the corners, because nothing has had time to make any, and nothing here is in the business of wearing things down. The passage does not look carved. It looks *opened*, the way a bud opens, from the inside.

Then the throat releases you and the light arrives all at once, without a source.

The ceiling is stone, but it is thin — a rind, a membrane, the last skin of rock between this place and the world above, and daylight comes through it the way light comes through the web of your hand held against a lamp. Not a shaft. Not a beam. The whole roof glows, pale gold shading to a greenish white toward the far side, and because the light comes from every part of it at once there are almost no shadows. Things are lit from above and from the sides and from the wet leaves of other things. You can see the shape of a leaf on the ground by the light that passed through the leaf.

The bowl is wide — a valley floor under a valley, an hour to walk across if you walked straight, and the far wall is visible only as a softness, a place where the green thins into haze. The air is thick and hot and holds its heat the way a closed mouth holds breath. It is wet. Not raining, exactly; the moisture arrives as a fine drifting condensation, a mist that beads on your arms and then decides not to run. Everything here is beaded. Every surface has a nap of water on it. When you stand still long enough, your own skin does the same.

And the plants fill it. All of it. There is no ground to see. The floor of the bowl is a woven mat of roots and buttresses so dense it bears your weight, and beneath that mat more roots, and beneath that the raw warm stone, and the roots go into the stone where there is no soil at all because no soil has been made yet. Nothing has decayed here to make any. The vegetation is blanched — pale green, bone, the color of things kept out of the sun — because the light it lives on is diffuse and comes from everywhere, and the leaves have gone wide and flat and slightly translucent to catch it. Some of them are faceted, ridged like the inside of a shell, scattering the glow. The canopy is not high. It is not a canopy at all; it is a *volume*. The plants stack. They layer into each other, fern over fern, leaf through leaf, a hundred kinds of holdfast and bladder and frond, and there is no clear up in it, only more of it, near-touching at every level, and the air between the leaves is as crowded as the leaves.

It is very quiet. That is the first thing that unsettles. A hot wet forest should roar with insects. This one does not. There is the drip — a slow, irregular ticking from the ceiling, condensation letting go and falling through the layers of leaf to land on a leaf below and stay there — and there is the enormous soft hush of a great many wet surfaces in a warm room, and that is all. Nothing calls. Once, crossing a low saddle of root, you see something: a pale thing the length of your forearm, many-legged, moving with great deliberation down a trunk and into a hollow, and it does not hurry and does not look at you, and it is the only animal you will see for hours.

The water does not go anywhere. It does not run. There is no stream, no channel, no groove cut in anything — nothing has been cut. The pools lie in the hollows of the root mat, still and warm and perfectly clear, and the ceilings above them sweat and the drops fall in and the pools give it back up as mist and the mist climbs and the ceiling takes it and it falls again. A closed circle. A wheel that turns and does not travel. This is the rule of the place, and once you have understood it you see it everywhere: the leaves on the root mat are not fallen. They are still attached, or they fell long ago and have not gone brown, have not curled, have not gone soft. There is no rot here. There is no seedling pushing up through litter, because there is no litter to push through, because nothing has ever finished dying. The forest is exactly as full as it was when it began, and it is exactly as full as it will be when you leave.

Near the center of the bowl, where the glow is brightest and the mist thinnest, there are the rooms. Three of them, that you find — maybe four. They are not built. They are grown: great boles of a vine-like thing that swelled into hollows and then stopped swelling, each hollow large enough to stand in, with thresholds that are the smooth pinch of a constriction the plant made and never widened. The walls inside are pale and faintly warm and beaded with water. There is no furniture, no ash, no mark of any hand. But the floors are smooth in a way that the rest of the bowl is not — an absence of the little ridges and knots you have learned to step over — and in the deepest one, the one that requires you to duck twice, there is a shallow basin grown into the floor, and it is full, and the water in it is the same temperature as the air.

You cannot tell how old any of it is. That is the strangest thing, stranger than the light. The stone is new — you can feel it, still warm, still unfinished, geology caught mid-sentence — and the forest on top of it is complete and mature and has clearly been complete for a long time. There is no young growth. There is no old growth. There is only *this*, in a state of having just arrived and having always been here, both at once, and the dripping marks no time at all because nothing the dripping touches is ever changed by it.

Climb back out through the sweating throat and the light fades behind you by degrees, and you carry the heat of it in your clothes for an hour afterward, and the smell — green and mineral and faintly sweet, like the breath of a thing asleep — stays with you longer than that.
