# The Held Room

You find it by falling, and the falling is the only fast thing here.

There is a crack in the flank of a drowned hill — a seam of paler stone, no wider than your shoulders — and beyond the seam the water changes. Not temperature, not salt: *age.* The water outside moves, has tides and purposes; the water inside has been held so long it has forgotten how to move. It meets you at the threshold like a hand and lets you through, and then closes behind you with a sound like a sentence ending.

You are inside a room. It is perhaps ten paces across. It is full — to the last fingerwidth of its ceiling — of cold water, and the water is not empty.

**The walls.** They are young. That is the first wrong thing, the thing your hands keep reporting and your mind keeps refusing. Everything here is *new*: the walls still carry their making-marks — blades and ridges and little unfiled fins of stone, edges that have never been rounded, facets that catch your palm like a broken tooth. Nothing in this room has been weathered. Nothing has been forgiven by water. The chamber was opened — or sealed — recently, in the way of stone, which is to say within the span of a few human lives, and it will take ten thousand patient years for the water to soften a single one of those edges. The room is a fresh wound that will not begin to heal until long after everyone who ever knew it is gone. In some places the walls are the color of wet slate. In some they are the color of old wood. Along one flank, where your fingers find a long smooth curve, they are the color of bone. You decide not to decide.

**The growth.** It is packed. There is no room between presences. Every surface is furred, tufted, layered — and the plants do not care that there is no light, because there is no light and they have made other arrangements. They are pale, all of them, the white of things kept under a stone: leaves like wet paper, leaves like the insides of ears, leaves that are not leaves but flat living sheets pressed against the rock and drinking through their whole skin. They grow toward pressure. They grow toward the slow tidal pulse of your body's heat, and when you swim you must part them with both hands like pushing through a crowd that is very polite and does not want to move. In the middle of the room the growth leaves the water entirely: a lattice of roots, pale and taut, spanning wall to wall, floor to ceiling, and hung with a second growth and a third — moss on root, threads on moss, a fine white down on the threads that lifts and settles with your passing and settles again. Three lives deep, everywhere. A forest with nowhere to spread has gone vertical, and inward, and into itself.

**The dark.** It is not empty dark. It has grain. Move slowly and it parts around you in slow sheets, and you can feel its texture on your face like the nap of velvet rubbed the wrong way. There is no light and no memory of light. Your eyes are useless and eventually stop insisting. It is the hands that see here, and the cold, and the pressure. Sight comes in through the skin.

**The marks.** Scattered, faint. On the eastern wall, a ledge has been squared — not much, a hand's width taken off to make a level place, and on the level place, a row of small drilled holes, five of them, evenly spaced, holding nothing. Below it, a stone that has been turned and set upright, deliberately, and then, just as deliberately, laid back down. There are cut marks on the great curved bone-colored flank: short parallel strokes, counting-something, though nothing that counts survives. Whoever made them came here with a purpose and a cutting edge, and left, and the room grew shut behind them, and the room has been quietly absorbing their marks ever since — softening the drilled holes at their rims, furring the squared ledge with white growth, the way a body absorbs a splinter. The made things are older than the room's own walls. The room grew *around* them. You cannot work out how, and you stop trying, because trying has a shape and the room does not fit it.

**The animals.** Rare. You will meet perhaps one. There is something in the root lattice the size of a thumb, all shell and no face, that opens when you are near and closes when you are nearer. There is a slow pale ribbon that crosses the ceiling once, on no schedule you can perceive, and is not there when you look again, and leaves a thread of slightly warmer water behind it. And at the far end, in the deepest tuft, something larger is breathing. Not breathing water. Breathing the way a sleeping thing breathes, slow and deep and entirely unhurried, one breath perhaps every several minutes, and you cannot tell whether it is an animal or a particularly old knot of root that has decided to be one. You leave it alone. This is the rule.

**The sound.** Almost none, and none of it trustworthy. Your own heartbeat arrives from the walls a half-second late and slightly altered, as though relayed by a courteous stranger. The plants creak. The rock ticks — once, twice — as it continues the very slow business of becoming itself. And when you speak, the room returns your voice with one word missing and one word added, and the added word is always a word you know but did not say.

**The rules.** Here they are, as best you can learn them:

The room is stable within a visit and not across visits. The squared ledge is on the east wall. It has also been on the north. The root lattice spans the ceiling. It has also not spanned anything, and hung free in the water like a net set for something that never came. Neither memory is wrong. The room is a place that is still deciding, and it resolves differently depending on how you arrive.

Edges soften here that are not edges of stone. Your skin's boundary loosens. Twice you touch a root and it is, briefly and without drama, your own wrist. You do not recoil. Recalling would be the strange thing.

Time is thick and does not run. You surface believing an hour has passed and find that the light outside has moved a great deal more than an hour, or a great deal less.

And the water does something at the threshold. At the seam, the two waters — the moving one and the held one — refuse to mix. They lie against each other like oil and oil, like two opinions. You can put your hand across the boundary and feel one half of it cold with current and the other half cold with stillness, and the line between them is thin and exact and has no business existing.

You leave the way you came, through the seam, and the held water closes behind you with that same sound — a sentence ending — and the hill goes back to being a hill, and the crack goes back to being a crack, and the whole small room goes on with its one occupation: **filling itself.** Slowly. Patiently. Growth by a hand's width a year, edge by an edge, mark by mark, until the walls forget their sharpness and the drilled holes close and the squared ledge is only a rumor in the stone, and the thing in the deepest tuft takes one more breath and lets it out, and the room is a little more itself than it was.
