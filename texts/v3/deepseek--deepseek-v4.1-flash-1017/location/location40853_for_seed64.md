# The Notch

The way to it is a chimney of air on the lee face of a tower that has no summit worth naming — the rock goes up into haze and does not come back down as anything you can see. Two kilometers of nothing on every side. The wind here does not gust; it is drawn, steadily, up the face, and has been drawn so for long enough that the stone has been fluted into smooth vertical channels, polished like the inside of a shell. You would not spot the notch from below. It is a shadow the width of a door, one of a thousand such shadows, and the only reason you find it is that the drawn air thickens as you climb and then, at the lip, stops being wind at all and becomes a pressure — a hand flat against your chest, guiding you in.

Inside, the world closes to the size of a chapel.

---

The notch is a pocket, scooped and rounded, maybe twenty paces from the entrance to the back wall and half that across. The walls lean together overhead and almost meet; there is a slot of pale sky above, no wider than an arm. The floor is a shallow bowl of dust and grit, packed hard, stirless. The walls are the same fluted stone as the face outside, but here the flutes have gone soft, rounded by centuries of the same air moving the same way, and every edge in the place is a worn edge. Nothing here is sharp. Nothing here has been sharp for a very long time.

The pocket is full.

Not full of objects — full of growth, and the growth has no gaps in it. It comes off the walls in a pelt a meter thick, hangs from the leaning ceiling in curtains that touch the floor, and where it touches the floor it splays and mats and rises again as new curtains. There is no path through it that does not involve pushing. It is the densest soft thing you have ever put your hands into, and it is nothing like any plant you know.

It is dry. Entirely, absolutely dry, and it crumbles faintly at the edges where you brush it, shedding a pale meal that hangs in the air and does not settle because the air will not let it. The strands are fine, chalk-white to bone to a tarnished straw color at the tips, and each one is a chain of tiny stiff segments — not hollow, not veined, not wet inside, but built, the way a shell is built, laid down grain by grain and abraded away at the same rate. It is not soft the way a leaf is soft. It is soft the way raw wool is soft, or the inside of a very old mattress, or the fur of an animal that has been dead a long while and has gone to mineral.

This is what passes for lush here. It is profuse and it is extravagant and it is everywhere, thick as a winter coat on every surface, and not one drop of it is water.

It lives on air. It combs the drawn wind for dust — the fine, salt-bright, crystalline dust that the wind carries up the face — and it holds it, and something in the segments takes the dust apart and adds a fraction of it to itself and lets the rest go. It also takes charge. That is the part that takes longest to notice: in the dim, the tips of the strands carry a faint standing glimmer, a blue-white sheen that is not reflected light. The pelt is faintly charged, all of it, the whole chapel of it, and where two curtains touch they do not quite touch. There is a hair's breadth of repulsion between them, everywhere, holding the whole mass in a kind of tautness. Put your hand in and the fleece leans away from your skin.

---

The light is a pearl. There is no sun in here and no disc of sky bright enough to hurt; the slot above is a lid of moving dust, and what comes down through it is diffuse and gray-gold and sourceless, and it does not change. Not from minute to minute. Not, you suspect, from year to year. There is no dawn in the notch and no evening. There is a permanent gray noon that is barely brighter than dusk.

And the sound.

The sound is enormous and it is the same sound.

The drawn air does not pass through the notch so much as it is *played* by it. The flutes of the walls, the exact throat of the entrance, the exact mass of the fleece and the exact resistance it offers — all of it together makes the pocket a single instrument, tuned, and what comes out is a chord. A low held roar underneath, broad as a river, and over it a set of high thin tones that are almost a chord and never resolve into one, and inside that the constant dry rustle of the pelt moving on itself, which is not random, because the pelt is combed one way and the air is combed one way and the two never change, so the rustle is a fixed figure repeated at the speed of the wind and the wind does not vary. It is loud enough that you shout without meaning to and then stop, because there is nothing to say and no one to say it to. It is loud enough that after an hour you cannot tell whether you are hearing it or feeling it. Everything vibrates. The floor under your boots, the wall under your palm, the air in your chest. A dropped pebble does not ring on the stone; it lies there and hums.

---

There is one built thing.

Halfway back, against the north wall, under the deepest curtain of fleece, there is a housing: a squat cylinder of riveted plate, taller than a person, its seams gone orange and its orange gone brown. It has ribs. It has a bolted hatch. It has a stack of louvered intakes around its middle, and the intakes are packed solid with fleece — deliberately, exactly, packed to a density that has not changed in a very long time and that is, you understand, the whole point. Inside it something turns. You can hear the turning as the floor of the chord, the fundamental, a slow heavy rotation that has not sped up or slowed down and will not. There are pipe stubs coming out of it, capped and flanged, going nowhere. There is a stub of ladder, three rungs and a rust-line, going up into the pelt. There is a footings-pad and a scatter of bolt heads in the dust and that is the extent of it. No other structure in the notch. No path to it. It was built, and it was run, and whoever ran it left, and it has been running ever since, and the fleece has grown into it and around it and through it at precisely the rate that keeps the intake at precisely the right resistance, so that the note holds.

The thing in the cylinder and the pelt do the same work. The cylinder does it more loudly, and older, and it is not clear which of them is the apprentice.

---

The rules here are few and they do not bend.

Nothing accumulates. Nothing depletes. The dust blows in and the dust is taken up and the fleece abrades and the dust blows out, and the sums are zero to the last grain. The wind is drawn up the face and the fleece slows the wind and the slowing is exactly what draws it, and the sums are zero. The stone is carved by the air and the air is shaped by the stone. The machine wears and the machine is fed, and the two rates were matched long before you got here and will be matched long after.

The equilibrium is total, and it is not peaceful; it is *held*. Disturb it and it closes over the disturbance like water you do not have. Shove an arm through the curtains and they lean back, and the chord wavers for a moment — the first variation in its history, maybe — and then the pelt comes back around your arm exactly as it was, and the note returns, and the sums are zero again.

There are no animals. Not an insect in the fleece, not a bird in the slot of sky, not a mite in the dust. Nothing here eats. Nothing here hides. The only motion in the notch is the motion that was always going to happen: the strands swaying their fixed sway, the dust going where dust goes, the slow rotation in the cylinder, the chord holding.

And no water. Not a drop, not a damp seam, not a bead on the cool stone in the small cold hours, because there are no cold hours and there is nothing to condense. The place is drier than any desert, because a desert at least remembers rain. This has no memory of it. You could stand in the middle of the most abundant living thing for a thousand kilometers and die of thirst, and it would not be cruelty; the abundance simply has no use for what you are made of.

---

You leave the way you came, out through the throat, and the pressure lets you go, and the chord drops behind you all at once into ordinary wind. Below, the face falls away into haze. Above, the rock goes up into it. The notch is a shadow the width of a door, and from thirty meters out you cannot find it again.

But the sound follows you down for a long way — not the wind, which is shapeless now, but that fixed high figure underneath it, still repeating, still at the same pitch, still exactly itself, as it was before you came and will be when the last of the rivets has gone to brown dust and the pelt has closed the hatch and something in there is still, contentedly, turning.
