# The Kiln

You come down through water that has no bottom to it, warm as a held breath, and the first thing you notice is that the pressure is gentle. There is no current here. Nothing moves except what you move, and what you move settles slowly, patiently, back into stillness. This is the top of a ridge—a summit drowned a long time ago—and the water above it is deep enough that the light has given up. What remains is a faint blue-brown haze, enough to know which way is up, not enough to see your own hand clearly. You feel your way down by temperature. It gets warmer.

The summit is small. Perhaps eighty paces across before it falls away into deeper dark on every side. And it is covered—entirely, edge to edge—with something built.

---

It is a mound of small rooms.

Not a city, not a temple, not a fortress. A mound the size of a cathedral, made of chambers the size of cupboards and closets and narrow beds, stacked and crowded and pressed together until their walls are shared, mortar to mortar, shoulder to shoulder. You can see, where the haze thins, that it has no natural stone beneath it: the ridge top is dressed and leveled and everything above that line is laid by hand. Cut blocks, fired bricks, courses of flat shale split with wedges and set in a paste of crushed shell and ash. No metal anywhere. The tool marks are still there, softened but legible—chisel scars, the rough scoops of bored holes, wedge slots in the corners of blocks where a lever went in. Whoever built it did it with hands and stones and patience, one room at a time, over a span of generations that has no meaning anymore.

It grew the way a wasp nest grows. That is the impression. A chamber was made; another was packed against it; a doorway was pinched shut and a new one opened an arm's length over; a floor was raised with rubble because the old floor had flooded; a room was filled solid and built over. There are rooms inside rooms. There are corridors you must turn sideways to enter, and then a space where four passages meet and you can almost stand. The whole mass is dense with itself—walls nearly touching walls, voids pressed against voids, a hundred small interiors per cubic stone.

---

The water inside is the same warm water. It moves nowhere. It has been in these rooms so long that it tastes of the mortar.

There is no weed. No algae films the walls, no green anywhere, nothing that grows. The stone is clean and pale and faintly greasy to the touch, and this is because it is being eaten—slowly, continuously, by everything that lives here.

They are everywhere. Small pale things the length of a finger joint, blind, soft-shelled, more legs than seems efficient, working the seams and the mortar lines in numbers so dense that a single block might carry a hundred of them. They rasp at the cement and swallow it and excrete something very close to it, in a slightly different color, and they pack it into the cracks. You can find their work everywhere once you look: repaired corners, filled fissures, a chip in a threshold patched with a paste that does not quite match. They maintain it. They have no interest in the building at all—the seams are simply where the food is, and the food is replenished because they keep the seams open, and the seams stay open because the building is repaired, and the building is repaired because the seams are where the food is. That is the whole of it. It is not a miracle. It is a habit, a very old habit, and it has held this mound in its exact shape for longer than the mound was ever a building.

There are larger things too, in the dark rooms. Something with a shell that you hear before you see, dragging. Something that folds itself into the pinch-corridors and does not move when you pass. Fish the color of the water, thin as a leaf, that turn away from your warmth. A mass of something soft in the largest chamber that pulses slowly and does not react to you at all. Life is abundant here and entirely uninterested. Nothing in this place has ever needed eyes.

---

It is almost silent.

Not the silence of air—water carries sound well—but a silence of things that have no reason to speak. There is the tick of stone cooling and warming across a long cycle you cannot perceive. There is the dry rasp of the small ones at their work, a sound like sand poured very slowly, which is the sound of the whole hill, always, if you put your ear to a wall. There is, very occasionally, a groan from some deep course settling by a fraction. And there is the sound of your own body, which is enormous here, and which the place does not answer.

The light is the faintest milkiness from the small ones themselves, a glow so weak it only tells you where the walls are, and the last blue memory of the sun far above. You will not see color. You will see edges.

---

The rules of this place are few.

Warmth comes up from below, through the ridge, and it does not vary. The water does not move. Nothing that falls here rises again—silt, bone, the flake of a chisel, all of it settles into the low corners of the low rooms and stays. Nothing grows; things only arrive and are eaten and are made into mortar. The building does not fall, and it does not change. It has reached the shape it will keep. The sea has rounded every corner of it, softened every carved line past reading, and then stopped, held there by the small ones and their patient patching. There is no next stage. This is the final form of a thing that was made by hands and is now kept by mouths.

You should not stay long. Not because it is dangerous—nothing here will harm you—but because the place is so settled, so utterly at the end of its own long business, that it begins to persuade you that you are too.
