# The Soke

You come up the valley and you can hear it before you can see it, which is the first thing anyone says about the Soke.

## The shelf

The mountain here has a shoulder — a broad broken terrace about a third of the way up, where the slope flattens against a wall of grey rock and then drops away into a gorge that the locals call the Throat. The shelf is travertine: pale, banded, riddled with the casts of old stems, and it is a thing the water built. The spring comes out of the rock wall at the back of the terrace, cold and steady, and it has been laying down stone for a very long time, and the town sits on the stone the water made, about three hundred people in a bowl of green.

It is not a big place. You could walk the whole of it in ten minutes if you could walk in a straight line, and you cannot. The Soke is terraced and stacked and grown together; it rises in steps and ladders and grafted arches up the shelf and then up into the trees, so that a third of the town is above the ground and the ground is above the ground, and the address of anything is a matter of which flight of steps and which trunk and how high.

## Everything is wet, and everything is alive

The air is the first thing. It is cool — never warm, never properly cold, but cool and heavy and it holds water the way a held breath holds heat. In the mornings the whole shelf is inside a cloud that has come up the Throat and got stuck on the terrace, and the cloud does not fall as rain so much as condense: it forms on the leaves and runs, and the running is the sound you hear. Every surface in the Soke that faces up is dripping by dawn. Every surface that faces down has moss on it. The moss is thick enough to press your thumb into, and it is the town's caulking, its insulation, its roof-felt, its bandage.

Water is not a feature here; it is the medium. The spring at the wall is the town's mother, and it is divided and subdivided by channels — some carved into the travertine, some cut into logs, some running through split stems of a woody reed that the Soke grows for exactly this purpose. The channels run along every terrace edge, drop over every stair, and turn the place into a series of small, continuous, domestic waterfalls. Under the walkways there are cisterns. Over the walkways there are gutters. The lower third of the Soke is half a step above amphibious: a wet, green, croaking understory where the steps are dished into basins by decades of feet and water, and where the buildings stand on piles of stone that are constantly being repointed because the wet keeps working them loose.

And the green. The vegetation here is not around the town. It *is* the town. Nothing in the Soke is built in the sense of assembled and left; everything is grown and then held. The walls are living: trunks of a pliant, quick, pale-wooded tree trained against a frame when young, grafted to its neighbours where they touch, so that a house is a single organism with many roots, and when you lean on a wall it has a grain. Floors are split poles laid on living bearers that are pruned back each year so they do not lift the floor. Roofs are layers of felted bark, then moss, then more moss, and the moss grows a crop of small pale flowers in the cool season, and the roofs of the Soke go white for a week and everyone knows it is that week.

The forest that the Soke is inside comes down the mountain and fills the Throat and goes on being itself in every direction. It is a wet, dense, layered forest — canopy, understory, floor — and it is not tidy. Fallen things rot in place. Everything is being taken apart by something else. There is no bare earth anywhere you can point to; the ground is a mat of roots and litter and fungus and it gives underfoot.

## The law of the cut

The central fact of life in the Soke is that the plant grows faster than the town can stay ahead of it. A grafted wall left alone for a season will close its own doorway. A floor-bearer will push up through the boards. The moss will eat a roof in two years. So the town is not built; it is *negotiated*, continuously, by cutting.

Everyone prunes. It is the basic civic labour, like fetching water, and it is done in the cool of the morning with hooked knives of hardwood and flint and chert, with bone saws, with wedges, with the copper that comes up from the lowlands and is treasured and resharpened until it is a sliver. There are no nails. Joints are grafts, ties, pegs, wedges, or the weight of stone. The sound of the Soke is the sound of a thousand small cuts — a constant irregular ticking and snapping under the water and the animals, like a forest being edited.

There is a guild, the Cut, who hold the pruning calendar and the grafting rites, and there is one law that is the whole constitution of the place: **no structure outlives its pruning.** Nothing is allowed to become permanent. A house is renewed by being cut back and regrown around its people; a stair is re-laid when the old one has been worn into a trough; a bridge is re-grafted every generation. The town has no ruins because it is not permitted to have them — an abandoned building is not left to fall, it is cut, and the cut wood becomes fuel and food and fibre and feed. The Soke eats its own past, and it does so on purpose, and it thinks this is wisdom.

The result is that the town has no fixed shape. It has a memory of a shape. The map is redrawn every spring by the Cut, and the map is a matter of argument, and the argument is the town's favourite sport. People will tell you, with total confidence, that the baker's steps are where the tanner's steps were, and someone will disagree, and both will be right in different decades.

## The crowd

And then there are the animals, and the animals are the reason the Soke is loud.

The Throat is a funnel, and the shelf is a resting place on it, and the Soke sits in the middle of one of the great movements of the mountain. The birds come through in numbers that stop being countable and start being weather. Swifts and martins in their season, in the hundreds of thousands, filling the Throat at dusk and pouring up over the terrace to roost in the wet rock wall and in the eaves of the grown houses, and the sound of them is not a sound you hear, it is a pressure on the chest. The town has a name for the hour: the Spill. When the Spill comes, the Soke stops. Work stops. Talking stops. You stand in the street in the failing light and let it happen to you, and the air is full of moving bodies and the whole terrace is a single chattering roar, and then it is over and the town goes back to its evening, a little shaken.

Below the birds there is everything else. Frogs in the cisterns and the dished steps, in three or four voices, all night. Crickets. Moths in numbers that coat the lamps and the walls and are swept up in the morning by children with baskets and eaten, fried, salted. Salamanders in the wet stone. Fish and eels in the channels, some of them stocked. Bats under the eaves, which the Soke tolerates because the bats eat the moths, and the Soke needs the moths eaten. Mice and rats in the granaries, and the small wild cats the Soke keeps half-tame and half-not. Otters in the Throat, which are trapped for their fur but mostly not. Leeches. A fly that bites and a fly that does not. Beetles the size of a thumb joint that the children race.

The people are outnumbered, and they know it, and it has shaped them. The Soke is not a place that conquered its surroundings. It is a place that has found a way to live inside a crowd, by cutting, by yielding, by eating what comes and by getting out of the way when the crowd is too big. There is a rule about the Spill. There is a rule about the night the eels run. There is a rule about not building on the seep-line at the foot of the wall, which everyone obeys and no one can quite explain, and which is probably about a collapse two hundred years ago.

## Churn

The Soke does not sit still. It is a turbulent place, not in the sense of disaster but in the sense of a thing that is always happening.

The water shifts its channels every season and the Cut follows it. The ground moves — the travertine slumps and cracks and is re-laid. A terrace gives way once a decade and a house goes down into the Throat with its family in it, and the town rebuilds on the stump, and the stump is why the new house is a little lower and the steps are a little steeper. The plants are cut and regrow and are cut. The birds come and go. The insects hatch. The fungi fruit overnight and are gathered at dawn. The town's shape in any given year is a compromise between what was there last year and what the growing thing wants.

Weather comes up the Throat in the afternoon and the whole shelf is briefly inside a cold white violence of wind and water, and then it clears, and steam comes off the moss, and the Soke comes out again and starts cutting. It is cool, it is bright-grey, it is endlessly wet, and it is loud enough that people stand close to talk and have learned to read each other's mouths. It smells of wet stone and cut sap and rot and smoke, and the smoke is constant, because the cut has to be burned or it piles up, and there are smouldering heaps at the terrace edges with children tending them.

You would not call it serene. You would call it *thriving*, in the way a wound thrives — busy, warm, crowded, healing and being wounded in the same motion, and never once quiet.
