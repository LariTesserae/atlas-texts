The sound is the first thing. Not a noise you hear so much as a pressure you feel, a constant, roaring presence that fills the air like a solid substance. It is the sound of the wind, but not the wind you know. This is a wind that has been squeezed and funneled through a vast, hidden architecture, a wind that screams through canyons of its own making.

The landscape is a desert of stone, a vast and broken plateau of sun-baked rock, ochre and rust-red, that stretches to a horizon shimmering with heat. But it is not empty. From this plateau, the land drops away into a great, winding chasm, a canyon so deep and narrow it is less a valley and more a crack in the world. This is where the life is. This is where the city is. This is the source of the sound. The wind, a ceaseless, dry gale from the wastes above, pours over the canyon's lip and plunges into the depths, and in doing so, it becomes a voice.

The city is not built on the canyon floor, for there is no floor, only a deeper and deeper descent into a warm, humid gloom. The city is built *into* the canyon walls, a sprawling, chaotic tapestry of structures clinging to the vertical stone. It is a place of wood and rope and stone, of a thousand generations of hand-craft. There is no metal here. Beams of sun-bleached and wind-scoured timber, lashed with woven cords, jut from the rock face. Platforms of layered, hardened hide and woven reeds cascade down the cliffside like the scales of some impossible, arboreal beast. Bridges of rope and plank, some ancient and fraying, others new and taut, swing precariously across the abyss, connecting one cluster of dwellings to another.

And everywhere, everywhere, is the vegetation. It is not a green world, but a world of deep, lustrous browns, of fibrous ochres, of the pale, waxy yellow of strange fungi. It clings to the stone in great, shaggy mats. It grows in the perpetual damp warmth of the canyon's depths, nourished by the unseen moisture and the mineral-rich dust the wind carries down. Vines as thick as a man's arm, their surfaces like rough bark, snake up and down the walls, their leaves broad and leathery, cupping the perpetual mist. These vines are the city's arteries, its ropes, its foundations. The people are weavers, carvers, and growers. Their world is one of wood, fiber, and stone, a sophisticated, non-metallic culture of immense ingenuity.

The air in the canyon is thick and warm, a constant, humid exhalation from the deeps. But above, on the plateau, the air is dry as old bone, the sun a hammer on the red rock. The two worlds are separated by a vertical mile of stone and a deafening wall of sound. The noise of the wind is a physical presence, a constant blast that shapes everything. It dictates the architecture—no flat roofs, no open spaces, every structure is a wedge or a teardrop, designed to let the gale slide around it. Conversation is a shouted, facial affair, a language of broad gestures and exaggerated lip movements. The people of the Wind-Scream, as they call themselves, are a hardy, sun-browned folk, their hearing attuned to the nuances of the roar, able to distinguish the subtle change in pitch that means a storm is coming or a rope-bridge is failing. They sleep in shifts, their minds accustomed to the endless noise, finding silence in the spaces between the wind's howls.

Rarely, a creature will appear. A great, silent moth with wings like stained glass, drifting on the updrafts, its flight a ghostly ballet against the rust-red stone. Or a "skitter," a many-legged, armored thing the size of a hand, that scuttles along the vine-ropes, its carapace a mosaic of living stone. The people are not hunters, but tenders. The skitters are tolerated, even encouraged, for they eat the smaller, more destructive pests. The great glass-winged moths are sacred, messengers of the deep, and to harm one is unthinkable.

The world here is a process, a slow, endless cycle. The wind carves the canyon deeper, grain by grain. The vines grow, are harvested for rope, and grow again. The city is always being repaired, its rope bridges rebuilt, its timber beams replaced. It is a place of immense, long-established balance, a delicate, vertical ecology that has weathered countless ages. The people do not fight the canyon; they are a part of its slow, erosive breathing. They are a cacophonous, vibrant, hidden heart in a vast and empty stone world, a pocket of lush, loud, and warm life suspended over a silent, impossible abyss.
