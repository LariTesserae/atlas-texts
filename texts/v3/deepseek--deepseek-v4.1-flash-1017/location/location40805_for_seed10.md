# The Flue Plain

High up, where a mountain range has finished being a mountain range, there is a shallow dish of country about a day's walk across, and it is filled with bog.

You come to it over a rim so low you don't notice you've crossed it. The range around it has been worn down for so long that it no longer has peaks, only a slow wrinkling of the crust, and this basin is the last place on the range where anything collects. Everything the weather brings, the dish keeps: rain, snowmelt, fog, the fine grit of the air. It has been keeping it for a very long time. The result is a lid of peat — not deep in the way that mountains are tall, but deep in the way that centuries are long. In places it is eight meters of compressed, half-digested plant matter, black and fibrous and cold, and it is still, at the bottom, digesting.

The plain is almost perfectly flat, and this is the first thing that unsettles people. Flatness at this elevation is wrong. You expect a summit. Instead the ground goes out and out, dark and soft, with a skin of moss so dense and continuous that it is less a surface than a substance — you do not walk *on* the plain, you walk *in* it, up to the ankle, sometimes to the knee, and the mat takes the shape of your foot and holds it for a while, then slowly forgets.

It is cold here the way a cellar is cold: not cruel, just constant, a few degrees above freezing and never quite warmer, except in rings. In the rings it is warm. We'll come to the rings.

## Water

Water is not a feature of this place. It is the state of the place. The mat is saturated; press it and it weeps. Between the mosses run channels — thousands of them, braided, anastomosing, none wider than a stride, all of them black with tannin and faintly tea-colored where they catch the light, wandering across the flat with no urgency because there is nowhere to fall. They carry almost no slope. The whole basin tilts by a few meters across its width, and that is enough to give the water a direction and a sound.

The sound of the water is the floor of the soundscape: a continuous hiss and cluck and gurgle and trickle, so many small voices that they merge into something like an audience, a low crowded murmur that never stops and never resolves. Along with it goes the squelch — the mat's own noise, the wet suck of every step, the pop of gas released from the mud below, a slow irregular blooping like something enormous and patient clearing its throat.

Above, the air is also water, nearly. The basin sits under a lid of cloud so persistent that the sun is an idea rather than an object. There are maybe twenty clear hours a year. The rest is fog, or the promise of fog, or the memory of fog: a cold gray-white that condenses on everything and makes every stem and hair and eyelash bead with droplets. Twice a day the fog thickens and the far end of the plain goes away entirely, and the world becomes forty meters across and there is no horizon at all.

## The stalks

The vegetation is total. There is no bare ground. Moss, liverwort, sedge, cotton-grass, low wiry shrubs, a dozen kinds of lichen crusting the higher hummocks — the plant mass is the substrate, the floor, the thing you stand on. The plain is one organism with a great many opinions.

But the plain is also *empty*, and this is the second thing that unsettles people. The plants are everywhere and the *bodies* are not. Walk an hour across the mat and you will see: moss, moss, a hummock, moss, a pool, moss. Nothing tall. Nothing with a trunk. The place is all ground and no vertical.

Except for the stacks.

They stand alone, three hundred meters apart, sometimes more — a scattering of pale blind towers across the whole basin, perhaps two hundred of them in all, rising twelve, fifteen, twenty meters out of the flat. They are not trees. They are hollow. Each is a single stem of layered, waxy, gray-green tissue, thicker than a person at the base, tapering, rootless-looking, ringed at intervals by collars of folded leaf tissue like valve seats. There are no branches. There is a crown, at the top, of pale translucent blades that hang and clatter.

And each one is warm. Each one breathes out.

The bog below is a slow fire that never lights. Bacteria digest the peat in the absence of air, and the digestion produces heat and gas and pressure, and the pressure must go somewhere. Over an age, the plants have solved for this. Their hollow stems are flues. Vapor rises through them, condensing, releasing its heat into the tissue, drawn upward by the difference, and out through the crown in a plume you can see from a kilometer off — a slow white feather that bends and thins. You can hear them: a low organ tone, each at its own pitch, the whole plain a dispersed chord. When the vapor is heavy they clatter. When the vapor is very heavy they shriek, a thin high whistle that carries, and the crown blades spin and the plume stands straight up and the mat around the base pulses, once, like a diaphragm.

Nothing here is built. You will find no post, no wall, no road, no cut, no mark of hands. The flues were not made. They grew, the way a lung grew, because a bog needed to vent.

## The rings

Around each flue's base, the outflow keeps a disc of ground twenty meters across thawed and warm, and that disc is where the world's life crowds in.

Fauna is abundant here, and it is *concentrated*. Between the flues the plain is nearly silent of animals — a few birds, a hare, a passing fox, a great deal of nothing. At a flue it is a bazaar. Insects in columns, so thick in the warm updraft that the light goes granular. Spiders in the columns. Birds nesting in the valve collars and the crown, loud, calling across the empty intervals to other flues, because the plain is open and a call carries. Frogs in the meltwater at the base, in a chorus that starts at dusk and does not stop. Small mammals in burrows in the warm peat, and the things that eat them. A single flue will hold more living mass than the hectare around it.

So the density of the place is a strange thing: you can walk half an hour seeing almost nothing, then step into a ring and be surrounded, deafened, mobbed. Low saturation, punctuated by anthills. Emptiness balanced against knots.

## The weather that doesn't come from the sky

The plain is turbulent in a way the sky does not explain.

The charge builds in the peat. It is a battery, has always been one — wet carbon, slow chemistry, an age of it. The flues bleed it upward. On most days it leaks away quietly and you feel it in your hair, in the fine hairs of your arms, a faint continuous static that makes the fog cling. On other days it does not leak. It accumulates, and the air gets that flat, held, teeth-aching quality, and then the plain discharges — not from the cloud, from the *ground*. A crack, then a long rolling boom that travels along the mat, close to the surface, and a bloom of pale blue-white that spreads out low and flat, like light poured on a floor.

These are the ground-storms. They are weather at ankle height. They form in one part of the basin and crawl, twenty or thirty meters a minute, following the wet channels, discharging every few seconds, and they can run for an hour, and they are loud — louder than anything else here, and this is already a loud place. Afterwards the mat is warm and smells of ozone and burnt sugar.

And the fog holds the light. This is the thing you will not get used to. Discharge in the fog does not flash and vanish. It lingers. It leaves shapes — pale standing figures of glow, hanging where the current went, and they *stay*, dimming over hours, drifting a little. You can walk around one. You can walk back an hour later and it is still there, faintly, a light with no source, resident, patient. Some of the older ones have been lit so long that the animals have stopped noticing them. They are, in a sense, the lamps of this place. The plain is lit by light that happened earlier, the way a room is lit by a fire someone built before you came in.

The light here is dim: a gray-green underwater glow under the cloud lid, everything in half-tone, and then the lights that are left over, and then the plumes.

The fog answers, too. Not the way a person answers. When the ground-storm passes, the shapes in the fog are not always where the discharge was.

## Rules of the world

Nothing here is young. There is no colonization, no regrowth, no new ground. The basin reached its final form a long time ago: a flattened, saturated, self-digesting dish, and everything in it has settled into the thing it is. The flues are all old. No new one has risen in living memory and probably not for a thousand years; they are a fixed set of two hundred lungs, and when one dies — and eventually one falls, black and hollow and cold — the ring around it cools, and the bazaar closes, and there is one more interval of nothing in the plain.

The place runs on its own decay and does not need anything from outside except water, which it has, and cold, which it has. It is industrial in the way that a termite mound is industrial: pressure, flow, work, vented heat, a factory with no engineer, scaled to the landscape, electrified, loud, and very old. It does not build. It metabolizes.

If you go, go knowing it will be wet and cold and noisy, that you will hear the plain before you see it — the murmur of the channels, the dispersed chord of the flues — and that after a while you will stop being able to tell which sounds are water and which are animals and which are the ground breathing. That is not a failure of attention. The distinction has worn away here with everything else.
