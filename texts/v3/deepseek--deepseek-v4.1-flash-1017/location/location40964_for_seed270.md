# The Pans

The plain goes on so long it stops reading as landscape and starts reading as a surface — a single poured sheet of pale mineral dust, the color of weak tea, settled and settled and settled again until there is nothing left to settle. It is cool here. Not cold; the air sits at the temperature of a cellar, and it moves, a slow dry wind that lifts the topmost millimeter of dust and carries it in long soft skeins an inch above the ground before setting it down again a little farther on. No water anywhere, not a damp seam or a salt stain or a cloud worth the name. The dust behaves like water instead: it pools, it flows around obstructions, it leaves tide lines on the crust where the wind has worried at it for a thousand years and given up.

Because the plain is old. It has been eroded into its final form and then left alone, and the form is flat. There are no hills to see, no ridge, no relief at all except — here and there, scattered like the marks of a dropped handful of coins — the pans.

That is the secret of this place. It is not the plain. The plain is just the lid. The pans are shallow sunken hollows, most of them no wider than a room, some small enough to lie down in, sunk a meter or two into the crust with soft rounded shoulders, as though something had pressed its thumb into the mineral while it was still soft and then gone away. You could walk across the flats for an hour and pass within three paces of one without seeing it, because the dust drifts level with the rim and the light is flat and even and forgiving. Then the ground goes quiet under your boot, and the sound changes, and you are standing at the lip of a bowl full of noise.

---

The pans are not empty. The pans are the opposite of empty.

Each one is a room packed to its walls with animals — layered, stacked, near-touching, so dense that the floor is not visible as floor, only as a churning texture, a living grain. There are flat coin-shaped things the size of a thumbnail that rasp the mineral crust in slow circles and make a continuous hiss like a hundred whetstones; there are long comb-bodied animals that drag themselves through the dust in files, sifting it, and there are small hard ones that cling to the pan walls in vertical curtains, so close together the wall looks shingled. Above them, mid-air, hangs a low cloud of flyers — not birds, nothing with feathers — flat-bodied, four-winged, moving in a slow circulating gyre, and their wings make a papery clatter that never stops. And under all of it, the floor of each pan breathes a low churring rumble, an almost-subsonic sawing, that you feel in the soles of your feet before you hear it.

It is loud. Not painfully, but persistently, in the way of a factory heard from the parking lot: a hundred overlapping processes, each one rhythmic, none of them in agreement. The pans are shaped like drums, and they amplify. A single animal scraping at one rim can be heard across the hollow; the whole bowl resonates, and the walls return the sound a half-beat late. In the evening, when the flyers descend and the combers lock into their slow files, the pans begin to answer each other — one hollow picking up a rhythm from the next and passing it along, a slow pulsing that runs across the flats in a chain, hollow to hollow to hollow, out past where you can see. Nobody knows what carries it. The animals never touch across the dust between.

---

The country is not empty of other things. Here and there the crust breaks the dust cover and you can see the bones of an industry: a stub of pipe, a rusted flange, a run of narrow rail half-swallowed by mineral. Once, a capped shaft with a headframe over it, the lattice gone to orange lace, and beside it a pump-jack still ticking over, slow and patient, its wheel turning in the dry wind because the wind is the only thing left to power it. The great works that scooped these pans out of the crust have been gone for a long time; the pans have outlived them, or the animals have. There is a heap of tailings, flattened and rounded by centuries of dust, indistinguishable now from a natural rise except that it is too regular. Everything human here has been reduced to its softest possible form, the way a stone in a river is reduced — nothing left but a shape that no longer catches on anything.

And the wildlife has moved in. This is what happens: the old works are the only relief in a flat world, so they gather the wind, and the wind gathers the dust, and the dust gathers the eaters of dust, and the eaters gather the eaten. The pump-jack's footing has a pan of its own, smaller, full of the flat rasping coins. The pipe stub is colonized by the wall-clinging curtains. The whole industrial remnant ticks and clatters with a life that has nothing to do with it, like a machine infested.

---

**What you should know, if you go down into one.**

The crust is thinner than it looks. It is a sinter, a mineral skin over loose dust, and it holds your weight the way a frozen puddle does — until it doesn't. The animals know where it is thick; watch where the curtains of wall-clingers are, and step there.

The life runs on the stone, not on light. There is no green here and nothing that wants the sun. The base of everything is the crust itself — the fine sulfate dust, the salt, the powdered mineral — eaten by the raspers and the combers, digested slow and cool, and everything above them eats them in turn. There are hunters in the pans: thin fast things the color of the dust, that go through the churn like a knife through a crowd and are gone before the crowd has closed. When the low churring stops for three breaths, that is a hunter passing, and every animal in the pan has gone still and flat at once, and the silence is louder than the noise was. Then the churring starts again, a little ragged, and the whole bowl resumes.

Do not stand at the lip at the change of the light. That is when the flyers come down, and there are a great many of them, and they come down all at once, and the pan goes from loud to a sound with no gaps in it at all.

---

Stand back from the hollow, out on the flats, and the plain is silent and pale and utterly still. The wind moves the dust. The pump-jack ticks. Somewhere out there, invisible, a chain of small rooms is pulsing in time, passing a rhythm along the crust, and if you put your ear to the ground you can hear it going away from you like a rumor. That is the whole of it: a vast dead sheet of mineral, cool and flat and finished, and under it, hidden in handfuls, the densest and noisiest and most vigorously alive little rooms you will ever be foolish enough to look into.
