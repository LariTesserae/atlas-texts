# The Lid and the Flat

The first wrong thing is the light, and it takes a while to notice it is wrong, because it is so even and so generous. There is no sun here. There is no direction the light comes from. It comes from above, from the whole of the ceiling at once — a dome of stone the better part of a mile across, crusted edge to edge with a pale mineral bloom that glows the color of a shell held up to a candle, bone-white with a faint warm undertone that is not warmth at all. The bloom is thin, a skin, a rime, and it grows. It grows a knuckle's depth in the span of a long life. In the places where it has been eaten away or scoured off, the raw stone underneath is dark, and those dark patches are the only shadows this world has — not shade thrown by objects, but holes in the light itself, drifting slowly across the ceiling like weather.

Beneath it lies the Flat. An old sea made this floor and then left, and came back, and left again, and in the leaving and returning it planed everything down to almost nothing. The floor of the cavern is a plain of pale compacted sediment, chalk-soft on top and hard beneath, that rises and falls by the height of a hand over a hundred paces. There are no hills. There are no cliffs, no boulders with edges. The walls of the cavern, where they can be seen at the rim of the light, are rounded and smooth as the inside of a bowl that has been licked clean, and the few stone outcrops on the floor have been worried down to humps and nubs. Everything here is in its last shape. The place has the look of something the water has been chewing on for a very long time and has very nearly finished.

It is cool. The air sits at the temperature of a cellar in early spring — cold enough that the water is always cold, mild enough that nobody dies of it. The air is heavy with damp and does not move much. Breath barely shows.

## The Breathing

Under the Flat there is still a sea, or the ghost of one, and it comes up through vents on no schedule anyone can predict. This is the Breathing. Sometimes the water rises a hand's depth, a skin of silver lying over the whole plain, and is gone by evening. Sometimes it comes chest-deep and fast, a wall of it moving across the floor without a wave on it, quiet as a table being set, and it will take a house and the house's stores and the goat-thing tethered outside it and be gone again by morning. Sometimes a year passes with no flood at all and the vents only sigh.

Between the floods there is rain. It condenses on the Lid continuously — the whole ceiling beaded, dripping, a fine drizzle that never quite stops and never quite becomes weather. It falls onto the chalky ground and is drunk by it. This is the damp that gets into everything: the bedding, the stored shell, the sinew cordage, the joints of the old. Pools gather in the low swells and stand there, cold and clear, with blind things in them no longer than a finger.

## The Town

There is a town out on the Flat, spread very wide, called by its people the Rack — though the word in their tongue means something closer to *the thing that holds the shape while it sets*. It is not much like a town as a surface-dweller would use the word. There are no streets. There are clusters — a dozen households here, a lone house a hundred paces on, a great empty plaza of wet stone with nothing in it at all, then another cluster, so that crossing the Rack takes the better part of a morning and most of the way you are walking through nothing.

The houses are low and wide and rounded, because a flood that meets a corner will take the corner off. They are built of the soft green-grey stone quarried from the cavern wall, of chalk blocks cut with chert and ground flat on quartz sand, of the ribs and shell-plates of the great sump-crabs, of hide scraped thin and stretched. Roofs are not solid. They are lattice and shell and hide, deliberately broken, so that the Lid's light comes through and the house is lit from above like a lantern. Nobody with any sense lives in a dark house here. It is not done.

And the Rack is layered, because it is built on itself. When a flood takes a house, the walls are salvaged down to the footings and the next house goes up on the same footings, and the footings of that house go on the footings of the one before, and the town has been doing this for so long that in places you walk on a terrace of old foundations a good height above the plain — a palimpsest of walls worn to stubs, each stub the ghost of a home whose name is gone. The oldest stones are smooth as river cobble. There are notches on them, cut deep, meant to be a record of something, and they have been worn past reading, and nobody knows what they said. The Rack's deepest memory is a set of blank stones.

The people work in stone and bone and hide. They knap chert for blades and scrapers, grind their axes on wet sand, twist sinew and gut into cord, and hammer the rare nodules of native copper they find in the sediment — cold-hammered, never melted, because they have almost no fire and hardly miss it, since the Lid gives them light for free. They keep a few fat-lamps for close work and burn rendered crab oil. They cook very little. They make a fermented paste of crushed crab-shell and cave-bloom and eat it with fish from the pools, and it is not good, and they are proud of it.

## What Lives Here

With no plants at all, the food web starts on the ceiling. The bloom is edible and mildly sweet, and it is grazed by lantern-ticks — thumbnail-sized, shelled, with a pale smear of light in them that makes them easy to find in the dark acres where the crust is gone. When a tick-swarm moves through, the Lid dims by a visible degree for a day or two, and the town notices, and the town is quieter than usual. Veil-moths drink the condensation and hang in grey curtains from the Lid, and they are the only thing here that flies. There are sump-crabs the size of a dog that ride the floods in and scavenge what the floods left, and there are eels in the vents, and blind fish, and the slow pale animals the town calls *the herd*, which are large and warm-bodied and have no eyes and drift across the Flat in loose lines, and the town follows the herd because the herd knows where the vents are going to open.

The fauna is everywhere and the place is still nearly silent. The chalk floor drinks sound. There is no echo anywhere in the cavern; a noise goes out from you and is simply absorbed, so that two people can stand thirty paces apart and find it easier to sign than to shout. The town speaks low, in a register that carries a few paces and no further. The children's games are hand-games. The flood warning is passed by touch from house to house along the foundations. Once or twice in a lifetime, something down in the vents makes a low groaning call, and it is the loudest sound anyone here will ever hear, and people stop where they are and listen to it and then go on with what they were doing.

## The Rule of Stillness

The bloom grows on anything that stays still. It is the strangest thing about the place and the most ordinary thing to anyone born there. Leave a tool on a shelf for a season and it comes back furred with light. Leave a rope coiled and it will be stiff with it. Leave a house unentered and the roof beams will be lit by the time you return. Stillness is how you get covered.

So the town is forever moving. Everything is turned, swept, handled, walked on, taken up and put down again. The foundations must be scrubbed every few years or they begin to glow and the notches (whatever they said) fill in. This is the labour that fills the Rack's days, and it is why a place so ancient and so settled is so relentlessly busy: a hundred small constant motions, all of them silent, all of them keeping the world from being quietly erased by light.

The dead are laid out on purpose. A body taken out past the last houses and left on the Flat will be lit over within a year — first a dusting at the edges, then a fur, then a soft full glow like a low fire, and then the shell of them is light and the light does not go out. There are acres of this. Walk out into the dark and you find them in ones and twos, glowing faint and patient, and some of them are very old and very bright and have been there since before the oldest readable stone. The town does not find this sad. It is the one thing here that does not get worn away.

## Weather

The Rack never knows what the year will be. Sometimes the sea stays down and the Flat is a dry pale waste for a decade and the pools shrink and the herd wanders far and the town grows fat and quarrelsome. Sometimes the floods come three times in a season and the lower clusters are abandoned and the town moves up onto its own terraces and the foundation stones are washed clean of a century of notches and no one minds much, because they were blank anyway. Sometimes a vent opens under a plaza in the night and by morning there is a cold spring where the plaza was, and a new pool, and by the next year a new cluster has gathered around it because the pool has fish. The light swells and dims. The crust is eaten and regrows. The floor is very slowly, very patiently, being made flatter still.

Nothing here is finished. Nothing here is going anywhere. The water keeps coming and going, the bloom keeps creeping over what holds still, and the town keeps sweeping it off with its hands, quietly, in the light.
