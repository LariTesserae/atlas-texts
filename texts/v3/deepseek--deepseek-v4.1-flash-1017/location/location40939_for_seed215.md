**The Sough**

The place is called the Sough, though that is more the sound the wind makes through its fresh fractures than a name anyone chose. It is a high plateau at the edge of a continental uplift, so vast that from its middle you can watch weather being made: cloud shadows cross for hours, rain arrives in slow curtains, and the horizon is less a line than a curvature. The air is thin and cold enough to fog your breath, but it is wet air. Water is always near, not gathered into mature rivers but held in shallow pans, seeps, joint-cracks, and the sweating skin of the stone. Every low place is a mirror. Every overhang has condensation beading on its underside. The Sough is wet without being drowned, cold without being frozen solid, and high without being a peak.

The terrain is raw. This is young stone—pale basalt, green-grey schist, mineral crusts—still in the first rough draft of geology. It has not had time to round. Scarps stand sharp, pressure ridges run in long parallel ribs, and the ground is split into slabs and stepped shelves. There is almost no soil, only grit in the cracks and a fine grey flour where water has worked at the rock for a season or two. Erosion has barely begun; there are no old valleys, no softened hills, no rounded river stones. When the plateau trembles, it is not ruin but construction. The Sough is a place where geology is still a verb.

Vegetation is sparse and stubborn: lichen crusts in orange and grey, wiregrass in wind-shadowed cracks, pale moss around seeps, dwarf willow no taller than a boot in the sheltered thermal pockets. It does not clothe the land. It annotates it. But fauna is abundant. Herds of small humped grazers move across the wet stone in loose lines, their coats dark with condensation. Rillflies rise in columns over the pans. Scarp swifts nest on the fresh ledges. Water shrews work the seep-lines, and something larger—lean, low, patient—follows the grazers at a distance. Life clusters around water and warmth, and because the water is everywhere near the surface, the total abundance is high even when any given view is mostly empty. The Sough is not crowded; it is populated in scatter.

The city is called Aumber, and it is a city the way a constellation is a shape. It is settlement-scale, organized, infrastructural—but spread across the plateau in a loose chain of neighborhoods, wellheads, cisterns, guide-rails, and weather masts. The buildings are accreted rather than excavated: pale faceted modules grown from local stone by slow printers, their surfaces channeled with capillaries, their roofs pitched to harvest condensation. Older modules are not ruins; they are absorbed into newer growth, decoupled when uplift cracks them, re-seeded a few meters away. The streets are shallow canals and raised stone walks. Water is managed like a public utility and treated like a hazard: every neighborhood has its seep, its meter, its overflow weir. Much of Aumber is emptiness threaded with infrastructure. You can walk a long time between presences and still be inside the city.

The technology is beyond modern but quiet. Phase-change lattices hold heat in the walls and keep the paths clear of ice. Weather spines draw water from the cold air and feed it into cisterns. Repair swarms move like mites over the capillary channels. Resonant anchors steady the fresh rock around foundations, and the stone printers wait for the plateau to finish its next small movement before laying another course. None of it is flashy. It behaves like plumbing, like weather, like animal husbandry. The rules of the world are plain: nothing here is old enough to be restored, so things are either growing or not yet grown. You do not dig a foundation; you seed one. You do not fight the uplift; you build joints into it. And you do not waste water, because water is both the reason the place is alive and the thing most likely to take a path apart.

Light is ordinary—grey daylight, cloud-shadow, the flat white of an overcast noon—and sound is mostly quiet. Water ticks in the cracks. Wind sighs through the wires and the ribbed stone. The grazers make soft lowing calls; the swifts are thin blades of sound; the repair swarms are silent. Even the city’s machines are muffled. What you notice is not noise but process: condensation forming, rain crossing, herds shifting, stone being laid, anchors adjusting, water finding its level. The Sough feels less like a place than a thing in the middle of becoming itself—vast, wet, cold, sparsely inhabited, and quietly busy everywhere you are not looking.
