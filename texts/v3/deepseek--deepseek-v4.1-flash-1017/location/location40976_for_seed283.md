## The Warm Floor

You feel it before you see anything: a wall of heat that is also a wall of water. Air at body temperature, saturated, so that stepping into it is like stepping into a mouth. The sweat does not dry. It gathers, beads, runs; every surface you touch leaves a wet print that stays. You are in a basin, low ground, a wide shallow sink of a valley whose bottom is nearly flat and nearly at the water table, and the whole floor of it is forest in the last stages of crowding itself out.

There is almost no light. Above, a closed canopy, and above that, apparently, a lid of cloud that has not broken in a long time. Noon here is a green-brown dusk, and dusk is only slightly darker. What light gets through comes down in columns through gaps where a giant has fallen, and those columns move as the canopy moves, so that the floor is stitched with wandering patches of dim gold. In them you can see the air: it is not clear, it is a suspension. Spores, pollen, fine dust of chewed leaf, midges, and a faint haze that is simply water choosing not to be rain.

**The wet.** There is no stream. Nothing flows. The water here does not run, it arrives and it stays, or it hangs. It hangs on every leaf in fat pendant drops that fall at intervals with a sound like a clock in another room. It pools in the basins of buttress roots, in old treefall hollows, in the prints of your own feet, which fill and become tiny ponds and are colonized before evening. These pools are tea-colored and still, their surfaces filmed with pollen and the bodies of small drowned things, and they are warm as a bath. Rain comes not as weather but as event, sudden and total, twenty minutes of it, and then an hour of dripping and steaming while the air gets heavier rather than lighter. The rest of the moisture comes by condensation, quietly, all night, so that every surface in the morning is furred with wet.

**The packed.** Nothing here has room. The ground is not ground but a deep soft mat of litter and root and old wood, spongy to the foot, and your steps make no sound at all. Roots run over the surface because the surface is where the air is; they cross each other, they graft, they form a lattice you must step over. Trunks stand close enough that you must turn sideways. Between them, lianas, and between the lianas, everything else: ferns in the crotches, aroids climbing, moss on the north of every stem, bromeliads holding their own private reservoirs with their own private communities in them. The leaves are enormous, waxy, and all of them come to a drip-tip, so that the whole understory is a slow irregular rain that never quite stops. It smells of wet earth and crushed green and, underneath, something yeastlike and sharp — a smell like a bakery left too long in the heat.

**The fauna.** This is the overwhelming thing, and the strange part is how quiet it is. Nothing here announces itself. But there is no surface without life on it, no dead thing that stays dead, no crumb that goes uneaten. Millipedes in slow armored processions over the roots. Beetles in everything. A fine constant tickle on the back of your neck that is not wind. Tree frogs, small and brown, in every water-holding leaf, silent until the rain and then not silent at all. Something larger moves in the canopy every few minutes and you never see it; you hear the branch, the rustle, the cascade of drops displaced, a call like a hinge.

And the ants — the ants are the industry of the place. Columns of leaf-cutters run on highways they have maintained for decades, worn smooth and slightly sunken and swept clean, and these roads cross the floor in every direction and go up into the trees. The columns carry their green confetti, a continuous rustling river of it, and where two roads cross there are junctions with traffic rules. Their nests are low mounds, and they breathe: dozens of openings, and out of them a warm exhalation, because inside, the fungus gardens are composting, and the whole colony is a furnace. In the darkness under the mounds, on a scale you cannot see, millions of workers are cutting and chewing and planting and weeding, feeding a crop that feeds them, regulating its temperature, removing its waste to heaps that steam faintly and stink sweetly and are themselves farmed by other things. There is no structure here that was built. There is no tool, no wall, no post, no mark of hands. But this is a factory floor and it has been running without interruption for longer than anything you know.

**The rules.** Nothing accumulates except the living. Fall a tree and it is gone in a season — chewed into the economy, distributed, converted, and the gap it leaves is a column of light and a scramble. The dead do not lie here. Only the alive pile up, and they pile until they choke each other, and then something falls and lets a little light in and the choking starts again somewhere else. This is an old, settled system; it has found its form and holds it. The channels in the litter are old channels. The ant roads are old roads. The place is not growing or declining, it is simply running, at temperature, processing.

**Time and sound.** Change comes in increments too slow to watch. A mound gains a hand's width in a decade. A hollow fills and becomes a root mat and then a hummock, over years. When the wind moves the canopy, the sound is enormous and then it stops and the quiet comes back — the drip, the distant hinge-call, and, if you put your ear against a trunk, a faint continuous rasping, the sound of chewing, everywhere under everything, so low you had mistaken it for the sound of your own blood.

Stay long enough and you stop being a visitor. The mosquitoes find the thin skin at your wrists. Something runs a thread from your shoulder to a branch, in an hour, because you are a surface now, and here every surface is used.
