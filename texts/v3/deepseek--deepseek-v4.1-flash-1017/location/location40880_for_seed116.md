# The Warm Shaft

Somewhere above the cloud line, inside a mountain that has otherwise been eaten down to a rounded shoulder of rock, there is a door. It is not marked. It is a slab of fitted stone, worn so smooth by centuries of driven rain that it reads as bedrock, and behind it a stair spirals down through warm, dripping dark into a chamber that should not exist at this elevation and does not, strictly speaking, belong to the mountain at all.

The chamber is a single vertical shaft, perhaps forty meters across, and it is full of water. Not flooded in the sense of disaster — flooded in the sense of settled, of a thing that has been full for a very long time and has made its peace with it. The water is warm, blood-warm, faintly mineral, and clear enough that from the surface you can see all the way down to where the shaft stops being a shaft and becomes a bowl.

And that is the first thing you notice: the bowl. Everything down there has been rounded. The place was built once — you can tell, because there is nothing natural about it, not one grain. The walls are dressed stone set in courses, the floor was tiled, there were stairs and landings and railings, and there were machines. But the water has been at it for an age, and the age has won. The tile is a soft mosaic of stubs. The stairs are ramps. The railings are knuckles. The whole built thing has slumped into the shape water prefers, which is a bowl, and the bowl is lined with a warm green fur of weed.

**The water.** It moves. There is a current, slow and deliberate, rising up one side of the shaft and sinking down the other, and it carries a fine silt of worn stone that catches the light. The light comes from a slit at the top, a constructed aperture in the capstone, and it drops through the water in a single column that sways as the surface breathes. The surface is not still — it is being fed, constantly, by sheets of water sliding down the walls, and it is being drained, constantly, by something below that you can hear but not see. Rain gets in. The mountain's rain, funneled through a hundred small channels cut for exactly this purpose, so that even now, with no one to operate it, the machine of the place still catches its weather.

**The machines.** They are still running. This is the strange heart of it. In the lower third of the shaft, half-swallowed by weed and rounded almost past recognition, the pumps turn. They are iron and they are ancient and they are loud — a deep, wet, rhythmic knocking that you feel in the sternum before you hear it, and over it the hiss and sluice of water being lifted and dropped, lifted and dropped. The noise fills the whole chamber; there is no quiet corner of it. Gears the size of doors grind against each other with a sound like a slow avalanche. Somewhere a belt, long since grown a pelt of algae, slaps and slaps. The pumps do not know they are ruins. They have been doing their one job since before the mountain was a mountain, and the job is this: keep the water moving, keep it warm, keep it from going still. If they stopped, the place would stagnate and die in a season. They have not stopped.

**The life.** The warmth and the motion have made a garden of it. The weed is everywhere the light reaches, and below the light there are pale things that do not need it — fans and fronds and soft corals of a kind that have no business this high above the sea. Fish live here, small and silver and unhurried, and they are not many; the water is too big for them, and they keep to the columns of light in loose, scattered schools. Eels coil in the dead machinery. Crabs the color of the tile pick their way across the bowl floor in ones and twos, never crowds. And on the walls, at the waterline, there is a band of small hard creatures that open and close with the rhythm of the pumps, as if the knocking were a tide.

Nothing here is dense. That is the other thing you notice. For all the life and all the noise, the place is mostly water and mostly empty. There is a great deal of room between one fish and the next, one machine and the next, one thought and the next. You could swim the whole width of it in a minute and a half and pass four living things.

**The rules.** The place has a few, and they are simple. The water stays warm or the machines fail; the machines fail or the water stays still; the water stays still and the garden dies and the bowl fills with silt and the whole thing becomes, at last, just rock again. So the rule of the world is this: keep moving. Everything here obeys it. The pumps obey it without knowing. The weed leans into the current. The eels ride it. Even the stone obeys, giving itself over grain by grain to the water, settling into the final shape it will hold until the light at the top closes and the last of the knocking stops.

Stand on the lip of the aperture, in the rain, with the warm air coming up at you and the sound of it rolling out of the dark like surf, and you would not guess any of this. It is the size of a room and it is older than the mountain, and it is entirely made, and it is still, after everything, at work.
