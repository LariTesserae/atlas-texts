The first thing is the wind. It is not a wind that blows; it is a wind that *exists*, a permanent, roaring inhabitant of this place. It scours the high plateau, a sound so vast and constant it ceases to be a noise and becomes the medium through which everything else is perceived. To speak here, you must shout into the gale and feel the words torn from your lips. The wind is the deafening silence of this world.

The plateau itself is a monument to a slow, patient violence. It is a high, sheer table of pale, layered rock, thrust up so far that the sky above feels thin and close. The stone is a library of deep time, written in strata of bleached bone-white, rust-red, and a faint, bruised purple. Erosion is the sole artist here, and it has been working for eons. The edges of the plateau are not cliffs but sculptures: balanced stones the size of houses, arches that frame the empty sky, and deep fissures where the wind has been sharpened by sand into a blade. Everything is settled, weathered, and in its final, patient form. Nothing is being built; everything is being slowly, beautifully unmade.

Water is a ghost here. It exists, but as a fleeting presence. The air itself is damp, a cool, thin vapor that clings to the stone and condenses in the deep, shaded cracks each night. By midday, under the bright, hard light of a sun that feels too close, the moisture is gone, save for the dark, weeping stains it leaves behind. There are no streams, only the occasional, startling pocket of water at the bottom of a deep pothole, cold and black and perfectly still, reflecting a circle of the harsh sky. Life clings to these rare wells.

The vegetation is sparse, a lesson in stubbornness. Nothing grows large. The flora is a low, tough carpet of things that look more mineral than plant: dry, wiry mosses the color of ash, lichens that crust the rocks in pale green and orange maps, and small, tough shrubs that huddle in the deepest fissures, their leaves reduced to waxy, protective needles. It is a world of miniature forests, of life that has learned to be small and patient and to hold on.

And yet, the place is not empty. It teems. The fauna is the true master of this high, loud, bright place. The most abundant life is in the air. Great, silver-winged insects, their bodies like polished metal, surf the roaring currents, their own hum a low counterpoint to the wind's scream. They move in vast, swirling clouds, catching the light and throwing it back in a million tiny flashes. There are birds, too, but they are not songbirds. They are gliders and soarers, with wingspans like sails, their calls sharp, piercing shrieks that are the only sounds that can cut through the wind. They nest in the most inaccessible crags, their colonies marked by a constant, raucous clamor. On the ground, life is furtive. Small, quick things scuttle between rocks—lizards with skin the texture and color of the stone, insects that are flattened and low to the ground. It is an ecology of the edge, of creatures perfectly tuned to a world of extreme height, loud noise, and relentless, beautiful light.

The world here operates on a simple, brutal rule: endurance is everything. The light is a physical force, bleaching color and casting razor-sharp shadows. The wind is a constant drain on energy and warmth. The coolness is a deep, internal chill that the bright sun only barely touches. Time feels different—not frozen, but stretched, measured in the slow, geological creep of the rock's erosion and the frantic, brief life-cycles of the airborne insects. There is no past or future, only this eternal, screaming, sun-drenched present. A person here is a temporary, fragile anomaly, a visitor to a place that is utterly, beautifully indifferent to their presence.
