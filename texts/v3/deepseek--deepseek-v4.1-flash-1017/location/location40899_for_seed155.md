The wind is the first thing, coming up the long slope from the lower valleys in slow, buffeting exhales. It carries a smell that is hard to name: wet stone, crushed stems, hot metal cooling, and the green reek of sap, all layered. The sky above is broad and pale, scoured, taking most of the light and reflecting it down flat on the land. At this height, the air is thin enough to feel like something you must chew. You are on a plateau, the top of an old world, and it goes on and on.

The ground is not what you'd expect. Underfoot, the ancient bedrock has been worn to a gentle, almost sculpted series of undulations, like the palimpsest of old hills, sanded down to their cores. But you can't see much of it. The substrate is green. Not a carpet, but a lattice. Mosses and lichens weld the cracked granite into a kind of cushion. Between the hummocks, low, woody shrubs—wind-twisted and patient—push up. And everywhere, the architecture of the plants: immense, grey-barked trees that have grown into each other over centuries, their roots braided over the rock like fingers. Their canopies are not a continuous ceiling; they are islands of shade that leave great gaps to the pale sky. Here and there, a vast, solitary tree stands alone in a bowl of its own leaf-fall, an island of specific, dense life. The vegetation doesn't carpet so much as it *occupies*, each cluster a deliberate, ancient statement in a landscape of open, cool air.

The town is built into this, not on it. It's a place of stairs and switchbacks, of timber catwalks bolted to the living trunks of the great trees. Buildings of stone and old, dark timber are wedged into the spaces between root-masses as huge as houses, their doors opening onto terraces of cultivated moss and tubers. The industrial layer is here, layered over the settlement like a second skin: riveted iron pipes that carry hot water from a central plant snake alongside the wooden walkways, dripping condensation onto the moss below. You can hear them: a constant, low thrumming and ticking, the sound of a pressured system breathing. A long, rusted conveyor bridge spans a gully between two great trees, its belt long since stilled, but the structure itself now serves as a trellis for a flowering vine that has buried half the mechanism in bloom. The air is filled with a thousand small, mechanical noises: the hiss of steam, the periodic clank of some slow, unseen engine, the grinding of a great wheel turning somewhere below, driving a lift. It's not the sound of frantic industry; it's the sound of a place that has settled into a rhythm of maintenance, a slow, careful husbandry of machinery and plant.

Water is a presence more than a feature. It hangs in the air as a persistent, cool dampness. The air condenses on every metal surface, causing the iron pipes to gleam darkly and the moss to squelch underfoot. Intermittent rain is driven across the plateau in sudden, silver veils, then stops, leaving everything dripping and bright. Small, cold pools have formed in the hollows of the ancient stone, and each is a small, self-contained world of silvery insects and reeds. The sound of water is everywhere: the drip from the canopies, the trickle of channels that have been carved through the living rock to guide runoff into rusted cisterns, and the occasional, startling roar of a great, hidden sluice being opened somewhere in the town, followed by a torrent of water and a burst of released steam.

There are almost no animals here. A few birds, perhaps, but they are small and silent and rare. The place is insect-song and rustling leaf and the constant, humming, ticking sounds of its own industry. The ecology is a three-way treaty between the ancient vegetable, the archaic mechanical, and the human, all at a cool truce. The rules of the world are simple enough: you do not build what you cannot repair, you do not cut what will grow back, and you listen to the sounds the place makes. The most important rule: you do not let the pressure drop, and you do not let the fire go out.

Because that is the weirdness, the small, persistent crack in reality here. The place is loud. Not with voices, but with a sound that is more than the sum of its parts. The trees, you see, are not just hosts. They are conductors. The metal pipes, driven deep into the ancient root systems, do not just carry water. They feed something back. The trees have learned the patterns of the machinery; they have absorbed and now express its rhythms. When the central plant is working at its peak, the leaves of the great trees vibrate with a hum that is an octave lower than the engines, a subsonic basso that you feel in your sternum. The very wood of the buildings, the living timber in the walls, sighs with the sound of distant gears. The people don't talk about it. They've learned to live with the impression that the place is thinking, a slow, vegetable thought in an industrial vocabulary, and that the whole plateau is one vast, settled, and slightly dreaming machine.
