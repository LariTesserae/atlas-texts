You hear it before you see it. Coming down the slope through the scrub, there's a low continuous roar that seems to come out of the ground itself — not the flat noise of a river but a layered thing, water and metal and something softer underneath, like breath. Then the ground gives way and the bowl opens under you.

It's a collapse, a limestone sink about two hundred and fifty paces across and sixty deep, and it has been here long enough that its shape looks intended. The walls have sloughed into gentle terraces; the rim is rounded and mossy; the water has found its channels centuries ago and keeps to them. Nothing about the place is raw. It has settled into itself the way a chair settles into a floor.

The first thing that strikes you is the roof. There is no sky to speak of. Vines and trees have grown in from the rim and closed over the top, and the canopy is thick enough that from above it looks like a green lid. Only near the eastern lip is there a gap — a slot maybe four paces wide where a grey-white light comes down and lands on the water. Everywhere else you are inside a room.

The second thing is the rain.

The bowl makes its own weather. Warm vapour rises off the pools and the wet plant mass, hits the cooler underside of the canopy, and condenses. It comes back down as a fine warm drizzle, almost continuous, so that nothing in the bowl is ever dry. Every leaf has a bead on its tip. Every rope is dark with water. The walls weep in long vertical stains. The sound of it is a constant patter, thousands of small impacts, and under that the bigger sounds: streams running down the terraces, a fall on the western side that drops the last eight metres into a plunge pool, gutters and spouts and overflows. The whole basin is a slow, patient still.

And it is full of plants, completely — there is no bare ground anywhere. The floor is a raft of root and waterlogged litter, spongy underfoot, that you can feel giving slightly with each step. From the rim hang curtains of root, some as thick as a man's thigh, dropping straight down to the floor and rooting there, so the bowl is colonised by columns. Between them, everything else: enormous pale aroids with leaves a pace across, ferns in layered shelves, mosses in every colour from bone to rust to a deep wet liver, vines that cross and recross until the middle air is a lattice. The plants are not decorative. They are the structure of the place, walls and floors and ceilings, and the town is built into them rather than among them.

The town occupies the northern third of the floor and climbs the lower wall in four or five stacked terraces. It is small — you could walk it end to end in a few minutes — but it is packed. A hundred-odd structures pressed into a space that would hold thirty elsewhere. Houses on stilts over the wet, houses wedged between root columns, houses built on the roofs of other houses, connected by stairs and ladders and rope catwalks that sway when you cross them. The materials are timber and salvaged metal and woven frond, all of it dark with damp, all of it patched, all of it in a state of being slowly repaired. Roofs are pitched steeply and all of them feed gutters. There are more gutters than there are walls. Water is caught at the eave and the root and the leaf and led down ceramic pipes and open channels to cisterns, and from the cisterns it goes to the works.

Because that is what the place is for. The plant mass weeps. All of it, constantly, a thin sweet sap that beads out of the bark and the cut stems and the leaf joints — not fast, but there is so much plant and so much time that the yield is enormous. Every surface in the bowl is a collecting surface. The gutters lead to channels, the channels to vats, the vats to the boiler house in the centre of the town, where two water wheels in the western fall drive the pumps and the crushers, and the sap is boiled down slow into a dark resin and poured into moulds and hauled up to the rim on a bucket hoist. The boilers are the loudest thing in the bowl. They hiss and knock and breathe, and the steam goes up into the canopy and becomes rain and comes back down, which the people here find funny in a tired sort of way.

Light is poor. The slot of sky gives one column of grey daylight that moves across the floor over the course of the day and is the closest thing to a clock. Everything else is filtered green and brown through the canopy, dim enough that lamps are kept lit in the lower terraces all day. The lamps are small and yellow and they reflect in the wet planks. In the afternoons the boiler house puts out a low orange glow that catches the steam and turns the middle of the bowl into something you can't quite see through.

The air is warm — thirty degrees, maybe more near the works — and so wet it feels like a hand on your face. You sweat and the sweat does not dry. Nothing dries. Cloth rots in a season, leather in two, iron needs painting every year. The people here dress in little and replace it often.

And nothing else lives here. This is the thing newcomers notice within a day and stop mentioning within a week. There are no insects over the standing water. No birds in the canopy, though it would hold thousands. No rats in the stores, no fish in the pools, no lizards on the warm stones. Nothing with a nervous system, except the people. They do not keep animals and they do not bring them. Ask why and you get a shrug and a guess — the sap, the air, the mineral in the water — but nobody has tested it and nobody much wants to. The bowl is a place for plants and machinery and people, in that order.

What sound there is, is all of those. The rain, the falls, the channels, the wheels, the boilers, the creak of the root columns taking on weight, the occasional deep complaint of a vine shifting, the knock of a loose shutter in the town. It does not stop. It is not unpleasant. It is the noise of a slow engine that has been running a long time and will keep running, and it presses in on you from the closed green ceiling until you stop hearing it as sound and start hearing it as the place itself.

Nothing here happens quickly. The plants grow, the sap runs, the water cycles up and comes down, the town is repaired piece by piece, the terraces shift a hand's width a decade. The wheels turn. The resin goes up in the bucket. The bowl keeps its shape and its weather and its one slot of light, and it will be very much like this in fifty years, and it was very much like this fifty years ago, and that is the whole of it.
