# The Warm Room

You find it by your face. Somewhere above the treeline, in a country of grey scree and wind, there is a fissure that breathes out air warmer than the air around it — a slow exhalation that smells of wet stone and something faintly sweet, like heated milk. The fissure is not impressive. It is a black slot in a slab, wide as your two hands, and the scree around it is bare and fresh and has never been walked on by anything with feet. There is no path. There is no reason to be here. Then you put your arm in and the warmth closes over it like water.

The entry is a crawl and then a squeeze and then a drop of perhaps a body's length into a room. It is small. Two rooms of a small house, laid on their side. You can stand, but you will not want to; you will crouch, and the ceiling will be close enough to touch, and your hands will find it warm.

It is entirely dark. Not dark as a moonless night is dark, which has texture and depth and a blue suggestion at the edges — this is a darkness with no floor to it. Your eyes will not adjust. They will keep trying for a long time, and then they will stop, and after that you will be inside your other senses, which is where the room wants you.

---

Everything is wet and nothing runs.

Water stands in shallow lenses across the floor, skin-deep, so still it reads as glass or as polished stone, and the stone beneath reads as water. The air is saturated — you are breathing a warm fog that condenses on your arms and does not drip off. Beads form on every surface and stay. They do not gather, do not gather weight, do not fall. There is no trickle anywhere. In a place this wet you expect, without knowing you expect it, the sound of moving water; and its absence is the loudest thing in the room. It is the sound of held breath.

The warmth is body-warm, or a shade more. It does not come from a vent you can find, or a spring, or a crack in the floor. It is simply the temperature of the room, in every direction, near the ceiling and in the corners, at the entry and at the far wall. There is no gradient. Hold a hand out and turn slowly and you will feel no change. Nothing here is warmer than anything else.

---

The stone is new. That is the thing your hands keep reporting and your mind keeps refusing. There is no roundness in it. No grain, no soil, no worn edge. The floor is a crust of pale nodules, each one a small sharp bloom the size of a knuckle, and they are packed so close that your palm rests on a dozen at once and each presses its own point into you. From the ceiling hang mineral fingers, thin as straws, some long enough to brush your hair. They are wet and glassy and flawless. Along the walls the rock has grown in needles, in threads, in small brittle combs. Nothing has been smoothed by water or wind or time, because nothing here has ever moved. It is geology at the moment of deciding to be geology. An egg of stone, half-made, and warm.

*Erosion* is a word from another world. Here is the other word: accretion. It is happening now. It is happening so slowly that a finger of mineral takes a human life to lengthen by the width of a fingernail — but it is happening, you can feel it as pressure, as the room very gently closing. It is the only motion permitted.

---

Life is here, thinly, in the warm dark.

On the ceiling, in the wet, there are threads — pale, colorless, rootless things that might be algae or might be the first cousin of algae, feeding on the heat and the mineral and nothing else. They are sparse. You will find them by touch, a soft nap on the hard stone, and only in three or four patches.

Animals are rare and you may not meet one. There are small snails with shells you can see through, that live their whole lives on one nodule and do not travel. There are mites in the condensation. Something with more legs than you would like lives in the space behind the low wall, and it is not interested in you, and it moves perhaps once a day. Nothing here is fast. Nothing here is hungry in the way outside things are hungry. They are all in equilibrium, all at rest, all exactly as they were.

---

People have been here. Not many, not often — but over a long time, one at a time, each leaving one thing.

There is a low wall of stacked stones at the back, knee-high, hand-sized pieces balanced without mortar, and it is not a wall so much as a bench, or a threshold, or the beginning of something a person meant to finish. There is a cairn beside it, four stones, the top one set slightly off-center. There is a ledge with a black stain of soot on it and a burned-down stub of a thing that was never quite a candle. There are pegs of wood driven into cracks — a dozen of them, some rotted to splinters and some perfectly sound, and you cannot tell by feel which are which except by how they give. There are small bowls of unfired clay. There are knotted cords. There is a bundle of sticks tied with sinew. There is a single worn scraper of some dark stone, set down flat and squarely, the way you set down a tool you intend to pick up again.

Dense — that is the word for it. The room is crowded, but not with space taken up; crowded the way a nest is crowded, everything laid against everything else, layer on layer, each object within a hand's reach of the next. And every one of them is exactly as it was left.

---

This is the room's rule, and once you understand it you will want to sit down.

Nothing here changes. A bowl of water set on the floor a hundred years ago is still full, still level, still sweet. A wooden peg does not dry out and does not rot. A cord does not slacken. Fire will burn if you make it — the warmth does not forbid it — but the ash will lie where it falls and the soot will not spread and the charcoal will stay sharp. Your own sweat does not dry on your skin. You will not feel thirsty in the way you expect to feel thirsty; the wet air gives you back what you lose.

And the stone grows. That is the single permitted change, and it is so slow that it is less a change than a fact. It grows *around* things. The wall of stacked stones is slightly furred with new mineral at its base. The oldest pegs are half-swallowed, their heads emerging from the wall as though from a pillow. In another age the bowls will be gone, and the cairn will be a lump, and the room will have closed its hand gently over every small thing anyone ever set down. Not a violence. A keeping. It does not destroy what it takes; it takes it into itself the way warm wax takes a fingerprint.

---

Sound: almost none. Your breath. Your pulse. The small wet click of your tongue. There is no drip, no trickle, no echo worth the name — the room is too small and too soft-surfaced and the damp air eats sound. If you speak, your voice comes back flat and close, like speaking into cloth. There may be a hum, so low you feel it in the sternum rather than hear it; it may be the warmth itself, or the room's held breath, or your own blood. It never varies. It was the same before you came.

And it will be the same after. This is the thing that will follow you out: that you could leave, and the room would not notice, and nothing in it would shift by a hair because you had been there. It is not indifferent. It is simply complete — a system that found its balance a very long time ago and has held it without effort since, warm and wet and dark and full, an embryo of stone that is in no hurry to be born.

Take nothing. Not because it is forbidden, but because the moment a thing leaves the fissure the wind will find it, and it will do all at once the hundred years of weathering it was spared, and crumble in your hand like a word half-spoken.
