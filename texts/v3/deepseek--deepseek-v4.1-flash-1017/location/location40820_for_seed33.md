# The Comb-Shelf at Phrase's End

Begin with the sound, because there is almost none, and what there is will tell you where you are before the eye consents to the scale of it.

The sound is a low, continuous groan — two tones, a fifth apart, rising and holding and falling away — like something enormous being asked to remember its own shape. It comes from the stone. The whole ridge, which is not a ridge but a city in the shape of a ridge, is settling. These are not the creaks of a new structure. This is the elastic give of mineral that has been holding a position for a very long time and has decided, slowly, to hold it slightly differently.

You are on the Comb-Shelf: a hundred and forty kilometres of wind-scoured stone teeth, each tooth a hundred to six hundred metres tall, each one worn by the dry air into fins and lobes and long vertical channels, so that from any point on the shelf the world is a corridor of standing combs with gaps between them. The air here is thin and cold — not freezing, but the kind of cold that lives at altitude, that smells of clean dust and nothing else, because there is nothing else to smell. Above you the sky is the colour of a knife's flat: not dark, not bright, a settled dimness that never fully resolves into day. There is a moon, or something moon-sized, and it moves too slowly to watch. Shadows here travel in minutes-per-metre. You can see the dark edge of one crossing a spar beside you and follow it with your hand.

Water is a rumour the place has never heard. The stone is salt-glazed and mineral; the wells in the low channels are dry and have been dry since before the oldest structure was built, and the oldest structure is indistinguishable from the rock it was built from. The builders, whoever they were, used the ground itself as their material, carving and stacking in the same gesture, so that the architecture is erosion and the erosion is architecture. The combs are hollow. In the larger ones, chambers open off the vertical channels in ranks, storey after storey, each chamber a shallow oval with a sill worn smooth by centuries of something entering and leaving. No tools, no metal, no marks of making except the shape. The marks of making would have worn away, if there had been any. There have never been any.

Between the combs runs the shelf floor: pale grit, mineral sand, chips of stone in sorted lanes by size, because though there is no water there is wind, and the wind does here what rivers do elsewhere — it grades, it polishes, it leaves its deposits in fans and dunes the colour of bone. The wind is thin and almost steady and carries no grain you can feel, only the faint abrasion of something too fine to be sand. Underfoot the grit is crunchy at the very surface and packed hard an inch down; walk it and you leave a line of prints, and the prints persist, because nothing hurries to fill them. Ahead of you, crossing the shelf, is a path worn into the grit by something that has come this way perhaps ten thousand times, perhaps for longer.

You are being watched, in the way of this place — rarely, and not as a rule, but *actually*, from time to time, by something.

The fauna is thin and strange, and it gives the shelf its only motion. Droppers: small globular things the size of a heart, translucent and bluish, that cling to the undersides of the overhangs and let go when the wind's tone deepens, falling forty metres at a time and vanishing into the grit where they land — not dying, the shelf's own logic seems to say, but *depositing*, and later, somewhere among the mineral lanes, a new dropper will be found attached and grown. Finches-that-are-not-finches: they are small quick shapes that are visible only as displacement, because they are the colour of the air and their bodies catch the light at angles that the eye refuses to resolve. When one moves, something like a smear of heat-shimmer crosses the gap between combs, and a faint sound — closer to a struck glass than a call — hangs for a moment and is gone. There are also the walkers, which is what the path belongs to: tall, slow, spindle-limbed things that are more leg than body, each limb terminating in a pad that leaves the identical print. They come up the shelf at intervals of days, always in single file, always on the same line, and though you will not see one if you watch the path, you will see one if you watch the sky above the shelf's far end, because they are tall enough to break the line between dimness and stone. What they eat, no one knows. There is nothing to eat. The shelf does not consider this a problem.

The wind's two-tone groan is not constant. When it fades, the quiet it leaves is of a different order — the quiet of an interior, of something that has drawn breath. In those pauses you can hear your own pulse, and the faint tick of the grit settling, and, very rarely, from deep inside a comb, a sound like a single syllable being said and re-said, worn down by repetition until it is only the shape of the mouth that made it. The builders, if they were builders, left their language in the stone's grain: the vertical channels are striated in a pattern that is not geological, fine horizontal bands at irregular intervals, and travellers who have counted them say the bands correspond, in some way nobody has verified twice the same way, to the shape of the combs and the reach of their shadows. I have not counted. I have stood under a comb at the moment its shadow touched the ground and felt the air change on my face, a pressure like a held note, and I have no explanation for it and neither does the shelf.

The weirdness here is not spectacle. It is the persistent sense that the rules have been *agreed to* and could be un-agreed, and that the shelf is aware of the agreement and is patient about it. Distances do not stay put in the way they should: from one point on the shelf, a comb two hundred metres away can read as close enough to touch; walk toward it and the intervening gaps multiply, and the comb recedes, and you arrive where you began, or at a comb identical to it, or at a comb that was not there when you set out. The moon-sized thing above does not set. It is not in the same place at the same time twice, but it is always at the same angle above the shelf's end. The path is always worn the same amount. The prints you leave behind you when you turn to look are sometimes not yours, and are sometimes older than the time you have been here.

Yet nothing in this is hostile. The shelf is not a trap; it is a *state*, long-established, weathered into its own equilibrium, and you are small within it and it does not mind you. The emptiness is the point. Between the combs the spaces are vast and the presences in them are few, and each presence is a whole, as if density had been exchanged for size — the droppers, the smear-birds, the walkers, the rare hum of the syllable, and, at intervals you cannot predict, something that might be a person going the other way on the path, tall and slow and spindle-limbed, who will not look at you and will not stop, and whose prints will not match your own.

And there is one more thing, which is why I began with the sound. Somewhere along the shelf, in a comb higher than the rest, a hollow has been left open to the wind, and the wind plays it. The two-toned groan is that hollow. It has been sounding since before the first path was worn and it will sound after the last path is forgotten, and the walkers orient to it — you can watch one far off, mid-stride, adjust its heading by half a degree when the tone falls. It is the nearest thing here to an instrument, and it was not made. The stone was carved around it and left. Whatever the shelf is for, the phrase is still being spoken; the mouth has simply worn to its shape.
