The place is called the Kettle by the people who winter there, though no one agrees on whether it’s named for the sound the ice makes, the shape of the terrain, or the way the water moves.

## Terrain

A high basin, roughly the size of a cathedral’s footprint or a bit more — you could walk its long axis in ten minutes if the ground let you, which it mostly doesn’t. The Kettle sits near a summit, a catch-basin in ancient rock that has been eroding longer than anyone’s counting. Every surface has settled into its own shape: the stone is rounded, scooped, pocked with basins and channels that hold water like a hundred small bowls. The erosion is not violent — it’s the slow, thorough erosion of a thing that has been getting rained on for a very long time and has stopped resisting. The rock has learned the shape of water.

Water is everywhere and always moving. A stream enters from the northwest — a thin, fast thing off the glacier-melt above — and it doesn’t stay in a channel. It braids across the basin floor, rejoining itself, splitting around drumlins of darker stone. The water level rises and falls by the hour. Pools that were ankle-deep become waist-deep when a melt-surge comes down. Geysers are not quite the word — there are vents where water pulses up from below in rhythmic surges, hot enough to steam, and everything downstream of a vent is warm and green while everything upstream is frozen. The basin floor is a patchwork of temperatures: scald and rime, sometimes under the same hand’s breadth of sky.

The ground between the water is mostly bare. Moss in the wet cracks, lichen in the dry ones, a few hard grasses with roots deeper than the plants are tall. Sparse vegetation is the rule, but it’s not absent — it’s just that the basin is too restless for anything to get established. Things grow, then get flooded, then grow again. The plants that survive are the ones that don’t mind being interrupted.

## Atmosphere

Cold, but not the dead cold of a still winter. The cold moves — it comes off the ice above and the water below and the wind carries it around like something with a current to it. Cold in the way a springhouse is cold: bracing, clean, smelling of wet stone and mineral. The air is dense with moisture. Breathe in and you can feel it going into your lungs heavy. Breathe out and you can see it. The whole basin condenses; every stone is beaded, every surface is damp or frozen depending on which side of which vent it sits.

The light is enormous. It comes down hard and direct and gets doubled by every pool, so the whole floor of the Kettle is a confusion of bright — sky in the water, water throwing sky back up, steam catching and scattering it. There are moments, in the surge, when the basin is all glare and you can’t tell water from light. Probably why there’s no metalwork here worth the name — nobody can see well enough to work it, and there isn’t the quiet to concentrate anyway.

## Sound

Almost none, and the almost is strange. The place should be roaring — water moving like that, vents pulsing — but it isn’t. The rock swallows sound. The current goes over rounded stone, not boulders; the vents bubble and hiss but the sound doesn’t carry. There is a constant hush, the sound of a great deal of water moving quietly, with occasional louder moments: a surge, a block of ice calving somewhere above, the flat crack of a frozen pool splitting. And underneath, the most characteristic sound, felt more than heard — a low, slow, human-hum of pressure moving under the basin. The water that goes down comes up somewhere else, and the somewhere else is right here too.

## Fauna

Life, everywhere — few things but many of them, dense on the ground and in the air. The Kettle fills with animals that cluster, live at close quarters, exploit every pocket of warmth and every dry shelf. On the vent-edges there are mats of crustacean-thing, something like a wristwatch-sized shrimp with a pale soft shell, that lives in the hot seeps and crawls in such numbers that the rock looks alive. They graze bacteria off stone and are grazed in turn by birds — greyish, hoarse, short-winged birds that don’t sing so much as chatter and clatter. They nest in the crevices above the waterline and fill the basin with quick motion and no song.

There are also the runners — small, furred, many-legged, living on the dry stone islands between channels. They move in stores and caches, hoard the grass seeds, and swim when they have to, which is often, because the water rises so fast. And there are things in the deep pools that don’t surface, only come and go as pale migrating shapes in the standing water, following the vents. All of it is quick, collective, satellites around the water. Nothing here is solitary; everything is in its hundreds. The basin floor is an anthill, organized at a scale larger than ants.

## Rules of the World

The Kettle is coherent if you accept its central rule: **here, everything moves and nothing builds.** The water is the primary reality. Stone and animal and plant and human all make their arrangements with reference to water movement, not to any fixed ground. The ground moves too — the basin is a creature of slow erosion and the occasional fast flood, and the stream shifts its channels by the week.

There is nothing permanent in the Kettle except the shape of the place and the fact of the water. The people who winter there are not quite a settlement — they are a shifting arrangement of shelters, a scab of a town on the basin’s rim, built of piled stone and hide and more stone, and rebuilt every year because the surges take something every year. The architecture is mostly about drainage and defensibility against the water: raised floors, lips and gutters, every shelter a boat-shape. It is layered — this year’s repairs on last year’s repairs on the year before’s, which is the closest the Kettle comes to archaeology. The people are sparable, few, and their rule-of-thumb is that you don’t stop the water, you go with it, get up early, move fast, and work together.

The strangest thing about the Kettle is that the water is not just water. It is, in a dense, near-animate way, *moving with purpose*. The surges have a rhythm nobody can predict but everyone respects; the water finds channels that were not there last week, and sometimes those channels cut uphill. The pools in the deeper hollows show strange light on still nights. The thing that abides the vent-surges and moves with the flood and holds its basin against all the erosion — the thing the people call the Kettle, when they mean the place’s own intention — that thing is mostly water, and it is not exactly the water.

The cold, the light, the noise-absorbing quiet, the teeming crowded animal life, the constant churn and dimness of memory that the basin suffers as a place — all of it comes out of that central, small, high pool of disturbance. The landscape is turbulent; the turbulence has a shape; the shape is a kettle holding it, and the water never stops going down and around and up.

To stand in the Kettle is to stand somewhere that has been doing this for a long time and will go on doing it, and which is courteous about it — it doesn’t stop for you, but it allows you to watch. The place is not strange; it is *thorough*. It figured out what it is long before you got there, and it is going to keep at it. The people who winter there quietly, in the hum, could tell you this. They might not use those words. They might just say: *keep moving, keep working, and get up high when it changes.*
