# The Brimming Floor

You come down into it rather than up to it, because the whole place sits in a shallow stone hollow, low as a cupped hand. There is no path so much as a place where the crust is thicker than elsewhere.

The air arrives first. It is warmer than your skin and heavier than air has any right to be — saturated, breathing-thick, so that your clothes go damp before you've taken ten steps and the hairs on your arms lie flat with moisture. It smells of wet chalk and warm iron. Nothing in it moves. There is no wind here and there never has been; the hollow is too low and too sheltered and the air has settled into it like water into a bowl and stayed.

Then the floor.

The floor is not a floor. It is a tessellation — tens of thousands of small calcite lips, each the size of a palm or a plate, each cupped and holding a shallow spoonful of water, and they are packed so tightly against one another that not a finger's width of bare stone shows anywhere in the hollow. They fit like a honeycomb that has forgotten geometry; like the backs of a thousand turtles pressed shoulder to shoulder; like the scutes of something enormous and asleep. The rims are not sharp. They have been rounded by long, patient existence into soft-shouldered lips, and every one of them brims. Every one. The water in them is blood-warm — thirty-four, thirty-five degrees — and perfectly still, and each little pool holds its own small circle of the dim, dim light.

You cannot walk without stepping into them. Your weight presses the crust and the water in each rim lifts and trembles and subsides, and somewhere two rims away another one answers, and under your boot sole there is a hollow drum-sound, because beneath the packed lips there is only a thin shell and then a warm black reservoir that goes down further than anyone has needed to know.

Nothing grows here. There is not a blade, not a weed, not a crust of lichen on the upper stones. The water is too mineral and too warm and the light too poor, and the place has the particular cleanness of a thing that has never had to make room for life. The only suggestion of green or gold is the sheen on the water's surface when you tilt your head — an oily iridescence, thin as breath on a mirror, which is either a film of something very simple or nothing at all. It is not possible to tell, and it does not change from one year to the next.

The walls of the hollow weep. Not dramatically — no streams, no falls. But every stone face above the floor is beaded and running with condensation, and the beads gather and swell and let go with a soft tick into the pools below. The upper walls carry the better part of the hollow's sound: a hundred tiny ticks a minute, unsynchronized, so the whole chamber has a fine-grained patter under it like rain heard through a wall. Under that, the constant low simmer of warm water finding its way out through the calcite — not a trickle, more a seethe, a breath — and under that, if you stand still long enough to hear it, the hollow resonance of the shell floor itself, which is not silence but the absence of any other sound to fill it.

The light comes from behind you and above, down a tilted throat of stone, and it enters the hollow already tired. It is the light of a heavy overcast filtered twice over; it does not so much illuminate as stain. The pools go pewter. The weeping walls go the color of old teeth. Further in, where the hollow narrows and the rims cluster smaller and tighter and the water lies deeper in them, the light gives out entirely and the floor simply continues into the dark, still brimming, still ticking, still warm — and there is no reason to think it stops.

Scattered through this, the marks of hands. Low walls of stacked travertine, knee-high, set in short arcs to steer the seep from one cluster of rims to another — and the rims behind them are larger, more perfect, better fed, so the walls have been doing their work a long time. A notch cut into a rim with a hand tool. A channel chiseled across six lips, worn smooth by water. A wooden scraper, blackened and swollen, laid across two rims as if set down at the end of a shift. A fire-blackened stone, the only dry thing in the hollow, in a niche where the condensation runs around it rather than over it. These are not ruins and they are not a village. They are the small, faint, occasional residue of a few people who come down with hand tools to skim the calcite cream from the pools and take it away in baskets, generation after generation, and who have never needed to build anything grander than a knee-high wall in a warm hole in the ground.

The fauna is almost an insult to the word. Pale things the length of a fingernail, blind, drifting in the deeper rims and turning slowly. A snail the color of the water, so that you see only the moving shadow of it. In the warm dark further in, something that leaves a wake. That is all. They do not school and they do not swarm and they do not add up to a sound.

Nothing about this place is going anywhere. The water seeps in and the water deposits and the water seeps out, and the rims are rounded, and they were rounded before anyone came, and the light is dim and it will be dim, and the air is warm and will stay warm. There is no season down here, no tide, no flood-mark, no gradient — not even decay, really, since decay needs something to eat and there is nothing. The temperature does not vary by a degree between the wet part of the year and the dry. The place has found the shape it wants to be and is holding it with enormous patience and no effort at all.

You stand in the middle of a honeycomb of small warm pools, in a room-sized hollow of weeping stone, breathing air like a bath, hearing a hundred ticks a minute and nothing else, and the strongest impression is not of strangeness but of a long, quiet, mineral contentment — a place that has already finished, and is content to go on not changing, brimming, in the dim.
