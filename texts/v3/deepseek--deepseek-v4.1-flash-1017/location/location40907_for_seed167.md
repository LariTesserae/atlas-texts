# The Drowned City of Vel

To reach Vel you descend. The surface, if there is one, lies far above and forgotten — no memory of sky reaches this place. The water is warm, blood-warm, and thick with a perpetual haze of drifting matter: shed shells, soft body fragments, the slow snow of decay and renewal. Light exists only as chemistry — the pale green glow of things that make their own illumination, the sudden blooming flare of a disturbed colony, the constellation of small lives winking on and off in the dark.

The city itself is grown, not built. Its towers are the accumulated work of ten thousand generations of shelled organisms, each adding its chamber to the last, the way coral builds reefs and wasps build nests and time builds sediment. The architecture is biological: spiraling calcified ramps that corkscrew upward, chambers designed for the flow of warm currents rather than any walking thing, passages sized for bodies that no longer exist — or never did. The walls are layered, striated, translucent in places where the water has worn them thin. You can see through them to the glow of the next chamber, the next street, the next life.

The city is quiet. Not silent — there is always the low, omnipresent hum of water moving through channels designed to move it, the wet percussion of something feeding in a nearby chamber, the distant groan of a structure settling into itself. But there is no speech here, no music, no machinery. Only the sounds that water and bodies make.

Life is everywhere, dense as a held breath. Things the size of a hand scuttle along the walls, grazing on the film of nutrients that accumulates on every surface. Things larger than you — much larger — drift through the open spaces between towers, their bodies glowing faintly, their passage stirring the water into slow spirals that persist long after they have gone. Something in a side chamber unfolds a fan of soft tentacles and waits. Something else brushes past your leg, a soft weight moving with purpose, and does not return.

The water carries memory. The city's inhabitants, such as they are, have learned to read it — the chemical signatures of what has passed, what has fed, what is dying, what is about to. They navigate by taste and temperature, by the faint electric signatures of other bodies. They are not intelligent in any way you would recognize, but they are aware, and the city is their body as much as their home. It has been here a long time. It will be here a long time after you have gone.

The light comes from everywhere and nowhere. Phosphorescent patches along the walls, the bloom of disturbed plankton, the slow pulse of something that might be a heart or might be a signal. In the distance, where the towers fade into darkness, the glow becomes a diffuse haze, as if the dark itself has a faint luminosity, as if the city extends into a depth that has no bottom.

You move through it slowly. The water resists — gently, persistently. There is no hurry here. There is time. The warmth presses against you, and the quiet, and the sense of being watched by something that has no eyes, and no interest in you at all. You are a passing thing. The city is a process. It goes on.
