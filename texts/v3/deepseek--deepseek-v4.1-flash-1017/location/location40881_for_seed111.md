# The Throat

You come up out of the cloud into a place where the mountain has not finished being a mountain.

At this height the rock is young — lifted and split within the span of a few human lifetimes, so recent that nothing has had time to soften it. Every edge is a fresh fracture. The scree underfoot is all corners; if you pick up a stone it cuts. There is no soil anywhere, only a grey-green film on the windward faces, lichen of a kind that seems to drink the air directly. The mountain is not old enough to have grown a skin, and it has the raw, glassy look of something that cooled too fast.

The Throat itself is a fissure, narrow, maybe twelve paces across at its widest, opening in the summit ridge and dropping further than you can see. It is a room. That is the honest way to put it — it has walls and a floor and a ceiling of sky, and it is about the size of a chapel. The walls are not vertical. They lean, and the lean is uneven, so the crack widens and pinches and widens again as it goes down, and at the narrow places you could touch both sides at once if you trusted the holds.

## Water

Nothing here flows in a channel. Water arrives the way a breath arrives on cold glass.

The cloud comes through the ridge most of the day, and the Throat sits directly in its path. Where the cloud touches the rock, the rock sweats. Beads form on the high faces until they are too heavy and let go, and all day there is a slow irregular pattering — not rain, a condensation, a liquid that has no source and falls only a short way. Some of it collects in a chain of shallow hollows worn into a shelf a few paces below the rim. Six pools, the largest no wider than your reach, each one fed by drips and each one too cold to hold your hand in comfortably. Their surfaces are never still; something is always landing.

The pools are young too. The rock around their rims is still sharp, not yet rounded by the water that made them. Whatever this place is going to become, it is in the middle of becoming it, slowly, on a scale of thousands of years, and you are seeing a very early frame.

## Light

There is not much of it. The Throat is deep and its lean means the sun only reaches the bottom around midday, and even then the light is a narrow incoming blade, moving down the wall like a hand feeling along a shelf, and then gone.

Most of the time it is dark in the way of a room with the curtains drawn — enough to make out shapes, not enough to name colours. The pools are the brightest things in it, because they hold whatever light exists and hand it back. At night, with the cloud sealing the opening, it is not merely dark but *closed* — no stars, no horizon, just the dripping and the sound.

And there is a faint light that is not light. The lichen on the lower faces, the ones that never dry, has a weak glow about it — the greenish nothing-colour you see when you close your eyes. It is not enough to see by. It is enough to tell you that the walls are not as far away as you thought.

## Sound

This is the loudest quiet place you have ever stood in.

The crack is a resonator. Its shape — narrow at the neck, wide at the belly — catches sound and gives it back, and the rock itself is that young glassy stone that rings. Strike the wall and it hums, and the hum takes a long time to fade. The mountain is an instrument and the Throat is its mouth.

But most of what you hear is not the wind. It is the chit.

## The chit

They are everywhere. That is the first and lasting fact of the place.

Each one is small — two fingers long, plated, pale as the inside of a shell, six-legged, flat enough to disappear into a crack the width of a coin. They are cold and they are slow, and there are so many of them that the wall is not a wall but a moving surface of them, layered three and four deep in the places where they cluster, near-touching, a saturation of small bodies.

They feed on the film — the lichen, the mineral skin the condensation leaves behind when it dries. There is no other food up here, so they are also each other's food; you will see one take another without ceremony, and the wall will close over the gap in a minute. Their ecology is a loop with only one strand in it.

And they drum. That is the noise. Each chit carries, gripped in its forelimbs, a little shard of the ringing stone — no, not carries, *keeps*; each one has its piece and does not let go — and at intervals it lifts the shard and strikes it against the wall. Not at random. The strikes run in waves along the surface, one part of the wall answering another, and the sum of it is a constant chiming rattle that fills the crack from bottom to top. You cannot find the rhythm. There is a rhythm. You can feel that there is one the way you can feel that a language is a language without speaking it.

They use things. That is the strangest part, and you will not be sure what to do with it. They carry shards, they carry pebbles, they move small objects from one ledge to another and leave them arranged. Nothing is built. Nothing is made. There is no structure in the Throat that was put there by intent — but the ledges are sorted, and the sorting is not geological, and no one can tell you who did it six hundred years ago because the answer is the chit, patient, one pebble per lifetime, a slow gradient of arrangement accumulating across durations no single one of them lives to see.

## The rules

Night comes into the Throat and the chit go quiet in layers, top first, then down. The drumming thins out and stops and what is left is the drip and the ring of the last few strikes fading in the crack.

In the morning the cloud returns and the walls sweat and the whole thing starts again.

Water comes from the air, not the sky. Food comes from the rock and from each other. Nothing is built; everything is arranged. The mountain is new and sharp and will be soft in a hundred thousand years, and it is currently in the one moment of its life when it rings.

Stand at the rim on a cold morning, with the cloud going through you, and the sound rising out of the split in the young stone, and you will feel the size of the thing: a room full of small presences, all of them chiming, none of them looking up, doing the slow work of a place that has only just started.
