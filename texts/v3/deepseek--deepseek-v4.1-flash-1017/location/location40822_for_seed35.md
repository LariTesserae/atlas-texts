# The Sinter

There is no water here, and the absence has a texture — it is not a lack but a material, packed into every crack and pore of the place, filling the role that air or moisture fills elsewhere. Everything is dry the way a fired pot is dry: not merely lacking wetness but transformed by its absence, ringing faintly when struck.

The Sinter is a small world, a handful of connected chambers beneath a plain of crusted salt, no more than a few hundred paces across in any direction. You can walk it end to end in a quarter hour, and yet it takes days to see, because it is dense — layered like a hive, like a struck bell full of nested rooms. The floors are not floors but shelves, crusted and terraced with mineral sinters, pale ochre and rust and a deep bruised red where the heat comes closest to the surface. The walls rise in sharp fresh facets, glassy where they fractured, still bright-edged, unweathered. This is a young cavity. It opened recently, in the slow accounting of stone — a few thousand years, perhaps less — and the break is still raw. You can find the matching faces across a gap, still answering each other in shape, waiting for a settling that will not come, because the stress has already equalized and the place has decided to stay exactly as it is.

Light is dim and sourceless-feeling, though it has sources: seams of hot mineral glowing dull orange in the lower cuts, the pale green-white of the growths, the intermittent amber of machine-lamps that nobody lit and nobody tends. None of it reaches far. The chambers are not dark, but they are never bright — everything is seen at the edge of visibility, in the way you see a room by the afterimage of a struck match.

And it is warm. Warm steadily, warm from below, warm without variation. There are no gradients here worth the name — no cool corner to retreat to, no draft to turn your face from. The heat rises out of the floor like a held breath and pools against the ceilings, and it has been doing so for as long as the machines have been doing what they do, which is either a very long time or no time at all, depending on how you count.

## The Standing Engines

Scattered through the chambers, never continuous, are machines. They are industrial and they are old and they are running. A pump-house of riveted plate stands alone on a sinter terrace, hammering; a rank of pressure vessels, banded and sweating nothing, sit in a side-cut behind a broken wall; a great flywheel turns in a shaft too narrow for it ever to have been brought in, and this is not a puzzle to be solved but simply a fact of the place. There are pipes that cross the ceilings and vanish into the rock and emerge somewhere else, still warm to the touch, still carrying their gas. There are intakes and exhausts. There is no water in any of it — the working fluid is gas, sealed, cycling, expanding and contracting against the deep heat and the cold of the upper rock, a Stirling logic written in iron.

Nobody operates them. There are faint marks of hands — worn plates, a broken ladder, a row of hooks — but the hands are gone or were never here, and the machinery does not need them. It has reached its equilibrium. Each engine has found the speed it will always turn, and it turns at that speed, and the sound of it has not changed in living memory because there is no living memory here, only the sound itself, continuous.

The sound is enormous. It is the first thing, before the light or the heat. The hammering of the pumps, the long bass groan of the flywheel shaft, the hiss and clack of valves, and beneath all of it the resonance of the chambers themselves — hard dry rock reflecting, returning, layering the noise until it has no direction. You cannot point to where the sound comes from. It is simply the medium, the way water would be the medium in a drowned place. And because it never changes — because every engine is at its settled speed and every valve at its settled rhythm — the loudness becomes a kind of silence after a while. The ear gives up looking for pattern where there is no variation to find.

## The Living Crust

Sparse things grow, but they are not plants in any sense that would matter to you. Near the hot seams, in the mineral dust, there are growths: pale, brittle, filigreed things like coral made of ash, lithophytes drinking the chemistry of the stone and the warmth and nothing else. They are few and far between, and each one is a small architecture, sharp-edged, fragile, dusted with its own shed. They do not spread. They have spread as far as they can.

But the fauna is everywhere, and it is abundant — dense, layered, near-touching, so that the surfaces of the Sinter are not surfaces but crowds. Chitinous things the size of a thumb joint move in the dust and under it. Things with too many legs cling to the undersides of pipes, riding the vibration. Things flat and pale as wafers press themselves to the warm floor and drink the heat directly, and things with long antennae feel their way along the walls in the dimness, and things that are mostly mouth live inside the exhausts of the machines where the gas is hottest. They do not need water. They are built for this: dry-bodied, dust-eating, metabolizing the mineral and the heat and each other, in loops so tight that nothing is wasted and nothing accumulates.

They nest in the machinery. This is the strangest fact of the place and the most ordinary-seeming once you have watched it a while: the engines and the fauna are not two things. The creatures clean the intakes — not out of service, but because the intakes are where the food is, the dust drawn in and caught, and their cleaning is the eating. They pack the dead spaces of the pumps with their egg-cases, which insulate, which keeps the bearings at the temperature they like. The exhaust heat is their summer and their nursery. The machines' constant noise is their weather, their clock, the thing they measure themselves against. Whether the engines run because the creatures tend them, or the creatures exist because the engines run, is a question with no edge here, because the loop closed long ago and holds.

## The Rules

Time does not pass in the Sinter in any way you can feel. There is no day, no season, no tide, no rain — nothing to mark a change against. The heat is the same. The sound is the same. The engines are at equilibrium and will remain so. The creatures are at equilibrium and will remain so. The fresh fracture-faces stay fresh, because nothing rounds them; there is no water to freeze in the cracks, no wind to sand them, no growth to prize them apart. The dust is young dust, made of shed husks and sinter flake, and it does not go anywhere, because there is nowhere for it to go. It settles. It is reprocessed. It settles again.

The only movement from outside is a slow seep of air through the upper cracks — the same seep, at the same rate, forever — carrying a thin ghost of the salt plain above, a mineral tang that the fauna have built their whole chemistry around. It is not a change. It is part of the loop. It has always been seeping and will always seep.

So the Sinter is loud and still at once, crowded and small, warm and sealed and dry. You can cross it in a quarter hour. You can stand in the middle of it, among the turning wheels and the patient crusted things and the chittering dark, and you will hear — if you listen past the hammering — that nothing here is going anywhere, and that this is not a trap but a completed thing, a place that has finished becoming itself and is now simply being it, continuously, at volume, in the warm and lightless dark.
