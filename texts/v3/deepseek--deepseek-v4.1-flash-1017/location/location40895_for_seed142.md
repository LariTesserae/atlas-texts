# The Warm Plateau of Ossuary Works

There is no water here, and hasn't been for longer than anyone counts. The plateau is stone — a dark, close-grained basalt worn down to softness, its edges rounded by an erosion so old it has finished its work and gone quiet. What remains is almost flat: long shallow swells and hollows, a landscape like a held breath, rising gently toward a horizon that never quite resolves. Underfoot the rock is warm. Not hot — warm the way a stone is warm hours after the sun has gone, except there is no sun to have gone, and the warmth comes from beneath, seeping up through seams and fissures in slow columns that you can feel on the backs of your hands and see, faintly, as a shimmer in the still air.

Because the air does not move. That is the first thing you notice, before you notice the dark. There is no wind. The warmth rises in its slow columns and pools and spreads, and the air above the plateau sits in place like water in a basin, thick and dry and carrying a taste of iron and hot dust. Sounds travel strangely in it — a step, a word, a distant clank, all of them arriving flat and close, as if the air were a wall a few paces off.

The light is nearly nothing. The sky is a low ceiling of unbroken dark, without stars, without moon, without any feature at all — not cloud, just a lid. What illumination there is comes from the ground and from the Works: the dull red of the heat-seams where they are widest, and the far-off orange glow of furnaces that have burned so long no one remembers them being lit. In the middle distance these sources blur into a general amber haze that hangs over the settlement and never lifts. Away from the Works, the dark is complete enough that you navigate by the warmth on your face — you can feel a furnace long before you see it.

And the Works are scattered, not crowded. That is the strangeness of it: a city the size of a city, a true settlement with roads and conduits and outbuildings, but the density of a village. Long low halls of riveted metal and fired brick stand alone on the plateau with a kilometer of bare warm stone between them. Conduits run out from the halls across the flats, thick pipes lagged in pale mineral wool, and they go nowhere in particular — they run to a junction, loop, return. The layout has the logic of infrastructure and the emptiness of a ruin, except nothing here is ruined. The metal is not rusted. The brick is not spalled. Everything is in repair, and there is almost no one to repair it.

The machinery turns. That is the sound, such as there is: a low continuous hum that you stop hearing after a while and then hear again, and the slow rhythmic knock of something large and patient, and now and then the clean ring of metal on metal, far off, unattended. Engines the size of houses drive shafts that drive other shafts. Furnaces draw from the heat below and feed it back into the ground through buried mains, or so the conduits suggest; the plateau is warm and the Works are warmer, and the two warmth seems to circulate. No one can say what is being made. Nothing is stacked anywhere in quantity. Nothing is loaded or shipped. The Works produce their own continuation and that is all; the process reached its balance an age ago and has held it, neither growing nor failing, the way a flame holds when fuel and air are exactly matched.

The people — there are a few — are as sparse as the buildings. They keep to the halls, or walk the long empty roads between them with the unhurried gait of those who have nowhere to be that isn't warm already. They do not tend the engines, mostly. They watch them. They know which sounds are correct, and they notice when a sound is not, and then they do something about it, and then they go back to watching. They wear pale cloth against the dust and they speak little, because the still air makes a conversation carry farther than it should, and there is a custom here of quiet. When they speak of the plateau they speak of it as finished. The land, they say, has settled. The Works have settled. There is nothing left to arrive at.

Fauna is rare and strange and slow. Small blind things live in the lagging of the conduits, pale and soft, feeding on the heat and the mineral crust; you find them only when you put your hand on a pipe and something under the wrapping shifts. Larger, rarer, there are the walkers — tall jointed shapes, seen perhaps twice in a season, moving across the flats at the pace of a shadow lengthening, their bodies dull and stone-colored, their legs finding the heat-seams and following them. They do not flee and they do not approach. They pass. The people here leave them alone and consider it a poor omen to be followed by one, though no one can say what the omen foretells, since nothing here ever happens.

This is the rule of the plateau, and it is worth stating plainly: nothing changes. There is no water to carry anything away and no wind to move it and no season to turn. Dust falls and lies where it falls. The seams do not widen; the halls do not settle; the engines do not wear, or wear so slowly that the wear is a thing of the far future and not this one. The warm stone is the oldest stable thing you will ever stand on, and it is in its final form, and it knows it. Time here is not stopped — the shafts turn, the furnaces breathe — but it has come to rest, the way a pendulum comes to rest, all its motion spent into a single held position.

Stand out on the flats between the halls, far enough that the amber haze is only a glow on the horizon, and the dark is total and the warmth comes up through your boots and the hum is so faint it might be your own blood, and you will understand why the people here do not hurry. There is nowhere else for anything to go. The plateau has already gone there.
