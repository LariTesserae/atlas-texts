The darkness here is total. It is not the darkness of a cloudy night, which has texture and depth, but a dense, mineral black that presses against the eyes. The air is warm, held in the stone's memory of a sun it has never seen, and it is utterly, profoundly still. The silence is the first thing you understand: not an absence of sound, but a presence. It is a silence so complete that the slow, patient crumbling of a single grain of sandstone, somewhere off in the dark, is a distinct and audible event.

The ground underfoot is a fine, powdery dust, the color of old bone, which has settled over everything and preserved the world in a state of perfect, airless calm. It gives way underfoot, but only slightly, the powder lying deep over a bedrock of coarse, crystalline stone that feels warm to a hand held against it. The terrain is a low, shallow basin, a gentle bowl perhaps only a few dozen paces across. You can feel its edge by walking until the dust deepens in a drift against a wall, or until the slope of the floor begins, almost imperceptibly, to rise. The basin is the whole world, a pocket of hush in a greater dark you can feel waiting beyond it.

The scenery is not built, but grown. From the floor, great slow formations rise. Gnarled, pale columns of stone, twisted like ancient trees caught in mid-stride, are slick with an oily sheen of moisture that never evaporates. They are not stalagmites in the way you might expect; they seem to have pushed upward from below with a slow, agonizing torsion, their surfaces smooth and worn as river stones. Between these columns, the ground is mostly bare. But in the hollows, in the lee of the stone, there are the lichens. They are the only vegetation left in this world, if you can call them that: flat, crusty patches of a color like dull copper, spreading in concentric rings that take decades to widen one finger's breadth. They are dry and brittle, but in the warm, still air, they have a smell—a faint, sharp scent of iron and clean soil.

The only sound is that of the world's slow respiration. It is not a breath, but the sound of sand sliding. High above in the unseen ceiling, fine grains of rock, warmed by some deep magmatic pulse, expand and contract, and a thin stream of dust might cascade down the side of a pillar. It whispers. It rustles. It sighs. It is the sound of a place settling into itself, an established equilibrium of pressure and wear. Every surface you touch is smoothed, sanded down by uncounted millennia of this gentle, abrasive air. Nothing here is sharp. The edges of everything have been rounded away.

And that is the final, overpowering rule of this place: it is in its own state. There are no creatures. No insects tick in the dust. No bats stir the air. There is no wind, no water, no fire, and no tool has ever marked the stone. The world is a still life in stone, a forgotten corner of a much larger, darker room. The emptiness is not a lack, but a quality. It is a place that has been held in a state of equilibrium for so long that time itself has become geological. You stand in the warm, dark hollow, breathing air that tastes of ancient dust and iron lichen, and you feel the profound, patient weight of a world that has not changed, and will not change, and does not notice you at all.
