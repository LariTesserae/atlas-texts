# The Vault of the Unfinished Rain

There is no sky here. There is a ceiling, but no sky—and the distinction matters, because the ceiling is where everything begins.

Call it the Lid: a single continuous surface, continental in extent, ribbed and paneled and seamed, running in slow curving courses that vanish into the blue haze long before they end. It is metal, or something that was poured and set and then forgotten by whoever poured it. It sweats. Condensation beads across its underside in rivers and drips and pours, and the pours have become permanent, so that from below the Lid looks composed of vertical silver threads: ten thousand waterfalls, ten thousand more, each one falling far enough that it dissolves into mist before it ever lands. The light comes from up there—from every seam and joint of the Lid, a cold white fluorescence, so that the whole world is lit like a cathedral lit from its own roof. There is no day and no night, only the light's slow dimming and returning, as though the building itself breathed, and the long spells when the batteries or the generators or whatever feeds the glow turn over.

Beneath the Lid: water. Not a sea with a surface—there is no surface, no wave-crease where air meets liquid, because there is no air. The water goes up into the mist zone and the mist zone goes up into the Lid and the distinction is a matter of degrees. You move through it as a diver moves through deep ocean, except the ocean is the room and the room is a hundred miles across and no one has found the far wall. It is cool everywhere—cold enough that the waterfalls of condensation feel like a steady pressure of snowmelt along every surface—and moving through it is like moving through a memory of cold, something that insinuates itself into whatever you use for skin.

The water is not empty. It is full of sound. The fountains are the loudest thing, of course: a roar that never stops, ten thousand waterfalls blending into a broadband hiss that you feel in your bones more than hear, that flattens every other sound into its substrate. But beneath the hiss there are other things. The hum of the Lid's lighting runs through everything—a sixty-cycle or fifty-cycle or some other cycle entirely, a low electric note that the whole architecture carries like a tuning fork. There are creaks and booms from far away, structural noises, the building settling against pressures that have not finished deciding what they are. There are engines somewhere—vast and slow and rhythmic, a heartbeat at the bottom of the world, all the pumps and circulators and oxygenators that keep the water from becoming something the fauna would not like. And there are the animals, of course. There are always the animals.

The fauna came in. Nobody brought them, or if somebody did, they stayed and multiplied and stopped being anybody's. They are the only soft edges in a world of seams and welds. Schools of pale, translucent fish-things ribbon through the middle depths in long slow rivers, following the currents that the fountains make; they are the size of your forearm and the size of your leg and there are more of them at any moment than you could count in a lifetime, and they move with the single-mindedness of a herd, a single organism in ten thousand parts that turns and turns and turns through the flooded halls. Deeper down, where the water goes dark and the light of the Lid is only a rumor, there are eels, or things like eels, ribbon-bodied and patient, that do not school but hunt alone, and there are shelled crawling things that graze the architecture itself, and there are blooms—not plants, exactly, not really anything you would put in a catalogue—that grow where the mineral content of the water gathers, strange pale fronds and lumps and mats that are the closest this world comes to vegetation, and they are sparse, and they are tough, and they are everywhere in the way that lichen is everywhere on a cold stone coast. The fish eat the blooms. The eels eat the fish. The crawling things eat what falls. It is a real ecology, small but complete, and it has been running long enough that it has found its balance.

The architecture is the thing the world is made of, and its scale is what makes the world vast. It comes up out of the depths as columns, as pillars, as struts and braces and buttresses, a forest of load-bearing structures whose tops are lost in the mist and whose bottoms are lost in the dark. They were built—there is no pretending otherwise; the seams are too regular, the symmetry too deliberate—but they were built for something much larger than the fish, and much longer ago, and no one living can tell you what the structure was for, because everyone lives in the spaces between the load-bearing members and regards them the way you might regard mountains: as features of the shape of the world, not objects anyone made. The columns are big enough to house a town, and some of them do. There are platforms and ledges and shelf-structures at the heights where the water is brightest, and the faunal blooms cluster on them in their sparse mats, and here and there are openings—hatches, ports, doors that lead into the columns themselves—into the interior spaces, into the dark and the pipes and the engines, where the heartbeat comes from and where generation after generation of this world's people have made their homes in the sealed, dry chambers that the water has not yet found a way into.

The people are the fishes' cousins, in a way. They came in too, or they stayed, or they were already here; the answer depends on which story you heard from which elder, and the elders disagree. They dive among the columns. They harvest the blooms and catch the slow schools with nets that are mostly patience. They keep the lights burning—the Lid's light is theirs to tend, the generators are theirs to feed—and they speak to the engines as though the engines were neighbors, because you do not want to be on bad terms with the thing that makes the heartbeat. They are cool-skinned and small-eyed and they move through the water without hurry. Their ancestors came here to escape a flood (some stories) or to build something too large to finish (others) or to work in a factory that had no other reason to exist than that it needed workers (others still, and the number of stories tells you that all of them are true in the way that stories are true). They swim up the long columns and out across the flooded halls and the fountains fall through them and the light from above falls through the water and everything glows and hums and rushes and drips.

And at the bottom of the world, if you dive far enough—farther than the light goes, farther than the eels go, into the pressure and the cold and the dark—there is the Base. The Feet of the columns. The place where the structure comes down out of the mist and ends, not in a floor, not in rock, not in anything you could recognize as foundation, but in a cut edge: a clean termination, like a drawing that stops. Nobody has reached the Base and come back with a clear account of it. The divers who go that deep speak of the water getting heavier and colder and more silent, the fountain-roar dying away behind them, the hum of the Lid fading to a memory, the dark pressing in, and then—the edge. The place where the columns stop being anchored to anything and simply cease. And beyond the edge, nothing to see: just water, and the faint possibility of more columns, and the sense that the building continues past the point where anyone from this side of anywhere has ever been.

They call it the Vault. Not because it is sealed, but because it is not, and the not-sealing is the thing they have not been able to explain.

The water flows. The fountains fall. The fish school and the eels follow and the lights come up and the lights go down and the engines keep their slow and patient rhythm, and somewhere in the mist between the Lid and the middle depths, the falling water catches the light and becomes silver, and the whole vast hollow of the world is full of a cold and moving brightness, and nobody knows what it is for.
