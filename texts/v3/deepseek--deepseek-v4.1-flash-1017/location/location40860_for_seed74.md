# The Nap

The basin is shallow and wide, and it is the only wet thing for a long way.

From the dry uplands you can see it as a change of color — a grey-green dish set in the pale scrub, ringed by a low lip of stone, and inside it, filling it nearly edge to edge, something that is not quite ground. It looks like a lawn that has been left too long, or like the felt under a billiard table, if the felt were half a meter deep and breathing.

They call it the nap. It is one plant, or it behaves as one — a continuous mat of fine upright fiber, grey-green at the tips, rust-brown a hand's depth down, that covers the basin floor and laps a little way up the slopes. It does not have leaves in any way you would point at. It has grain, the way cloth has grain. It is warm to the touch when the air is cold, and it holds water the way a sponge holds water, and it gives that water back all day, slowly, as vapor. That is the whole reason the town is here: the nap makes its own weather. A hundred meters above it, the air is dry and the light is hard. At mat level, the air is cool and soft and smells of wet rope and cut hay, and every surface that is not moving has a film of water on it by morning.

So the basin is damp in the way a cellar is damp, not in the way a river is wet. There is no river. There is a seep — one, at the low eastern end, a palm-wide upwelling of cold water that comes out of a crack and has never been known to stop or to increase — and everything else is condensation. Water arrives from the air, not from the sky, though the sky does contribute: perhaps forty days a year, a fine grey rain that does not fall so much as hang and then descend, and is drunk by the nap before it has run a meter.

---

The town is small. You can walk across it in twenty minutes at a slow pace, and you will want to walk slowly, because the walking is the interesting part. There is very little solid ground; the bedrock shows through as a scatter of low stony islands, and on these the old buildings sit — heavy, pale, worked smooth by two or three centuries of damp, their corners rounded the way a river stone is rounded, their window sills worn into shallow saddles by runoff. Everything between the islands is deck: plank and slab and railed catwalk, laid on piles driven into the nap, kept a hand's width above it, black with age and slick with the perpetual condensation. The deck is the town's real street plan, and it is generous — much wider than the town needs, running out to platforms and stairs and landing stages that lead to nothing at all. The town was laid out by people expecting ten times the people. There is a great deal of space between presences. You can stand on a good plank bridge over the mat and hear nothing but the tick of water finding a gutter.

The architecture is layered because it has been repaired by everyone who ever lived here and demolished by no one. Iron sheds lean against stone halls; a brick chimney rises out of a timber roof and is stayed with wire; glazed cloth roofs are strung over courtyards on cable, and under the cloth it is always dusk and always dripping. Every roof — cloth, slate, or iron — is built to gather. They call it *stilling*. The underside of a roof is cooler than the air, the vapor condenses, and the water runs down the slope into gutters, and the gutters run into iron troughs that line every street at knee height, and the troughs run to cisterns, and the cisterns run to the works. The whole town is a device for wringing the air. On a still morning you can hear it working: not a roar, a papery, distributive trickle, thousands of small arrivals, water finding its way downhill through the town's ironwork. It is the loudest thing here and it is not loud.

---

The industry is cloth, and the cloth is the nap.

The harvest is not a stripping. It is a haircut. Crews go out on the deck with long flexible shears and take the top inch, walking in lines, and in a season the mat has closed over the cut and is ready again. A cut lane is a paler green for a month. The cut fiber goes to the drying sheds — long low iron buildings on the bedrock islands, kept hot by stoves, where the stuff is hung in hanks and the water leaves it and is caught, because they catch everything. Then carding, then pressing. The presses are screw presses, old ones, driven by belts from a turbine in the seep-channel; the belts are the only fast-moving thing in the basin and they make a slow clacking thud that carries. The finished sheet is dense, grey, waterproof, and slightly warm; it goes out of the basin on the backs of mules along the east road and comes back as money and iron and coffee, and it is used for roofing and for coats and for something in the big coastal cities that nobody here has seen.

So the town has two products: felt, and water. The water is the humbler trade and the more constant one. It leaves in iron tanks on the same mule train, because the uplands are dry and will pay for it.

---

Light here is normal and unremarkable and mostly comes from a high grey-white sky, so that shadows are soft and there are few of them. The basin catches about an hour of direct sun in the middle of the day, and for that hour the nap steams visibly and the whole dish of it goes bright and the drying sheds are thrown open and the smell of hot wet fiber is very strong. Then the light goes flat again.

Sounds: the trickle of the stilling; the belt-clack of the presses when they run, which is not every day; the shears; someone calling across a wide gap; the flat clop of a mule on plank. Otherwise quiet. There are no engines running continuously, because the town is small and half its machinery is idle and the rest is turned off when there is nothing to press.

Fauna is thin. There are moths in the drying sheds, a small dun species that lives in the hanks and is tolerated because it does not seem to eat much. There is one bird — a low, deliberate wader, grey, the size of a pigeon, that walks the gutters and the troughs and picks things out of them, unafraid, and nests under the eaves of the cistern house; nobody has ever seen more than nine of them and nobody has ever seen a young one. In the nap itself there is something slow and soft-bodied that leaves a smooth trail, and the harvest crews step over the trails without comment, and nobody has dug for it.

---

The rules of the place, as anyone here would tell you:

Nothing is dry. If you want a thing dry you must heat it or you must keep it moving, and preferably both.

Nothing heavy goes off the stone. A loaded cart on the deck will hold; a loaded cart left overnight will be found a hand's width lower in the morning, and the nap will have closed politely over the wheels. So everything that must be heavy stays on an island, and everything else is carried.

You do not cut below the rust line. The brown part is the part that makes the water.

The mat is tended, not owned. There is a rotation, and it is old, and it is written on a board in the guild hall in a hand nobody now can read fluently, and it is followed anyway, because the year they did not follow it the seep ran low for a decade.

And the small strangeness that everyone has stopped noticing: the nap knows weight. Step off the deck onto it and it takes your foot with a slight yielding, then stiffens under your sole into something with the spring of a boardwalk, so that you can walk out across the basin if you go steadily. Stand still too long, or run, and it softens and you go down to the knee in warm grey fiber and have to be hauled out, laughing, by whoever is nearest. It closes behind you. It always closes behind you. The cut lanes heal, the cart ruts heal, the trails of the soft thing heal; the mat is a surface with no memory, which is why the town's whole history is written in iron and stone and not in the ground, and why the ground is the one thing here you cannot build on and cannot trust, and cannot do without.
