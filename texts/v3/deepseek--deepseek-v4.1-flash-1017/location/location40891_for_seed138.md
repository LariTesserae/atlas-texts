The place is a thermal basin, a shallow, steaming wound in the earth that has been built over so completely that it's hard to tell where the natural landscape ends and the machine begins. It is a single, sprawling complex, a hamlet-sized knot of industry nestled in a low, young depression. The ground is new, geologically speaking—fresh, dark basalt that has cracked into sharp, angular plates, still raw and unsoftened by time or weather. The constant heat and moisture prevent the slow, patient work of erosion; instead, the rock is being chemically etched and fractured, its edges remaining dangerously keen.

Everywhere, water is present. It weeps from the faces of the basalt, condensing from the thick air before it even has a chance to fall as rain. It trickles in steady, warm streams down the metal flanks of the machinery, pooling in the sharp-edged crevices of the rock. It creates a constant, percussive soundtrack: the sizzle of a droplet hitting a hot pipe, the hollow *plink* into a brimming puddle, the low hiss of steam venting from a thousand seams. The air itself is a syrupy, suffocating blanket, thick with humidity and the smells of hot metal, ozone, and sulfur. It beads on every surface, running in rivulets, and makes the world feel perpetually fresh from a storm that never ends. This is a post-rain landscape locked in the moment after the downpour, where the clouds have not parted and the world is wrapped in a dim, grey-green light. The sun is a rumour, a diffuse brightness in the perpetual overcast. The primary illumination is the orange and sickly-yellow glow of furnaces and the sharp, flickering blue-white of arc-lamps, their light bleeding into the steam.

Vegetation is a memory. A few hardy, pale-leaved scraggles cling to the edges of the basin, drinking steam, but their struggle is a losing one. They are brittle, scorched, and few. The masters of this place are the fauna, and they are abundant. Not birds or mammals, but creatures of the boil and the rust: tiny, iridescent beetles that skate on the surface of scalding pools; pale, blind crustaceans that scuttle in the warm, dark spaces under the pipes; clouds of small, black flies that dance in the hot vapour. They are the true natives, their ecology built around the constant, life-giving heat and water.

The built environment is not an addition to the landscape; it *is* the landscape. It's a dense, layered labyrinth of industry, a town of pipes. Massive, riveted conduits snake over and under one another, forming a second, metallic geography of valleys and bridges. They are supported by skeletal towers and walkways of perforated steel, all slick with condensation. Vast, cylindrical tanks, their paint long since blistered away by the heat, hold unknown fluids. The architecture is purely functional, an accretion of different eras of construction, adding layers upon layers of infrastructure over the geothermal vents that are the source of all this activity. Massive engines with exposed pistons pump with a slow, rhythmic *thud-hiss* that is the heartbeat of the place. The sound is a constant, roaring symphony of release: the shriek of steam whistles, the clang of metal on metal, the deep bass hum of generators.

The rules of this world are simple and absolute. Nothing changes. This is not a place in transition; it is a system in perfect, frozen equilibrium. For all its noise and apparent chaos, the dynamic is zero. The amount of steam vented is precisely balanced by the heat drawn from below. The water level in every pool is in a state of constant, unwavering fullness. The machinery, though it seems on the verge of shaking itself apart, has run in this same state for as long as anyone can remember and will continue to do so. It's a self-regulating, stable machine-landscape. The people here, if they exist, are not builders but attendants, their lives bound to the unchanging rhythm of the great, wet, hot machine in which they live.
