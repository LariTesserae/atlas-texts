# The Knuckles

The range was a mountain once, and before that it was taller still; what remains is a plateau at great altitude, worn down through a span of time that has no useful number, until the peaks became knuckles and the knuckles became knolls and the knolls are now mostly a soft gray-green tableland with a lace of gullies cut into it. The rock is old enough to be tired. It weathers into clay and grit and layered shelves that crumble under a boot, and it is so deeply settled into its final form that the geologists who came once and left said the place had stopped arguing with the sky. They were nearly right.

Altitude here means thin air and a cold that lives in the marrow rather than the skin. It also means cloud. The plateau sits at the height where the weather comes apart — where a front climbing the long southern slope loses its nerve and spreads out flat and gray and stays. Most days the sky is a low ceiling the color of wet slate. The sun appears as a pale coin, sometimes, around midday. Because of this the light is dim and diffuse and comes from everywhere at once, greenish through the vegetation, gray over the bare stone, and the lamps in the town are lit at all hours because it is easier than deciding.

The damp is not the damp of rain, though rain comes — brief, cold, sideways, gone. The damp here is condensation. It is a place where water arrives by touching things. Every surface above a certain height is a water-collector: the moss beads, the rails bead, the ropes bead, your coat beads, and by evening the whole plateau is quietly sweating. The gullies gather this. The gullies are the veins of the place, and they run with a thin quick water that appears and disappears, pooling in rock basins worn round as font-stones, dropping over shelves, soaking into the deep cracks. Pockets of water in a larger dryness is one way to say it, except the dryness is only the exposed knuckles, and there is not much of them.

In the gullies, lush. Lush to the point of saturation — a green that has been packed in. Dwarf cloud-forest in the deep cuts: trees no taller than a person, trunks twisted and mossed to the crown, their branches furred with lichen and hanging with the pale threads of things that live on fog. Ferns in the wet shade. Sphagnum in the flat-bottomed basins, holding water like a saturated sponge and releasing it slowly, so that the streams run clear and steady even in the dry weeks. Everything grows close and layered and touching. You cannot walk a gully floor without brushing three kinds of green off your shoulders.

The town is called Conder, and it is built into the largest of the gully systems the way sediment settles — following the low ground, terraced into the walls, layered. Roofs step down the valley in overlapping shelves of slate and tar; retaining walls hold back the moss and the slope; stair-lanes switchback down between houses that are half-built and half-carved, their back rooms being the rock itself. It is a town of maybe a few thousand, dense for its size, everything near-touching and stacked. Footpaths are wet stone. The gutters are channels. There is a mill at the bottom where the water finally gathers enough volume to turn things, and a scattering of workshops up the walls, and above it all the lattices.

The lattices are what Conder is for. Great frames of wire and mesh and strung fiber raised on the ridge-lines and across the gully mouths, angled into the prevailing fog, and they do nothing but stand there and be touched by cloud. The water beads, runs, gathers, drips into troughs and channels and pipes and comes down into the town, where it is measured. This is the whole economy and the whole law. Water rights in Conder are counted in drips per hour off a given lattice, and the old families hold the ones on the upper ridges where the fog is thickest, and the newer ones hold the scaffolds that only wet in a good year. There is a gauge-house with brass float-meters and a ledger, and a woman who reads it, and everyone knows her name. The industrial hum of the place is the hum of condensers and pumps and the mill, low and constant, and the hiss of fog against mesh.

The old rock breathes. This is the strangeness, and the people have stopped remarking on it. In the deepest cracks, where the gullies bottom out, warm damp air seeps up from the mountain's residual heat — the plateau is ancient but not cold all the way down, and it exhales. The seeps are warm, a few degrees above the air, and they smell of wet iron and old stone. The houses built over them are the warm houses; there are bath-houses; there are three greenhouses on the north wall of the town that grow things nobody else at this altitude can grow, and they steam. People arrange their lives around the exhalations the way other towns arrange theirs around a river. And there is a habit, among the oldest families, of saying the mountain knows where the water should go — because the fog does pool strangely here, gathering in specific hollows regardless of the wind's direction, and the gullies run where the fissures want them, and the whole drainage of the plateau feels less like erosion than like a thing remembering its shape.

The fauna is thin. A few pale moths in the lamplight. Water-beetles in the basins. Small brown birds that nest in the lattice frames and are tolerated. Eels in the millpond that came up somehow and stayed. In the upper gullies, where the moss is deepest and nobody goes, there is something larger and slow that browses the dwarf forest and leaves the ferns cropped in a neat line, and is seen perhaps twice a season, and is never photographed. And in the warm seeps there are creatures that live only in the warm water — flat, blind, unhurried things that the townspeople call embers and do not eat.

Sound is mostly quiet and mostly water: the drip off the lattices, the mutter of the mill, the hiss, the run of the gullies, footsteps on wet stone, a door. Voices carry oddly in the fog and are often the only sign of another person. A bell rings at the gauge-house when the flow crosses a threshold, and that is the town's clock.

It is a place that is active without being urgent. The rock has finished its long argument and settled. But the water never stops moving — condensing, gathering, running, soaking, rising warm and condensing again — and the moss grows and is cropped, and the fog comes in on its own schedule and pools where it likes, and the condensers drip their measured drips into the channels, and somewhere up the gully the slow thing browses the ferns in a line. Nothing here is in a hurry. Everything here is in motion.
