# The Foundry Plateau

There is a high place where the ground is warm through the sole of a boot, and the warmth is not the sun's.

It is a plateau the size of an empire, lifted so far that the sky has thinned to a rumor, and yet it is warm — warm from underneath, from a slow body of heat that the continent is lying on top of. Nothing about it is cold. Nothing about it is dry, either, though no sea is anywhere near. The moisture is its own: a standing haze that condenses on every surface and runs down in threads, gathers in the seams of the rock, pools in the low places that the young stone hasn't yet learned to drain. Rain comes in columns you can watch cross the distance — a gray leg walking over the plateau, then lifting, then setting down somewhere else. Between the columns the air is thick enough to drink.

The rock is new. You can see it: the scarps are still sharp, the fracture faces still glassy, black and blue-black where they've been split and not yet softened. Nothing here has been rounded off. The plateau lifted and split itself open maybe a hundred years ago, geologically speaking a birth-cry, and the plants have already come up over the wounds. Lush is too soft a word. The vegetation is *insistent*. It takes the raw stone as a surface to grip and does not wait for soil that doesn't exist yet; roots go into cracks and lever, and the levering is one of the ways the rock keeps breaking.

The color is wrong for a forest. Not green — iron-rust, bruise-violet, a coppery ochre that catches the little light there is and holds it. The leaves are broad, thick, waxy, and they drip continuously; every leaf is a condenser. From a distance, the canopy reads as a low ceiling of wet metal.

And it is dim. The sky is not clear anywhere. A lid of cloud sits on the plateau's shoulders and turns slowly, and through it comes a light like late afternoon in winter, though the air is warm as blood. Nothing casts a hard shadow. The eye gives up on distance and settles for nearness. Your own hands look tanned, then green, then tanned again, as the cloud above shifts. Sometimes the light *flickers* — a stutter, a dimming and returning, not lightning, something deeper in the weather's behavior, as though the whole plateau's illumination were being drawn through a failing filament.

Because — and this is the thing you notice second, after the warmth — the place is not wild the way a forest is wild. It is *organized*. It is doing work.

---

Under the canopy there are organs.

Call them trees if you like. They are hollow. Their interiors are flues, and they run hot; you can put a palm on a trunk and feel the temperature rise as you go up. They draw in damp air at the root and give it back at the crown, and the giving-back is visible — steam rising from the canopy in slow, steady plumes, hundreds of them, a city's worth of chimneys with no city beneath. Some of the growths are not trees but vessels: bladder-shaped, taut, creaking, taking in moisture until they are heavy and then releasing it all at once with a sound like a door closing in another room. The forest breathes. The whole plateau surface rises and falls, an inch or so, twice an hour, and it isn't the ground — it's everything growing on it.

There are pipes. Not made pipes; grown ones, vascular, mineral-crusted, running along the ground between the trunks, carrying warm fluid from the vents to the higher ground, and other vessels carrying it back down. There are heat exchangers: fans of thin plates, translucent, flushed with blood, opening and closing to the wind. There are settling basins, natural travertine terraces where the runoff pools and steams and leaves behind shelves of white and yellow and green sinter, and in those basins the water is being separated into what the plateau wants and what it doesn't. There are grinding places where the rock is being eaten — literally consumed, by mats of something that reduces stone to a slurry and extracts from it a dark, greasy metal that crusts the downstream surfaces in iridescent films.

Nothing here was built. No hands laid any of it. It grew into industry the way other places grow into forests, because on this plateau the winning strategy turned out to be *refining* — and once one organism learned to run a heat engine, everything else learned to plug into it.

---

The animals are the labor and the circulation.

They are small, mostly, and there are a great many of them, layered — a whole economy of bodies at every height, near-touching, so that the understory is never actually empty when you look. Mites in the vent mouths, farming a fuzz of pale fungus on the warm stone, harvesting it, carrying it. Ribbon-things that live inside the pipes and keep them clear, and whose passage you can hear as a wet clicking that runs away from you down the length of a vessel. Gliders with wide, thin membranes that ride the steam plumes from crown to crown and never touch the ground for days. Crawlers under the leaf litter moving mineral upward, grain by grain, in their bodies, from the low wet places to the high dry ones, and dying there, and being the soil. There are things that eat heat — that is the honest way to say it; they nest in the hottest vents and their bodies do something with the gradient.

Everything is going up. That is the rule of the place, and once you see it you cannot unsee it. Water goes up as vapor. Mineral goes up in bodies. Heat goes up through the flues. The plateau is an elevator, patient and continuous, and the storm is its exhaust.

Almost nothing comes down except rain.

---

The sound is the strange part, given how much is moving.

It is quiet — not silent, but the air is so thick and so high and so humid that sound dies in it quickly. A shout goes four meters and stops. The forest's industry is audible only as pressure: a low tone you feel in the sternum rather than hear, the breath of the bladders, the click of the ribbon-things, the sinter terraces trickling. When the wind comes through, it does not roar; it *sighs*, and the sigh arrives late, and the canopy's motion arrives after that, as though the place were running a fraction behind itself.

And the lightning is silent. That is the one that unsettles. The lid of cloud over the plateau churns with storms — real ones, violent ones, the whole turbulent machinery of a world's weather being generated in one place — and you see the flashes, wide and pale and frequent, and you hear nothing at all. The thunder is being spent somewhere above the cloud or below the ground; it never reaches you. You stand in a warm dripping forest and watch the sky flicker without a sound.

---

The rules here are not the ones you know, and they break in small, specific ways rather than all at once.

Things repeat. You will see a shape — a bladder-vessel, say, gnarled into a particular three-lobed silhouette — and then, a hundred meters on, the same shape again, and again, at different sizes, as though the plateau had one idea and could not stop applying it. Sometimes the repetition slips its scale: the fan of plates you saw at chest height returns as a structure spanning a valley. The place is a fractal with a stutter in it.

Time runs as a process rather than a passage. The storms come in cycles; the vents open and close on cycles; the whole surface pulses. Move with the cycle and the plateau feels like a single continuous operation, a factory running its shift. Break the cycle — stop, stand still, refuse to go up — and the place becomes incoherent, and your sense of how long you have been here goes with it. People who linger in the low wet basins report that the sinter terraces are building the same shelf over and over, that the water is separating into the same fractions, that they have watched this exact afternoon before.

And the light knows when you're looking. That is the rumor, and the rumor is the kindest version. What is true is that the flickering in the cloud-lid has a rhythm that is not weather, and that when it dims, the bioluminescence comes up from the understory to meet it — bruise-violet, rust-orange, a slow pulse moving through the mats and the fans and the wet roots — and the pulse moves *toward* you from all directions at once, converging, as if the plateau had noticed a new input and were routing it.

Nothing here was made. Nothing here is trying to make anything. And yet the whole vast warm dim dripping young and violent thing is unmistakably, unceasingly at work — an industry with no owner, no product, and no end, running its shifts under a silent storm, moving matter patiently upward, and occasionally, when the light stutters, turning just slightly to look at you.
