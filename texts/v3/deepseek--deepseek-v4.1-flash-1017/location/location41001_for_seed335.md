# The Breathing Sump

You would walk over it. That's the first thing. There's no approach, no vantage, no silhouette on the horizon — the marsh just continues, hummock after hummock of frost-heaved turf, and then the ground gives a soft, surprised little collapse under your boot and there it is, a hand's width below the peat surface: a hole the size of a dinner plate, exhaling warm air.

The pocket lies under the lip of a peat bank, a room-sized hollow maybe three meters across, roofed by a tangle of dead larch roots and a crust of frozen turf. It is very new. Last spring's thaw split the peat here, and the young basalt beneath it — a shallow blister of lava that never got far before the ice took it — broke along raw fracture lines. Nothing is rounded. Every edge in here is sharp enough to open a knuckle, and every sharp edge has already been claimed by moss in the first inch of its life: a green fuzz along the fracture lips, bright as paint on a cut. There has been no time for smoothing. The place is a wound that has decided to be a room.

Down the walls, water weeps. Not a stream — a thousand slow arrivals. Condensation beads on the cold stone, gathers, and lets go, and each bead runs a little way and is caught by moss or hangs and drops. The rock is black-grey where it's wet, pale where it's dry, so the walls are striped like something measured. Where the water has been longest, it has left stains: ochre, rust-orange, a sulfur yellow, a chalky bloom of white. It looks painted. It looks worked. It looks like the inside of something that has been running for years and has never once been serviced.

Because this is a factory, or as close as the world comes to one without anyone building it. The pocket has a piston. Somewhere below the floor — under the shallow sump of black water that fills the bottom third of the hollow — a fracture in the young rock takes meltwater from the bog above and deep warm water from a source nobody has ever seen. The two meet. Gas slugs. Pressure gathers in the crack, the water in the sump trembles, rises, and then the pocket *exhales*: a wet knock, a hiss, a gulp, and a fresh pulse of warm water slides up over the lip of the sump and runs back down into it. The cycle is about nine seconds. Nine seconds, all day, all night, whether anyone is here or not.

And it is loud. Absurdly loud for the size of it. The hollow is a resonator — a hard little chamber of wet basalt and taut root — and every sound in it arrives twice. The knock doubles and rolls. The drip off the roof is a tick, then a wooden *tonk* a half-second later. When the sump pulses, the whole space fills with a low rushing that isn't water falling anywhere, just water moving, and it sounds like a turbine heard through a wall. Under that, a drone: wind finding a fracture somewhere above and being tuned by it, a flat hum that never quite resolves. Below that, the springtails. A million tiny impacts on the water film, a static hiss like rain on a tin roof, except there is no tin.

The light is one slot. A gap in the turf roof, no wider than two fingers, admitting a blade of grey daylight that lands on the far wall and moves — slowly, over hours — across the rust and the moss, and by afternoon has found the sump and made a small bright coin of it. That is all the light there is. In the dim, the stains look like a diagram. The blade of light makes them look like a diagram being read.

The air is cold. Near freezing, most of the year, cold enough that your breath joins the fog already hanging in the hollow and the condensation on the walls is not only the rock's. But the water is warm. Not hot — a bath left standing an hour — and where it runs, it keeps a thread of warmth against the stone, and the moss grows in a ring around the sump like a fire circle. Everything in here is arranged around that ring. Cold room, warm floor. That's the rule.

The other rule is the clock. Nine seconds. The animals keep it. The amphipods in the sump ride the pulse up and let it carry them back, feeding on what the surge stirs loose. The caddis larvae in their cases of sand and root-bark have built on the walls at exactly the height the water reaches. The springtails on the film go still when the pulse gathers and scatter when it breaks, a whole trembling population moving in time, and if you watch them long enough you start to feel the interval in your teeth.

Fauna is abundant here and also nearly absent, and both are true at once. There are few individuals — you could count the beetles on one hand, the shrew visits alone, the dipper comes down the slot twice a day and leaves — but the variety is startling, and it arrives in pulses. For most of a week the hollow will hold six kinds of life at low number, and then a hatch comes off the sump and for two hours the air is thick with midges, the walls crawl, the water is a churn of larvae, and then it is over and the place is as hollow as a drum again. Vast spaces between presences. The pocket is intimate and it is also mostly empty, and the emptiness has a sound to it, because the piston keeps running through it.

Something unusual happens in the cold snaps. The young basalt, stressed and quartz-veined, takes a charge. It is faint enough that you only notice it as a lifting of the hair on your arms, as a taste of pennies in the fog, as a small dry spark when a drop leaves the roof and doesn't quite land where physics says it should. Nothing is hurt by it. It just makes the place feel more like a machine than a cave — like something with a coil in it, waiting.

And nothing here was made. That's worth saying plainly. No pipe, no brick, no bolt, nothing laid by hands. The factory is just what the ground does when warm water and cold air and young cracked stone are left alone together in a small enough room for long enough. It hums, it knocks, it vents, it stains itself ochre and yellow, it keeps nine-second time, and it produces — moss, midges, warm water, a coin of light that crosses the wall and goes out. Then the roof turf freezes harder in the evening, the slot narrows, the hum goes up a tone, and the piston keeps going, and the pocket goes on breathing under the snow, with nobody standing over it to hear.
