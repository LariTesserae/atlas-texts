# The Bowl of Hesk

You come upon it from above, which is the only way to come upon it. The plateau is pale stone and thin grass, cold enough that the wind has a bite, and then the ground simply drops — not a cliff, but a slumped, mossy lip, the whole thing maybe two hundred paces across. From the rim you look down into a brightness that seems wrong for the size of it.

The bowl is a sinkhole, and it is crowded.

## The light

The sun never climbs high here. It comes in low over the southern rim and floods the bowl in a single long slab of glare that lasts most of the day, broken only by a short, cold dusk that comes on fast. The light is white and hard and it does not warm you. Shadow is short and stays put; you can stand in the same spot at dawn and at noon and your shadow will barely have moved. On the north wall the shadow is permanent, and that is where the moss is thinnest and the stone shows.

Everything in the bowl is lit twice: once by the sun, and once by the sun bouncing off the pale bowl wall opposite. The whole place is bright the way a snowfield is bright, and just as cold.

## The damp

There is a seep at the bottom, one spring, and it does not vary. It comes up through a cracked stone basin and runs in threads a finger wide through channels cut by hands a very long time ago, into a series of shallow stone tanks, and then down into the moss and gone. The air holds the water. Nothing here is wet to the touch unless you press it, but everything is damp: the stone is damp, the wood is damp, your wool gets heavy by afternoon. Condensation beads on the undersides of roofs and on the handles of tools. There is a smell of wet stone and cold green.

## The town

The Hesk is built into the bowl itself — terraced, stacked, driven in. Houses of split stone and root-wood are wedged into the slope on both sides, and above them more houses sitting on those houses, so the bowl's wall has become a single continuous built face, rooms behind rooms, ladders between, hatchways in floors. There is no street. There is a narrow switchback path and a great many ladders, and people hand things up and down rather than carry them along. Roofs are pitched shallow and weighted with stone. Doors are low and the doorways rounded, the jambs worn into curves by centuries of shoulders.

The technique is hand-work all through. Stone split with wedges, not sawn; wood worked with antler and bone and a little soft copper that is hammered, never cast. Joints are pegged or lashed. Nothing is nailed. The stones of the path are rounded like river cobbles from being walked on, not from water.

It is old and it is settled. Nothing in the Hesk is under construction. Nothing is falling down. Every wall has been the same height for longer than anyone counts, and the repairs — and there are always repairs — are done in exactly the manner of the original, because no other manner exists.

## The green

The moss is the dominant fact of the place. It covers the bowl wall above the houses and the tank rims and the roofs in a thick, cold, saturated green that is almost aggressive. Ferns grow in the seep channels. Small twisted trees, no taller than a person, grow out of the terraces and are pruned to shape and have been pruned to shape for so long that they have forgotten how to be any other shape. In the tanks, watercress and something pale and frilled that is harvested with the fingers.

The lushness is not generous. It is dense and damp and patient, and it grows because the seep does not stop, not because the sun is kind.

## The fauna, and the silence

The bowl is thick with animals. Midges in columns over the tanks. Small brown birds that nest in the eaves and in the moss and move constantly, flicking from roof to roof. A fat grey moth that rests flat on the stone and does not fly when you approach. Mites in the moss, so many that a handful of it is half animal. Fish in the tanks, pale, slow. Something that comes out at dusk and is heard — no. Nothing is heard.

That is the oddness of the Hesk, and anyone who stays a day feels it before they can name it. There are hundreds of birds and the place is silent. The moss eats the sound. The shape of the bowl eats the sound. Footsteps do not echo. A dropped tool makes a flat dead clack and is over. Voices come out of your mouth smaller than you meant them. The birds open their beaks and nothing arrives. You find yourself whispering, then find that whispering is no quieter than speaking, and that neither carries past a few paces.

People here have learned to touch each other to get attention. They knock on the stone — two knocks, pause, one — rather than call.

## The rules of the place

The Hesk is in equilibrium, and the equilibrium is exact. The seep yields what the tanks hold and the tanks hold what the town drinks. The town is the size the water allows and no larger and has not been larger or smaller in living memory or in the memory of the walls. When someone dies a room stands empty for a while and then is occupied, and it is always the same room.

Nothing is exported because nothing comes in. There is no road up the bowl wall; there is a rope and a worn notch and a system of hauling that a person learns as a child.

And the seasons barely touch it. The cold is a constant, not a season. The light is a constant, not a season. The green is a constant. A visitor who stays a month and comes back a decade later will find the same moss on the same stones, the same pruned trees, the same low doors, the same flat silence, the same slab of white light lying across the bowl at the same angle.

It is not a stagnant place. It is a finished one, or one that has arrived and has no intention of going anywhere. It holds its people the way the tanks hold the water: exactly, and without spilling.
