# The Great Carpet of Veth

There is no water here. Not a drop, not a memory of it. Yet the ground is green.

Veth is a world of low, rolling rises—you would not call them hills, not quite—stretching in every direction to a horizon that keeps receding as you approach it. The land is not flat; it swells and settles, swells and settles, like a slow breath taken over kilometers. From any vantage you can climb (and there are few enough of those), you see green to the edge of sight, then another band of green beyond that, faint with distance.

## Terrain and Materials

The surface is not soil in any sense you would recognize. It is a fabric—a woven mat of living tissue, meters deep, that covers the entire visible world. The mat is built by the carpet-weavers: colonial organisms the size of your thumb that extrude fibrous strands in graded colors, binding dust and mineral grit into a flexible, load-bearing sheet. They work endlessly, tirelessly, at a pace too slow to see but fast enough (over seasons) that paths close up behind travelers, that a fallen pillar is half-swallowed in a year.

The mat has grain. It has nap. Step on it and it gives slightly, then firms. In places the weave is coarse and springy; in others it is dense and smooth as packed felt. Its colors run from deep bottle-green through yellow-green to a pale blue-green in the low, damp-feeling hollows, and the pattern shifts with the age of the weave, so the land reads like a vast and patient tapestry perpetually revising itself.

Beneath the mat, bedrock—layered and striated, but shallow. The mat is not deep. It is a skin, not a soil. Drill down three or four meters and you hit cold stone. This is a world still coming into being. The tectonic forces have not finished their work; the land flexes, rises, settles, splits. Cracks appear in the mat, and the weavers knit them shut. New mineral wells up along fault lines in chalky ridges—raw geology, unweathered, sharp-edged, too new to have been rounded. There are no rivers because there is no water, but there are channels: broad, shallow swales where the mat is thin and the stone is close, and through these channels flows a slow tide of something else.

## The Air and the Atmosphere

The atmosphere is thick, warm, and carries a weight you feel in your chest. It smells of cellulose and faint ammonia, of crushed green matter, of hot stone and something resinous and animal. The light is strong and diffuse—a white-gold sun of respectable size but unfocused heat, filtered through a permanent high haze of airborne fibers and dust. Shadows are soft and green-tinged. At midday the whole world seems to glow from within, the mat transmitting light a little, so that the ground itself is luminous in the way a leaf held to the sun is luminous.

The air moves. It is never still. Great slow gyres of wind circle the world, driven by the temperature difference between the warm equator and the cooler poles (there are poles; I have not seen them, but the winds tell of them). These winds carry the fibers—the weavers' reproductive threads, the cast-off strands of the mat, the spores of the canopy forms. The air is a medium of transport and exchange. Nothing here is isolated. The wind is the circulatory system of the world.

## Sounds

Sound is almost absent. Not silent—there is always the sub-audible hum of the mat itself, a deep and constant vibration as billions of weavers work, which you feel in your feet and in your teeth before you hear it. And there are the wind sounds: the high thin whistle of the fibers, the low moan of the swales, the rattling hiss when the wind combs through a stand of loft-forms. But there are no birds. No insects that sing. The fauna of Veth is quiet—soft-footed, slow-moving, deep-voiced in registers too low or too high for easy hearing. The loudest thing in the landscape is a falling pillar, and pillars do not fall often.

## The Canopy Forms

The vegetation is not trees. Or rather, it is trees the way the mat is soil—the same word, a different category. The tall forms on Veth are columns: single stalks, arm-thick at the base, rising straight for ten, twenty, thirty meters, unbranched, each one topped with a crown of stiff, fibrous fronds that catch the light. They grow in stands, in groves, in forests that stretch for hundreds of kilometers, and they are spaced with a regularity that looks deliberate but is not—it is the resource competition of the mat, the weavers refusing to thicken the ground beneath a column that has claimed its patch.

The columns are hollow. Inside each one is a columnar ecosystem: pale, soft-bodied climbers that never see the sun, that feed on the column's inner tissue and on each other, that live and die in a vertical world sealed away from the wind. The column itself is a single organism, or once was—it is not clear whether the inner ecology is symbiont or parasite or simply opportunist. The columns do not seem to die. They grow, they thicken, they lean, and eventually they fall, and when they fall the mat swallows them over the course of decades, and the inner climbers are released into the open air and die, and the weavers break down the wood into fiber.

## The Fauna

Life is abundant and it is everywhere. It is in the mat—a whole interstitial fauna of burrowers and browsers, things the size of a hand and things the size of a curl of hair, moving through the weave in slow channels, eating the mat, being eaten by the mat. It is in the columns. It is in the air—the fibers carry riders, tiny colonial things that drift on the wind and settle and grow, and the wind is full of them, so that to breathe is to inhale a thin broth of life.

The larger fauna are grazers and browsers, quadrupedal or hexapodal or something in between, low-slung and patient, moving in herds along the swales where travel is easiest. They are not fast. Nothing here is fast. The pace of the world is slow and the pace of its creatures matches it. There are predators—large, solitary, soft-footed, with wide flat feet that distribute weight on the mat and a habit of stillness—and there is a great deal of quiet, patient watching.

## The Built World

And there is the city. I have not told you about the city.

It is not a city in the sense of walls and streets and doors. It is a pattern in the mat, a regularity of form that extends for kilometers, that is clearly not weaver-made but is made of weavers and for them. The mat here is thickened, layered, built up into terraces and platforms and low towers, woven in precise rhythmic patterns that repeat and vary and repeat. The entire structure is alive—it grows, it repairs itself, it is tended. Passageways run through it at the scale of a human body, and larger passageways at the scale of a herd, and the surfaces are smooth and worn with use.

The columns here grow in arcs and rings, planted and trained into alignment, their crowns interwoven to form a high canopy that filters the light into a green dimness. The air moves differently here—slower, channeled, warm. The sound is the sound of a thousand small things working.

Who built it? The weavers, or something that directs the weavers, or something that once directed them and is gone. The city is old. It is being added to. It is in no way finished. And it is empty of any presence that would call it a city—the weavers do not live in it, not exactly; they live in the mat and the mat is everywhere, and this portion of the mat has simply been shaped. But the shaping is not random and not utilitarian. It is ornamental. It is aspirational. It is a great slow work of art, self-repairing, self-extending, and there is no one here to see it at all.

## Rules of the World

- Nothing is mineral unless it is stone. Everything else is grown.
- There is no water. The mat is self-sealing. It loses moisture by breathing and recaptures it by condensation on the tall crowns, which drip nothing—the moisture is absorbed directly. Water as a liquid does not exist on Veth; it moves as vapor and is gathered directly by the tissues that need it.
- Nothing decays into soil. The weavers reclaim everything. There is no fossil record, no sediment. The past is eaten.
- The world is young but the ecology is old. The land is still forming; the mat has had time to establish, to evolve, to build. The two processes are concurrent. Veth is being made and has already been made, and the evidence of both is everywhere in the ripples and flexures of a landscape that is still settling into itself.
- Distance is large. Weather is local but the gyres are planetary. There are storms of wind-borne fiber that darken the sky and coat everything in a fine green dust and then are absorbed. There are still periods. There are periods of great wind.

## What It Feels Like

To stand on Veth is to stand on something alive and patient and enormous. The ground is warm. It pulses very slightly with the work of the weavers, a vibration below the threshold of hearing that you feel in your spine. You are surrounded by green, by light filtered green, by a green horizon dissolving into a green haze. The air is thick and smells of living matter. You are not alone—the mat is full of movement, the columns are full of movement, the air is full of riders—but the movement is quiet and slow and it does not acknowledge you. The world is busy and it is silent and it is making itself more itself, meter by meter, season by season, and you are standing on a vast and unfinished work that has no author and no audience and does not require either.
