# The Kept Shore

High on the mountain there is a crack that is not worth noticing, and behind it a chamber that should not fit inside the mountain.

You enter crouched. The passage is cold and narrow and smells of nothing at all — not stone, not dust, not the iron tang of the deep. Then the floor drops away gently and you are standing on a beach.

It is thirty paces across. It is a shoreline. There is a flat of pale, granular mineral — not salt, though it looks like salt, grains faintly translucent, faintly blue at their hearts, that crunch and shift underfoot with a sound like the smallest possible glass. It slopes down into a shallow basin floored with finer material, and around that basin the sediment is arranged in the unmistakable architecture of water: rills braiding toward the low point, a fan of a delta where a channel met the flat, a ridge of sorted grains marking a high-water line, a scatter of rounded pebbles that have been rolled and rolled and rolled. The walls of the chamber rise in scalloped shelves, smoothed and undercut, the way a sea undercuts a cliff.

There has never been water here. There is no water here. The air is dry past dryness — a dryness you can feel pulling at your eyes. And yet the place is a coast, and it is a coast still being made. If you lie down and watch a single rill, you will see a grain move. Two grains. A grain a minute, downhill, in the direction the water would have carried it, following a slope defined by a sea that isn't there.

## The light

The chamber has no opening but the crack behind you, and no sky. It is roofed in black rock a long stone's throw up, and yet it is lit — dim, even, sourceless, the color of a winter afternoon an hour before dark. The light seems to come from the air itself, or from the pale floor, or from nowhere; there are no shadows in the usual sense. Objects rest on the ground without darkening it. You will notice, after a while, that your own shadow is not missing but *wrong* — a soft greyness that lies toward the center of the chamber regardless of where you stand, as though the light had a preference.

It is cold, but not bitterly. The cold is even, patient, unvarying — a cold that has settled in and has no intention of leaving. It is not the cold of altitude. It is the cold of a room that has been closed for a very long time. In the low channels the cold is thicker. You can feel its surface against your ankle like the skin of a pool. Wading into a channel is wading into cold that has depth and a boundary, and nothing else.

## The pins

Scattered across the flat, maybe a dozen of them, are the only made things. They are thin black blades, knee-high, set upright in the sediment at irregular intervals — some tilted, one fallen and half-buried. The grains have drifted around their bases into small dunes, which means they have been here long enough for the place to have grown around them.

They are warmer than the air. Not by much. They are the only warm things in the chamber, and the warmth does not fade with distance from them, only stops. They cast no shadows, and they make no sound, and they are not connected to anything, and they are not, in any sense you could defend, machines — but when you put your palm near one you feel a faint, fine pressure, like the pressure in your ears before weather, and you understand that something is being *held*.

The whole chamber has the quality of a thing being held. Kept. A coast, kept — maintained in its coastness by a handful of black pins that have been warm for a very long time.

## The tide

Once in a long while, the tide comes in.

It takes hours. You feel it first as a change in the pressure, a slow swell of not-quite-sound. The blades brighten very faintly, like coals breathed on. Then a front moves across the flat — invisible, but not undetectable: the cold thickens and rolls, and the grains lift and tumble and settle into new arrangement, and the rills run. Roughened patches smooth. New braids open. A ridge of sorted grains builds itself a finger's width higher and stops.

The front passes over you and there is a moment — brief, unambiguous — in which you are *wet*. Not damp. Wet. A total, cell-deep wetness, the wetness of standing in surf, and the air is dry the whole time, and your clothes are dry, and nothing on you is wet. Then the front moves on, and the flat behind it is rearranged and dry and has never had water on it, and the tide goes out the way it came.

The tide is not on a schedule you can learn. It comes, and it takes its hours, and it goes, and then the flat is still for a long time.

## The room that does not agree

The chamber does not hold still about its own size. Pace it and it is thirty paces. Pace it again and it is thirty-four, then twenty-nine, then thirty-one, converging on something it will not tell you. The roof is sometimes close and sometimes far. The crack you came in by is where you left it, but the walk back to it takes a slightly different number of steps each time. None of this is violent. Nothing flickers or shrieks. The room simply declines to be a fixed quantity and has been declining for a very long time, and has settled into it, the way the rock has settled into its scalloped and undercut shape.

There are other small disagreements. Sound arrives slightly late; a footfall lands and then, a half-beat after, lands again. The cold and the light do not agree about which way is down. If you sit still long enough you will begin to remember standing on this shore in weather — spray, wind, the particular grey of a sea fog — and the memory will have the texture of a real one, and it will not be yours.

## Sound and time

Almost none. The chamber is quieter than quiet; it has the silence of a place with no wind, no water, no animals, nothing that moves on its own. What there is, is small: the settling tick of grains, the click of the rock contracting in the cold, your own blood. Under it, if you listen past the point where listening stops being useful, a very low continuous tone, felt more than heard, the sound of something being held up.

Nothing here is fast. The rills advance by grains. The tide takes hours and comes rarely. The dunes around the pins have grown over centuries at the pace of dust. If you came back in a hundred years you would find the same chamber, thirty-two paces across, a little more deeply undercut, the pins a little more buried, the tide still arriving on its unlearnable schedule and still, for a few minutes each time, making the dry air wet.

It is a small place, and a patient one, and it is wrong in a way that has been wrong for so long that it has become a kind of rightness. It is a shore without a sea, kept by warm black pins, under a stone roof, lit by nothing, eroding into its own idea of a coastline. Stand at the delta and look out across the flat and you will feel the pressure change, and know the tide is coming, and know you have hours, and wait.

There is no wind. There is no water. There is no one. The grains move anyway.
