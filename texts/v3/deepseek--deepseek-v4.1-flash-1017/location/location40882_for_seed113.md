# KETTLE

The survey office calls it Kettle, for the sound.

You come down through a throat of rock that is still damp on the inside, still slick — the walls sweat. The stair is cut in one piece, no seams, treads a hand-span too deep for comfortable walking and a full pace too wide between landings, so you descend in an awkward half-lunge, your lamp throwing a shivering coin of light that never quite reaches the next turn. Warm air comes up to meet you. It smells of wet stone and cut greenery and something sweet going over — fruit left too long in a warm room. By the fortieth step your clothes are clinging. By the hundredth you have stopped noticing your own sweat, because the whole world is sweating.

The stair lets out onto a gallery over a street, and the street is where the noise lives.

---

**Terrain.** The cavern is new. You can see it in the rock: no rounded shoulders, no patient polish, nothing worn. The walls break in crisp planes and sharp arrises, fracture faces bright as a broken plate, and where the roof has shed its first skin of grit you can see the raw bedding planes still holding their original geometry, as though the room had been opened by a knife last month and would still ring if you struck it. The ceiling is not far above the rooftops and it is not smooth; it drips everywhere, in a staggered irregular percussion, and the drops are warm.

The floor of the cavern is a shallow basin, and the basin is full — not with a lake, but with a town standing in water up to its ankles and sometimes its knees. Channels run through everything, cut channels, deliberate channels, with crisp lips and clean throats; they carry a fast tea-coloured current from a source you cannot see toward a sink you cannot see. Every so often the current stumbles over a drop and adds its voice to the general noise. Between the channels, the ground is a mosaic of flat wet stone with a tread pattern still sharp on it, as if the masons had walked off this morning.

**Built.** It is a town. That must be said plainly, because everything else about it argues otherwise. There are plazas, stairways, arcades, lintels, balconies over the water, a public well with a carved coping, gutters that run true, an aqueduct that crosses the whole basin on three tall thin piers and does not waver. There are doorways. There are windows with sills. There is a square with a low bench in it.

And there is no one. There has never been anyone. The town has room for — the survey's guess — several thousand, and it holds none, and the emptiness is not the emptiness of a place abandoned, which is full of small wreckage. It is the emptiness of a place that was finished and never occupied. The bench is dry on top. Nobody has ever sat on it long enough to wear it.

The distances are the strange part. You walk a long time between things. A single lamp on a single bracket will light a hall the size of a market and the hall will hold that one lamp and a step and nothing else, and beyond it another hall, and another, each with one bench or one basin or one hook, and the spaces between them so vast and so clean that your footsteps come back to you late and altered. The town is built for a crowd and furnished for a hermit.

**Material and making.** The construction is of one substance, and that substance is the bedrock, persuaded. Walk to the edge of the finished town — it is not far, the town is small and the raw cave is smaller — and you find the working face: a soft wet zone, a hand's width, where the stone is neither stone nor shape but something between, warm to the touch, faintly yielding, faintly humming. Beyond it the cave wall, untouched, sharp, wet. Behind it the town: seamless. There are no mortar lines. The arch and its pier and the lintel and the floor are the same continuous grain, quarried in place and re-ordered in place, atom by atom, along paths that follow the water. The channels are not cut into the floor; the floor grew around the channels.

It advances. You can watch it if you are patient: the wet zone creeps a finger's width in an hour, and behind it the surface dulls from glossy to matte over the course of a day, like a glaze curing. The town is being built at the rate of a slow afternoon. It is nearly done. In the oldest quarter the matte has begun to take on a faint bloom where the condensation sits, and that is the only patina in Kettle.

Everything is plumbed. The town is organised around water the way other towns are organised around roads: every structure has an intake and a drain, every room a gutter, every gutter a destination. The water is warm — a little above body heat — and where it runs fast over a lip it gives up a pale cold light, so that the channels are the town's only streetlamps, and they are dim, and they are the colour of weak tea.

**Light.** There is no sun and no substitute for one. Kettle exists in a permanent late dusk, lit by three weak things at once: the luminous water, the luminous planting, and the fact that the stone itself is pale enough to hold a grudge of light and give it back slowly. Colours go to amber and olive. Shadows are long and soft and never entirely leave. You will find yourself leaning toward the channels to read.

**Ecology.** The planting is lush and it is everywhere and it is entirely alone. It was set deliberately — you can see the intent, the terraces, the trained vines on the trained trellises, the hanging gardens slung under the aqueduct like washing — and then it was let go, or it was never tended, and it has gone gloriously feral in a warm wet dark. Mats of pale moss climb the walls in sheets and swallow the sharp edges. Flat-leaved things with no need of depth crowd the gutters. There are stalks taller than a person with heads of seed that never blow anywhere useful. There are fruit trees in the plaza, in tubs, and their fruit is ripe and falling and there is nothing at all to eat it. It rots in the channel-lips and sweetens the air. It seeds itself into the cracks. The next generation is already up and crowding its parent.

Nothing eats anything in Kettle. There are no insects in the air, no fish in the channels, no birds, no rats in the granaries — there are no granaries. The ecology is one half of an ecology, the producing half, running at full tilt with no consumer to close the loop. It is the loudest kind of lush: unrestrained, unpruned, overripe, profligate, all of it aimed at a future that will not arrive. Pollen drifts in the warm air and settles on the water and goes out with the current. Somewhere below, the sink is filling with it.

The rule of the place is simple and nobody has had to enforce it, because nobody has ever come to test it: nothing with a nervous system lives here. The survey's instruments say it plainly. The air is warm, wet, breathable, and — the word in the report is *unwelcoming* — it will not keep an animal. Caged specimens brought down for a shift grow quiet and then still. It is not poison. It is more like a room that has not yet been asked to make space for you.

**Sound.** Kettle is loud, and it is loud in the way a house is loud at night when you are the only one in it. Water, constantly, from every direction and at every pitch: the flat hiss of the channels, the kettledrum of drips on hollow stone, the long boom of a fall somewhere in the dark that you will never find, and under all of it the resonance of the cavern itself, tuned by its own sharp fresh shape into a low continuous hum that swells when the current rises and sinks when it falls. Add the working face: a sound like a kettle just before it sings, at the very edge of hearing, all the time, from the direction of the future.

And every so often — a dropped seed-head, a stone shedding its first grit, a gutter overflowing its lip — the whole basin will take the sound and carry it away and bring it back, and for a moment the town sounds inhabited.

**Rules of the world.** Water is the clock. When the source above runs heavy, the channels rise, the luminous lips brighten, the working face advances faster, and the hum climbs; when the source thins, the town dims and the building pauses and the whole basin waits with the patience of a filled vessel. The town is not abandoned. It is *unoccupied*, which is different: it is being made ready, on schedule, by something that has not yet finished and has not yet said who it is making ready for.

**Atmosphere.** You stand on the gallery with your lamp and look down at a small wet lit town with sharp new edges and nobody in it, and warm water running through its streets, and a garden going over into fruit and rot and seed, and the hum of it all around you, and the fine warm drizzle of the roof coming down on the roofs; and you understand that you are early — absurdly, insultingly early — and that the correct thing to do is to leave quietly and come back in a year, or ten, or whenever the wet zone has stopped moving and the first door is opened, and see who walks in.
