# The Weeping Shelf

It is a shoulder of stone hung high enough that the weather has mostly been left below. You come up through cloud and come out of it wet, and then the light hits you — a hard, white, thin-air light that has nothing between it and the rock, so that every wet surface is a blade of glare. The rock is dark: slate-black, iron-red in the seams, bone-pale where it has been scrubbed. Everything is glazed. There is no green anywhere, not a stain of it, not a thread. The stone looks scoured, as if something has gone over it patiently with a cloth for a very long time.

The Shelf is not flat. It is a broad terrace of stepped ledges and shallow basins, tilted a few degrees, with a rim that falls away into cloud on three sides. Water is everywhere but there is no river. Water arrives as film and thread and seep: a skin of it sliding down the tilted faces, a hundred hairline channels cut into the stone and running with a glassy cord each, pools in the hollows no wider than a hand and pools the size of a room, all of them brimming and all of them overflowing in slow, deliberate sheets. High air is dry air, and this much water at this height should not be here; it condenses out of the sky constantly, and out of the rock itself, and out of the air on the underside of every overhang, where it beads and hangs and drips. Some of it drips upward. Not often. Not dramatically. A drop will hesitate, swell, and travel the wrong way along the lip of a ledge, and you will watch it and think you mistook which way was down, and then you will look at your own boots and find that you did not.

The sound is the first thing that convinces you the place is inhabited, and the last thing that convinces you it is not. Water over stone at this density is loud — a continuous hiss and clatter and patter, a thousand small mouths talking, water into water, water onto slab, water down the channels like strings being plucked. Under it, a boom. Slow, deep, regular, felt in the soles before it is heard: some cavity or throat in the rock taking the falling water and drumming with it. The interval is not steady. It lengthens and shortens with the mist. And the echoes do not behave. A sound you make comes back to you early — before you have finished making it — so that your own footfall arrives as a question and is answered by the step you have not yet taken. Sounds come up from below the rim as if from just behind you: a cascade you cannot see, a hollow knocking, once what is unmistakably the creak of a rope under load, though nothing on the Shelf is strung or loaded or has been for a long time.

There was building here. Not much, and not grand, and not recent. Dry-stone: low walls that follow the contour, a hand's width of stacked slab laid without mortar, now rounded at the top by centuries of water. Cairns, some knee-high, some taller than a person, every stone of them worn smooth and each cairn slightly wrong in its balance. Channels cut by hand into the living rock, running from basin to basin, still doing their work, their edges softened into lips. A floor of fitted slabs, half a hut, the rest scattered downhill. Postholes with grey splinters in them. A frame of wood gone to silver and shreds. Braided hide, once, now a black cord on a stone. You will find a tool: a wedge of tough stone, a hammer of the same, a bowl ground into a boulder until it is deep enough to hold a head. No metal. Nothing that was ever sharp for long. Whoever worked this rock did it with patience and hands and the water's own channels, and then stopped, and the Shelf went on rounding their work into itself until the walls and the ledges and the natural steps were of a piece, and you could no longer say confidently where the mountain ended and the making began.

That is the rule of the place: it is in balance, and the balance is not yours. The erosion here is not damage; it is agreement. Everything has settled into the shape the water wanted, and the water has settled into the shape the stone wanted, and the two have been married so long that neither is the author. The basins hold what they hold. The channels run where they were cut and also where they were not. The whole terrace agrees with itself, and if you try to walk it as a map you will find the agreement excludes you.

Because distances are not kept here. A stretch of the Shelf that you crossed at midday will be ahead of you again in the afternoon, intact and wet and unmistakable, and this time it will take you an hour to cross what took you ten minutes, or ten minutes to cross what took an hour, and there will be no accounting for it. Reflections in the still basins do not show the sky you are under; they show a softer light, a lower cloud, weather that is not happening. Pools sit brim-full in basins that are visibly tilted and do not spill, held by some surface tension that has nothing to do with water. The mist comes up in banks, thick enough to blind, and goes, and when it goes the light is brighter than it was and the boom has changed its pace. The place is dense with small presences — every hollow has its pool, every ledge its thread, every rise its cairn, every surface its glaze and glare and running — so that standing anywhere on the Shelf you are within arm's reach of a dozen separate small systems all going at once, and the air between them is full of the noise of them.

Nothing lives here. This is not a dead place; it has never been the kind of place that would have had life and lost it. There is no rot, no smell of green, no insect, no bird, no burrow. The smell is mineral and cold and clean, wet stone and iron. The only motion is water, mist, light, and the slow traveling of the boom through the rock. Whatever the Shelf is doing — and it is doing something, measuring or keeping or waiting, the channels like a scribble of notation, the cairns like markers set for a reader — it is doing it without witness, and has been for a very long time, and will go on. You can stand at the rim with the cloud boiling below you and the white light on the wet black steps and the water talking and the drum going under it, and feel that you have walked into the middle of a sentence in a language that is all nouns, and that the sentence does not require you to finish it.
