The place has no name that fits in a mouth. Call it the Salt Spire, if you need to call it anything.

It rises from a dead sea — not water, but a sea of fine, hot dust that behaves like water, shifting in slow waves under a sky the color of a bruised peach. The Spire is a single stone column, three or four kilometers high, its base lost in the dust-sea's haze. It is not a landmark; it is a world. The dust-sea, when the wind is right, throws up a thin, stinging mist that coats everything in a film of salt. This is the rain here. It condenses at night, when the stone cools just enough, and beads on the surfaces like sweat. By midday it is gone.

The Spire itself is a city, but not in any way you would recognize. It is a honeycomb, a termite mound built by something that thought in iron and glass. The stone — a dark, veined basalt, warm to the touch even in the pre-dawn — is pierced by thousands of irregular openings, horizontal and vertical, connected by a lattice of exterior pipes and gantries. The pipes are the color of dried blood and they carry steam, always steam, the Spire's only weather. The steam vents from the higher levels in a constant, silent exhalation, drifting down and dissolving into the dust-sea's dry air before it reaches the ground. This is why the place is damp in its way: a permanent, rising humidity that clings to the metal and leaves dark, weeping stains on the stone. The air tastes of hot iron and old brine.

The density is staggering. The city is not built out; it is built *in*. Every few meters of vertical ascent is a new terrace, a new tier of habitation and industry. The architecture is unplanned, agglutinative — an accretion of centuries, level upon level, each one built from the rubble of the one below it. The structures are mostly low, flat-roofed, pieced together from salvaged plates and cast-off machine parts. It is a shantytown of industry. You can see where a great gear has been used as a floor, where a turbine housing has been repurposed as a communal oven. The sound is a low, pervasive hum — the vibration of distant machinery, the gurgle of the pipes, the occasional metallic groan of a terrace settling. It is never loud, but it is never silent. It is the sound of a place that is still, very slowly, being born.

There is no vegetation. There is no soil. There is only the Spire, the dust, and the people. And what people — they are as adapted as the architecture. They are thin, tall, with skin the color of the dust and eyes that have learned to see in the perpetual dimness of the lower tiers. They wear filters over their mouths against the salt. They are quiet, deliberate. They move through the gantries and ladders with the unhurried certainty of things that have nowhere else to be. They are not many. The Spire could hold thousands; it holds perhaps a few hundred. Their presence is felt more than seen, a flicker in a high window, a shadow on a distant stair.

The industry is what built the Spire and what keeps it alive. It is not the industry of production but of *maintenance*. The great machines, the engines that power the city's heat and light, are ancient and failing. They are tended by a class of engineers who are also priests, who coax the failing turbines with ritual and repair. The heart of the Spire is a vast, open shaft near its peak, glowing with a dull orange light from the furnace-heat of the main engine. The smoke from this engine is the city's only cloud. The rules are simple: keep the engine running, and the Spire lives. Let it fail, and the dust-sea will eventually find its way in.

The elevation is the defining fact of existence. The lower tiers are hot, choked with dust and the smell of rot and salt. The higher you climb, the cooler and cleaner it gets, but the more precarious. The wind is stronger up there, a constant, dry howl that threatens to tear the gantries from the stone. The highest terraces are mostly abandoned, their structures half-eaten by the abrasive salt-wind, a monument to an attempt to build higher than the ecosphere allowed.

Yet there is water. Not in rivers or pools, but in the veins of the Spire itself. The stone is porous, and deep within it, there are reservoirs of hot, mineral-rich water, under immense pressure. The city taps these veins with drills and pipes, drawing the water up to cool and to drink. It is this water that makes the place possible, and it is this water that makes the place strange. In the deepest, abandoned shafts, where the drills have broken into a major vein, the water pools in the dark. And in those pools, there is life — not plants, but something simpler. A film of pale, glowing bacteria, or something like it, that feeds on the minerals and the heat. It is the only life native to the Spire, and it is beautiful in a way that has nothing to do with comfort. It clings to the rock in sheets of soft, blue-green light, and in the silence of the deep places, you can watch it grow, a millimeter a year.

The people do not go to the deep places. They say the water there is bad. But sometimes, a child will find a droplet of it on a pipe higher up, and it will glow faintly in their palm, a tiny, living star in a world of salt and stone and steam. That is the Spire's only magic. It is enough.
