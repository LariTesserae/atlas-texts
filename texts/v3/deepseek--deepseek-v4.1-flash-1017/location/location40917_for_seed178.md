# The Kettle

The valley is new and it is not finished. You can see that before you can see anything else, because the fog doesn’t hide the shape of it — it translates it. The fog is a ceiling of warm breath over cold ground, and through it come the shapes of raw, grey, angled stone: scarps with no soil on them yet, fresh slabs tilted at the angle a landslide left them, waterfall-cut notches that were not there last season. Nothing here has been rounded off. The rain and the river have only just started their work, and the river is winning faster than anyone expected, because the river is the reason the valley exists at all. The floor is still going down. Somewhere beneath the moss and the gravel there is a fault with an opinion, and the opinion is *wider*.

The sound arrives before the geography does, and it never leaves. It is not one roar; it is a stacked, argumentative mass of roars — a dozen waterfalls the size of buildings, a braided channel that splits and rejoins and splits again, water hammering into plunge pools, gravel banks slumping, boulders grinding along a bed they have not yet learned. Underneath all that, lower and constant, is the hiss and clatter of condensation: the entire valley is dripping. Water springs out of the warm ground at thirty-five degrees and hits air at three, and the fog it makes is so thick that ten metres away a person is a suggestion. You do not look at this place first. You listen to it, and your ears give you a map that your eyes can only confirm.

Light is a rumour. There is a sun, presumably; there are hours when the grey above the fog goes from charcoal to pewter, and that is midday. On a good evening the fog lifts twenty metres and the valley becomes briefly enormous — a low green trough running away to the north, steaming along its whole length like something freshly opened, walls black and wet and close on either side. Then the lid comes down again. In the dark hours the only lights are the water’s: the warm springs glowing faintly with their own heat, faint orange seams of reflected nothing, and — further up the valley, where the gradient steepens — a row of small pale lights that are not natural and have not been visited in some time.

The ground is a patchwork of temperatures, and that is the first rule of the world here: the warm side and the cold side. Along the fault, the water comes up hot; the moss there is thick as a mattress, green that has never needed sunlight to be green because the heat feeds it and the fog waters it. Two hundred metres away, out of the warm water’s reach, the pools have ice rims and the gravel is frozen into a crust that crunches and then gives. You can walk from summer to winter and back in a minute. The warm moss is hot to the hand and full of insects. The cold pools are so clear you can watch a fish turn over.

The vegetation is dense and it is low. Nothing tall has had time, and nothing tall would stand: the banks undercut themselves, and everything rooted is leaning, clutching, growing sideways, growing on other plants. There are ferns that go up your arm in one season, liverworts in sheets over the warm rock, pale grasses, a scrub of willow and something like birch that never gets above head height and grows in a permanent crouch away from the water. The moss is layered on itself three deep in places — old generations holding up new ones, saturated, dripping, sprouting small pale things that aren’t flowers because there’s too little light for flowers. The whole valley floor is felted. Step off the stone and you sink.

And it is crowded. Life here is packed in and touching. Insects rise off the warm pools in columns and get shredded by the spray; there are birds that work the waterfall margins — dippers, wagtails, small dark quick things — and they have given up on song, because song is useless when the water is deafening; they signal with wing-flashes and with calls pitched so low you feel them in the sternum more than hear them. Frogs in the warm pools are the only animal making a noise that holds its own against the river, and they do it in a chorus that pulses and stops and pulses, and when it stops you notice the water has changed pitch, and that is how you know a bank has gone somewhere upstream. Under the moss there are small mammals in numbers. In the cold water there are fish. In the warm water there are things that should probably not be that happy at thirty-five degrees.

Of people there is little sign; of *building* there is a scattering. Up the valley, along the steep section where the gradient gives the water more to do, someone — or something — put things down. Not a settlement. A thin guyed mast, four metres, silent and vibrating. A cable strung across the gorge with nothing visibly hanging from it. A shell the size of a hut, seamless, with no door, warm to the touch, humming at a frequency just under the river so that you feel it in your teeth before you find it. Down at the braid plain, a node has been half-excavated by the river and is still working: a dark faceted thing the size of a chest, cables trailing out of the gravel, the water going around it because the water has already been told how. This is machinery from a place further along than here, dropped in and left to mind itself. It maintains itself. It is losing, slowly, to the valley, which is the most interesting thing about it — you can find a mast half-swallowed in slumped gravel, still humming, still holding itself upright with a patience that suggests it has recalculated and intends to keep recalculating.

The valley does not hold a shape. That is the second rule. The river is young and it redraws the map on a schedule that isn’t a schedule — a pool that was a pool is a chute; a gravel bar becomes an island and then a bar again; a new waterfall appears where the bank let go and cuts its own plunge pool and moves ten metres upstream in a week. The fog thickens when the springs run hotter, and thins when they don’t. The ground lifts and settles. Nothing stays where it is put, which is why the built things are scattered and few and why they are always, always in the wrong place by the time anyone could come back to them.

You navigate by sound-shadow. Behind a boulder the roar drops to a manageable droning; on the open moss it is a physical pressure, a hand on the chest. You learn that a footstep is inaudible, that a shout is inaudible, that the only way to be heard is to touch. You learn that everything you carry becomes soaked and then becomes cold, and that the warm water is the only thing standing between you and a rapid, quiet loss of heat. And you learn, in the dark, in the fog, in the constant hammering of a valley that is still deciding what it is — that quiet is not available here, and isn’t wanted. The place is loud the way a workshop is loud. Something is being made. It is early, and it is going to keep going.
