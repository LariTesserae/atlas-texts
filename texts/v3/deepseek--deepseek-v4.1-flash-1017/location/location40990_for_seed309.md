# The Understory of Vell

You descend through a crack in the world and into a humid green roar.

The air hits first — warm and heavy, thick as breath on a window, carrying the smell of wet bark and hot machinery and something sweetly rotting. It condenses on every surface. Water beads on the undersides of leaves, gathers in the seams of metal, drips in a constant irregular percussion from the ceiling forty meters above. The ceiling is root. Vast buttressed slabs of it, pale and knobbled, knuckled together into a stone-like vault that is in fact the underside of a forest floor — a forest you will never see, trees whose trunks vanish upward into the dark like the pillars of an impossible nave.

This is Vell: a burrow-city, a warren-hollow, an under-forest.

Light comes in three kinds, all of them dim. First, the pale lichen-glow — a cold fungal blue-green smeared across root and wall, enough to see by but never enough to see well. Second, the lamps: industrial, orange, hissing, strung on cables between the root-pillars, each with its own small halo of insects. Third, and strangest, the bioluminescence of the great plants themselves — the ones that have made a home down here and changed to suit it. There are bladder-vines that pulse a slow violet when something brushes them. There are shelf-fungi the size of cartwheels that throw off a soft amber whenever the humidity spikes, so the whole cavern flickers gently with every shift in the air. And there are the flower-trees — rootstock trees that never grew a trunk, only a stiff crown of waxy blooms at head-height, each bloom lit from within like a cupped hand around a candle.

The vegetation is total. It is not that plants grow here; it is that the place *is* plant, and everything else — the city, the roads, the machinery — has been built inside it like a ship inside a bottle. The walls are living bark, split and re-split by growth, healed over with resin and moss. The ground is a lattice of root, packed with leaf-litter and mud and the soft give of decay, and over all of it a creeping skin of pale moss that glows faintly where it has been walked on. Vines have been trained, or have trained themselves, into arches over the pathways; some carry wire, some carry water, some carry current and hum faintly with it, so you can feel the city's power in the plants as a low buzzing in your teeth.

The city is small — you could walk its whole length in ten minutes, edge to edge — but it is dense with industry and hollowed out over generations, so there is more of it than there is space for it. Buildings are grown and built at once: frames of riveted iron and hand-hewn timber wrapped in centuries of vine, roofs of corrugated metal sagging under moss, chimneys of stacked stone with fruiting bodies puffing spores from the cracks. There are terraces of root stepped into the cavern walls, each one holding sheds and workshops and rows of cultivated fungus in bins. There are catwalks overhead, swaying, crowded with people and cargo and the occasional large beetle kept on a tether. There are pipes everywhere — fat, sweating, copper-green — and they weep condensation in constant thin streams, so that each pathway has its own little rain to walk under.

And there are the engines. This is the sound that owns the place. Not one engine — dozens, small ones, scattered through the burrow like heartbeats in a body: pumps that keep the water moving, fans that keep the air from going stale, generators in iron sheds rattling the moss off their own roofs. They are loud, all of them, and together they make a constant mechanical weather — a grinding, thudding, whistling bed under everything else, so loud that people here speak in a shout and hear in a half-shout, and the birds have learned to sing in the gaps.

Because the fauna is the fourth and loudest thing. Insects first: beetles the size of fists trundling the paths, moths with wings like dusted felt, clouds of midges orbiting every lamp, millipedes that climb the walls in drifts and are harvested for food and for medicine. There are the flit-lizards that live in the root-cracks and come out to snap at the midges. There are the burrow-hounds, hairless and pale and kept as guards, who bark at everything and are answered by everything. There are birds — bright, raucous, transplanted from the world above and gone feral — that nest in the machinery and steal from the drying-racks and shriek at the flit-lizards. There are the pale things that live deeper, in the flooded seams, and are only heard. And there is the sound of them all layered under the engines and over the drip: a dense, chattering, living noise, the sound of a place that is never quiet and never, ever still.

Water is not abundant but it is everywhere. It hangs. It drips. It pools in the low seams and runs in narrow channels cut into the root, and the whole city is arranged around the channels. The main one, the Seam, runs the length of Vell and is crossed by seven little bridges, and on its banks are the washing-steps and the condenser-houses and the tanneries that use the flow. Above, the condensation gathers and falls as a fine constant drizzle in some chambers and as fat irregular drops in others, and people here have a particular walk — a shoulder-roll, a grace — for moving through it without minding.

The rule of the world, the thing everyone here knows in their body, is this: *nothing stays built.* The forest above is not done growing. The roots are not dead. Everything here is held in a slow press, and the city survives by yielding to it — by building around the new root, by cutting back the vine, by re-hanging the lamp on the other side when the bracket is swallowed. Houses here have been rebuilt three, four, seven times in place of themselves. The bridges are re-decked every season. There is an office of the Grow, whose whole job is to watch the walls and say which building will have to move. Nothing is ruined; everything is *maintained*, endlessly, against a patient and enormous living pressure that is not hostile, only indifferent, and very strong.

And it is a place of long settled strangeness, too — the way a thing gets when it has been itself for a long time. The people have a look: muffled ears, moss-stained hands, lamp-tanned faces, a calm that comes from living under a drip. They keep the old shrines in the root-clefts and leave out the first fruit of every harvest for the pale things in the deep water. They know which bladder-vines mean rain and which mean the pumps are failing. They have catalogued the bioluminescent pulses into a kind of calendar. They are not afraid of the dark; they have lived in it for generations and have made it warm.

Above them, the forest goes on and does not know they are there. Below them, the water goes on and does not care. And in the little lit hollow between, the engines grind, the beetles trundle, the vines pulse violet, the drops fall, and Vell is exactly, loudly, damply itself — a green machine in a green machine, tended by people who long ago stopped expecting the world to hold still.
