# The Sud

You come up onto the Sud from below, and the first thing is the glare. It is a plateau of white and ochre and liver-red, sitting so high that the sun feels closer than it should, and it is soaked. Not raining, necessarily — the air itself is wet, a warm weight you breathe rather than see through, and everything on the plateau is wet with it, glistening, beaded, running in thin films down every slope. The stone sweats. You watch a boulder in the middle distance and it is not a boulder you would recognize from anywhere else: it is a sinter cone, a lump of mineral laid down by water over centuries, weeping from a hundred pores, and the weeping has painted it in bands of rust and cream and a green that looks like copper and is.

There is no green here that is alive. Nothing grows. The ground between the stone is crust — a crust that thickens and re-dissolves with the thermal cycles, crunchy underfoot in the morning, gelatinous by afternoon, and it is the closest thing to a lawn the Sud has. Pale, mineral, faintly warm under the hand. If you scrape it you find brine beneath, and the brine is where everything on this plateau actually lives.

## The water

It comes up, not down. The Sud is punctured all over with vents and springs and slow seeps, and the water they push is hot — not boiling everywhere, but hot enough that you never quite dry, and it is saturated. It carries the mountain's insides in solution: sulfur, boron, soda, iron, arsenic, lithium. Where it pools it leaves rings. Where it runs it leaves channels of chalk-white and orange, and the channels fork and braid in patterns that look designed and are not. Mist rises from the bigger pools in slow columns, and the columns lean with the wind, and at midday the wind is not much and the columns stand straight up like something being poured the wrong way.

The plateau has no rivers in the usual sense. It has *threads* — shallow, warm, mineral-bright threads of water that wander toward the edge and then stop, because the edge is a cliff and the water goes over it as steam. Most of the rainfall of the Sud never reaches the lowlands as water at all. It evaporates off the rim in a fog bank you can see from a day's walk away, a white apron hanging in the air under the plateau's lip.

Erosion here is old and patient and finished with its work. The shapes are settled, rounded, in their long argument with water and mostly satisfied. This is not a landscape of fresh collapse. It is a landscape that has been wet for a very long time and has come to an understanding with it.

## The light

High, hot, bright past comfortable. The sun does not so much illuminate the Sud as *bleach* it, and the mist sees to it that the light arrives from everywhere at once, scattered, so shadows are shallow and the whole plateau glows like the inside of a lamp. Whites go to a blue that hurts. The reds go to coral. Everything has a sheen on it — the water film, the wet crust, the glassy sinter — and the ground throws back as much as it takes.

In the late afternoons the light goes copper and the mist turns the air into a warm fog you can see the sun move through, a disc of glare sliding down toward the rim. Night does not get dark. The mist holds the light and the vents hold the heat and there is a long grey luminous dusk in which it is possible to see perfectly well and impossible to say why.

## The town

Oveth is out in the middle of it, sprawled, low, and sparse. It is a working town and it is not a dense one. There is the condenser yard, the boiling pans, the long white terraces where brine is run out to evaporate and raked for its salts. There are the wellheads, each one a squat housing over a vent, connected to the pans by pipes lagged in pale cloth and running with a tick and a shiver. There are the workers' quarters, laid out in rows along a raised causeway of sintered stone, each house built of the plateau's own precipitate — blocks cast from slurry and left to set, so the walls are banded and slightly glassy and every building looks grown rather than built. There are the tall thin stacks of the calcining sheds, and the sheds themselves, and the sheds' long low roofs of corrugated metal gone white with mineral bloom.

And between all of these things there is a great deal of space. Oveth is a town where you walk. Between the yard and the wellheads, between the quarters and the pans, there are long stretches of open crust, the mist standing between the buildings, the pipes running off alone into it. It is organized — surveyed, numbered, laid to a grid you can see at the wellheads if not at the yards — and yet low-saturation, and quiet, and you can stand in the middle of it and see maybe three other people and a rail of pipe and a lot of white ground going away into the glare. The town's density is not in its buildings. It is in its infrastructure, its wells and lines and terraces, drawn out thin over the plateau like a nervous system on a large animal.

Industry here is not loud. It cannot be. The Sud is a place where sound does not carry and the machinery is designed accordingly — sealed, cushioned, run at pressure rather than at speed. The condensers sigh. The pans tick. Somewhere a pump runs with a deep, felt, nearly subaudible throb that you notice in your teeth before your ears.

## The fauna

Abundance, here, does not look like abundance. There are no herds on the horizon and nothing sings. But put your hand in a warm pool and the pool is full. The vents are the pasture, and everything that lives on the Sud is a grazer of chemistry: soft-bodied, pale, translucent things that browse the mineral crust and the bacterial films on the crust and each other. They are slow and they are everywhere and they are mostly small — the pulp-mouthed *kettles* that clump on the sinter in their hundreds and don't move when you approach; the flat ribbon-things that lie in the warm threads like wet rags and drift an inch when the flow shifts; the little armored crawlers with shells of their own precipitated salt, which they shed and regrow as the crust beneath them dissolves and re-forms.

Above all this, the *veils* — the largest living thing the Sud has. They come off the vents in the morning as loose assemblies of membrane, near-transparent, trailing filaments, drifting in the steam columns on their own heat. A veil is the size of a bedsheet and weighs almost nothing and flies like the idea of a bird rather than a bird. They are colonies more than creatures, or so the Oveth people say. They keep the steam and they drink it and they come apart at the edges and rejoin. Whole flocks of them stand in the mist at noon, hundreds, thinly scattered, barely visible except where the light catches an edge — a sky full of pale tissue, silent, grazing a column of water vapor while it rises.

Nothing on the Sud vocalizes. There is no call, no birdsong, no insect drone. The fauna have no voices to speak of and no need for them. What you hear from the living things is a faint wet rustle when the kettles shift, and the soft collapsing click of a veil folding on itself, and that is all.

## The sound

Which is to say: near to silence. This is the strangest thing about the Sud after the light. The air is so wet that sound dies in it. There is no echo. A shout goes six or seven meters and is gone, swallowed by the hanging water, and the mist closes over the place it went. The vents huff. The water drips — from the sinter, from the roofs, from the lagged pipes, a slow irregular tapping that is the only rhythm the plateau offers and which you stop hearing after an hour and then notice again, and it is the same. A pump's felt throb. The hiss of a pan.

Walk out between the wellheads, away from the yard, and the quiet is practically absolute. You can hear the crust crunch and then you can hear nothing and then you can hear your own pulse. The mist takes the rest.

## The rules of the world

The Sud keeps a few firm rules and the people of Oveth have built their lives in obedience to them.

Water comes up hot and comes up loaded. Everything worth having is in the brine and everything worth having is got by boiling the water back off it. The town exists because of this and would evaporate with it.

The crust is alive enough to be a nuisance. It grows on anything that stays still — on the pans, on the roofs, on the pipes, on the boots of anyone who stands too long in one place. It is scraped and it comes back. The buildings bloom white at the joints where water lingers.

Nothing metal stays bright. Everything is lagged, painted, or sacrificed.

The heat is not the enemy the water is. You can be hot. You cannot be dry, and the people of Oveth have stopped trying; they wear cloth that stays wet and count it a kindness.

And the mist: the mist is the town's weather and its ceiling and its mobile. It closes to five meters in the early morning and opens to a kilometer at noon and closes again toward dusk, and the day's work is arranged around which of these it currently is. You do not navigate Oveth by landmarks. You navigate by pipe runs and wellhead numbers and by the particular pitch of the throb you can feel in your feet.

## The gut of it

Stand on the causeway at noon in the white glare and the warm fog and the soundlessness, with the pans ticking and the veils standing in their columns above the vent field and the whole plateau sweating around you, and the Sud reads as a place that is thoroughly, self-sufficiently occupied — not by people, who are recent and thin on the ground, but by the water and the minerals and the patient translucent grazers of chemistry that have been here since before there was anything here to graze but heat. The town is a small, organized, deliberate intrusion. The plateau has made its peace with the town the way it made its peace with erosion: slowly, and on its own terms, and by wetting it.
