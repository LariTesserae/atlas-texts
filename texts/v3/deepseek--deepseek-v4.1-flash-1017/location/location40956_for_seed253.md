You arrive by sound. There is no horizon to cross, no shore, no surface above or floor below that announces itself — the water is the same at every depth, and then your hand finds something, and the place begins.

It is cool in the way a cellar is cool: steady, unhurried, the temperature of a thing that stopped changing long ago. The water is clear past the point of usefulness. You can see your own hand if it is close. Past that, the dark is nearly complete, and after a while you stop looking into it and start listening instead.

It is loud. That is the first thing that makes it a place and not just depth. A low continuous note, broad as a cathedral, and stacked inside it other notes that hold and hold and never resolve. Not a roar; a chord. The water carries it forever in every direction, and because nothing here absorbs it, nothing ever quite stops.

What you are standing on — because you are standing; the pressure holds you to it — is not stone. It has grain, but the grain spirals. It is pale, if there were light to make it pale, and faintly yielding, like the inner pith of a stalk that grew to the size of a country. Press hard and it gives a little and then holds. It is the surface of a plant that long ago stopped being a plant and became a ground.

The ground runs flat for a long way and then ends, not in a cliff but in a rounded shoulder, and beyond the shoulder there is water going down, and the material beneath you narrows into stems and columns and goes down into the black and is lost. These are highlands. They hang. A chain of them, or a scatter of them — you cannot tell the extent, only that from any one summit you can hear no other, and that the abyss between them is deeper than sound. Soundings dropped from the shoulders are never heard to land.

On the flat summits, sparse and far apart, are the structures.

They are grown. That is the only word that fits and it is not quite right, because grown things have joints and knots and scars, and these have none. A tower rises out of the plateau as one continuous piece with the plateau, the same grain spiraling up it, no seam, no footing, no place where you could say the tower begins and the ground ends. Arches. Chambers with doorways. Galleries open along one side, worn smooth at the lip as if by centuries of shoulders. Spirals of ramp that climb nothing to nowhere. Stairways, unmistakably stairways, rising through the dark, each tread dished in the middle where feet would have gone — and nothing has feet here, and nothing walks, and the dishing is only water and time.

You can hear a doorway before you reach it. The sound bends around the opening; the chord thickens at the threshold and thins as you pass through. The whole architecture is legible this way, chamber and corridor and hollow, written in resonance, laid out for something that reads with its body and its ears. It is difficult not to conclude it was made to be read that way.

It is also empty. Not abandoned — abandoned would leave something. There is no debris, no silt, no fall of dust on the ledges. In a world this old, on surfaces this still, you would expect sediment the way you expect shadow; there is none. Nothing rots here. The edges are ancient, worn round as river stones, but the wear is finished. Nothing is currently wearing. The dark is not the dark of a place in decay. It is the dark of a place that has been put down and left in its final form.

The animals are everywhere and in knots. Soft, blind, pale things, most of them small, graze the surfaces in slow drifts; there are larger shapes that pass without shape, and something that groans, at intervals so even that after a while the groan stops being an event and becomes part of the chord. The knots are loud — a whole chamber screaming thinly with feeding and finding — and then you swim out of it and the screaming recedes and there is the chord again, unchanged. That is the rhythm of the place: a long silence with a hard bright noise somewhere inside it, then a long silence.

There is almost no light. What there is comes from the animals, brief and biological, a green flare when something is disturbed, a faint sheen left on the smooth curves where a drift of them has passed. It is never enough to see by. It is enough to remind you that the surfaces are not bare rock but something that still, in some slow grudging way, lives.

Because it does. Cut into the material and it closes; not fast, but over days, the wound drawing shut and the grain re-forming over it, spiraling as before. Nothing here is ever permanently broken. Nothing here is ever entirely dead, either — nothing gets to finish. That is the rule of the world, and it takes a while to feel how strange it is: no decay, no accumulation, no repair that anyone performs. The place maintains itself and has always maintained itself, and there is no one to thank for it and no sign there ever was.

Above you, very far up, is the underside of the same world: a ceiling of growth with roots hanging from it, a mirror of the highlands, dark on dark. Between the two — between the flattened summits and their reflection — the chord holds. The water does not move enough to be called a current. The same animals call on the same intervals. The same green flickers and goes out. Nothing is arriving and nothing is leaving and nothing is wearing away.

And once, if you stay long enough with your hand flat against the ground, you will feel the one movement in the world: a pulse, enormous and slow, rising through the material and through you and passing on, the whole drowned highland flexing by an amount too small to measure. It is not a heartbeat, quite. It is more like the held breath of something that has been holding it since before there were shores, and will go on holding it, and does not intend to let go.
