# The Salt Reach

You get in through a crack, not a cave mouth — a fissure in the ceiling of the world above, narrow enough to scrape both shoulders, dropping through a hundred metres of clean grey rock into warm air that smells of struck matches and hot pennies. There is no ladder. Someone has cut shallow footholds in the shaft wall, spiral-wise, and they are as crisp as the day they were cut. That is the first sign of how this place works.

Then the shaft opens and you are standing on the floor of a volume too large to have a far side.

## The shape of it

It is not a cavern in the sense of a room. It is a country, roofed. The floor rolls away in long shallow swells of crystalline salt and sulfur, banded in cream and bone and a bruised yellow, and it does not end so much as fade into the dark before any wall arrives. Off to one side a series of terraces steps down and down, each edge a fresh fracture, each face glassy where it broke. Above, the roof comes in at maybe a hundred and fifty metres, or maybe four hundred — the dark refuses to be measured — supported on nothing, an unsupported span of mineral that should not hold and does, thick with hanging clusters you take at first for stalactites.

Everything is sharp. That is the overwhelming fact of the place. There is no rounding anywhere, no worn edge, no softened corner. The salt breaks in blades and needles and clean conchoidal flakes. Walk on it and it cuts. The terraces have lips like struck flint. This is a young place, geologically speaking — a wound in the earth opened moments ago by the reckoning of rock — and it has never once been sanded down, because there is nothing here to do the sanding. No water. No wind. No freeze, no thaw, no rain, no river, no tide. Nothing has ever touched these edges except the moment they broke.

And nothing ever will. That is the second fact, and the larger one.

## Air, heat, dryness

The air is thick. You feel it in your chest before you feel anything else — dense, heavy, high-pressure, warm as the inside of a hand. It is not humid; it is the opposite of humid, so dry that your lips crack within the hour and the dryness stops being an inconvenience and becomes a kind of metaphysical fact. There is no water in this place. Not a drip, not a seep, not a damp seam, not a crust of dried anything. The word *thirst* will stay in your mouth long after the sensation leaves it, because there is nothing here for thirst to refer to.

And it is warm — evenly, thoroughly, insultingly warm. Every surface is the same temperature. The floor, the roof, the walls, the animals, the air: all identical. A thermometer would tell you the same number wherever you put it, which is not how a world is supposed to behave. Heat is supposed to move; heat is supposed to have a direction. Here the warmth simply *is*, a condition rather than a flow, and after a while the absence of any gradient starts to feel like a held breath that will never be let out.

## Dark, and the field of lights

It is dark. Not the dark of a moonless night, which has a sky in it, but a total, unarguable dark that begins a metre from your face. And yet, after your eyes give up and then, surprisingly, adjust, you see that the dark is *populated*.

They glow. The animals. Faintly — a dim, cold, sulphurous luminescence, no brighter than the last coal of a fire — and there are so many of them, layered floor to roof, that the far plain resolves into a scattered field of points. It looks like a city seen from a hill at the very end of dusk. It is the only light there is, and it never changes: the same points in the same places, not drifting, not flickering, not going out. Come back in a century and the constellation will be identical, star for star.

## The animals

They are dense. That is the thing you must understand before you take a step — that the floor is not floor. It is a pavement of bodies, close-packed, near-touching, so that you cannot put your foot down without crushing something. They are waxy, powdery, mineral things: domed and matte, or spindle-legged, or fringed, or hanging in clusters from the roof in shapes that mimic the rock until one of them very slowly opens. Some are translucent and you can see the structure inside them, and the structure is crystal. They have no blood. Crush one and it puffs a fine dry dust and a sharp smell of sulfur and hot stone, and then it is simply gone into the air, and the air, which does not move, holds the dust as a faint visible cloud for a long time.

They are not made of water and they do not want water. Their chemistry is dry and hot and slow — sulfur and salt and heat, redox without solvent, reactions that would be impossible in a wet world and are merely leisurely here. They do not eat each other so much as *overgrow* each other, and so slowly that the overgrowth is indistinguishable from a friendship.

And they are quiet. There is no birdsong in this dark, no croak, no chirr, no howl. Their communication is contact and pressure and the exchange of chemicals through touching skins; the dense crowding is the conversation. What sound there is sits right at the threshold of hearing: a low continuous hum from the whole field of them, like a room full of people holding one soft note, or like the sound of your own blood when you lie still enough.

## Sound behaving wrongly

Shout. Go on.

Nothing comes back. No echo, no return, no booming answer off a wall that must be there somewhere. The dense warm air and the powdery salt and the close-packed bodies swallow it whole, and the silence afterwards is worse than the silence before, because you have learned that this place does not throw your voice back at you. It takes it.

Your footsteps are loud — shockingly loud, crunch and grind and the wet small sounds of your own body, which sound obscene here — and your breathing is loud, and you will find yourself breathing shallowly to be polite.

## The buildings

Scattered across the salt, a day's walk apart, sometimes a week's: stonework.

Low walls, dry-laid, of cut salt blocks, running for ten metres and then stopping, unfinished. Cairns of stacked plates, waist-high, some toppled, some four courses tall. Small beehive shelters, doorless, with a floor of flat sulfur and a bench of flat sulfur and nothing else. A few hammered copper cups, a copper awl, a copper blade: bright as the hour they were made, because there is nothing here to tarnish them. No rust, no patina, no dust of corrosion. Primitive stuff, hand-work, no mechanism anywhere, no wheel, no writing — but the joins are good, and the walls are true.

You will look at these and think: *ruins. Someone left.* And then you will notice that the walls are not for anything. They do not hold off weather, because there is no weather. They do not pen animals, because the animals do not move. And you will notice the fresh chip-scars on the newest block, and the faint pale dust of a cut made perhaps a decade ago, and you will understand that the masons are still at work — that they have simply been at work for a very long time, one stone a decade, and that a half-built wall in this place looks exactly like a ruin, forever.

Whether the masons are people or animals, no one will tell you. The ones you will suspect are the spindle-legged ones, and they are not talking.

## The rules of the world

Nothing decays. Nothing rusts, moulders, dries out, dampens, settles, slumps, or drifts. There is no erosion because there is no agent of erosion, and there is no growth because there is nothing to grow on. The temperature is the same everywhere and always. The air does not stir. The same animals occupy the same places. There are no seasons here, no days, no tides, no cycles; there is not even the slow gradient of a thing cooling, because this place was made and then held, and the holding is the whole of its behaviour.

Time, in the Salt Reach, is not a river. It is a shelf.

And against all of that, you arrive — a soft wet warm-blooded sack of water, ninety-eight degrees of it, breathing out moisture with every breath, radiating heat in every direction like a lamp in a dark room.

You are the gradient. You are the only gradient.

It takes about an hour to notice the leaning. The nearest animals tilt toward you, a millimetre at a time, the way a plant turns to a window — nothing so crude as pursuit, just a slow correction of orientation, a great many small mineral bodies deciding, in the most leisurely possible way, that *that* direction is marginally warmer than *this* one. Overnight, they will be closer. Not much. Enough that in the morning you will step back and find the near-touching crowd has become the touching crowd, and the ones you crushed last night have been quietly overgrown by their neighbours, who are now leaning.

This is the only event this place has ever hosted. You brought a temperature difference into a world that had none, and everything here is responding to it at the speed of stone.

When you leave, it will stop. The leaning will arrest at whatever angle it reached. The crowd will hold that position, tilted slightly toward the space where you were, and it will hold it forever, because nothing here ever relaxes, and nothing here ever forgets, and nothing here is ever, ever going to change.
