The first thing is the air. It is not air so much as a warm, saturated broth you move through, thick as a hand passed slowly over a candle. It smells of wet earth, of crushed green stems, of something faintly sweet and overripe—fruit fallen and forgotten in the heat. The light is a dim, pearly green, filtered through a canopy that never parts enough to show the sky. There is no sun here, only a diffuse, directional glow from a ceiling of leaves that seems to come from everywhere at once. It is not dark; it is a hot, breathing twilight that presses on the skin.

The ground is young. It is a soft, unstable skin of mud and mineral slick, barely cool enough to solidify. It gives underfoot, not with a squelch but with a slow, resentful parting, like pushing through something that is trying to close again. Thin crusts of pinkish salt and grey mineral bloom crackle under boots. Beneath them, water is always just there, a sheen that rises to meet any pressure. The land is not eroding; it is being built. Every great, horned, three-legged thing that wades through the shallows leaves a print that fills with hot, cloudy seepage and begins, within a day, to crust over with a strange pearly-green scum. The landscape is under construction, a raw, wet geological sketch that has not yet decided on its final form.

The vegetation is a wall. It is a dense, layered tangle of stems and thorns and broad, sweating leaves that sweat with you. There are no trees, not as such, but there are pillars of living fibre, thick as a man's leg, wrapped in vines and hanging moss that is not moss but a kind of soft, absorptive algae. The air is so hot the leaves drip constantly, not with rain but with their own condensation, a ceaseless warm drizzle from every surface. The plants are loud in their own way—there is a constant, faint popping as some pods split their seams, a rustle of wet fibres rearranging themselves, a low, resonant hum from the hollow reeds that line every standing puddle. The green is almost too much. It is a saturated, aggressive green that seems to drink the dim light and give nothing back.

And the sound. The sound is the overwhelming thing. It is not one noise but a wall of them, a dense, layered fabric of calls and clicks and bellows and chirrups that never stops. The air is thick with flying bodies: fat, slow, iridescent beetles, clouds of tiny biting flies that move in wedges, clumsy, four-winged things that drone with the pitch of a broken cello. Something in the reeds makes a sound like a wet finger drawn around a glass. Something else, huge and hidden, exhales in a long, bubbling sigh every few minutes from the deeper muck to the east. The wet ground is a chorus: the plop of hidden things, the slow, sucking footfalls of something large moving through the trees not forty paces away, the fizz of the salt crusts dissolving in the rising damp. The sound is not chaos; it is a dense, complex orchestration that seems to have been playing the same note for a very long time.

The animals are everywhere. They are not merely present; they are the texture of the place. A thing like a lizard but with a shell of wet, grey clay moves up a stem in slow, deliberate inches. A worm, pale and blind, as long as your arm, slides across the mud without a wake. Fish—or something like them—move through the standing water at knee height, not swimming but pushing slowly through the dense, muddy medium. They are round and soft and covered in fine grey hairs. There are no birds, no things of the air, only a low, dense darkness of creatures that hum and drone and crawl. The fauna are as lush and layered as the vegetation, and they are everywhere, touching each other, touching the plants, filling every crevice in a mass of small, wet, pulsing life.

The water is not a lake or a river. It is a condition. The marsh is a single, shallow basin, and everything in it is at the edge of drowning. The water is not deep—it is below the knee, mostly—but it is everywhere, rising and falling with a tide so slow and small it is imperceptible. It is warm, and it is thick with silt and with the tiny, suspended bodies of things that have died and not yet dissolved. In places, it forms still, clear pools where the mineral crust has not formed, and in these pools you can see the bottom, which is not a bottom at all but a thick, soft mat of pale, fibrous root and the collected remains of the marsh itself.

This is a place of equilibrium. It has been hot, and wet, and dense, and loud, and dim, and young for a long time, and it will be so for a long time yet. Nothing is changing. The water rises and falls imperceptibly. The leaves drip. The huge, hidden thing exhales. The flies move in their wedges. The pods pop and split and drip their seeds into the wet, waiting earth, which accepts them and does not change. It is not a place that waits for you; it is a place that has always been waiting, and will continue to wait, patient and loud and growing, long after your boots have stopped sinking into it.
