# Vess, in the Bason

You come down through a series of wet stairs and the temperature drops the whole way, two degrees a flight, until the air is the temperature of a cellar in autumn and stays there. There is no weather. There is only the ceiling, which the people here call the Lid, and the water coming through it.

The Bason is a dissolved room in limestone, and it is small — if you carried a parish church down here and shook it out, the town would just about fill it. The Lid is low enough that in places you can reach up and press your palm into the seep, feel it beading and fattening and letting go. The floor is not flat. It has been dissolving into terraces for a very long time, and the town is built on those terraces, stacked four and five deep, so that a street is a stair and a roof is somebody else's yard. Everything is wet. Not raining — nothing so active — but wet the way a stone in a springhouse is wet, a permanent beaded skin that makes iron go soft and orange and makes timber swell until it grips its own nails.

Nothing here has a sharp edge. Not the doorframes, not the stair nosings, not the iron, not the stone. Two centuries of condensation have rounded all of it the way a river rounds a bottle. The town didn't fight this; the buildings that insisted on staying square are gone, undermined and flooded and quarried for their own good blocks, and the ones that remain lean with the strata and bulge where the water wanted them to bulge. A door in Vess is the shape of a door the way a worn coin is the shape of a face — recognizable, but no longer arguing.

**Light** comes from lamps, and there are fewer of them than there are people who would like one. They are set far apart along the terraces, oil or else a shunted trickle of the town's own current, and between them are long stretches of nothing but the green-white bloom of damp on stone and, now and then, a smear of living light where something has settled. The effect is not darkness so much as a series of small rooms of visibility with weather between them. Down in the lowest gallery, where the water stands, the lamps are reflected and the town appears to be twice as tall as it is, and twice as empty.

Because it is sparse, this town. It is urban in its bones — there is a grid here, there are rights of way, there is a drainage easement four hundred years old that is still enforced, there is a mill and a smelter and a pumping station and a company that sells pressure — but the flesh on those bones is thin. Rooms sit empty for decades and are used anyway, by bats, by children, by the water. You can walk ten minutes between one lit doorway and the next. There are more stairs than there are people to climb them.

**The water is the industry.** It gathers in a high gallery nobody lives in, a crack-riddled attic of a place, and comes down through the town in cut channels and ceramic pipes and one very old iron main that weeps at every joint. Along the way it turns things. It turns an undershot wheel in the mill, slowly, all day, all night, at the pace of a man walking with a bad leg. It turns a smaller wheel, and a smaller, and it fills a weighted accumulator that hisses and settles and gives the smelter its push in long patient strokes. There are engines here — real ones, big-bellied, with brass and bearing and pressure gauges whose needles you can watch move if you have the patience of a fern — but they are not driven hard. Nothing in Vess is driven hard. The whole plant runs off a fall of water no taller than a house, and it runs at the speed that fall allows, which is slow, and which nobody has been able to improve on in three hundred years without the water simply finding another way down.

So the work is patient work. Pumps that lift a little. Bellows that breathe. A lathe that turns at the rate of a turning thought. The smelter's outflow runs warm for thirty feet and then is ordinary cold water again, and that thirty feet is the most valuable real estate in the Bason, because of what grows in it.

**The bloom.** They call it the bloom. In the warm, mineral-rich, endlessly condensing outflow, a skin forms — part limestone, part something that is not quite mineral and not quite alive, gray-cream at first, then veined, then oddly structured. It accretes. It takes the shape of whatever it grows on. Leave a wrench in the channel for a season and you will pull out a wrench made of bloom, exact to the knurl. Leave a bucket, and the bucket. The town harvests this: cuts it, dries it in the warm galleries, sells it as a fine abrasive and a filter medium and a thing that rich people far away set in rings. It is the only export.

And in the older galleries, where the channels were abandoned and the water still seeps through, the bloom has been growing on things nobody put there. There are casts in the low ceilings like the impressions of hands. There is a shape on the wall of the old pump-house that is either a face or a place where the water ran in a face-shaped way, and the people of Vess do not discuss which. It is understood, without being said, that the bloom remembers. It is also understood that this is not useful, and so it is left alone.

**Sound.** Almost none. The wheel. The drip, which is not a rhythm but a scatter — a thousand small arrivals, some near, some far, which together read as quiet rather than as noise. The hiss of the accumulator every minute or so. Felt boots. A cough three terraces up that you will hear and think about. Voices are kept low here, not from reverence but because the stone carries and because there is nothing to shout over.

**The fauna are everywhere.** More life than people, by a wide margin. Blind shrimp in the cisterns, pale and quick. Eels in the flooded lower gallery, fat ones, fed on the town's leavings, some of them older than the current miller. Moths that drink condensation off the lamp housings and fold themselves flat against the warm pipes. A species of small pale crab that lives only in the smelter outflow and that the children are forbidden to handle and handle anyway. Bats in the empty rooms, in numbers. Worms in the wet felt, in the timber, in the bloom itself, which they seem to cultivate — the bloom grows faster where the worms are, and no one knows what they take from it. And in the drowned lowest level, where the lamps are shut off to save oil, there are things that come to the edge of the light and are not eels, and that the eels avoid.

The town has rules about this, and they are old and specific and nobody remembers the reasons. Do not cut bloom from a living channel. Do not carry a lamp into the fourth gallery alone. Do not name the wheel. Do not let the water stand still anywhere inside the town — every pan and sump and basin must have its trickle out, because still water here does not stagnate, it structures. That last one is on a brass plate bolted to the mill door, and it is the only piece of text in Vess that anyone has bothered to make permanent.

**Time here is not slow; it is patient.** The town is not dying and it is not growing. The water table has dropped four inches in living memory and the lowest lamps are set accordingly. The bloom in the old pump-house is maybe a millimeter thicker than it was when your grandmother was a girl. The wheel turns. The accumulator hisses and settles. Somewhere above, far above, the weather that makes the seep is happening, and will keep happening, and will send its water down at the rate it always has — a little more in some decades, a little less in others, and the town will move its intakes and shrug, the way a sleeper shifts when the room gets cold, without quite waking.
