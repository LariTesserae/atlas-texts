# The Crown of the Stack

You come up the inside of the Stack, which is the only way up, and the way up is a ladder in a shaft of hot moving air, and by the time you reach the top you have stopped hearing anything but the roar.

The Stack is a chimney a hundred and some meters around at its base and it narrows as it climbs, and it climbs a great deal further than that. It is made — all of it, every meter, cast and riveted and bolted, plates overlapping like scales, no stone anywhere beneath it, no bedrock, no seam of country rock it was founded on. It is founded on itself, on its own footing, which sits in a plain of its own slag. The plates at the bottom are thick as a hand and green-black with a century of weather; the plates at the top are thin as a coin and have gone the colour of dried blood and then of tea. The wind has rounded every edge. Rain has cut shallow vertical channels in the seams and filled them with a rust that flowers outward in ferns of orange. It is not decaying. It has simply arrived at the shape it will keep.

And at the top, the Stack opens into the Crown.

The Crown is a room. That is the whole of it, and it is worth saying plainly because everything else about the place argues against it: it is a room, perhaps twenty meters across, glass and iron, a lantern built to keep the weather off the mouth of the flue and let the heat out and the light in. The ribs curve up and meet at a hub. The glass is old and has gone milky, and some panes are gone entirely, and where they are gone the plants have come through or gone out. It is small. You can walk the whole of it in twenty paces and you will have seen everything in it.

There is almost nothing in it. That is the second thing. The floor is a grid of cast plates, sagging slightly between their supports, and the room above that floor is nearly all air — a great deal of air, more than you expect a room to hold, because the walls curve away and the ceiling is high and there is nothing in the volume but a few things standing in it and the light. Vast space between presences. The emptiness is not neglect; it is the room's proportion. It was built to be mostly nothing.

---

**The sound.**

The Stack breathes. Somewhere far below, the works that drive it are still running, or still coasting, and the flue draws, and the roar of it comes up through the floor plates and fills the Crown completely. It is deafening and it is constant and it is *slow* — a low tone, deeper than a voice, that you feel in the sternum and the jaw, with a shiver of overtones off the iron ribs that changes as the air changes. It does not pulse. Over minutes it will deepen a little and then shallow, and you will not be sure you heard it move. Nothing about it hurries.

It is the loudest place most people will ever stand in, and nothing is happening in it. That is the thing to understand. The noise is the sound of an enormous patient process with no events in it. You can stay an hour and nothing will occur.

---

**The wet and the warmth.**

The floor is warm. Not hot — warm, the way a stone wall is warm at the end of a sunny day, blood-warm, and it is warmest toward the center where the flue mouth is, under a grate. The warmth comes up and meets the cold of the altitude and the two of them make a permanent fog.

The Crown is damp in the way a bathroom is damp, in the way a greenhouse is damp: not raining, not wet through, but beaded. Every iron surface holds a skin of condensation. The glass runs with it. It gathers under the sag of the floor plates in shallow pools you can put a palm in, and the pools are warm, and they are absolutely clear, and they taste of iron. The air is wet enough that your breath does not show. Now and then a drip will find a resonance in a plate and you will hear a single note under the roar, and then not again for a while.

---

**What grows.**

Everything grows. This is the strangest fact of the Crown and the one that takes longest to accept: a room of glass and hot wet iron at the top of a chimney, with no soil in it at all, and it is a garden.

The moss came first and is everywhere, in the seams, along the shaded side of every rib, a pelt of it, green-black and then, where the warmth is strongest, a luminous yellow-green. Ferns have rooted in the rust — actual roots, working into the laminated flake of the old plates, splitting them slowly. There are a few woody things, stunted and old, grown in the corners where the condensation runs; one of them is in flower and you will not recognize the flower. Everything grows toward the center, toward the warmth and the updraft. Everything is bent. The whole vegetation of the room leans inward like a congregation.

The material it grows in is not soil. It is sinter and rust and the fine grey dust that the flue throws off, and the fibrous old lagging that has come loose from the pipes, and it is entirely manufactured and entirely dead and the plants have made a living of it anyway, over a long time, patiently.

---

**What lives.**

Birds, mostly. They nest in the ribs and in the gaps where panes have gone. They are small and brown and they are used to the roar — they must be, they were hatched in it — and they move through the Crown as if it were silent, which is the surest sign that they are not hearing what you are hearing. There are insects in the wet seams, in numbers, in the warm pools. There is something larger that lives under the floor grate near the flue mouth, and you will probably only hear it, a slow dry shifting, and it will not come out. And there is the ordinary traffic of things that came up the Stack because it is warm and stayed because it is warm: you are not the first.

They are abundant and they are sparse at the same time, which is the paradox of the place. There is a great deal of life here and it is all in the seams and the corners and the shallow places, leaving the middle of the room empty. The volume belongs to the air and the sound.

---

**The light and the view.**

The light is ordinary and that is what makes it strange. Daylight comes through the milky glass and is scattered and even and grey-white, no shadows worth the name, the light of a bright overcast at altitude. When a pane is missing you get a shaft, but the air is so wet that the shaft is visible as a shaft, a pale bar standing in the room, and the birds fly through it.

Out beyond the glass there is air, and then more air. The elevation is extreme enough that the ground has stopped being a thing you can see. There is weather below you that you are above. On a clear day there is a haze, and in the haze, very far off, two or three other stacks, dun-coloured, thin, with their own faint plumes — but they are so distant and so few that they only deepen the emptiness rather than relieve it. Mostly there is nothing. The emptiness outside matches the emptiness inside and you begin to feel that the Crown is not a room in a landscape but a small built pocket of warmth and noise hung in a very great deal of nothing.

---

**The rules of the world here.**

It changes on its own clock. The roar deepens and shallows over a cycle too long to sit through. The damp thickens and thins. A fern puts out one frond. The warmth rises a degree and falls back. Nothing here rewards attention at the scale of an hour; everything here rewards attention at the scale of a season, and the Crown has been keeping its own patient balance for longer than the ladder has been climbed.

You may take the water from the pools. You may take the warmth. You may sleep. The birds will ignore you. The roar will not stop, and after a while you will stop being able to tell whether it is outside you or in your chest, and that is the ordinary condition of the place. The Crown is a room, small, high, made entirely of made things, wet and warm and overgrown, holding up its glass against a great deal of air it will never keep out, roaring patiently at nothing.

Walk it once. It is twenty paces. Then sit down in the middle of the emptiness and let it be loud at you, and wait, and see whether the slow thing the place is doing becomes something you can feel. It will, eventually. It takes a while. Everything here does.
