# The Lampwell

You come down through a crack that barely admits you, and the brightness is already waiting before you're through — a light with no shaft, no opening, no source you can find. It fills the hollow like water fills a bowl, saturating, and it does not thin as it goes. The far wall is as lit as the near one. There is no gradient here. The corners are bright. The undersides of stones are bright. You keep looking up for the hole and there is only ceiling, seamless and pale.

The chamber is small — a room, a chapel, the inside of a cupped hand. Perhaps thirty paces across, and the roof low enough that you want to stoop for no reason you can name. It is warm. Not the warmth of trapped air but something closer to blood-warm, steady, unbothered by hour or season. The stone is soft with age: everything rounded, everything settled into the shape it has decided to keep. Erosion has been at work here a long time and has finished the argument. There are no sharp edges anywhere. The walls look like they were carved by someone patient with a wet thumb.

Water is not much in evidence and yet is everywhere in the way of a thing recently gone. The floor is damp-dark in patches, dry in others, and the dryness looks deliberate, like the damp is choosing. Condensation stands in a fine bead on the ceiling, gathers, and lets go — a drop every so often, landing somewhere with a small flat sound. The drips have been landing in the same places for so long that they have made shallow cups in the stone, and the cups hold a finger's depth of water, and the water is warm. A seep runs along one base of the wall, thin as a bootlace, going nowhere, arriving from nowhere. It smells of wet chalk and something faintly sweet, like bruised stems.

The vegetation is a rumour of vegetation. Pale threads in the seams where the damp lives, mosses that have given up being green, a few things like fern-fronds that are almost white and grow slowly enough that you could watch a leaf unfurl across a season and still have time to make tea. They cluster where the drips fall. Between the clusters there is a great deal of bare, bright stone — the emptiness here is the dominant fact, an airy hollow where almost nothing stands between you and the far side. You could walk across it in a dozen strides and feel the whole time that you were crossing something enormous.

The animals are small and they are many, which is not the same as crowded. Midges hang in a column of their own making near the center, a slow drifting spindle that turns without wind. Moths with no eyes rest on the warm wall with their wings folded flat, and when they move they move like something remembering how. Blind things live in the drip-cups: thin crustaceans, a fingernail long, that swim up to the surface and back down as though checking on the ceiling. They are not alarmed by you. Nothing here is alarmed by you. The quiet is not the quiet of held breath but of creatures that have never needed to hurry.

And there is the other thing. On the floor, in a dozen places, there are arrangements of pebbles — small stone stacks and short arcs and little paired piles, set down and left. They are not random; you can see the care in them, the choosing of similar sizes, the way the flat ones are laid. Something did this. Something with a hand, or something that uses a hand's worth of attention. But nothing here is built. Nothing endures. The stacks slump. The arcs scatter. Come back and the pebbles are in new configurations, or the same ones, or the arrangement is half-collapsed and clearly being reconsidered. Whatever makes them makes them the way the seep runs and the drops fall — as a process, not a product. There are no tools lying about except the pebbles themselves, which are the tools, and there are no walls but the ones the water made.

The strangeness gathers in the light. It is too even, too patient, too bright for a hole in the ground. Sometimes, very slowly, the brightness seems to thicken at one end of the chamber and thin at the other, over hours, like a tide, and the mosses lean that way, and the midge spindle drifts, and then it comes back. Nobody would notice unless they stayed long, and staying long is easy, and it is warm, and the drip-cups are the right size for the palm.

So you stay. The quiet has a floor to it, a soft level below which nothing goes. Water, now and then. A moth's dry tick against stone. The crawfish breaking the surface of a cup with a sound like a kiss. The light does not change and does not tire and does not explain itself, and the small arrangements of pebbles wait to be rearranged by whatever it is that is patient enough to be here and nowhere else.
