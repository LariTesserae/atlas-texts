# The Basin of Held Water

**Approach**

You come up over the shoulder of a ridge and the world drops into a bowl.

The pass itself is the first strangeness: a cut of scree and shattered slate, raw-edged, the sort of slope that should be sliding and rattling every spring. It doesn't. The stones lie exactly as they fell, ages ago, and no further. You can pick one up and it leaves a clean, sharp-edged socket in the ground, and every other stone holds its place.

**The Land**

The basin is high and cool and open — a shallow stone bowl a few hours across, ringed by ridges that are young in the way of recently broken things. There are no rounded shoulders to the hills, no soft alluvial skirts to the slopes. The gullies are freshly incised. The cliff faces are planar and bright, unfretted by the little weathering that softens old country. Everywhere the land has the look of having been made last week and then left alone, which, in a sense, it was.

It is green, though — lush in a way that sits oddly on such angular ground. Moss fills the joints like mortar. Ferns hang out of the rock cracks in dense beards. The wet meadows at the basin floor are thick with sedge and pale-flowered herbs, and the orchard terraces are heavy with fruit. Green covers and green fills, and it does not seem to spread or recede. The people here say the green holds its breath.

**Water**

Water is everywhere and it is patient. The basin catches cloud. Almost every morning a fog fills the floor to knee height and then to chest height, and it lifts by mid-morning into a grey lid that never quite opens. A permanent band of drizzle hangs over the north ridge — a fixed column of rain you can walk around, ten paces wide, falling steadily on the same strip of scree, all day, every day, and the water that lands there runs in fixed threads down grooves cut once and cut no deeper.

The rivers are clear and cold and shallow, running over clean stone with no sand in them. The pools do not silt. The falls do not undercut their lips. The whole basin is a plumbing system that was finished and then sealed.

People have taken full advantage. The settlement's infrastructure is stone: a chain of cisterns stepped down the eastern slope, fed by condensation channels — long shallow gutters of dressed rock that collect the morning fog as it runs off — and by the rain column, which they have walled and flumed and led into their works. There are retaining walls, stair-streets, drainage cuts, weirs, and a great covered aqueduct of dry-laid slabs that carries meltwater from the high notch to the lowest terraces. All of it lichened. None of it crumbling. There is no maintenance that anyone can remember, because nothing needs any.

**The City**

The city is not a crowding. It is a town-sized scatter — a wide, low weave of stone clusters across the basin floor and up onto the terraces, with orchards and wet pasture and standing pools between every cluster. Walls are dry-laid rubble and dressed slab, roofs of slate and turf. There is copper — hinges, gutters, tools, bright buttons on doors — and there is no iron. The craft is hand-work: the quarry, the chisel, the wedge, the mallet, the wheel.

You can walk a minute between doorways. The lanes are wide and green. Sheep graze the roofs and the long commons; children play in the drains. But everything is built — every slope is terraced, every stream is bridged and banked, every meadow has a wall around it, and the lanes are paved with fitted stone. It is a city that has decided to be a landscape, and a landscape that has been thoroughly built.

**Fauna**

And it is thick with animals, in a way the sparse human scatter is not. This is the place's real abundance. Herds of small dark deer move through the orchard terraces on fixed rounds. Marmots sit on every wall. Cranes wade in the meadows in long lines, and herons stand in the pools for so long they appear to be furniture. Birds nest in every gap in the masonry — swifts, martins, a chough with a copper bell on its leg that the town regards as a sort of civic official. Frog-calls at dusk come in choruses so regular that the fishers set their hours by which chorus is singing.

There are also the hovering flies — midges and small black insects that hang in the damp air in little clouds, not drifting, not swarming, just held. You can put a hand among them and they part and close. The children catch them in jars and let them out again, and they return to the same air.

**Sound**

The place is quiet, and the quiet is a substance. The moss and the turf and the fog swallow everything. Footsteps on the stair-streets are dull. The rain column has a sound but it is a narrow sound and you can step out of it. The rivers speak, but they speak with the same voice they had a century ago — the same pitch over the same stones. There is one wind, coming always from the same quarter at the same strength, so that the orchard trees are all shaped the same way and all lean together, and the sound they make is the same sound every hour. It is not silence. It is a piece of music stopped on a held chord.

**The Rules of the World**

Here is the thing, and everyone who lives here knows it and almost nobody says it plainly:

Nothing wears.

Stone breaks, but does not weather. A fresh-cut edge stays fresh. Copper dulls to a patina and stays at that patina. Wood is the exception and the anomaly — timber still darkens and softens and must be replaced, and so the town's entire economy, along with its entire sense of time, turns on wood. Doors and beams and cart-shafts are the only things that age. They are the only clocks. A household's status can be read off how new its lintel is.

The water runs but does not carve. The rain falls but does not widen the rain column. The moss is thick but it does not go further up the wall than it has gone. Seedlings come up in the same slots each spring, and only in those slots. The world was set at a certain level of lushness and a certain sharpness of stone and remains exactly at that setting, which is why the land looks young and the city looks old at once, and why both look untouched.

The people do not experience this as a curse. They experience it as a floor under their feet. There is no famine-drift, no flood-scar, no soil washing down. When they need to build, they take the stone from a wall or a quarry face, and what they leave behind is as clean as what they take. They demolish and rebuild in the same footprint, generation after generation, so that the town is a hundred rebuildings stacked side by side rather than in depth: the walls are patched and relaid, endlessly, but the plan does not change, because there is no reason it should. They quarry. They build. They replace the lintel. They quarry again.

**A last image**

Toward evening, the fog returns to the floor of the basin and the drizzle column goes on falling in its fixed ten paces. The cranes come in to the meadow in their usual lines. The flies hang in their usual air. The herons do not move. Someone's chimney — the only thing in the valley that is visibly spending itself — puts up a thin line of smoke, and the one wind takes it, and it goes the way all smoke goes here, always the same way, over the raw bright ridge and out of sight.
