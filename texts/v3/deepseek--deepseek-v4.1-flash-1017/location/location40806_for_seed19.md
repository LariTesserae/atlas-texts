# The Levelled

You come up onto it from below for two days, and then the ground simply stops rising and refuses to do anything else. There is no summit, no ridge, no crest to cross. One step you are climbing, and the next you are walking on the top of the world, and the top of the world is flat.

It is high — high enough that the air is a thin, cold liquor, that your breath comes short and your heartbeat is audible in your ears. But the height is the only drama it has. Everything else has been worn away. This is a mountain range that was ground down to a plain over an unimaginable stretch of time and then, by some slow contortion of the crust, lifted back up as a plain. The peaks are gone. The valleys are gone. What remains is the levelled floor of an old, old world, a peneplain raised like a table on legs of stone.

From the rim you can see it whole. It runs out for a day's walk in every direction, pale and level, and then it does not end so much as *diminish* — the horizon is a hard line, because there is nothing in the air to soften it. No haze. No water to make a mist. The sky above is a deep, dry, ringing blue, and the line where it meets the ground is sharp enough to cut yourself on.

## The Ground

There is no water here. Not a pool, not a seep, not a bead of dew in the mornings, because there are no mornings wet enough to make one. The rock is dry to the bone and the dust is dry in a way that is almost aggressive — it does not cling, it does not darken, it only powders away from your fingers.

Because there is no water, there is almost no chemistry. Nothing dissolves. Nothing rusts. The erosion that made this place was not the patient work of rain but the abrasion of wind and grit over spans of time long enough that the word "ancient" feels like a courtesy. The stone here is in its final form. It has been in its final form for longer than the mountains it came from were tall. You can pick up a chip of chert and understand that it will still be a chip of chert when the sun is cold.

The surface is a pavement of gravel and salt-flat hardpan and low drifts of dust that have not been moved by wind in a very long time, because there is very little wind. The dust lies in graded sheets, sorted by size, and the sorting is old and settled and no longer going anywhere. It is a landscape that has finished.

Sparse life clings to it — a grey-green crust, lichen and something lichen-like that is not quite lichen, growing on the north sides of stones at a rate of perhaps a millimetre a century. It is not abundant. It is not meant to be. It is a garnish on a very old table.

## The Quiet

The quiet here is not the absence of sound. It is sound in its settled state.

There is no wind to speak of, so there is no wind-noise. There is no water, so there is no water-noise. When you set your boot down, the crunch of grit is small and close and does not travel. You learn quickly that this place has no echo. You can shout, and the shout goes out and simply keeps going out, thinning, until it is gone, and nothing comes back to you. Sound does not return here. It only leaves.

And yet the place is not empty. Far from it.

## The Fauna

It is under your feet before you know it, and then it is everywhere, and then you understand that it has been everywhere the whole time.

They are small — thumb-sized, most of them, though the size varies so widely and so continuously that the word "size" starts to feel like a mistake. They are pale, soft-bodied, many-legged, dust-coloured on top and faintly translucent beneath, and they lie in layers. Three, four, five deep, pressed against one another in a continuous crust over the hardpan, near-touching, overlapping, a living pavement that extends past the horizon and probably does not stop at the horizon.

They are overwhelming in number and they are utterly still. This is the thing you have to sit with, on the ground, for a while, before your mind accepts it: millions of animals, uncountable, layered thick, and none of them moving. They are not dead. You can see the slow pulse of whatever passes for a heart, one beat every minute or so, and the faintest rise and fall of the crust as they breathe in unison — a slow, tidal breathing that is the largest and quietest thing on the plateau.

They are in torpor because movement costs, and there is no gradient here to move along. There is nothing to chase. There is nothing to flee. There is no water to find. There is no season. They lie in their layers and they wait, and because there is nothing to wait for, they are not really waiting. They are simply persisting, in the settled equilibrium of a world that has finished.

When you walk, you wake them. Your footfall presses down through three layers, and the pressure spreads outward in a slow ring — a ripple of small bodies shifting, uncoupling, curling, and then re-settling behind you. The ripple travels perhaps ten metres and dies. And here is the thing: within a minute the crust is smooth again. Within an hour there is no trace that you walked. The place heals instantly and perfectly, because it is always healing toward the same state, and the same state is the only state it has.

## The Breakdown

You will try to count them, and this is where the ground starts to slip.

You cannot count them, because they do not hold still at the boundaries. Lift one — carefully, it is soft and cool and heavier than it looks — and you will find that it is two, or that it is one that is also, at its trailing edge, the one beside it. They share. They merge at the margins and separate again. A leg belongs to a body you cannot find. Something that was a whole animal five seconds ago is now a segment of something larger, and something larger is now three small things, and none of it seems to trouble any of them.

It is not that they are strange. It is that the idea of *individual* does not take hold here. The same is true, if you look, of almost everything. The cairns on the ridge — you will count them, and then count them again, and get a different number, not because they change but because your counting will not stay attached to them. The distance to the horizon is not measurable; the plain has no scale cues, no trees, no houses, nothing of known size, and so your eye slides across it and reports "near" and "far" at random. A stone you fix your gaze on seems to be an hour's walk away; you reach it in ten minutes, or in three hours. The light is even and shadowless at midday, and a shadowless plain has no depth.

And there are no dead. No carapace, no shell, no bone, no husk. Nothing here leaves remains. When one of them stops — if one of them stops — it becomes gravel, and the gravel is indistinguishable from the ground, and the ground is made of gravel, so the ground is made of them, or they are made of the ground, and the distinction is the kind of distinction this place does not support.

## The Works

There are people, or there were. Scattered across the plateau, rarely — a day apart, sometimes two — you find the marks of hands.

They are small and old and made with stone tools and no metal at all. A ring of upright slabs, knee-high, with a flat stone in the middle, and no indication of what it was for. A dry-stone wall, thirty paces long, running out into nothing and stopping, built without mortar, each stone chosen and set with a patience that the place seems to teach. A cairn on a rise that is barely a rise. A hollow scraped in the hardpan and lined with flat stones, roofed with more flat stones, big enough for one person to lie in and be cold. Bone tools, yes — but the bone is not from here; it is old and sun-bleached and it came up from below, carried by someone who climbed two days to get here and then carved a small, careful thing and left it in a stone ring.

The works have not decayed, because there is nothing here to decay them. They are exactly as old as they look and they will look the same in a thousand years.

## The Rules

The rule of this world is that nothing accumulates. No gradient builds. The dust does not pile into dunes; the animals do not swarm; the lichen does not spread. Everything that could become more becomes the same instead. Disturbance is absorbed. The ripple dies. The crust smooths. The shout goes out and does not come back.

It is not a dead place. It is the opposite — it is so densely alive that the aliveness has become a kind of stillness, saturated past the point of change. There are more living things under your boots than you have ever seen in a forest, and they are all breathing together, slowly, once a minute, in layers, out to the hard horizon.

You stay as long as you can stand the quiet. Then you go down, and the sound of your own descent — the grit, the breath, the small clicks of stone — returns to you as an echo at last, and it is startling, and it is a relief, and you did not realise until that moment how much you had been missing the courtesy of the world talking back.
