# The Weeping Basin

High in a fold of warm hills, where the air is thick enough to drink, there is a bowl of stone that has been crying for a very long time.

The basin is not large — you could walk its rim in an easy quarter-hour, and from the center you could throw a stone to any edge. It is a single feature, a room carved by no hand, a cathedral the size of a grove. Its walls rise steep, not cliffs but shoulders of rock rounded and settled into themselves, worn by millennia of their own slow weeping. The stone is a soft pale grey veined with rust, and it is always wet. Not running — weeping. Moisture beads out of the rock face in the thousands, gathers into fat pendant drops, and falls. The whole inner wall glistens, dark where water has darkened it, and everywhere the small sound of it: *tick. tick-tick. tick.* Not a drip, quite — softer, the sound of something leaking through cloth. The floor of the basin catches all of it and holds it.

The floor is a warm shallow pool the color of weak tea, and beneath the water the rock continues, not flat but ridged and pitted, so the water lies in unequal sheets, some finger-deep, some ankle-deep, some places where a knuckle of stone breaks the surface and stays beaded. Nothing is crisp here. The water moves so slowly that you have to watch a leaf for a long time to see that it is moving at all — a slow gyre toward the low side, where the rim has a notch and the pool quietly overflows and finds its way down the outer face of the hill. It has been doing this, apparently, for a very long time. The notch is smooth as a worn step. The outer stone beneath it has been polished by water into a pale slipping curve.

Everything is warm. The water is warm — not hot, but warmer than the air at dawn, warm the way a stone stays warm after sun. The air rises off it faintly. The basin breathes, in a way; there is a soft continuous welling of warm mist that collects near the walls and never quite lifts, so the upper rims of the basin are always veiled, always a little further away than they look. You can see the rim. You cannot quite see what is beyond it. The light that gets down here is green and aqueous, filtered by the mist and by what grows above.

And something does grow above. The hills rimming the basin are thick with it — a low matted forest of trees that lean inward over the water, their trunks dark and liveried with moss, their roots clutching the outer stone. The canopy is not tall; nothing here is tall. But it is close and dense and warm, broad leaves held out like palms to catch the mist, some of them enormous and smooth, some fringed, some furred with fine hair that holds the moisture in tiny glass beads until the beads grow too heavy. The greenery does not quite come down into the basin. It stops at the rim, reaching over, dripping from above — a fringe of roots and creepers that dangle and do not root, some of them touching the mist but not the water. The boundary is clean and strange and seemingly old. Nothing green grows in the pool. The pool is stone and water and nothing else.

The only vegetation inside is what falls. Leaves come down continually — the big ones, the small ones, curling as they dry in the warm air and then uncurling as the wet takes them back, sinking and softening. The basin floor is littered with them in drifts, brown against the pale stone, gathered in the hollows, floating in pale rafts on the shallows and turning slowly. Nothing in the pool consumes them. They simply soften and settle and add themselves to whatever soft grey sediment lies under the water, and over long years they have built a floor of themselves.

Here is the rule of the place: it is a slow world. Nothing here does anything quickly. The drops fall at their own pace. The gyre turns, a leaf crossing the pool in the time it would take a person to forget they were watching. The mist rises and falls with a rhythm too long to notice. You could sit on the rim for an hour and the basin would not change. You could sit for a year and it would change by a leaf. It has been a long time settling into itself, and it is in its own state now, weathered and patient and in balance, asking nothing.

It is also — quietly, in a way that builds on you — strange. You begin to notice that the drops do not all fall from the same places. There are faces in the rock, not carved — never carved, this was never carved — but the stone is shaped in such a way that the water gathers at certain points in certain patterns: a brow, a long hollow cheek, a running line for a mouth. They are not quite faces. They are the suggestion of faces, the way a word is on the tip of the tongue. There are perhaps nine or ten of them, spaced around the wall at about the height a person would stand, and each weeps from eyes that are only hollows, and each drips into the pool with its own small sound, slightly out of time with its neighbors. The sounds almost have a pattern. They almost resolve. They never quite do. The faces do not move. But the water that gathers on them makes slow slow changes in the pattern of the light, and if you watch one long enough your eye begins to insist it has changed, and you cannot tell whether it has.

And there is something else, the quietest thing: the water remembers. Touch the pool and the whole surface registers it — not a ripple, not visibly, but a slow bloom of warmth that spreads outward, and for a long time after your hand is out the place is not the same and is slowly becoming the same again. It takes hours. The basin will recover from you in an afternoon and no one would ever know you were there. That is a kind of courtesy, and also a kind of warning.

Little else moves. There are no animals — no birds in the green above, no insects on the water, no fish, nothing that swims or creeps. It is a place emptied of fauna a long time ago, or a place they never found. The silence is not absolute; it is made of the ticking of the water, the micro-sound of leaves landing, the faint sigh of the outer overflow. But nothing calls, nothing answers. The sounds are all the sounds of things doing what they have always done.

You can stay on the rim. That is the honest way to be here — to sit above the mist on the dry outer stone and watch a leaf make its crossing, and let the basin keep its slow counsel. Or you could go down, and stand among the half-faces, and hunch to feel the drops landing on you, and let the warm water take your ankles, and be for a while a thing the basin is slowly recovering from. It will not stop you. It has no rules that can be broken, only a pace that cannot be hurried, and the strong sense — as the mist closes behind you and the sounds resolve into something that is almost a pattern and then is not — that it has been keeping this slow appointment longer than anything that would notice you could remember.
