Here is a description of the landscape.

---

The world here is a single, bright room, held deep within a warm and breathing sea. It is a place of constructed order, a pocket of air and light sunk into the living water. The ceiling is a dome of thick, green-tinted glass, held in a lattice of pale, non-ferrous metal—a bronze-like alloy that does not corrode. This dome is the sky, and it is a sky of shifting, liquid light. The sun is a distant, wavering smear, but at the dome’s apex, a great, multi-faceted lamp hangs from the central pillar. It burns with a steady, warm-white intensity, a miniature sun for this drowned world, ensuring the brightness is absolute and constant.

The water is the second sky. It presses against the dome with immense, patient force, and you can feel it in the low, constant thrum that vibrates in your bones. It is a sound, not a silence: the groan of the metal frame, the hiss of the water’s friction against the glass, the deep, resonant moans of the structure’s anchoring cables as they strain against the currents. The water itself is warm, almost blood-warm, and it carries a faint, mineral scent through the station's ventilation—a smell of salt and old stone. Beyond the dome, it is not a dark abyss but a murky, blue-green infinity, alive with the motion of waves that are felt rather than seen. The only view is of the water’s shifting skin, the caustic patterns of light dancing across the exterior surface.

The floor is a single, continuous slab of cast, polished concrete, cool underfoot and slightly slick with condensation. It is a perfect circle, the room a perfect hemisphere. The space is not large; a dozen long strides would cross it. The air is kept in constant, loud motion. A massive ventilation system, with great fans of carved wood and brass, works to scrub the carbon dioxide from the air and cycle it back into the dome. This is the loudest sound, a deep, rhythmic *whoosh* and *thrum* that is the heartbeat of the place. There is no soil, no natural substrate. Life is kept in ceramic troughs arranged in a sparse ring near the walls.

The vegetation is meager, a collection of tough, useful plants. There are creeping vines with tough, waxy leaves, trained to climb trellises made of bone, not wood. Their purpose is to help purify the air, their roots submerged in a hydroponic solution of grey water. The most prominent plant is a single, strange tree, not tall but squat and thick-trunked, with bark like rough-cast ceramic and leaves that are wide, pale, and almost translucent, straining for the synthetic light. Its fruit are hard, woody pods, not for eating but for their fibrous material, which is harvested for cordage. The plants are silent, but their leaves constantly tremble from the fans.

There are no animals. The only movement is the dance of dust motes in the bright light and the slow, deliberate turning of the great fans. The only other presences are the tools of maintenance. A wooden workbench stands against one curved wall, its surface scarred and stained. Racks of hand tools—wrenches of bone and bronze, chisels of sharpened shell, wooden mallets—hang in perfect, orderly rows. The world is defined by its rules of maintenance: the constant negotiation between the constructed and the submerged. A slow, persistent leak in one of the dome’s seals is a daily battle, met with a bronze pan and a mop. The condensation on the floor is swept into shallow gutters that line the perimeter, filtered, and returned to the plants.

The atmosphere is one of focused, warm, and noisy isolation. It is a place of work and observation, a small, bright outpost in the vast, liquid warmth. It is a machine for living, and for tending to the small, fragile life it contains. Every surface is made, every process is controlled, and the only wildness is the immense, patient pressure of the sea, a wildness that is a constant, loud, and intimate companion.
