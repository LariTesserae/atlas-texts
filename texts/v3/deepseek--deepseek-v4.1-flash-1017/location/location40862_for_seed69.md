**Kettlemoor**

Kettlemoor is a highland continent under a lid. It stands high enough that the air should be thin and cold, but the rock beneath it is warm; warm water rises through old fractures, and the rain that condenses on the plateau is warm rain. The sky is a black ceiling of cloud and steam. There is no day here, not in any ordinary sense. Darkness is the medium. The only light is local and made by the place itself—furnace mouths, electric arcs, the pale blue of certain fungi, the low red glow of vent water—so the darkness is never empty, but it is never lifted.

The terrain is a basalt tableland cut by old rivers and shallow basins. There are peat flats, sinkholes, springs, braided channels, and long drowned hollows where the water table sits close enough to the surface that every step presses water up around the boot. The ground is a sponge. The channels are old and settled into their beds; the hills are rounded; the sinkholes have lips of moss and fern. Nothing looks raw. The place has been wet for a long time, and erosion here is not a process you catch in the act so much as a habit the land has settled into.

The materials are black basalt, pale peat, iron, wet concrete, rust, brass, glass, fungal cellulose, and reed. Scattered through the marsh are industrial works: pump stations like iron chapels, gas refineries, boiler towers, distillation stacks, pipe roads running on low trestles, electric lines strung between them. They are not a city. They are nodes—isolated, occasional, each a small island of machinery in a vast wet dark. They draw warm brine and methane from the peat, and their engines run continuously. Pumps beat. Turbines whine. Steam vents. Some stations are crewed by small teams; others are automated and visited when something fails. The marsh grows over all of them.

The vegetation is lush and layered. Black-leaved reeds stand in the shallows; pale moss carpets the peat; fern trees lift their croziers through the fog; bladderworts and vine mats and fungal canopies fill the spaces between. The plants grow in tiers—moss on peat, reeds on moss, ferns on reeds, vines on ferns—so that the whole place feels near-touching, saturated, crowded with growth. Spores and pollen hang in the warm air. The leaves are not green so much as deep and glossy; they catch the local lights and throw them back in wet flashes.

The fauna is abundant and loud. Blind herons stalk the channels. Marsh hogs root through the peat. Sump deer move in small groups, their coats slick with condensation. Glass-winged flies cloud around the warm outfalls. Lamp eels coil in the spring pools. Burrowing crayfish tick against the roots. Reed choirs—frogs and insects and things that are neither—fill the darkness with a continuous metallic chorus. Steam bats stitch the air above the fens. The sound is constant: calls, splashing, chewing, wingbeats, water falling, steam venting, rain on reed and iron, and beneath it all the engines, near and far, a low industrial pulse.

Kettlemoor is strange in ways that feel native to it. Sound arrives late here. An engine has an echo that comes back after the engine has stopped. The water table breathes like a tide, though there is no sea. The moss grows into the shapes of pipes, or the pipes grow moss in patterns that look deliberate. The plants respond to vibration; the fauna have learned the industrial schedule. The weather is self-contained: storm cells form over the plateau and circle the rim, raining into the fens, never leaving. The place is vast enough for that—large enough that a storm can live its whole life inside it, large enough that travel between stations takes days, large enough that the plateau has its own weather and its own physics of sound.

The rules are simple enough to learn and hard enough to ignore. Water is always near the surface. Heat comes from below. There is no daylight; orient by sound and warmth. The marsh reclaims structures. Industry is scattered and must be maintained. The water table rises and falls. The weather cycles. Growth and predation continue. Kettlemoor is not a landscape with industry in it; it is a warm, wet, dark highland that has learned to include industry. The engines are another kind of animal. The marsh is loud, dense, old, and active, and it is in its own state.
