# The Provenance

High on a plateau of new stone — stone that has never been rained on, never been scoured, never had a single edge worn round — there is a hollow about the size of a room. You can cross it in a dozen paces. It is the only soft place for a long way in any direction, and it is not soft in any way you would recognize.

The floor of the hollow is a single slab of pale crystalline rock, pinkish where the sun hits it, still faintly warm from underneath, because it has not finished forming. It is not old rock. It has no history of weather on it. Its edges are razor-keen, its facets still glassy, its fractures bright. There is no soil, no gravel, no dust — dust is the product of grinding, and nothing here has ground. The slab is cracked into a loose cellular pattern, and up out of those cracks the vegetation comes.

It is lush. That is the word for it, and it is also a lie, because there is no water anywhere in the hollow — not a puddle, not a damp seam, not a bead of condensation on a leaf. What comes up out of the cracks instead feeds on the rock directly: on the hot vapor of mineral sublimating out of the stone's unfinished interior, on the heat gradient between the warm slab and the cool thin air, on the slow chemistry of a crust that is still cooking itself. The plants are pale green shot through with something pinker and more metallic. Their stems look poured rather than grown. Their leaves are thin plates, some translucent, some opaque as porcelain, and they do not wilt. They rustle, faintly, when the thermal wind moves — a sound like paper handled by someone careful.

They are scattered, not massed. Each plant stands alone with a clear space of bare bright stone around it, as though each one had claimed a territory, or as though the hollow could only support so many at a time. A low thicket of the plated ones against the western wall. Three tall spindle-things in the middle, spiraling unevenly upward, each with its own aura of heat-shimmer. A cluster of small hard knots in a corner that might be seedlings or might be the beginnings of something else entirely.

Because that is the trouble with the hollow, and it is a delight too: the categories will not stay put.

A spindle that was a plant last decade is now, at its lower third, unmistakably stone — the same pink crystal as the floor, continuous with it, and the upper third is still green and still growing. Elsewhere a crystal has begun to unfurl into leaves. There are forms in the hollow that are being built by biology and forms that are being built by geology, and the two processes have not agreed on a boundary, and so there isn't one. Whatever line divides "growing" from "crystallizing" here is a matter of convention and probably of mood. Nothing in the hollow enforces it.

The air is hot and dry and smells of hot stone and something like ozone and something like a struck match. It is thin — this is high country, the sky above the hollow is a hard pale blue washed nearly white at the edges, and the sunlight arrives with no mercy and no filter, laying down sharp black shadows that do not move much. But the light is ordinary light. That is worth saying. The sun is a sun; it does what suns do; nothing here is dim or glaucous or askew. The wrongness is in the ground, not the sky.

And the hollow behaves like a factory.

Not built — nothing is built. There is no wall, no post, no tool, no mark of a hand anywhere in it. But the processes running in this small warm room are industrial in their logic. Vapor rises from the slab in the heat of the day, condenses on the underside of plated leaves, and runs — not as water, as a thin hot liquid that is closer to a molten salt — back down the stems, carrying dissolved stone with it. Along the cracks, mineral sublimate deposits in graded layers, and the layers are sorted, and the sorting is precise: this band is one salt, that band another, as clean as if it had been distilled. Which it has. The hollow is a distillation apparatus running unattended for centuries, with no operator and no product, refining the plateau's raw rock into things shaped like leaves.

It is mostly quiet. There is no water sound, and no animal sound — there are no animals, none, not an insect, not a bird crossing, not a burrow or a dropping or a track. The quiet is not empty so much as attentive. Underneath it runs a low hum you feel more than hear, the slab's own settling, the sound of a thing cooling at a pace too slow to call cooling. Occasionally a tick as a new facet releases. Occasionally a long soft creak of thermal expansion traveling through the floor. In the late afternoon, when the rock's stored heat comes off it, there is a faint dry hiss — the sublimating vapor, escaping. That is the whole soundscape. It would take a patient listener an hour to be sure they had heard everything.

Nothing happens quickly. A leaf that is green this year may be a pinnacle of pink crystal in sixty. The hollow's changes run on the timescale of the stone's unfinished business, and the stone is in no hurry to finish. The warmth rises and falls with the sun. The vapor rises and condenses and runs back down. The plants take the rock apart a molecule at a time and the rock, in its slow exhalation, takes some of them back. Snow does not fall here and rain does not fall here and nothing is washed away, and so nothing is worn down, and the hollow keeps its sharp bright edges the way an idea keeps its edges when no one has had time to argue with it yet.

You could stand in the middle of it and turn around once and see the whole thing. It would not look like much — a small bare shelf of pink rock with some strange pale plants on it, high up in a dry country, under a bleached sky. It would look like the beginning of something, a place the world had started to make a garden out of and then walked away from before it decided on the rules.
