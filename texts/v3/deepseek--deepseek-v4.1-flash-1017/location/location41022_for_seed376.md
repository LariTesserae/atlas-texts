The place has no name that is spoken, but if it needed one, it would be called the Chiming Weald. It is a high country, a broad and weathered plateau of old, rounded stone that has been in its own settled state for a very long time. The hills do not thrust upward; they swell like the backs of sleeping animals, and the valleys between them are shallow and filled with a cold, damp breath that never quite disperses. The air is a perpetual twilight of mist, a condensation environment where the world is soft-edged and distances are unreliable. Rain does not so much fall as condense onto every surface, a fine, persistent drizzle that beads on leaves and stone, swelling the rivers that thread the valleys.

And it is the vegetation that defines everything. This is not a forest; it is a single, continuous, living mass. The plant life is total, a substrate that has overwhelmed the terrain. Trees do not grow in groves but in a seamless, undulating fabric of bark and branch, their roots intertwined into a single vast network beneath the moss-choked soil. The ground itself is a deep, spongy carpet of lichen and liverwort, a quarter-meter thick, that squelches with every footfall and holds the cold moisture like a sponge. This is plant-as-architecture. The canopy is a low, dense ceiling of interlocking boughs, heavy with beards of pale moss that hang like tattered banners, dripping. Everything is layered upon everything else. Vines thick as a man's thigh wrap the greater trunks, and from them grow ferns and flowering mosses, creating a three-dimensional lattice of life that fills every available space. To walk here is to push through a dense, living tapestry.

The silence is the most profound feature. There are no birds, no insects that sing, no rustle of small fauna—the animal life is rare, almost nonexistent, save for a few pale, slow-moving things that live in the deep leaf-litter and are never seen. The quiet is not empty; it is a presence. It is the sound of a world holding its breath. The only noises are the constant, soft *plip-plip-plip* of condensation dripping from leaf to leaf, and the low, pervasive groan of the vegetation itself. It is a sound of immense, slow pressure.

Because the world is not still. The dynamic is turbulent, a chaotic, hyperactive energy that contradicts the silence. The plant mass is in constant, restless motion. It is not the motion of wind, for there is no wind. It is an internal, metabolic churning. Great limbs of the integrated forest shift and settle with a sound like the grinding of millstones far away. Pale, fleshy flowers bloom in a matter of minutes, exhale a puff of spores, and wither in an hour. The "industrial" nature of the place is here, in this relentless, mechanized biological production. It is a factory of growth and decay running at full tilt, a self-devouring engine of creation. You can feel the heat of it, a faint, damp warmth radiating from the fermenting heart of the thicket, a stark contrast to the cold air. The vegetation is not just alive; it is *working*, processing, transforming, in a way that feels less like nature and more like a vast, organic machine left running for eons.

The rules of this world are strange. Nothing is permanent, yet nothing is new. The forms are long-established, the stone is ancient and weathered into smooth, balanced shapes by millennia of damp and slow, chemical erosion. But the living matter that clings to it is in a state of perpetual, furious flux. It is a place of pure process, a landscape where the only constant is the churning, silent, cold and relentless transformation of matter into more of itself.
