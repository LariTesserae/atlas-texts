# The Loud

Maps call it Basin 9. The people who work its edges call it the Loud, and they say it the way you'd say a person's name.

You hear it from the rim, eight kilometres out, before the haze lets you see anything at all. It comes up through the roadbed first — a continuous shudder in the soles, like standing on the chest of something asleep. Then the air takes over and it becomes a wall: a roar with edges in it, whistles, stutters, a hundred thousand rising and falling tones layered until they stop being tones and become weather. Some of it is animal. Most of it is not. It never stops. Crews who spend a season down there come back with a particular way of speaking, flattened and careful, as though they're still picking their words out of a noise.

**The land is new.** That's the first thing to understand, and it explains nearly everything else. The basin is not being worn away; it is being pushed up. Something beneath it — warm, slow, patient, and far too large — is lifting a floor of sediment out of the water table a few centimetres a year, and the result is a country that looks freshly cut. The channels have raw, sharp banks, all their edges still crisp. The mud faces are clean planes, unweathered. Nothing here has had time to round off. Erosion is what happens elsewhere, in some older country; here the process runs the other way, and the land remembers being underwater the way a child remembers a dream. Few things in the basin are older than thirty years, and the oldest thing you can stand on is a mat.

The mat is the ground. It is not quite soil and not quite raft — root mass, silt, the shed husks of a great many small animals, all bound together by a white filamentous lattice that gives under the boot with a sound like a struck drum. Every step rings. Stand still and you can feel the pulse of other walkers through it, kilometres off, transmitted as cleanly as a plucked string.

**Water is intermittent here, and that is its character.** Not a river, not a marsh — a scatter. Warm shallow pools that appear in the low spots after the rain squalls and last three days or thirty. Braided threads of channel that open and close like something breathing. Condensation everywhere: it beads on the filament, hangs in the leaf-axils, collects in the hollows of the built things, so that even in a dry week the whole basin glistens. Rain comes in hard short bursts, warm as bathwater, and afterwards the air is so thick you can see the shape of your own breath hanging.

**The light is dim and amber and does not change much.** Sunlight gets through the high haze — spores, water, dust, the slow exhalation of ten thousand square kilometres of growing things — and arrives as a smear, never sharper than late afternoon. Shadows are soft and ambiguous. There is no true night and no true noon. What light there is gets supplemented from below: the filaments glow faintly in the wet, blue-white, brighter where the ground is thin, and the pools carry it, and the whole basin at its dimmest looks lit from inside.

And it is **lush**. Almost obscenely so. Everything grows on everything: vines that root in other vines, ferns the size of rooms, soft-leaved things that fold at a touch and reopen minutes later, mats of weed that skim the pools and turn the water solid green. The air smells of cut sap and warm mud and something faintly electrical. But the green is not entirely vegetable. The white filament runs through the roots as much as through the mud — a lattice, nanoscale-fine, self-repairing, and continuous over distances your eye insists are empty. It threads the leaves. It threads the animals. When a plant here is damaged, the filament mends it before the plant has finished noticing.

**The animals are everywhere and mostly small.** Insects and near-insects, amphibian things with too many joints, jumping invertebrates that travel in coordinated sheets and land like thrown gravel. They chorus in overlapping pulses that synchronise, break, and resynchronise — a structure in the noise you can learn to follow after a few days. And then, rarely, the large ones. Hull-beasts: slow dome-shelled grazers built of the same white ceramic as the filament, who move perhaps a kilometre a week and whose footfalls you feel an hour before you see them. Cloud-fliers that come over in their thousands at dusk and are heard as a single descending tone. Things in the pools that are only ever a disturbance in the weed.

**There is a town here, and almost nobody is in it.** The construction is real and substantial — a settlement's worth of it — but spread across a valley and continuous with the marsh, so that you come upon a span of grown bridge and walk it for an hour before reaching the structure it serves. Towers of stacked, accreted floors, ceramic and pale. Platforms. Staircases to nowhere in particular. Woven roofs that shed the rain in a fine curtain. Some of it is clearly old, sinking into the silt. Some of it is clearly *growing*: new floors laid on overnight, new spans reaching out across the channels, doors that were not there last season, a stair that now arrives at a room with no other entrance. Whatever builds it is diligent and has no interest in occupants. Nobody has walked on most of these surfaces. They are being made anyway, with great care, for a purpose that has not yet required anyone's presence.

**The rules of the place are mostly rules about sound.** The ground is an instrument, and the basin is tuned. Loudness is not just a nuisance here; it is a material. Sound moves silt. Certain frequencies make the weed bloom and the filament brighten; others make the water stand up in ridges and travel. The roar has grammar — a low chord that means the lift has shifted and a channel will open within the day, a rising figure that precedes the hull-beasts, a silence-with-pressure that means a squall. People who work here learn to walk in time with the chorus so their footsteps vanish into it. They learn that a shout carries four kilometres and that the basin answers. They learn not to stand on ground that has started to sing.

And they learn that the maps are worthless. The basin is younger than every survey of it. Channels close. Pools migrate. Structures appear. The mat thickens and the whole floor rises another hand's width, and the warm dark below it, where the lift is working, is always nearer than you think.
