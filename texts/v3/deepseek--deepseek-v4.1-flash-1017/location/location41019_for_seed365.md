# Ammer, on the Fresh Mountain

You come up through cloud, and the cloud does not end when you arrive. It only gets nearer to you — thinning from weather into a room-sized fog that sits in the cleft and breathes. This is the first thing to understand: the air here is not between you and the place. It is part of the furnishings. It beads on your sleeves. It condenses in the small of your back where the pack sits. It collects in the hollow of your collarbone and runs when you bend. People in Ammer are always faintly wet, and they have stopped noticing, the way fish have stopped noticing.

The mountain beneath is new. That is the second thing. It came up — is still coming up — from some slow argument between plates, and it has not yet been worn into softness. The rock is bright where anything has cut it, almost white; the streams run milky with rock flour, and the gorge below the town is a raw V, its walls still shedding. Every wet season, something falls. The ledges are sharp. Nothing has had time to round.

---

The cleft runs roughly north, and the town is stacked in it like something nested rather than built. Six or seven terraces, each one shored against the rock by a lattice of coppiced wood and rope, each one half the width it could be. There is room in Ammer for four hundred people. There are, most years, thirty-one. Perhaps thirty-four.

So the town is mostly empty rooms, and the empty rooms are the loudest thing about it, which is to say: they are not loud at all. Doorways open onto nothing. A hearth cold so long the moss has come in across the flagstones like a green tide, blunted every corner. Roofs sagging in the middle where no one has re-tied them. In one room a loom stands with a half-finished bolt so old the weft has felted into a single soft mass. In another, a wooden bowl on a shelf, and in the bowl, water, and in the water, a small pale animal that has been there long enough to be a resident and not a visitor.

Ammer shrinks. It does not grow. The terraces that fall in the wet season are not rebuilt; the people move inward, up, tighten. This is not tragedy here; it is housekeeping. The mountain is giving them stone faster than they can use it and taking their buildings back at the same rate.

---

Nothing is dry, but not much is wet either, in the way of standing water. The water is distributed — it is a property of surfaces rather than a body of anything. It hangs. It seeps. Every roof has a drip-catch: a slab of slate, a gutter of hollowed alder, a stone basin. The catch fills slowly and is drunk slowly and tastes of moss, of wet stone, of the mountain's own faint chalk. In the mornings the fog is so thick in the cleft that the far terraces are only a suggestion of structure, and the ropes between them are strings of beads where the moisture has gathered.

The rain, when it comes, comes hard and brief and cold, and then the fog closes over the wound. Afterward the rock steams. The mosses swell visibly — you can watch them do it, half a finger's width in an hour — and the whole cleft smells of wet iron and crushed green.

And underneath everything, always, the drip. Not a sound so much as a pulse. Ammer's true silence is a silence of dripping, which is not silence at all but is close enough that the people there will tell you it is quiet and mean it.

---

The forest does not have soil to stand in. That is why it climbs. Thin mats of moss on the fresh rock, root-hair fine, holding a film of water; fern growing out of the moss; vine growing out of the fern; a small hard tree growing out of the vine's crotch, thirty feet up, holding its own weight by grip rather than by ground. The whole slope is a lattice of things holding other things. Below it, if you dig, is not earth but rubble and bright shattered rock and then more rock.

And here is the strangeness that makes Ammer Ammer: the ropes have rooted.

The town is held together by rope — that is the engineering, that is the entire high technology of this place, knot and cordage and coppiced stake, no iron worth speaking of, a little traded copper kept for edges. The ropes run from terrace to terrace, house to rock, rock to rock. And the mountain is wet enough, and the air rich enough, and the vegetation hungry enough that rope left alone is colonized. Moss first, in the twist of it. Then a thread-fine creeper. Then, within a decade, the rope is a vine that remembers being a rope — it has kept the shape, kept the tension, and it is now green and growing and holding the terrace up more surely than hemp ever did. The oldest spans in Ammer are alive. They thicken. They have to be pruned or they will lift the roof they were meant to hold.

The town is thus half-grown. Walls of split stake that have sprouted. A doorframe that flowered one spring and was left, because it was beautiful, and had to be re-hung around.

---

The law of the place follows from the damp. **Stillness is how things are claimed.** Leave a thing untouched for a season and the moss finds it. Leave it a year and the moss has thickened and something has nested and the surface has gone soft, and by the second year you may find the thing has been quietly unmade — the adze handle furred into loam, the folded blanket become a bed for something with too many legs, the empty room no longer yours, because in Ammer a room you have not entered for a year is not a room you own. It is a room that has been given.

So people touch things. They walk the empty rooms on a rotation. They handle the tools they are not using. They knock the moss off the thresholds with the flat of a hand and let it fall and do not mourn it. There are perhaps two hundred small daily acts of maintenance in Ammer that are not maintenance of anything useful but maintenance of the claim.

The mountain also rises. Silently, imperceptibly, a few finger-widths a decade, and the town has a stone with notches cut in it, and once a year in the cold season the whole town goes out with mallets and wedges and re-ties — slackens here, takes up there, resets the rope-beds against the living rock. It is the closest thing to a festival. Nobody shouts. There is a good deal of quiet cooperation at height, hand signals across the cleft, and a dull padded knocking that does not carry.

---

The animals are everywhere. This is the fact that visitors cannot get used to and residents have stopped seeing.

They are not, mostly, loud animals. That is the trick of it. The overwhelming life of Ammer is soft-bodied, damp-skinned, and nearly mute. The millipede herds are the great herds of the place — pale, segmented, each the length of a hand, moving through the moss in their thousands, and the sound of a herd grazing is like the sound of a held breath. They crop the moss from the ropes and the roofs and the thresholds, which is why the people tolerate them, follow them, herd them gently from span to span with switches. The millipedes are the town's mowers. They are also, being numerous and slow and made of meat, the basis of everything else.

Snails with shells thin enough to see the animal's pulse through. Glass-winged flies that hang in the fog in columns and do not disperse when you walk through them; you simply pass through the column and they close behind you. Moths the size of two hands, so heavy with moisture that they cannot fly well and tumble along the terraces at dusk like blown leaves, and their wings, when they rest, are wet enough to drink.

And the heron-thing. There is a heron-thing. Four legs, too tall, too thin, wet grey fur rather than feathers, and it walks the rope spans at night with a delicacy that makes no sound whatsoever — no creak, no shift, a slow placement of weight that the ropes accept without comment. It hunts the millipede herds. It has never harmed a person. It will stand in an empty room for an hour and look at you and then leave, and you will not have heard it arrive or go. There are perhaps three of them on this face of the mountain. Nobody knows where they nest. Nobody has looked very hard.

In the cisterns, small pale things. In the moss, mites in their millions, a whole nation per handspan. In the wet rot of the oldest roofs, a soft white crawling economy that exists nowhere else and has no name in the town's speech, only a gesture — the fingers spread and turned down.

None of it makes noise enough to notice. There is no birdsong in Ammer. The birds here are few and small and know better. The overwhelming fauna of the place announces itself as texture, as movement at the edge of vision, as the sensation of the ground being slightly more alive than ground should be.

---

The people speak quietly because speech does not carry. The cleft swallows it — the moss drinks it, the fog baffles it, the wet rock takes it and does nothing with it. A shout across the terraces arrives as a mumble. So Ammer developed hands. There is a whole signed language of the town, most of it about rope and weather and the location of herds, and it is used at distances of ten feet as often as a hundred, out of habit, out of a sense that a spoken word is a thing you should be close enough to give someone.

They work wood and stone and rope and bone. They wedge the bright new rock, which splits willingly, sharp and fresh and not yet stubborn. They coppice the vines. They keep a few goats on the highest terrace, which is a mistake every year and continues anyway. They eat moss-crop and millipede — mashed, smoked, kept in leaf-wrapped bricks — and snails, and a flat sour bread made from a grain that will grow in rubble if you are patient, and there is not much of it and nobody minds.

The dimness never lifts. The cleft faces the wrong way, or the fog does not care to lift, and the light in Ammer is a pearl-grey that has no hour in it. Midday is a slightly brighter grey. The people's eyes are large and their skin is pale and they are very good at seeing the difference between one grey and another. At night, when it is truly dark, the cistern-jars give off a faint cold light from the things living in them — enough to find a doorway, not enough to find your footing. Everyone knows their footing.

---

Stand on the highest terrace at the hour when the fog pulses — it does pulse, once or twice a day, drawing out through the cleft and back in, a slow tidal breath of the mountain — and you will see the town below you as a set of green shelves, ropes beaded with water, roofs furred, herds moving over them in their patient thousands, three or four lamps burning in a hundred empty rooms, and thirty-odd people touching things, keeping things, holding their small claim against the wet and the growing and the mountain's slow silent insistence on going up.

It is not a place that is being built. It is a place being kept, one hand at a time, by people who know exactly how little is needed and how much of that little must be done every single day.
