# The Chord

## The floor

The ground is a basket.

You feel it before you see it, because it gives underfoot — a hand's depth, then it holds — and warm water presses up around your feet. It is a mat of root, pale as peeled willow, laid in patterns. Herringbone here. Concentric coils there. Long parallel bands that run sixty metres and then turn, all together, as though something had changed its mind. No zone repeats another's pattern. The weave thickens where weight has fallen on it, taking up slack, and the finest strands at the surface are always faintly moving, tightening and loosening by millimetres a day.

The whole basin is a shallow dome — a kilometre and a half across, a few metres high at its crown — and the dome is the mat. Water seeps from the crown outward in every direction, a few centimetres deep, warm, moving slower than a slow walk, and eventually it reaches the rim and goes over the edge in threads. That is the only direction anything travels here, and it takes days.

Under the mat there is no ground. There is warm air and hanging fringe of root, and far below, cloud, and the cloud does not clear.

## The columns

From the mat, sparsely, in clusters, rise the columns. The nearest cluster to you might be nine of them; the next is a hundred metres off across open water and open weave, and the next beyond that, and then the mist takes them.

They are not trees. They are pale green-white, jointed at intervals of three to eight metres, ridged longitudinally, and glazed with something that takes a shine in the wet — mineral, silica-bright, so they look half-plant, half-kiln. Diameters run from a wrist to four metres across. Heights you cannot always check, because the tops of the tallest go into the cloud that lies in the basin, and the cloud does not lift.

They are hollow. Each joint is a floor. Each interval between joints is a room.

A room: the walls beaded all over with condensation, which gathers, runs in the grooves, and collects in the dished centre of the floor, which is translucent enough to pass light. Daylight comes through the walls the way it comes through a lampshade — green-gold, sourceless, the whole chamber glowing. The air inside is body-warm and so wet it has visible weight. Sound inside is out of proportion. A whisper, in there, crosses the chamber and comes back to you fattened. A spoken word is a shout. A shout is a fog of noise that takes a long time to thin.

The rims of the joints are thickened and lipped, like healed wounds. Nothing here was cut. Everything here is grown, and grown things are not wild.

## The water

Each column is a fountain that runs inside itself.

Its outer skin is cool — cooler than the air, because water is moving under it — and the air gives its moisture up to it all day. The water gathers, climbs the wall by whatever slow capillary argument the wall makes, and arrives at the crown. There it spills, and falls down the column's own hollow middle. It strikes the pool on the first floor. Overflows. Strikes the next. And the next. All the way down, into the mat, out into the weave, creeping to the rim, over. And lifted again.

This is the only machine in the place and it has no parts you could name as parts. It is warm. It does not stop. It does not vary. Every column in the basin is doing exactly this, at its own rate, and has been for as long as the mat has, and will be.

## The sound

It is loud. You hear it from far off — before you see the rim, you hear it, and you would mistake it for a falls.

Underneath everything is a wash: hundreds of small strikes per second, water on water, on membrane, on wet mineral, so dense it stops being separate hits and becomes weather — like heavy rain heard through a good roof, except that no rain is falling. It is condensation. It is always condensing.

Above that wash sits the chord.

Because the columns come in sizes, and because a chamber rings at its own note, and because the water inside each one falls at a steady rate and strikes its floor-pools at a steady rate, each column is an instrument and cannot stop playing. The clusters are not tuned to each other. They were not tuned. But the sizes fall into families, and the families beat against each other, so the chord has a roughness in it — a slow throb, a disagreement that never resolves, that has been not-resolving since before anything could listen. Sometimes a beat almost closes and you wait for it and it does not close.

And at the openings — you will feel this before you understand it — the columns breathe. Every fall of water pushes a slug of air out through the vents and draws it back. Put your palm near an opening and warm air pulses against it, out and in, out and in, each column at its own rate. Nine columns breathing at nine rates around you, the dull red lining of the vents stirring.

That dull red lining is the third plant here, and the only soft thing. A fur, a beard, rust-coloured, growing only in the lee of the openings where the breath is. It is not structural. It is the only furnishing in the world.

## The openings

The vents are the doors.

They are at consistent heights — a sill at the level of your chest, a lip above your head — and they are shaped. Not irregularly shaped: shaped, in the way a bell is shaped. Rounded, lipped, thickened at the rim, wider at the base than the top. There are chambers you can enter by stooping, and chambers you cannot enter at all, and the ones you can't are the same shape as the ones you can, only larger, as though scaled up by a hand that got the proportion right and the size wrong. From one opening you can often see another, two hundred metres off, level with it, framed.

Everything here is sized. Nothing here is sized to fit.

The grazers are the only animals large enough to see: finger-long, segmented, blind, pale as the mat, living in the wet grooves of the walls and eating the red beard. They are rare enough that you might cross the whole basin and see three. There are drifting jelly colonies in the shallow weave-water that are more plant than animal and do not count. There is nothing else.

Nothing flies. No wings, no seeds in the air, no pollen, no insects in the warm column of the air. The air is empty of flight, and full of sound, and the sound is not made by anything that could hear it.

## The edge

Walk outward for three hours and the weave begins to come apart.

The bands loosen. The herringbone stops resolving. Individual strands lift out of the mat and reach out over nothing — over the fringe-root and the warm updraught and the cloud a thousand metres down — and they are half-woven out there, always half-woven, the pattern beginning to be legible in them and never becoming legible. The strands are soft at the tips, translucent, not yet hardened, still deciding.

Water runs off the fringe in threads, breaks into drops, and falls away. You can hear the drops going a long way down. The chord is behind you now and the hiss of the falling threads is in front, and they are both constant, and they are both loud.

The fringe reaches out. The fringe frays back. It does both at exactly the same rate, and so the edge of the world is a wave that does not move, permanently in the act of being born, permanently coming apart at the same speed, and it is not getting bigger and not getting smaller and it has never been anything else.

## The rules of the world

Nothing here arrives and nothing leaves. The water is the same water, going round. The sound is the same sound. The tissue is the same tissue, broken down and taken back into the mat at exactly the rate it is laid down.

There is no soil. There is no sediment, no dust, no silt in the weave-water — it runs clear because the mat takes everything back before it can settle. This is why there is no geology here, and no ruins, and no age: no patina, no weathering, no worn stair, nothing to show that anything has been happening for a long time. The place looks new in every part. It may be new. It may be old. There is no way to tell from the landscape, because the landscape has no past in it.

Growth equals decay, everywhere, exactly. This is not stillness — everything is in motion, water and air and sound all moving constantly — but the motions cancel, so the sum does not change. Listen for an hour. Listen for a day. The ensemble is identical. The individual droplets are random and the randomness is stationary, so the chord is a fixed object you can memorise and then be unsurprised by forever. There is no clock in the basin. No tide, no flower opening, no animal that comes and goes. The light will tell you roughly where the sun is, and that is all the time you get.

Nothing dries. The air is at saturation. Your clothes will not dry. Your skin will not dry. Leave a thing on the mat for long enough and it stays exactly as wet as it was, and then it stops being the thing and starts being the mat, because the mat takes everything back.

And there are no tools. You will look for the maker. The patterns differ by zone, as if by many hands or by one hand over many patient seasons; the openings are sized in a way that implies a single intention; the spacing of the clusters has a rhythm you can nearly tap. You will look for the carvings and the scaffolding and the abandoned workings, and there are none, because there was never any cutting. The world is its own artifact. The sound is what the architecture is for, and the architecture is sized to the sound, and nothing anywhere in the basin is the size the architecture is sized for.

At dusk the light comes through the walls low and orange and the clouds inside the basin turn the colour of the water. The chambers go dim. And in the dark the floor-pools, where the falling water has been striking them all day, hold a faint cold glow of their own — so the columns stand lit from inside, from the bottom up, each one a dim green lantern with a chord coming out of it, breathing at its vents, weeping on the inside, going nowhere, perfectly.

You are standing on the roof of the world and it is a basket, and it is singing.
