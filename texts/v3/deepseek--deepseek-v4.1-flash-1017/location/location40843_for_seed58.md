## The Kettle

The place is a sinkhole town called the Kettle: a bell-shaped cavity under a dry plain, no more than a few hundred paces across, but packed to its walls with tiers of buildings. It is below the surface entirely. There is no sky, only a hot rock ceiling that sweats. The cavity is young — a fresh collapse, its basalt faces still sharp, unrounded, splintered where the floor dropped away. Ledges jut; cracks run clean and new. Nothing here has had time to soften.

Heat comes from below. The floor is warm through boot soles, and in the lower galleries the air is thick enough to feel like breath. Water comes from above, but not as weather. It condenses on the cool underside of the ceiling and on every cold pipe, gathers, and falls in slow, intermittent rain — not everywhere, only where cold metal crosses the warm updraft. The town is organized around that fact. Roofs are catchments. Streets are gutters. Factories are condensers. Puddles stand in sumps and old foundation pits; the walls are damp to the touch; salt crusts bloom where moisture stops. It is not wet country. It is dryness with pockets of water in it, and a ceiling that occasionally drips.

The architecture is dense and stacked. There are few open spaces. Buildings cling to the cavity walls and rise in narrow terraces: brick shells furred with salt, corrugated sheds, ceramic-lined tanks, rooms built into fissures and then built over, so that the settlement reads as layers of construction rather than a plan. Catwalks bridge the gap; ladders drop through floors; pipes run in bundles along every surface, some lagged with cloth, some bare and beaded with condensation. The place is small enough to cross in minutes if you know the ways, but it is not simple. It is vertical and crowded. Sounds have nowhere to go but into each other.

Industry is everywhere but modest in scale — not a vast manufactory, a hamlet of machinery. Piston pumps thud in cellar rooms. Heat exchangers tick as they shed scale. Condenser sheds drip into troughs. A foundry glows at the lower level, its mouth orange, its chimney venting into the rock ceiling where the vapour joins the general sweat and comes back down somewhere colder. Wiring loops between buildings in insulated runs; gauge glass glows green and amber; sodium lamps make small dim islands. The light is perpetual dusk, except near furnace mouths, where it is red and dry.

Vegetation is sparse and patient. It grows where cool damp meets mineral: threadlike lichen on pipe lagging, salt-moss in the seams of wet brick, a fur of pale green in the runoff channels. Nothing larger. There are no animals at all — no insects in the sumps, no rats in the grain stores, no birds. The silence is not empty; it is muffled and close. Water drips. A pump cycles. Somewhere a valve sighs. The rock itself gives off a low, almost subaudible warmth, and the dense buildings knock the sound down before it travels.

The Kettle is active in the way a machine is active and the way a young hole in the ground is active. Convection cells move the hot air in slow loops. The ceiling rain starts and stops. Pumps cycle. New cracks open and old ones weep. Mineral skins form on wet metal and are scraped off. The whole settlement is still settling: fresh rock shifts, lintels crack, and the town repairs itself in patches. Nothing is worn smooth yet. The edges are sharp; the rust is new; the streets are gutters; the roofs are catchment. The rule of the place is simple: heat rises, water condenses, and everything built here is arranged to make the two meet where it can be used.
