# The Sump

You hear it before you see it, and what you hear is not weather.

The land drops gently for a kilometer or more — a shallow dish, no rim worth the name, the horizon a soft warm nothing at the edge of vision. The ground is pale: a stone the color of wet newspaper, eroded into long parallel ribs and troughs that run roughly north to south, like the underside of something that once had a purpose. Between the ribs the stone is slick and dark, and the dark is water.

The water does not flow. It *pulses*. Every fourteen or fifteen seconds a low thud moves through the stone underfoot — not loud, but felt in the soles and the teeth — and with it the water in the troughs rises perhaps a hand's width, surges a few meters, and settles back. The surge passes down the length of the basin in a slow wave, trough to trough, so the sound arrives as a rolling series of clucks and gulps and long wet hisses, punctuated by the thuds. It is loud. It is the loudest thing here, and it does not stop, and after an hour you will stop hearing it as noise and start hearing it as *something running*.

That is the first strangeness, and it is only a strangeness if you insist on asking who built it. Nothing built it. The troughs are solution channels — warm mineral water has been eating this stone for a very long time and has finished the job. What is left is the shape the water wanted, and now the water simply occupies what it made. The terrain is not being eroded anymore. It is eroded. The system is complete, and it will go on being complete for as long as anything here lasts, because there is nothing left to remove.

Under the ribs, the stone is hollow in places, and the hollows are what make the pulse. Where the channels deepen, warm water collects in chambers and then is displaced by water coming from below — the basin sits on something warm and pressurized and patient — and the chambers vent that pressure up through narrow throats in the rock, and the throat is the thud, and the thud is the whole acoustic signature of the place. There are hundreds of these throats, distributed unevenly, so the rhythm is a canon rather than a march: near ones strike sharp, far ones arrive late and muffled, and every so often two or three fall into step for a minute and then drift apart. Nothing coordinates them. Nothing needs to.

The air is warm — body-warm, a few degrees above skin — and it is wet. Not raining, usually, but the moisture is in everything: a fine condensation beads on the stone in the early hours, and the trough-water gives off a faint steam where it is shallowest, and there are days when a warm drizzle comes down out of a sky the color of a lampshade and does not so much fall as hang. When it does rain, the rain does not change the water level. The basin is in balance. It takes what comes and gives what goes and the pulsing continues exactly as before, fourteen seconds, fifteen seconds, fourteen.

The light is ordinary — flat, diffuse, the sort of light you get through high overcast with the sun somewhere behind it, bright enough to see by and too soft to cast real shadows. It does not vary much. You will not see a sunrise here. The day is a long pale plateau and the night is a slightly deeper gray and both of them are loud in exactly the same way.

There is almost no green. There is nothing green at all, in fact, and after a while that starts to feel like a held breath. The stone is gray and bone and liver-brown where it is wet. But the water leaves things behind: as it evaporates from the ribs it deposits a crust, pale and faintly warm to the touch, a soft mineral nap that fills the grain of the rock and makes it feel like felt. This crust is slowly replacing the stone it sits on — the water brings up mineral and the mineral sets, and the channel walls are being lined and thickened and healed from within at roughly the rate the water washes them away. That is why the system is finished. Erosion and deposition are holding hands. The basin has been in this state for longer than anyone has been counting, and it will be in this state when the counting stops.

Life is rare and does not announce itself. There are pale things in the deeper chambers — soft-bodied, no eyes, a hand's length, that rise with the surge and are carried a few meters and settle and wait for the next one. You will see perhaps three in a day if you look. There are also flat clots of something on the crust in the wettest hollows, not quite animal and not quite mineral, that thicken in the damp and thin when it dries and do nothing else observable. Nothing here is quick. Nothing here is numerous. The basin is not empty, but it is not populated either; it is the kind of place where a single living thing is an event.

The rule of the place, if you want one, is this: **the sound is the only clock.** There is no weather to speak of, no season, no growth, no decay you can measure, no structure standing or fallen, no artifact, no footprint that the crust won't fill in over a season or two. Time here is not passing in any way you can mark except by counting the thuds, and the thuds do not care how many you count. People who work the basin — and some do, for the mineral crust, which is worth something — learn to sleep through it in a week and then find they cannot sleep without it for the rest of their lives. They describe the place as *quiet*, which is the most alarming thing anyone says about it.

And there is one more thing, which is the reason the crust-harvesters keep odd hours. Every so often — not on a schedule anyone has pinned down, but not rarely either — the canon resolves. The throats fall into phase, all of them, for perhaps half a minute, and the whole basin speaks with one voice, a single enormous soft *whump* that you feel in your sternum and your sinuses and the arches of your feet, and then the water in every trough rises at once and holds, trembling, and then lets go and the rhythm scatters back into its loose unruly round. It is not a machine. There is no one operating it. But in that half-minute the basin sounds exactly like something enormous turning over in its sleep, and everyone down in the troughs stops what they are doing and waits for it to finish, and nobody has ever been able to say why.
