# The Breathing Ground

You come up onto it by a long ramp of weathered stone, and it takes a while to understand that the floor is wrong. Not the color — the color is unremarkable, a dull green-brown going gray in the haze. It's that the floor gives. It takes your weight and returns it with a slight delay, like a mattress that has been slept on. And it is loud here. Not the wind. The ground itself, and everything on it, and then the ground again.

## Terrain

The plateau is high and old and has been worn into its own shape and then left alone. The bedrock is planed down to low knuckles and shallow basins; the old channels that drained it have long since filled with plant and water and stopped draining. Nothing here is in the middle of becoming something else. It settled a long time ago into exactly this, and the processes that continue are cycles, not events.

The whole surface — every square meter of it, as far as any horizon — is a single continuous mat of plant matter, thirty to sixty centimeters thick, layered like felt. The top is a fused sheet of tiny leaves, almost a membrane, silvered in the heat. Beneath that: a thatch of living stems and holdfasts, tough and springy. Beneath that: a hot, dark, composting middle full of old dead material, where the water sits and the heat gathers and the air is different from the air above it.

The mat holds water the way a sponge does. It does not run off. Rain that falls here is absorbed and then released slowly from below, so the surface is a dry crust at noon and soaked at dawn, and the water table is not under the ground but *inside* it. Where the mat sags into an old basin, water stands in a lens under a film of plant, warm and brown and perfectly still, sometimes a hundred meters across. There are perhaps a dozen within a day's walk and they are the only places that look like places. Away from them, the plateau is a hot, damp-inside, dry-outside plain with an enormous sky and almost nothing rising from it.

## Emptiness and the few verticals

This is the thing the scale does: you can walk from dark to dark and pass three objects.

The pillars are the great exception. Some kind of emergent plant, twenty or forty meters tall, spaced ten or twenty kilometers apart — a woody column sheathed in its own foliage, standing in a ring of shade that is the only shade for a day in any direction. Each pillar is its own small country: its own insects, its own perched soil, its own birds. People navigate by them. A pillar is not a landmark because it is tall; it is a landmark because it is *named*, individually, and everyone who lives out here knows all eleven of them within a season's range and can tell you what's at the foot of each.

Between the pillars and the water lenses there is stone: knobs of bedrock, low shelves, the occasional cut bank two or three meters high where an old river worked its way down before the mat closed over it. And that is the inventory. That is the whole list of what rises.

## The damp

The moisture is intermittent and it runs on a clock. At night the mat cools and the air above it condenses, and by first light everything is beaded and the surface is soft enough to leave footprints that fill and darken and stay. By midmorning it has gone back up. The crust re-forms, pale and slightly crisp underfoot. The heat comes on — the surface can be uncomfortable barefoot by noon — and the mat exhales: warm, humid, faintly sweet air rising out of the composting middle through a million small chimneys made by burrowing things. The chimneys are the only texture on the horizon, and there are so many of them that the middle distance shimmers with the rising vapor and you cannot see far along the ground at all.

Then, at dusk, it reverses. The mat inhales, or something like it, and the moisture comes back down out of the cooling air.

## Sound

The first hour after dawn is the loudest thing most people will ever experience.

It is not a roar. It is *layered* — a chorus with structure, low to high, near to far, and it gets louder as it goes, because the mat transmits. Vibration travels through the felted middle layer for kilometers with very little loss, and everything that lives in the mat has evolved to use it. When a sawyer — a squat, armored animal the size of a dog — scrapes the surface to feed, the scraping arrives a kilometer away as a distinct, identifiable sound, altered by the ground it crossed. When a herd of mowers moves through, you hear them before you see them and you feel them in your feet before you hear them.

And the mat answers. Not an echo — a reply. The middle layer is full of resonant chambers, old root voids, gas pockets, water lenses. A sound put into it comes out the other side changed: filtered, delayed, sometimes transposed down. A call made at one place arrives somewhere else as a different call. The animals have built their entire social world on this. Predators hunt by listening for the transposition. Prey give alarm in frequencies that the mat carries badly, and escape in the confusion.

Which means that walking is a form of shouting. Every step you take is announced in all directions, and within a minute of standing still on the mat at dawn, things begin to arrive to find out what you are.

The other sound is slower and much lower. In the hour before sunrise, when the mat is wet and the gas exchange is at its peak, it produces a tone — a single vast subsonic hum that you do not hear so much as feel in the sternum and the teeth. It is not loud in the way the chorus is loud; it is large in the way the ground is large. People set their travel by it. When the tone fades, the day has begun.

## Fauna

Overwhelming is the right word, and it is overwhelming mostly in *number* and in *attention*. The mat is a habitat the size of a country with a hundred billion residents.

The mowers are the most visible: heavy, slow, herd animals that crop the top membrane in strips, leaving mown lanes a few meters wide that run for tens of kilometers and are visible from the pillars. The lanes are how the land is grazed — a shifting mosaic, always in motion, never the same two seasons running. The burrowers make the chimneys and the chimneys make the vapor. The sawyers make the noise. Above it all there are insects in numbers that defeat description, in layers, in columns, in drifting clouds that form over the water lenses at dusk and move like weather. There are flightless runners that go in loose flocks and can outpace a person easily and do so for no reason at all. There are things that live entirely in the mat's middle layer and never come out and are known only by their voices.

And it is deafening because it is *all talking*. The plateau is one enormous conversation and you have just stepped into it, loudly, and everything has an opinion about that.

## The built

Human presence here is a scattering of small, faint, half-alive things.

The people who live out here — few, spread thin, one household per horizon — do not build so much as *train*. Their tools are bone, fire-hardened hardwood, knapped chert, and now and then a small piece of traded metal kept sharp for a lifetime and inherited. Their shelters are frames of bone and wood over which they coax the mat to grow, and then keep coaxing, so that a house is a living thing with a growing shape, patched and pruned, warm inside, breathing. A settlement of thirty people is a low green mound with a doorway in it and you would walk past it at fifty meters.

What's strange is what they leave behind. Nothing here decays properly — the mat incorporates. An abandoned shelter doesn't fall down; it goes feral, keeps growing, and after two hundred years it is a wall of trained plant standing in the open, still alive, still shaped like a house, hollow inside, occupied by animals. Cut a line across the mat and the scar heals over decades into a raised seam that runs for kilometers. There are seams out here older than anyone's memory, and the people read them the way you'd read a map: this one was a firebreak, this one was a boundary, this one nobody knows, it's just there, and it is *still healing*.

So the archaeology is confused. A thing that looks like a ruin might be a building, or a plant that ate a building, or a plant that someone a very long time ago persuaded into the shape of a building which has since been persuaded into something else.

## The rules of the world

The land is one organism or a few, and nobody is certain which, and it does not especially matter to daily life. What matters:

**It is a commons and a body.** Cutting it is like cutting skin. It heals. It heals slowly, and it remembers the shape of the cut. You do not clear land here. You negotiate with it — drain here, graft there, seed the other thing, wait.

**You navigate by ear.** The heat haze and the chimneys and the flatness mean you cannot see more than a kilometer along the ground for most of the day. But the mat's timbre changes over buried rock, over water lenses, over the packed fill of old ruins. People know the sound of their home ground the way you know a face. A practiced traveler can cross a day of emptiness by listening to the floor.

**Water is a place, not a substance.** It comes at dawn and it sits in lenses. Between the lenses the land is dry on top and wet underneath and you must not confuse the two.

**Nothing is in a hurry.** The processes here are daily and seasonal and centennial, and none of them are catastrophic. There is no flood, no fire that takes the mat, no year that ruins the country. There is heat, dew, exhale, chorus, herd, scar, heal.

## Weather of its own

The plateau makes its own weather because it is big enough to. The mat's heat lifts convection cells off the surface in the afternoon, and these become small, wandering storms — columns of rain that walk across the country visibly, a kilometer wide, dropping water on one patch and moving on, and the mat absorbing all of it and releasing none. In the wet season these come in trains. In the dry season there is only the haze and the dawn dew and the tone.
