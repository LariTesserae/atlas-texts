# The Sump Terraces

The place announces itself as moisture before it announces itself as anything else. Air condenses on every exposed surface here—metal, stone, glass, the palms of your hands—so that touching anything is a small wet transaction. The warmth makes the water generous: it rises from below, falls from above, gathers in a hundred small basins and channels, and then finds its way to the edge to leap away into the fog.

It is high. You can feel it in the thinness of the air and the way the clouds are not above you but around you and sometimes beneath you. Looking down from any of the terraces, the world disappears into white. There is no visible ground below. There is only the structure itself, appearing to hold itself up out of the mist by sheer insistence.

And it is almost entirely made.

The terraces are a construction of black iron and pale-green ceramic, with pipes and grates and bolted plates, with reservoirs like cupped hands and spillways like piano keys. The whole place is perhaps a hundred meters across—small enough to walk in an afternoon, large enough to shelter a hundred kinds of occupation—and the work of decades. Every surface has been worked: the walls are tiled or peened, the floors of the upper terraces are set with a lattice of drainage slots and copper inlays, the stairways wind around columns that are themselves bundles of smaller pipes. Above, an arcing trellis of iron ribs holds climbing things and shields out only some of the light, so that the whole interior is dappled.

Nothing here is virgin. There is no wild soil. The mosses and ferns and creeping vines that occupy every available ledge grow in the nutrient film that slides down the walls; the trees in the two large upper basins have their roots in gravels that workers laid down. The lushness is rampant and yet entirely the product of this machinery—water brings the mineral wealth; the warmth incubates; the iron holds everything in place.

And so the structure is being *eaten* by what it grows. You can see it clearly in the lowest terrace: a railing that has been overgrown, a valve manifold buried under a living sleeve, a pipe that has split where a root got in and now weeps in a thin, constant stream onto the tiles below. The maintenance crews call this "the patient work." There are too few of them. The terraces were built in a hurry and are now kept in a kind of slow, negotiated decay. New work goes on constantly, side by side with the old: a section of scaffolding, a freshly cast bracket, a patch of bolts still bright. The whole place has the texture of something that has never quite finished arriving, even as it drowns in its own productivity.

## Sound and motion

It is mostly quiet—but "mostly quiet" is not silent. The water sets the baseline: a wet, patient percussion of drips into basins of varying depths, so that the whole structure is faintly tuned. There is the slap-and-fizz of overflow where a spout drops into a pool, the hiss of condensers, and below it all the low organ hum of the pumps. Only rarely does something louder happen—a valve opening, a whole terrace sluicing itself, a machine in a far room starting up. Mostly the place just talks to itself in water.

The air moves too, slow thermal circulation rising from the warm lower levels, carrying a smell of rust and green and hot stone. Birds are scarce—a few small drab things that nest in the trellis and stalk the drainage channels. The real animals are insects, a great many of them, silverflies and water-midges, working the standing water. And things in the pools: the terraces' reservoirs hold a green, teeming soup that the crews harvest at intervals with a long-handled net.

## The world's rules

1. **Water runs downhill through everything, and downhill is the wrong way.** The terraces step *upward* toward their own source—the water arrives at the top from a condenser array, descends through the gardens and channels, and is finally expelled at the bottom edge into the fog, where it becomes mist and feeds the condensers again. The whole small world is a closed loop, and the weather it generates is its own.
2. **The one prohibition is un-mediated contact.** The structure is sound—really it is—but the land it is anchored to is not ready. The fog conceals a slope that is still the raw material of a mountain; below the lowest terrace everything is slumping scree and unfinished rock. The builders' solution was the terraces themselves: you never touch the unfinished earth, you only walk iron.
3. **Patience is the law of the gardens.** Nothing may be uprooted and removed. When a pipe is lost to the overgrowth, the overgrowth is routed, not destroyed. The vegetal occupation is part of the water cycle now; to clear it would break the filtration that keeps the condensers working.

## The people

A handful of workers keep the place—perhaps a dozen, and their children, and two old men who claim to remember the earliest construction and may be lying. They dress against the wet. They eat what the pools and the trellised gardens give: greens that taste strongly of iron, small pale insects pressed into cakes, the occasional fowl. There is no enemy, no great mystery, no signal from below the fog—only the daily, amiable work of holding a constructed mountain together while the green slowly finds every bolt, and the water, patient and generous and everywhere, returns to the sky.
