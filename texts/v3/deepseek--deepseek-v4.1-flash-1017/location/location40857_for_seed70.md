**The Warm Drowned Highlands**

The place is a highland that has been filled to its brim with warm water. It sits far above the surrounding low country, a broad shoulder of young rock whose basins, saddles, and crooked valleys are all submerged beneath a single continuous sea. From above, the surface looks almost level, a pale green-gray skin breathing under ordinary daylight. Below it, the drowned terrain is still raw: knife-edged ridges, fresh scarps, gullies whose walls have not yet softened, fractures that hold their angles like broken glass. There is very little sand. The rock is young enough that it still looks recently made — pumice with glassy edges, dark seams of cooling stone, pale mineral crusts that have not been worn down. Erosion has begun, but only just; it works in slow scratches and thin films, not yet in rounded hills.

The water is warm throughout most of its depth, heated from below by the young rock and held by a humid air lid that rarely breaks. It behaves like a sea — it has weight, weather, a slow stratified movement — but it is strange in the mouth: fresh at depth, with only a thin saline lens near the surface. Light reaches far into it, not as a dim blue but as a green-gold ordinary light, sifted through floating spores and the layered leaves of the aquatic forest. The surface is not a boundary so much as a ceiling of warm rain that never quite falls; sometimes low cloud drifts into the upper meters and dissolves there, and for a while the air and the water trade places.

Vegetation is the dominant presence. It is not kelp, not coral, not anything that belongs only to the sea. It is a drowned woodland that has learned to stand in water: tall stalks anchored to the sharp rock, tough ribbon leaves that stream in the slow current, gas-bladders like knots of pale glass holding the canopy up toward the light. The plants grow in dense layered stands — low mats of creeping weed over the rock, middle stories of broad floating fronds, upper canopies that touch the surface and lie flat there in warm patches. They flower underwater. Their pollen drifts in slow clouds, not snow but a suspended bloom, and the water is crowded with it: every cubic meter holds blades, spores, shed leaf-fragments, tiny bubbles, the fine dust of young rock. Density here is not a crowd of animals. It is a saturation of plant matter, layered and near-touching, so that swimming through it feels like moving through a warm, green room that keeps folding closed behind you.

Fauna is rare. There are no shoals, no herds, no noisy flocks. A few transparent grazers move through the leaves, so thin they are mostly a disturbance in the light. Something with a soft shell clings to the pumice in the shallower saddles. Somewhere lower, in the cold seams between ridges, there may be slower things that never rise. But mostly the place is empty of animals, and their absence is part of its quiet. The sound is muffled and low: the slow sigh of water moving over stone, the occasional pop of a gas-bladder releasing, the faint creak of a stalk bending. Nothing hurries. The currents cross a ridge over days. A leaf falls and drifts for a season before it settles. The whole water body seems to inhale and exhale across long intervals, a pressure change so gradual that you notice it only when the plant canopy has lifted a meter and then, much later, settled again.

There are no structures here, no paths, no cut stone, no devices, no marks of making. The place is untouched in the sense that nothing has been built in it or taken from it. Its rules are biological and geological only: light feeds the plants, the plants thicken the water, the young rock warms the water, the water moves slowly enough that the forest is not torn apart. The sharp terrain is a young terrain, still expressing its own fractures. The warmth is a trapped warmth, held by height and humidity. The strangeness is not in any single anomaly but in the combination: a high sea that is warm and fresh, a drowned woodland flowering under ordinary light, a dense living volume with almost no animals in it, a landscape that is fully submerged yet still reads as upland — ridges, valleys, saddles, a whole regional topography lying quietly under its own warm ceiling.
