# The Throat

You hear it before you understand it, and you never stop hearing it.

It is not a noise in the usual sense — not something that happens and then ends. It is a condition of the place, the way cold is a condition of the place. A single low roar, vast and unmoving, as though the earth here is holding one long note. It fills the space so completely that after a while you stop calling it sound and start calling it pressure.

## The shape of the hollow

The ground falls away into a shaft — not a deep mountain shaft, but a low-country sink, a hole opened in flat limestone country where the water table sits close under the soil. The lip is soft with moss and old leaf-fall. Below the lip, the walls are worn into long vertical flutes, grooved and rounded by centuries of water that is no longer there to do the work. The stone is pale grey, dry to the touch on the upper walls, damp lower down. Everything about the shape says *settled* — the flutes are smooth, the ledges are rounded, the rubble at each landing has come to rest and stayed. Nothing is falling. Nothing is being cut. The place finished becoming itself a long time ago and has been keeping its shape since.

You climb down by ledges that were never cut for feet but accept them anyway. The air gets colder as you descend — not sharp, just steadily, patiently cold, the cold of a cellar that has never once been warm. Your breath goes white and hangs. It does not rise and disperse the way breath does outside; it drifts, thick and slow, and thins into the general damp.

Because there is damp everywhere and very little water anywhere. The walls sweat. Beads gather on the stone in the night-cold and run a little and stop. Drips fall from the flutes at long, irregular intervals — and each one, when it lands, is swallowed whole by the roar. You see the drip strike a ledge and you do not hear it. This is the strangest thing about the place: it is full of small sounds and you are deaf to all of them.

## The green room

A third of the way down, on a shelf that juts into the shaft, there is a pocket of living green.

It should not be there. There is almost no light — a grey seepage from the mouth above, gone by mid-morning, and below that nothing but the dark. And yet the shelf is thick with growth: pale mosses in cushions, ferns with translucent fronds, a mat of low things that have never needed sun. They are fed by the condensation, by the drip, by the slow mineral sweat of the walls. The green is not the green of a meadow. It is the green of things grown in the dark — soft, greyish, faintly luminous where the damp catches what little light there is.

It is a room, not a landscape. You could lie down in it and touch all four of its edges. Beyond it, in every direction, the hollow opens out and there is almost nothing — a vast cold vacancy of stone and air, the far wall a suggestion, a paleness, a nothing. The green room is the only density in the place. Everything else is the space between.

This is the balance of the hollow: one small lushness, held in an enormous emptiness, kept alive by moisture that arrives a drop at a time.

## The sound

The roar comes from below and from the throat above and from the stone itself, and you cannot separate these. Somewhere far under the floor — under the reachable floor — a body of water moves through a passage too deep to enter, and the shaft is shaped to gather that movement and hold it. Wind crosses the mouth. The two mix in the fluted column and become one tone, and the tone is the loudest thing for a day's walk in any direction.

It does not vary. That is the thing to understand. A river outside would rise and fall; a wind would gust. This sound has the steadiness of a held breath, and its slowness is the slowness of geology — the water below is patient, the wind is weak, the stone is enormous, and the three of them together make a roar that changes over years and not over minutes. You could sit here a week and hear the same note. You could come back in a decade and it would have shifted a little, and you would not be able to say how.

The sound presses. It is in your chest and your teeth. It makes sleep a negotiation. It is why the fauna here are what they are.

## What lives here

Rare, and quiet, and adapted to a world that is all pressure and no signal. Small pale things that move by touch along the walls. A kind of long-legged insect that walks the drip-lines and seems to feel the water through its feet. Once, on the third ledge, a shape the size of a hand that unfolded slowly and folded again — something that had no eyes and did not need them, that read the roar the way you read a page, finding in its constancy the tiny differences that mean *prey* or *danger* or *the drip has moved*.

Nothing here calls. There would be no point. The roar takes every voice and gives nothing back. The creatures of the hollow communicate in touches, in vibrations too fine to see, in the long slow chemistry of things that share a wall.

## The marks of hands

On the shelf across from the green room there is a structure. It is small and half-fallen and very old. Dry-stone, hand-set, no mortar — a low curved wall that once sheltered something, a hearth-ring, a scatter of flat stones laid as a floor that the damp has lifted and tilted one by one. There was a roof; there is now the memory of a roof in the way the stones lean. Beside it, cut into the soft wall, a shallow stair of five steps going nowhere, worn round at the edges by feet that used it for a long time and then stopped.

Whoever built here used hand tools and simple metal, or no metal at all. They built one thing, in one place, facing the green. They did not build more. This is the only mark of hands in the whole hollow, and it is fading at the rate stone fades — slowly, in balance, in its own state. It does not feel abandoned. It feels finished.

## The rules

The place keeps to a few laws and keeps them without exception.

- **Water arrives, it does not flow.** Nothing runs here. Moisture comes as vapour and leaves as drip, and the drip is the clock.
- **Sound is constant and indivisible.** You cannot locate it, muffle it, or outlast it. You can only become used to it, and you will, and then the silence when you leave will be the thing that deafens you.
- **Light is nearly absent and entirely borrowed.** What there is comes from above and is gone by midday. Below the green room it is dark enough that your eyes stop being useful and your hands become your eyes.
- **Growth is local and slow.** The green room does not spread. It has found its edges — where the drip reaches, where the rock holds moisture, where the cold is just bearable — and it stays inside them.
- **Nothing here is hurried.** The water below moves at the speed of water in stone. The stair wears at the speed of feet. The moss grows at the speed of moss in the dark. The roar has been the same roar for longer than the stair has existed.

## Standing in it

You stand on a ledge with your back to cold fluted stone and the roar filling you to the edges, and the green room glows faintly across the shaft, and beyond it and above it and below it the hollow opens into a darkness that is not frightening so much as *large* — a vacancy with one small living thing in it, and one small built thing, and you.

The damp settles on your coat. A drip falls and you do not hear it. Your breath goes white and drifts and is swallowed. Cold, patient, loud, nearly empty, and quietly, stubbornly alive in one tucked-away green place — the hollow keeps its note, and will keep it after you have climbed back into the wind and forgotten how to hear anything but this.
