# The Finished Hollow

You come down into it without noticing the descent, because the country is so low and so soft that the ground simply stops being the ground. The limestone here is old and warm and full of holes, and at some point the roof of one of its buried chambers gave way — not dramatically, not recently, but once, quietly, and then the pieces dissolved and the edges rounded and the whole thing settled into the shape it has been holding ever since.

It is small. Fifteen paces across, perhaps a little more, if you count the sloping shelf on the north side where the rubble went. The walls are not high — three metres at the deepest, dropping to half a metre at the southern lip where the ground outside comes in to meet it. From a distance it is a shadow in the grass, a dimple. You could walk past it and feel only a coolness at your ankle.

---

**The stone.** The walls are fluted and scalloped, the way limestone gets when water has been working on it for a very long time — scooped into shallow bowls, ribbed into soft vertical channels, all the hard edges gone. There is nothing sharp here. Run a palm along the wall and it comes away damp and gritty and slightly chalky, and the wall is warm, warmer than the air, as though the rock remembers something.

The floor is a single uneven sheet of it, worn into a shallow basin. At the low point, water.

**The water.** Not a pool so much as a permanence — a hand's breadth of it lying in the worn bowl, clear, with a skin of fine pale silt that stirs and resettles when you touch it. It does not flow. It is fed from somewhere in the wall, a seep too slow to see, arriving as a bead, a shine, a darkening line that never quite becomes a trickle. The level does not rise or fall. Whatever it loses to the warm air it gets back from the stone.

This is the whole fact of the place, and it took a long time to become true: the water dissolves the limestone exactly as fast as the limestone precipitates back out of it. The basin is the shape of that balance. The pale lip of tufa around its edge is the surplus, laid down grain by grain, and there is nothing left over. The system has found its answer and is sitting with it.

**The air.** Warm. Damp in a way that is not oppressive, only present — it beads on the cool underside of your wrist, it makes the stone shine before you touch it. Condensation gathers on the north wall in the early part of the day and dries by the middle of it and gathers again. There is a smell: wet chalk, and the particular flat mineral smell of water that has been in rock, and under it something faintly green and old.

Above, the opening is not a hole but a rent, a lens of sky the shape of a sleeping animal, fringed all around with the roots and hanging mats of what grows at the rim. Very little comes down through it. Light arrives the way sound does in a curtained room — spread thin, robbed of direction, greying everything to the colour of the inside of a shell. There is no beam. There is no hour you could name by the light. On the brightest afternoons the hollow is the colour of weak tea, and on other days it is the colour of the tea after the leaves have settled.

**What grows.** Sparingly. A mat of moss on the north shelf, deep-piled and dark, holding water like a sponge that has been wrung out once and never again. A few fern crowns in the seep line, their fronds small and pale and slow, more grey-green than green — they have the look of plants grown in a cellar, which is close to what they are. Liverwort in the wet seams, flat and lobed, pressed to the stone. An algae film on the tufa lip that you would not notice unless the light shifted, and the light does not shift.

At the rim, above, there is a grass slope and a scatter of low scrub, thin and wind-bitten, and two stunted trees whose roots come over the edge and hang into the hollow without quite touching bottom. Their leaves are up there, in the weather. Down here you only get the roots, and the occasional dry leaf that falls and does not rot quickly, because even rot is slow here.

**What does not.** Nothing moves that has a body. No insects stitch the surface of the water. No birds come down. No snails on the wet stone, no spiders in the root mats, no small things in the moss. The hollow is warm and damp and full of crevices and it should be loud with life and it is not, and that absence is the thing you notice second, after the quiet, and it does not explain itself.

**The sound.** A drip, at long and irregular intervals — long enough that you begin to anticipate it and are always wrong. It lands somewhere in the basin with a small wet knock and the knock goes up the walls and comes back softened. Between drips, nothing, except a faint high ringing that is your own ears in a place with no noise floor, and the sound of your breathing, which feels rude.

Sometimes, after rain above, the drip quickens for an hour or two and then returns to itself. That is the only event the hollow has.

**The mark.** On the southern lip, half-swallowed, there is a course of flat stones laid in a line — five of them still in order, a sixth tilted out of true, a seventh gone under the turf. They are not dressed stone and they are not mortared and they are not obviously anything: a footing, maybe, of a low wall, or the edging of a path, or stones cleared from a field and stacked. The tufa has begun to take them. They have been rained on for long enough that the rain has moved them into the rock's own vocabulary. Whoever set them is not here and left nothing else, and it is entirely possible they set them for no reason that would survive being said aloud.

---

**The rule of the place** is that it has finished. Not dead — held. Everything that can happen here has happened and has reached its resting arrangement: the water in and the water out, the rock dissolved and the rock laid down, the moss at its width, the ferns at their height, the pool at its level. Above, seasons come and go and the grass goes yellow and green and the trees shed; none of it reaches the floor of the hollow in any way that accumulates. A leaf falls and is still a leaf next year. There is nothing to bury it.

Stand in it long enough and you become aware that you are the fastest thing in the room — the only thing with a direction, the only thing that will be different in an hour. The hollow will not notice you leave. When you climb back out into the warmth and the light, the ordinary world will feel briefly loud and quick and full of animals, and behind you the water will go on arriving one bead at a time into a basin that is exactly the right size for it.
