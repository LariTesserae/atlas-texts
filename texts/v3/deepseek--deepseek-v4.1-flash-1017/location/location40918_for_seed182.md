# The Vey

The heat arrives first. Not the dry, aggressive heat of open country—this is a breathing warmth, close and wet, air heavy as a held breath. The Vey is a pocket in the earth, a rough geode of space maybe three hundred paces across, enclosed by walls of dark, damp stone that sweat slow rivulets into the basin below. In the beginning, these walls would have been sharp—fresh-cut stone from some ancient collapse or volcanic splitting—but that era is young yet in geological time. The edges have merely begun to soften.

The whole space sits a stone's throw below the surface. You don't climb down to it; you seep in sideways, through a passage that narrows until you're on hands and knees in warm mud, then widens without warning into the Vey proper. The ceiling of this underground world is low, maybe forty feet at its highest point, and it isn't stone at all in places but a thick tangle of root and vine pressing down through cracks, a living mat that drips constantly. Condensation gathers on every surface and falls in irregular patters—not rain, not really, but something more like the room's pulse made visible. The ground underfoot is packed earth, dark and almost black, slick with moisture, the kind of mud that holds your footprint for hours.

The light is dim and sourceless, faintly luminescent. Something in the walls—a lichen, a mineral impurity, a strange moss that grows in thin ochre streaks—emits a low, steady glow. It's enough to see by, barely, and it paints everything in shades of old bronze and wet shadow. Your eyes adjust after a while, and you understand that the Vey has its own perpetual dusk, a twilight that never changes, never brightens, never fully darkens.

## The City

The Vey is small, but it is not empty. Far from it. The floor of this place is a city in miniature, or maybe a city in cross-section—a place built layer upon layer over what must be many generations, each one settling onto the bones of the last. Rough wooden platforms buttress the walls at irregular heights, generating a three-dimensional street map of catwalks and ladders and swaying bridges of knotted vine-rope. Structures lean out from the stone: workshops with cluttered roofs, drying racks hung with bundles, glass-fronted cabinets of specimens (Terrans, Veylins), stacked sleeping platforms, chambers for processing. Between them all, the space is dense with things—copper pipes for the hot springs, iron vats bubbling with something sharp-smelling, coils of wire glowing amber, hand-cranked conveyor belts moving crates of root-fiber, mechanical hoists lifting water from the low pools. Everywhere the built world overlaps with the living one: roots pressed into service as structural supports, ropes of mycelium cultivated for strength, the walls themselves carved back to make room and then built out again with timber and packed earth.

This is industrial in a way that feels old, too. Not any single innovation breaking free from the last, but rather a thousand small improvements layered up over generations until the Vey is one breathing apparatus, each part integrated with the others. The pipes that carry hot water from deep thermal sources also run alongside the walls and heat the sleeping quarters above; the exhaust from the fungus-cultivation vats is captured to warm the nursery chambers; the low river itself—there is a river, a slow, shallow flow of hot water across the lower reaches of the basin floor—serves as both transport and power for a series of waterwheels and sluices that operate in the semi-darkness with strange, patient grace. The heat is everywhere: in the air, in the water, in the walls themselves. It presses on you.

The scale is *small*—you could walk across it in a few minutes. But the place is not designed for walking straight. Paths curve around structures, duck under overhangs, climb and descend. The overall shape is more like a nautilus than a grid, and moving through the Vey means committing to a pace, learning to wait for the catwalk to be free, for the sluice-gate to open, for the hand-powered lift to descend. People who live here grow an instinct for the rhythm of the place, for the breathing of its machinery, for the moments when a given path will be open.

The architecture itself seems to be in constant, subtle transition—which makes sense, because the whole Vey is young. The rock is still warm from its formation, the fractures fresh, the stone *wet* in a way that speaks of recent water and recent heat. Someone built this place fast and then kept building, never fully finishing. Edges are raw, joints improvised, materials reused. There are no monuments, no grand civic architecture, no plans for a hundred years from now. People grew this place like a scab over a wound; the wound is the rock itself, and the building is ongoing.

## The Living Dark

The vegetation is lush to the point of being intrusive. This is what happens when a hot, wet, enclosed environment is given light and left alone—things *grow*. Thick mats of pale fern, engineered fungus (Vey-cultivated), and bioluminescent lichen drape every surface. The walls are a vertical garden of roots and vines reaching toward that dim ochre glow, and the water itself carries floating mats of vegetation, root-fiber cultivated for raw material. You are never not touching a plant here, never not brushing something living.

There are animals, but few enough to make each appearance notable. Small flying things—something like a moth or bat, insectile and soft—move through the middle air. Pale crustaceans inhabit the shallow river, harvested and used in food and medicine. Deep in the fungal alcoves, blind fish swim in tanks (some cultivated intentionally, some wild and siphoned in). The Vey is quiet, mostly. This is not a loud place. The dominant sounds are: water dripping in irregular patterns, the creak of wood slowly shifting in the dampness, the occasional hiss of a sluice-gate opening or closing, the low *thrum* of the distillation vats operating in their patient cycles. When people speak, it's quiet speech, unhurried. Sound dies here, absorbed by the soft materials, by the wetness, by the packed earth walls.

## The Rules

The Vey works because it has a rhythm. Everything is connected. The hot springs warm the water; the water powers the wheels and sluices; the sluices regulate the flow to the fungus beds; the fungus beds feed the settlement; the exhaust heat from the fungus beds warms the air for the people; the people maintain the system; the system, left alone, would silt up and die. So the rhythm is maintained. The whole place has a *cycle*—a four-part cycle (heat, water, growth, harvest), each phase lasting roughly a day, marked by shifts in the light, the pace of change, who is awake and who is resting.

Because the space is so packed—density the highest among these parameters—everyone lives inside each other's awareness. There is no privacy, not really, and this is not seen as a problem. The physical layout enforces co-presence: if someone opens a sluice-gate on the upper walkway, the vibration moves down through the supports and anyone touching a related line feels it. The Vey operates like a single organism, and its people are cells within it.

## The Weird

Something is wrong here. Not obviously, not immediately, but look longer. The light doesn't quite behave correctly—the glow from the lichen seems to *respond* to presence, brightening or dimming in ways that don't correlate with the lichen's own needs or the presence of movement. The water, when you watch it, has a kind of surface tension that doesn't quite track with its temperature; it beads up more than physics would allow. The root structures—well.

There are markings. Not painted, not carved, but grown *into* the fungus and vine—patterns that repeat across the settlement with the patience of something experimental, something trying to see what the pattern does. The Vey is a young place. It has not yet decided what it will be. Maybe the markings belong to that process: what the space needs to become, testing itself against the people, waiting for them to understand. The Vey is not just dense and hot and packed and built; it is *thinking*, and it is slow, and it is patient, and it will not be rushed.

In between the industrial rhythms and the constant work, in those moments of waiting while the sluice-gate cycles or the distillation vat rumblings ease, you can feel it. The rock is warm. The water is running. Something is watching from the walls.
