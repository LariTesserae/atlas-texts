## Sallow Bottom

Sallow Bottom is a small industrial town built in the bed of a shallow lake that drained only recently enough that the land still looks surprised. The basin is low and cool, ringed by a soft rim of alder and willow, and the whole place can be crossed on foot in fifteen minutes. The outlet stream has cut a young ravine at one end — raw clay banks, sharp undercuts, fresh islands of gravel, none of it rounded or settled. Water is not everywhere, but it is never far: braided channels appear and vanish through the reed beds, puddles stand in old foundation pits, condensation beads on every cold rail and window, and rain comes in brief, deliberate passes rather than as a permanent drizzle. The air smells of wet brick, moss, coal smoke, and the green sourness of standing water.

The town is urban in organization and sparse in fabric. There are streets, gutters, retaining walls, loading docks, electric poles, and a grid of alleys — but half the blocks are wet meadow between buildings. The paper mill, the felt works, the pump house, and the small foundry sit apart from one another with long gaps of nettle and willow scrub between them. Construction is layered and practical: tarred timber piles, blackened brick, green-stained copper roofs, corrugated iron, concrete aprons, greased rails. No one builds cellars. Houses and workshops stand on screw piles that can be raised or lowered a few inches at a time, and some of the smaller sheds sit on rails so they can be winched aside when the creek chooses a new thread. The infrastructure is denser than the buildings: pipes run along the boardwalks, cisterns hang under eaves, and low-voltage lines loop from pole to pole with a faint, audible hum.

Vegetation is lush and opportunistic. Moss furrows the warm pipes, ferns crowd the shaded sides of the foundry, nettles and willowherb take the vacant lots, and reed beds filter the shallow water at the basin’s edge. The cool temperature keeps everything green rather than rank: breath fogs in the morning, metal is cold to the touch, and the light is ordinary — sometimes bright and thin, sometimes clouded, but never gloomy or strange. Fauna is abundant and unimpressed by industry. Frogs colonize the cooling ponds, herons stalk the braided channels, kingfishers work the young ravine, bats pour out from under the mill roof at dusk, and foxes trot through the wet meadows between shifts. Insects cloud around the electric lamps; mice and shrews live in the woodpiles.

It is a loud place for its size. The paper mill’s flywheels and stampers set a low mechanical pulse; steam vents hiss; the pump house clanks on a slow cycle; water gates thud; rain rattles on corrugated roofs; frogs and birds fill the gaps between machine sounds. The noise is layered rather than chaotic — industry, water, and animals all speaking at once in a place small enough that you can hear the edges of each sound.

The rules of the world here are drainage rules. The creek has right-of-way over every street. The Drainage Board is the real government; its survey marks decide where a building may stand and when it must be jacked up or moved. Roofs drain to cisterns, foundations are adjustable, and no wall is expected to stay dry. The town’s oddness is not magic so much as a habit of slow migration: the lakebed is still settling, the young stream still braiding, and old foundations quietly become ponds. Streets are renumbered when a channel shifts; sheds are winched onto new piles; the whole settlement moves by inches across a generation, patient as the water. Sallow Bottom feels less like a town that conquered its ground than like a small machine for living with a young river — damp, green, loud, and always, slowly, rearranging itself.
