The place is called the Verdant Loom, though no one who lives there calls it anything. The name is for cartographers, and cartographers do not last.

It begins where the ground forgets to be ground. The terrain is a continuous, living mat—roots the size of cathedral columns plunging into a warm, shallow murk, threading through soil so new and damp it crumbles like wet cake. There is water everywhere and nowhere: not a river, not a lake, but a pervasive sweat. The air is thick with it, warm as breath, and it beads on every surface. Pockets of standing water collect in the crooks of limbs and in the cup-shaped hollows of leaves big enough to bathe in, and the dome of sky above is a diffuse, luminous white, like the inside of a cloud lit from within, so that the light comes from all directions at once—mottled, heavy, saturated with green.

The city is entirely plant. Not built *by* plants nor built of dead plant matter, but *grown*—a hot, damp, deafening city of the living tissue of a world where plants never learned to be small. It is a metropolis of vascular architecture: platforms of dense, interwoven vines that groan underfoot, pumping blood-warm sap. Bridges of thorn and braided root span gaps that exhale steam. Towers rise not in straight lines but in the twisted, seeking spirals of things reaching for light, their surfaces bark-armored or slick with membrane, their interiors hollow and echoing and—yes—habitable, if you can call it that. Doorways are not built but grown, sealed behind you by a contraction of fibers. Stairs are ladders of rigid bract that flex in rhythm with something's slow and distant pulse.

The scale is regional but the density is impossible. It is a town that has metastasized into a valley, everything touching everything else. There is no sky, only a canopy—layered light-filters of leaf and lens, a ceiling of translucent tissue that glows with the diffuse white of a world lit from nowhere. The giant leaves shift, and the light they cast sweeps like slow searchlights, mottling the undercity with shifting shade. The heat doesn't come from above but from within, a metabolic warmth generated by the sheer mass of things growing, digesting, and dying all at once. The air is a pressure on the skin.

The sound is constant, a municipal roar more suited to a factory floor—the Loom doesn't hum, it *labors*. There is the pumping: a deep, wet, systolic throb that comes up through every surface, the heartbeat of a water-moving system with no central heart, just a billion local pulsing bodies. There is the wind: not a breeze, but a churning turbulence that funnels through the vascular architecture, through the hollow towers and the gaps between the giant leaves, creating a chorus of whistles and moans from a thousand throats, a babel of root-flutes and leaf-drums. And always the creaking, popping, and grinding: the sound of growth under pressure, of tensions being redistributed, of living architecture constantly testing itself and reforming.

There are no animals. Nothing walks, flies, or crawls except the city itself. But still it moves. Tendrils grope and lash out, seeking, gripping. Vines wind themselves around any available surface and constrict, as if testing it. A patch of ground you watched a moment ago is now covered in a new layer of moss, ferns, and something wound tight. The entire environment is a single dynamic system, hyperactive, turbulent, reorganizing itself continuously. The ground you walk on is actively growing, the path you came by is already overgrown, and the ceiling above shifts. It is a world in creation and destruction simultaneously, an anthill's energy without the ants. The sap in one conduit would boil over and scald the area, while in another a constriction would burst a vessel and gush a warm, clear, slightly sweet liquid that hardens quickly into a clear resin.

The rules of the world are few but strict. Paths do not stay paths. Sound is not a distraction—it is a pressure, an instruction. The most critical rule: if you find yourself in a comfortable clearing, a place where the growth is low and you can see some kind of sky, do not stay. The Clears, as cartographers mark them, are not places of safety. They are the city's swallowing reflex, pockets it opens up in the dense mass, and the longer you linger, the more likely that the cathedral of vine and bract behind you will close over the entrance, sealing you into a slowly tightening stomach.

The scale is open, regional—the city stretches for a walk of many days between one edge and another, a valley-sized expanse of living tissue. It is urban, yes, but without a single constructed artifact. Architecture is simply how the living mass organizes itself—layered, strategic, responsive. The city is, in some sense, trying to become alive, but its strategy is confusing. There is no center. The blooming things and the strangling things bloom and strangle everywhere. There is no plan. It is the pure expression of a world where the only law is growth, where everything is food, where the floor beneath your foot is the same as the wall growing around your arm, and the sound is not that of a place inhabited, but of a place still trying to decide what it will become.
