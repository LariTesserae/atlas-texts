# The Sump

The plain is dry in the way that only a place with a water table just beneath it can be dry — cracked, pale, tufted with grass that has given up on height and gone in for toughness instead. It runs flat for a long way. You would not stop here. There is nothing to stop for: a line of low scrub, a wind that carries grit, a horizon that does not resolve into anything. The ground underfoot is firm and rings faintly when you stamp.

Then it dips. Not a hill, not a valley — a fold, a soft crumple in the flatness that you could walk past at twenty meters and never notice. Follow it, because the scrub thickens along it, and the thickening is the tell. The vegetation here is not lush; it is *concentrated*. Everything that has managed to grow in this country has crowded into this one seam, and it is packed shoulder to shoulder — thorn, creeper, a stubborn fig with its roots in a crack — all of it leaning inward, downhill, toward the smell.

Because there is a smell. Warm stone, wet chalk, iron, and something greenly bacterial underneath. It is not a bad smell. It is the smell of a place with water in it.

The fold becomes a lip, and the lip is sharp. This is the thing that unsettles you first: the edges are *fresh*. The rock at the rim is clean-faced and angular, fractured in planes that still hold their edges, no soil on the ledges, no lichen softening the breaks. Somebody's been quarrying here, you think, and then you look down and see that nobody has. The ground simply opened. Not long ago, in geological terms. Possibly not long ago at all — there is a fig tree down there growing out of a fissure that is far too young to have a tree that size in it, which means the tree was here first and the ground opened around it, and it has spent every year since leaning toward the light it can now suddenly see.

Down is a bowl. Call it forty meters across. The local name for it is the Sump, and it earns it.

The walls are the color of old bone where they are dry and the color of tea where the water runs down them, and the water runs down them constantly — not in streams, in *sweat*. The whole bowl condenses. It is warm down there, a good five degrees warmer than the plain, and the warmth comes up out of the floor, where a spring has broken through the collapse rubble and is pooling. Warm mineral water, faintly sulfurous, faintly salty, the temperature of a bath you'd get into reluctantly. It has filled the bottom third of the bowl to a depth of perhaps two meters and it is still, except where it isn't.

Stand at the rim and the noise is almost nothing. That's the trick of the place. It is packed — dense, crowded, thrumming with presences — and it is quiet. A drip. A tick. Insects, but a low, contented insect sound, not a frenzy. The water lapping at a concrete edge with a soft slap that repeats on a period of maybe four seconds. And under all of it, so low you feel it in your teeth before you hear it, a hum.

The hum is the works.

Somebody, a long time ago and not so long ago, built down here. The remains are scattered and partial and entirely functional in the way that neglected machinery sometimes stubbornly is: a concrete apron cut into the bowl's floor, now half-drowned and cracked; a run of pipe as thick as your thigh climbing the north wall in three doglegs; a shed at the top of the pipe, corrugated, roof half gone, containing a chair, a hurricane lamp with soot in it, a coil of wire, a hand-cranked grease gun, and a logbook that has been rained on so many times it has become a single object with the texture of a wasp nest. Men came here to pump water or brine or something out of the ground, and they built the small hard infrastructure of that intention — a settling tank, a sluice gate, a rack of valves — and the ground opened up beneath their works and swallowed the bottom of it, and they did not come back for all of it.

Except the pump still turns.

Not ghost-driven. Water-driven. The spring comes up under pressure and somebody, at some point, was clever enough to hang a small turbine in the flow and let the water pay for its own extraction. The turbine is the size of a bucket and it sits in a stone housing at the back of the pool and it turns, always, at a rate set by the spring, and it drives a shaft, and the shaft drives a pump, and the pump lifts a thin thread of water up the north wall and over the rim, where it runs away down the fold and is drunk by the scrub. It has been doing this, unattended, for a length of time nobody has established. It ticks as it turns. Every fourth second, the lapping. Every fourth second, the tick. The pool rises a finger's width and falls back. The whole bowl breathes on a four-second cycle, and everything in it has learned the rhythm.

The density is what gets you. This is the only water for kilometers, and everything knows. The pool is *crowded*. Frogs in the margins, dozens, sitting in the wet rubble in rows like something counted them. Snails on every submerged surface, in sheets. A film of algae on the concrete that is being grazed down by small fish that should not be able to get here and are here anyway, silver and quick, and something longer and slower under the overhang that is grazing the grazers. Birds come down at dusk in a loose spiral — swifts, a pair of kites, a heron that has claimed the settling tank and stands in it with the patience of a statue. Bats out of the fissures at last light. The air at the rim is thick with them at the turnover, and the sound is still almost nothing, just the wingbeats and the lapping and the tick. A predator's world, running at a small scale, in a confined space, at a pace you can watch.

And the water preserves.

That is the strangeness, and it is not dramatic — it is just wrong in a way you notice gradually. Nothing rots in the pool. The mineral load, the warmth, the low oxygen, whatever the chemistry is: things that fall in do not go away. There are birds down there on the bottom, in the shallows, perfect and dark and months old, feathers intact, eyes intact, settled into the silt in a drift. There are snails in that drift whose shells are still glossy. It is not a graveyard, exactly; it's a *collection*. The spring keeps what it takes. The bowl is packed not only with the living but with everything that has ever come down here to drink, layered in together, and the algae grows over them and the fish swim through them and the four-second tide moves them a little, gently, back and forth.

Water is still finding its way out. Every wet season the runoff cuts a new channel in the raw rock at the rim, sharp and clean and unresolvable, and every dry season the rock spalls and the lip retreats another hand's width toward the plain. The bowl is growing. Slowly, in the way that a thing with no soil to hold it grows. In a hundred years it will be wider and shallower and the pump will be under water or above it and the chair will be gone. In a thousand it will be a dip in a flat plain with a fig tree in it, and someone will walk past at twenty meters and not notice.

Right now it is a warm, wet, crowded, quiet little room in the middle of a dry country, breathing every four seconds, keeping everything it is given.
