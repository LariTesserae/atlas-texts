# The Hum

There is a hollow in the world, and it has been hollow for so long that the land has forgotten it was ever anything else. The rim of it is low, gentle, worn down by ages of rain into soft lips of earth. You would not see it from a distance. You would walk across the plain and the ground would simply begin to descend, and the air would change.

## The Shape of It

The hollow is perhaps a cathedral's length across. No more. A single feature, a bowl in the earth, and at its center a seep—not a spring, not a pool, just the ground remembering water. The water comes up slow through the soil, not in a rush, and it does not go anywhere. It sits in the lowest fold of the land and it breathes. Condensation beads on everything. The air is cool and heavy and it tastes of wet stone and green things. Rain comes, sometimes, in thin sheets that do not fall so much as arrive, and the hollow holds the wetness in its cupped hands for a long time after the sky has forgotten.

The water is not deep. It is the kind of water you could lie down in and still see the sky. It is dark with tannin, or with something older than tannin, and its surface is never quite still, because the land itself is always, very slightly, settling.

## The Green

Everything here is plant.

Not a forest—forest implies trees, and trees imply death, and things do not die here in any way that leaves a gap. The hollow is filled with a single continuous body of vegetation, a green mass that has grown into itself so many times over that it has become its own architecture. Vines thick as a man's waist. Vines thin as hair, and the hair-vines are the ones that matter, the ones that make the sound. Leaves the size of doors, leathery, dark-veined, weeping condensation in slow drips. Moss as deep as a mattress. Ferns that unroll themselves and then do not wither, just grow heavier and lower and become part of the floor.

The plants do not compete. They have finished competing. They have arrived, over unimaginable time, at an arrangement so complete that nothing needs to change. A leaf unfurls, lives, thickens, and then simply becomes a lower layer of the same leaf—there is no rot, no fall, no litter. The green recycles itself into itself. You can see this in the way the substrate gives underfoot: not soil, not compost, but a plant-flesh so deeply aged it has become soft and structural at once, like bread that has been baked for a thousand years.

## The Water in the Green

The seep is not separate from the plants. The plants have grown into it and through it. Roots hang in the water like something drowned and patient. The water is where the moisture lives between rains—not a river, not a pool, but a persistent wetness that keeps everything damp and glistening and cool. Small pockets of standing water appear in folds of the green flesh, each one a tiny dark mirror that holds the sky for a day and then is absorbed. The air is saturated. You feel it on your skin before you understand it. Your clothes darken. Your hair curls.

## The Sound

It is loud.

The hum is the first thing you hear and the last thing you stop hearing. It comes from the thin vines—the hair-vines—which are hollow and taut and strung between the larger growths like the strings of an instrument nobody plays. But something does play them. The moisture. The slow seep of water through the plant mass, the drip and trickle and the constant almost-imperceptible settling of the hollow, all of it vibrates the vines, and the vines sing.

It is a chord that does not resolve. Not a drone, exactly—it has too much movement for that—but a steady, layered, low sound, like a hive the size of a room, like a struck wire that never stops ringing. The seep gurgles underneath it. Water falls, somewhere, at irregular intervals, into a pool with a hollow plink. The plants themselves, the big leaves, creak when they shift, which is rare, but the small ones—the thin green fingers, the tendrils—they whisper against each other constantly, a sound like breath, like a crowd murmuring in another room.

You raise your voice and the hollow takes it and folds it into the hum and you cannot hear yourself.

## The Light

Normal light, but filtered. The canopy of the hollow is not leaves over sky—it is leaves over leaves over leaves over leaves, and the light that comes through is green and diffuse and without direction. There are no shadows here that point. Everything is lit from everywhere and nowhere. The water on the leaves catches this light and holds it, so the whole hollow glitters faintly, wetly, like the inside of something alive.

At the edges of the hollow, where the plant mass thins against the rim, the light is brighter and the plants are smaller and the hum is quieter. But only just. The rim is not an exit. It is a gradient.

## The Animals

They are there. They are not the point.

Fauna lives in the interstices of the plant mass—small things, quick things, things that have learned to move through the green without disturbing it, because disturbance is not tolerated here. Beetles with flat shells that slide between leaves. Pale things like eels that live in the seep and move through the roots. Insects that hang in the wet air and do not bite and do not swarm but simply exist, in numbers, in the spaces between the vines. Their sounds are part of the hum. You will hear a chirp and not be able to locate it and then stop caring.

There are larger things, maybe. It is hard to tell. The hollow is small and dense and the eye cannot penetrate the green. Something moves, deep in the plant mass, and then does not move again. You do not see it. You are not meant to.

## The Old Industry

And buried in the green, there is metal.

Not structures—nothing built stands here, the plants have swallowed everything that was ever placed—but metal nonetheless. Pipes, the size of a thigh, corroded to the color of moss, running through the plant-flesh like veins that are not veins. The green has grown around them, through them, has incorporated them. Vats, half-sunk in the substrate, their surfaces furred with algae and lichen, their shapes barely legible beneath the growth. Machinery that has not turned in ages, but which the plants have learned to grow through without breaking—the tendrils follow the lines of the old pipes, the roots trace the seams of the old vessels, so that the plant mass and the machinery have become one continuous thing, neither functional nor dead.

The hum may come from here. It may not. The pipes carry water now, or they carry sound, or they carry nothing. The seep rises through them and the plants drink from them and the vines strung between them sing. It is not clear where the machinery ends and the ecology begins. It has been too long. There is no gradient. There is only the hollow, and the hum, and the green.

## The Rules

The hollow does not change. It has finished changing. What you see is what it has settled into, over a time so long it has no measure in human terms. The plants do not grow, exactly—they thicken, they settle, they replace themselves into the same shapes. The water does not rise or fall. The hum does not waver. The animals live their small lives and their bodies join the plant-flesh and the plant-flesh holds them and nothing is lost.

You could stay here for a very long time and see nothing happen. You could map every vine and find it unchanged a season later. The hollow is not frozen—it is complete. It is a thing that has arrived at what it is and has no reason to become anything else.

The rain comes. The seep breathes. The vines sing their one chord. The green grows softly into itself, forever, and the metal sleeps beneath it, and the animals move in the spaces, and the light is green and wet and without shadows.

There is no exit because there is nothing to exit. There is only the hollow, and the hum, and the slow patient wetness of a place that has been here longer than here has been a word.
