Here is a description of that place.

---

You are suspended in a high, cold sea. It is not the crushing black of the abyss, but a high-latitude ocean, grey-green and deep, where the sun filters down through the water as a diffuse, silvery light. The cold is a constant, gentle pressure on the skin, a chill that seeps into the bones but does not bite. There is no surface to see, no floor to touch; you are held in a vast, three-dimensional volume, and the world extends in every direction.

This is the Aethelgard, the Forest of the Deep. The primary feature of this world is a single, colossal plant. It is a spire of living matter that begins far below in the lightless depths and climbs for what seems like an eternity, a pillar of the world. It is not a tree, nor a coral, but something between. Its trunk is a city.

The Aethelgard’s trunk is a lattice of dense, interlaced growth, a honeycomb of vast, hollowed-out chambers and winding corridors. The "wood" of the plant is a porous, grey-green material—light, strong, and alive. It is not built; it is grown. The city is a settlement that has been cultivated for generations, its inhabitants shaping the plant's growth with simple tools of bone and stone, guiding and pruning new chambers and passages as the spire climbs ever so slowly toward the unseen sun. The settlement is organized and layered, a vertical hamlet where every home is a smooth-walled cyst in the living fabric of the plant. Bioluminescent fungi, cultivated in shallow bowls, provide a soft, steady glow in the deeper interiors, while the outer chambers are open to the filtering light of the sea.

The place is not crowded. There is a profound sense of space, both within the plant-city's chambers and in the water around it. The inhabitants—a quiet, amphibious people—move with a slow, deliberate grace, their bodies adapted to the cold and the buoyancy. They are sparse, scattered across the many levels of the spire, and a chamber may sit empty for long stretches, given over to the slow, patient work of the plant itself.

The air, for those who can breathe it, is thick with sound. It is a world of constant, low-frequency vibration. The primary sound is the plant itself: the deep, resonant groan of its trunk flexing in the slow currents, the thrumming pulse of sap moving through its miles-long veins, the creak and pop of new growth. This is the world's heartbeat, a sound so pervasive it is almost silence. Layered over it is the abundant fauna: the chittering of small, bioluminescent crustaceans that swarm in the thousands, the percussive clicks and whistles of hunting fish, the deep, booming call of something immense and unseen that patrols the outer dark. The inhabitants have learned to read this symphony, to hear the warning in the plant's groan or the promise of food in the chitter of a specific shrimp.

The rules of this world are the rules of the Aethelgard. The plant is the substrate, the architecture, and the law. Life is a process of negotiation with its slow, patient growth. The people are not masters but partners, living in its shelter and tending its health. The water is the medium of all things—of movement, of sound, of communication. There is no fire here, no metal. Heat is precious, generated by the body and conserved in the still, dense water. The light is a gift from above, a constant, gentle presence that dictates the rhythm of life more than any sun's passage. The world is in balance, a settled and ancient form. Nothing is new here, only grown; nothing is destroyed, only reabsorbed. The erosion that has shaped the spire is the slow, patient work of water and time, a process that has been in its own state for countless ages, and the city is a weathered part of it.

The place feels primordial and complete. It is a world unto itself, a vertical island of life in the deep, where everything is connected by the vast, living body of the Aethelgard, and the slow, patient pulse of its life is the only clock that matters.
