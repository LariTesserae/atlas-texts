# The Rain-Cup

You come on it from below, which is the only honest way, because from above the cloud closes and there is no seeing anything at all.

The ridge is young — that is the first fact of the place, and everything else follows from it. Not young as a forest is young, but young as a wound is young. The stone has no softness anywhere. It breaks in planes, in blades, in glassy conchoidal shells, and the edges it takes are edges no weather has taught to be gentler. There is no soil on it worth the name. There is no rounding. You climb a staircase that was never cut, laid naked by uplift and fracture, and every handhold is a fresh surprise that could cut you open.

And at the crest, held in a saddle of that raw material, is a cup. Maybe forty strides across. A basin of unweathered rock that some very recent violence — a collapse, an upthrow, something the mountain did to itself — punched into the ridge, and which has since done nothing but fill.

It is full to the lip. Water stands in it the way water stands in a cup set out in a storm: not placid, not still, but *held*, perpetually at the moment of overflowing, fed faster than it can leave. The overflow is a notch on the western side, and through that notch the whole basin empties in a white rope of falling water down a gully it has barely begun to cut. That gully is the only erosion you will find here, and it is a foetus — a raw groove in rock that still rings underfoot, still shedding fresh chips.

## The Sound

You hear it an hour before you arrive.

Not the roar of a waterfall, though there is one. The Rain-Cup does not have a roar. It has a *racket* — a layered, chaotic, overwhelming noise with no bottom to it. Rain on open water, and rain on water already beaten into foam, and wind coming over the saddle in shears. The overflow thundering in its young channel. Debris — chips of stone, spurts of gravel loosened by the tremor of water — clattering down the gully walls. And beneath all of it a continuous grinding, a low ongoing *work*, as though something under the floor were turning in its sleep.

Stand on the rim and you cannot hear yourself speak. You cannot hear a companion shout. You feel their voice in your chest more than your ears. The noise has a rhythm that is almost a pattern — the gully sounds a chord as different volumes of water pass, the stone underfoot ticks at intervals like a slow enormous clock being wound — and after an hour of it you begin to suspect, wrongly or not, that it is *organized*.

## The Water

It is cold. Rain-cold, melt-cold, snowmelt from higher still, and there is a bite to it that gets into the wrists in a minute. It is also almost impossibly clear — not blue, not green, just an absence of colour with the basin's floor showing through, sharp as through a lens.

The floor shows through because there is nothing in the water to hide it. No weed. No cloud of silt. A few threads of something drifting. The basin is as empty as a room before the furniture comes. You can see every stone of the bottom, every fracture in it, and among them the new-made things: skins of pale mineral laid down in thin sheets, sinter and vein-frost, crystal crusts spreading over the rock in the places where the water is shallow and slow. This is not a lake wearing down its bed. It is a lake *adding to* it. The mountain is building itself in the bottom of its own cup, a film at a time, and it is doing it while you watch.

That is the rule of the place. Nothing here is being taken away. The rock is arriving.

## Light and Air

The overcast is bright — the flat, merciless, shadowless light of high storm, under which every colour is true and none are flattered. The water is silver by default and turns briefly to lead, to pewter, to a cold enamel white where the wind tears it. The raw rock reads as grey and rust and a green so dark it is nearly black. There are no shadows to soften the breaks in it, so the whole basin reads as an argument in stone. When the cloud thins — rarely — the water goes hard and bright and the whole pocket of the saddle feels briefly, deceptively, warm.

The air moves constantly and coldly; it smells of wet mineral, of struck flint, of the inside of a well.

## What Lives

Very little, and it lives at the margins.

At the overflow lip, where spray keeps things wet but not drowned, there are cushions of moss — hard, dark, wind-combed — and crusts of lichen on the stones that stick out of the water, orange and grey and the pale green of new copper. One low shrub has rooted in a crack on the leeward side and grown sideways because upward is not survivable. It is the largest plant for a long way in any direction.

In the water: almost nothing. You will look for a long time before you find the small pale things that live in the shallows under the mineral skins — eyeless, slow, no longer than a fingernail, uninterested in you. Once, twice a day, a bird comes over the saddle, drops into the lee of the cairn to shake itself, and is gone. That is the whole census.

## What People Left

Somebody has been here. That is unmistakable once you look at the leeward rim: three posts of hand-cut wood, driven into cracks in the rock, roped and braced, half of the rope gone and the rest of it swollen and grey. A cairn — maybe a hundred stones, balanced with real care, in a place where balancing is possible. Iron staples hammered into a sheer face, rusting into the raw stone in bright orange runs. A board with marks cut into it — notches, a cross, a hand's-width measure — in a scale you don't recognize and a hand you can't date. These are not ruins. They are *marks*: the scattered, faint, manual work of people with simple tools, who came, measured something, and left the measuring behind. Whoever they were, they had no metal worth speaking of, or none they were willing to spend, and they did not stay.

## The Place Becoming Itself

At the storm's peak, the Rain-Cup does something you will not believe and cannot quite disprove. The tremor of the water and the ticking of the stone come into phase, and the floor of the basin *lifts* — not visibly, not by any span you could put a stick to, but the mineral skins thicken across an afternoon, the crusts close over, new grey spreads where bare rock was that morning, and the whole cup brims a little fuller and a little higher than the day before. The mountain is growing *into* its own pocket, filling it from below, patient and quick at the same time. In fifty years, in a hundred, the cup will be shallower and the notch will have worked a little deeper and the balance will tip, and there will be a spill, and the raw basin will be gone.

For now it is exactly this: a small cold room of water perched high on a mountain that has not yet decided what it is, hammered by a storm that will not let up, loud enough to erase thought, empty as a held breath — and every hour, a little more stone than the hour before. A place still in the middle of being made, with the making audible.
