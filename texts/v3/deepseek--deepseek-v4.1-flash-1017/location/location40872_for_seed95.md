# The Mizzle Downs

## Terrain

The land rises and rises, and then keeps rising past the point where rising should end, until it becomes something else entirely — a vast, wrinkled plateau that seems less like a mountaintop than the scalp of an ancient giant. This is a place worn down to its bones. Every ridge is rounded, every hollow smoothed, the whole expanse polished by a hundred million years of rain that has come and gone, come and gone, until the stone itself seems tired. There are no cliffs here, no dramatic edges. The elevation is high but the relief is gentle, a slow sea of ground that swells and falls in long, patient waves measured in kilometers.

The rock is old beyond reckoning — grey and mauve and sometimes a strange bruised green where minerals have leached — and it lies close to the surface in great flat pavements, like the vertebrae of something enormous. Between these stone backs, soil has gathered in shallow pockets, thin and dark and slightly sour-smelling, the color of old tea.

## Water

The dampness does not announce itself. There are no rivers, no lakes, no streams you could point to on a map. Instead, the water lives in the air and in the small places. Mornings begin with a fog so thick and warm it feels like breathing through flannel, a fog that beads on every surface and drips in slow, fat drops that make a sound like a clock you can't quite locate. The stone pavements are perpetually dark with moisture, slick in places where a particular kind of green-black algae has taken hold, and every hollow that holds soil holds a little water too, a shallow broth of mud and root and the patient chemistry of decay.

There are puddles that never quite dry, and puddles that exist for a week after the rare, warm rain and then vanish, leaving only a ring of brighter lichen behind. The rain itself, when it comes — and it comes perhaps sixty times a year, in short, soft, warm bursts — does not fall so much as materialize, condensing out of a sky that never quite commits to cloud. And then the fog comes back, and the dripping resumes, and the whole place settles into its customary state of gentle, pervasive wetness.

## Vegetation

Sparse, yes, but not barren. The plants here have learned to be small and tough and strange. Nothing grows tall because nothing needs to — there is no competition for light, only for water and for the thin mineral soil. The dominant green is not grass but a kind of low, wiry shrub that spreads in flat spirals, its leaves tiny and thick and coated in a resin that smells like turpentine and honey when crushed. These shrubs — the locals of this place, the old settlers — grow in colonies that can be centuries old, each one a maze of woody stems no higher than a hand's breadth, spreading outward in slow rings like the growth of coral.

Between the shrubs, the ground is a mosaic: lichens in more colors than seems reasonable — rust, sulfur, a deep violet that looks almost black until the light hits it — crusting the open stone; mosses in the hollows, some of them carnivorous, their tiny cups gleaming with a sweet-smelling fluid that attracts the abundant insect life; and a strange, pale, almost translucent plant that has no leaves at all, only a network of thread-like stems that lie flat against the rock and photosynthesize through their skin.

In the deepest hollows, where water gathers and soil is thickest, there are things that might once have been trees — low, contorted forms with bark like scales and branches that grow sideways rather than up, their canopies pressed flat against the ground like hands. They flower, briefly, in a violent magenta that seems out of place in this muted world.

## Fauna

This is where the downs come alive beyond anything the vegetation suggests. The place is *teeming* — not with large animals, but with a density of small life that fills every niche, every crevice, every layer of the air and ground and water. Insects are the great population: beetles in their thousands, ground-dwelling and armored, moving through the moss like tiny cattle; flies that hover in the damp air in great columns, their wings catching the light; a kind of cricket that has lost its wings entirely and communicates by drumming its abdomen against hollow stems, so that the whole plateau hums faintly in the warm afternoons like a distant engine.

There are spiders everywhere, of course — web-builders in the shrubs, and a large, slow, dark species that lives in the cracks of the stone pavements and emerges at dawn to drink the condensation from the rock. There are snails, their shells banded in brown and cream, that travel between the damp pockets and leave trails that shine silver in the fog.

And then there are the vertebrates, few in species but enormous in number. A small, round, brown frog that seems to live in every puddle, every wet hollow, its call a single low note repeated at long intervals, so that at any given moment you might hear three frogs calling from different directions, each one answering something you cannot hear. A lizard, no longer than a finger, that basks on the warm stone in the brief afternoons and vanishes at the first footstep. A bird — the only bird, the only large animal — a kind of ground-dwelling, almost flightless thing the size of a chicken, with mottled grey-brown plumage and a long, curved beak that it uses to probe the soil for grubs. These birds move in loose flocks of perhaps a dozen, and they are silent except for a soft, conversational clucking, and they have no fear of anything because there is nothing here to fear.

## Sound

The place is quiet, but not silent. The quiet is a texture, a background hum made of small sounds: the drip of condensation, the wind that never quite stops moving through the low shrubs (a sound like soft applause, like the rustle of a crowd settling), the distant clucking of the birds, the frog-note, the cricket-drum, the faint papery sound of beetles moving through dry lichen. At night, the frog-call multiplies, and the whole plateau becomes a loose, overlapping chorus of single notes, each one distinct, each one patient, the sound of a hundred small creatures announcing themselves to no one in particular.

## Atmosphere

The light is ordinary but the air does strange things to it. In the mornings, the fog turns everything to a soft grey pearl, and the colors of the lichens glow with a strange inner light, as if the stone itself were mildly radioactive. In the afternoons, when the fog lifts — and it does lift, sometimes, for whole hours at a time — the sky is a pale, washed-out blue, and the sun is bright but not hot, and the whole plateau reveals itself as a place of long views and impossible distances, the ground going on and on in its wrinkled way until it fades into a haze that might be more fog coming, or might be the edge of the world.

## Rules of the World

This is a place where nothing is in a hurry and nothing is wasted. Every dead thing is eaten, every drop of water is used, every surface is colonized. The dense, layered life here exists in a state of elaborate mutualism: the frogs eat the insects, the birds eat the frogs and the snails and the beetles, the spiders eat everything, and everything that dies becomes soil for the lichens and the shrubs, which feed the insects, which feed the frogs. The whole system runs on a slow, steady pulse of decay and renewal, driven by the patient drip of condensation and the occasional warm rain.

## The Sense of the Place

Standing on the mizzle downs, you have the feeling of being on the roof of a world that has been settling for a very long time. The elevation is real — the air is thin enough that you notice it climbing, and the distances are vast — but the land itself has achieved a kind of final form, a state of gentle, worn-out equilibrium. There is nothing here that wants to be a mountain. There is nothing here that wants to be anything other than what it is: a high, damp, teeming, patient place, covered in small life, breathing in fog and breathing out water, waiting for the next rain, which will come, which always comes, eventually.
