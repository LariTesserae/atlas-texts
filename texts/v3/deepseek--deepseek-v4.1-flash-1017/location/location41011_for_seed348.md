# Settlow

You come on it from above, which is the only way to come on it, because Settlow is a hollow — a shallow bowl in cold grey country, maybe four hundred paces across, sunk a little below the level of the moor so that the horizon closes at the rim and the sky is the largest thing you can see. From the rim you look down into a small, complete world. A ring of low buildings. A sheet of standing water at the bottom, grey and unreflective as pewter. A long shed of riveted plate that hums. And between the buildings, along the water, up the walls, over every surface, a texture that at first reads as gravel and then, when your eyes adjust, begins, independently, to move.

There is nothing green. Not a lichen, not a stain, not a thread of moss in a gutter. The damp that would elsewhere raise a fur of life on every stone raises something else here.

## The hollow

The ground is wet. Not soaked — damp, the way a cellar wall is damp, a fine even film that condenses out of the cool air and does not quite dry. Rain comes in bands, brief and unhurried, three or four times a day, and the water goes where water goes: into the gutters cut in the paving, along the channels worn in the stone, down to the sump at the bottom. The paving is old and rounded. Every edge in Settlow has been taken off by weather — doorframes sag into soft shoulders, the ironwork of the sheds is scalloped like the inside of a shell, the thresholds are dished where a thousand years of feet and water have gone over them. It is not decay. It is a shape that has arrived, and having arrived, stays.

The sump is ankle-deep and warmer than the air. It is not water with animals in it. It is animals with water in them. Put your hand in and the water does not part — it clicks, gently, all around your wrist.

## The works

The industry is one long shed and its outbuildings, maybe a hundred paces end to end, roofed in overlapping plates that drip steadily at every seam. Inside, the machines run. Big screws turning in troughs, belts slapping between pulleys, a great slow wheel at the far end with a rhythm you feel in your teeth before you hear it. The drive is not obvious. There is no boiler you can point to, no furnace, no cable coming in from outside. The belts turn because the troughs are full, and the troughs are full because the belts turn.

The smell is wet iron and something faintly ammoniac, and under it the clean mineral smell of cold stone. Everything is warm to the touch, but only to the height of your knee.

## The creatures

They have no name that is not a diminutive. People here call them **the grain**.

They are small — thumbnail-sized, five or six to a palm if you scoop — and they are dark, and their backs are made of the same grey mineral as the ground they stand on, because they make their shells out of what they drink. They come up out of the sump in slow tides. They cross the paving in sheets, not fast, not in a hurry, in a direction that seems to be decided somewhere else. They climb the walls and go into the machines and come out of the machines and go back down to the water. They eat the fine metal dust the works shed, and the dust of the paving, and each other, and they drink the condensation off every cold surface in the hollow. In their millions they are a body of warmth; that is why the air is cool at your face and mild at your shins.

They also make the sound. A ticking, sifting, pouring sound, continuous, like rain on gravel that never stops raining. It has a pitch. It is in fact a chord — not a metaphor, a chord, low and major and slightly out of tune — and the machinery plays the rhythm under it. **Settlow has one note and it is always being played.** Conversation here is done at a shout or not at all, and people have learned to say a great deal with their faces.

At dusk, things with wings come up out of the pipes and the eaves, all of them, thickening the air over the sump until you cannot see the far bank. They are gone by full dark. They are gone at the same time every night and have always been.

And there are the tall ones. Six or seven of them, standing in the sump on legs like jointed stilts, motionless, facing the same direction, all day, every day. Sometimes one is facing a different direction. Nothing about this is remarked upon. They are not statues. They breathe.

Nothing here is vermin and nothing is a plague. The grain is not infesting Settlow. Settlow is what it built, or what was built for it — the sheds, the troughs, the screws, the wheel, the gutters that lead the rain exactly where the water is wanted. Nobody knows which. The question is asked about as often as one asks which came first, the valley or the river.

## The crust

Where shells are shed and bodies are not eaten, they accumulate, and over centuries they have welded into a mineral crust — a grey fur two fingers thick on the north walls, thicker in the lee of the sheds, hard as limestone and warm as a hand. The houses here are built of it. It is quarried off the old walls, set in courses, and the courses are full of the small curved shapes of things that used to be alive, so that every building in Settlow is visibly made of animals, and looks it, and does not feel morbid at all, because there is no wood here. There is nothing to build with that is not stone, metal, shell, or hide. There has never been a plant in Settlow to make a board or a rope or a rag.

## The people

A few hundred of them, in low houses along two streets and a third street that is really a path. They keep the gutters. They quarry the crust. They mend the plates of the shed roof. They do not feed the grain; the grain feeds itself. They are, on the whole, cheerful and unhurried, and they do not want anything from you except that you not stand in the troughs.

They are also the thinnest part of the place. A visitor feels this quickly: the humans are a margin, not the substance. The town is theirs the way a reef is a diver's.

## The rules

**Nothing here is going anywhere.** The population of the grain does not rise and does not fall. The wheel turns at one speed. The rain comes three or four times a day and the gutters take it. The winged things rise at dusk. The tall ones face the same direction. The chord is the same chord. A visitor's footprints fill in with grain within a day and their smell is gone in two, and this is not hostility — it is the same way a lake absorbs a dropped stone. Nothing is excluded and nothing is added.

**Everything that would change is eaten, or grown over, or replaced.** Break a plate on the shed roof and the trough beneath it will be full of grain by morning, working the fracture, and the mend that appears is not a repair anyone made.

**Nothing enters and nothing leaves.** There is no road out that stays a road. People have gone. The grain does not follow them up the slope and the sound fades behind them, and they are not, so far as anyone knows, unhappy about it.

## Weather and light

The sky is a low even undifferentiated grey, brighter at noon and dimmer at the ends of the day and otherwise unemphatic. There are no sunsets here worth the name. Colour comes from the works — a dull red at the vents, a wet blue where the light falls on standing water — and from the crust, which in certain dampnesses takes on a faint violet sheen, like the inside of a mussel, and goes grey again when you look away.

It is cool. It will always be cool at your face. And it is loud, and it is warm at the ankle, and it is busy everywhere you look, and it has not changed since the crust was two fingers thick instead of three. Settlow is not dying and it is not growing. It is finished. What you are hearing is the sound of a place that has entirely arrived.
