# The Cradle

You come down into it without noticing the descent, because the whole land is a shallow bowl and the mist takes the tops of things at ten feet, and the mist and the stone are the same color — a wet, warm, milky gray like the inside of a closed eye. There is no day here to arrive in. There is a general dimness that thickens and thins, and a faint greenish pallor that comes up off the wet films on the stone, just enough to put an edge on the pools. Beyond twenty feet there is only sound.

And the sound is ticking.

Not loud. A hundred small kettles far off, a fine continuous precipitation of noise: water dropping from a hundred rims onto a hundred water surfaces at a hundred different intervals, and every one of them is depositing its load of dissolved stone where it lands. One drop every four seconds. One every eleven. A slow, patient arithmetic, and under it the hiss of steam and the deep, rare knock of a shelf letting go somewhere out in the dark and falling into a pool, and then a long time of nothing but the ticking.

The ground gives underfoot. That is the first thing. It is not mud and it is not stone; it is stone that has been recently water and will be water again, and it takes the print of your heel and holds it, and by morning the print will be rimmed in cream.

---

The Cradle is a town and no one lives in it. On the survey maps it is a single word in an otherwise empty basin — *Cradle* — because the first people to find it could not decide whether it was a city or a hot spring and settled on a word that meant neither.

It is low. Nothing here stands more than two storeys, and most of it does not stand at all — it brims. The whole town is a staircase of shallow basins stepping up a gentle slope, each one ringed by a lip of pale stone an inch or two high, each one brimming and spilling into the next. One terrace, and then another behind it, and another; from above, if the vapor ever lifted enough to see, it would look like a hillside of full cups. Over centuries the lips have thickened and risen. Some are waist-high. Some are chest-high. Some have closed over their pools entirely, arching across them, and where several have grown into each other you get roofs and walls and openings, and those are the streets — the gaps between dams, worn low in the middle by water that has been going through them for a very long time.

The stone is banded. Every wall is a ledger: a cream band for a wet decade, a rust band for a dry one, a porous crumbly year, a dense one, a band shot through with black ferny manganese, a band full of the hollow casts of stems that lived and died and were sealed over. It is warm. Not hot — blood heat, a little more, the temperature of something asleep.

Everything here is soft-edged. Nothing is sharp. Corners have gone round; thresholds have sagged; the whole town has the eroded, settled, entirely-in-its-own-state look of a thing that has been exactly this long enough to be finished with becoming. It is old and it is not decaying. Where the water runs fast it cuts, where it slows it deposits, and the two have been so nearly equal for so long that the town holds its shape the way a river holds its bed.

---

Here is what makes it a town and not a spring.

There are stairs. Low, wide, even-treaded, dished in the middle where feet would go — and the tread is exactly the length of a stride, and the riser is exactly the height of a comfortable step.

There are benches along the walls, and the benches are hollowed where hips would sit. There are niches at the height of a hand and shelves at the height of an eye, each with a lip along the front to keep things from rolling off. There are thresholds, thickened and rounded by passage. There are basins at the right height to wash in, and in the middle of the larger chambers, raised slabs, and the slabs are the length of a person and a little more, and some of them are dished too.

None of it is carved. There are no tool marks in the entire basin, no chisel-scar, no seam, nothing set on anything. It grew. The way a rim grows, the way a band grows, the way a deposit thickens where the water hesitates. The town builds itself by the same rule that builds a lip around a puddle, and it has been doing so for long enough that the rule has produced staircases and shelves and beds.

And it does not stop. Set a cup down on a shelf and leave it a month and it will have a ring of translucent stone around its base, and in a year you will have to break it out. Leave a thing long enough and it is not on the stone, it is in it. The town does not distinguish between its own growth and yours.

No animal lives here. Nothing moves that is not water. No insects come for the warmth, no birds for the insects; the air is hot and foul with sulfur and wet chalk and hot iron and something faintly like clean laundry, and nothing breathes it but you. There is no ecology of mouths. Yet the town is furnished, and furnished for bodies, and the seats are worn.

Nobody has ever sat in them.

---

The water does not always obey. In some of the stairwells it climbs — a thin sheet going up the steps, against the fall, slow and unbothered, and it is only capillary action in the porous stone except that it is faster than capillary action, and after a while you stop finding that strange. Some of the pools drain and refill on a cycle of hours, and the sound of a pool draining is a low glissando of pitch as the water finds a lower hole and the chamber behind it becomes a mouth and hums.

The chambers are bigger inside than out. Not enormously. You step through a doorway in a mound you could put your arms around, and you count nine paces to the far wall, and there is no nine paces of stone. Two paces of impossible room. It is the kind of wrongness you can convince yourself you misjudged, right up until you go back out and pace the outside.

The mist breathes on a cycle of some hours: it thickens until you walk with your hands out, then it draws down and the whole town surfaces at once, pale humps and bright rings going away further than you thought it went, terrace after terrace, and then it closes again and there is only the ticking and the green glow of the films on the wet stone and the warm rain.

Because it does rain, indoors, always. The vapor rises, finds the cooler undersides of the arches, and comes back down as a fine warm drizzle that has never stopped in the deep chambers. Stand in one long enough and you are soaked through, pleasantly, and your clothes will be stiff with mineral by morning.

The vegetation is barely that: pale filaments in the wet hollows, felt-like mats, stains that are green the way wet stone is green. They are not eating light — there is no light to eat — and they may not be eating anything at all. They simply persist, and where they persist long enough the stone takes their color into a band, so that the walls are striped with the ghosts of things that were nearly alive.

---

You sleep, if you sleep here, to the drops. Four seconds. Eleven seconds. A sound like a clock that has forgotten what it was counting and gone on counting anyway. The stone under your back is warm and slightly yielding, and in the morning there will be a shallow depression where you lay, exactly your shape, and it will be comfortable, and it will be there for the next one.

You leave. The mist takes the town back at twenty feet. Behind you the ticking goes on, drop by drop, lifting the rims another fraction of a hair, thickening the thresholds, deepening the seats by the width of a finger's wear each century, furnishing rooms for no one, patient, unhurried, entirely in balance, and getting, very slowly, more exactly right.
