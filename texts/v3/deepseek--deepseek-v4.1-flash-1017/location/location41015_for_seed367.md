The air here is the first thing you notice, and the last thing you understand. It is warm and thick, heavy as a held breath, and it moves. Not wind, exactly — more a slow tidal breathing, in and out, as if the entire basin were a lung. The humidity clings to your skin without ever condensing into droplets. There is water here, you realize, but not in any form you can drink. It hangs suspended in the air itself, a saturated warmth that never falls as rain and never pools on the ground. The ground is dry. It has always been dry. But the air is a river you can walk through.

You are standing in a bowl. The basin is high — you climbed for two days to reach the rim, and the plateau stretched out behind you like the back of a sleeping animal, flat and ancient and worn down to almost nothing. There are no peaks here. The mountains gave up their height long ago, eroded into gentle swells and shallow valleys, and this basin is one of the last depressions left in a landscape that has otherwise been planed smooth by a million years of patient wind. The walls of the basin rise gently around you, not cliffs but long slopes of soft, pale stone, furred with growth. They are low enough that you can see the sky above — a dim, milky sky, the color of old bone, with no sun visible, only a diffuse glow that seems to come from everywhere at once. It is neither day nor night. It is the light of a deep forest floor, or a cave mouth, or the inside of a cloud.

The vegetation begins at the rim and does not stop. It is not a forest in any ordinary sense. There are no trees with trunks and crowns, no understory and canopy. Instead the plants grow in layers, stacked and interwoven, each layer rooted not in soil but in the layer below it. The lowest growth is a mat of pale, rubbery creepers, their leaves thick and waxy, their roots shallow and gripping the bare rock. Above them, shrubs with hollow stems that store the air's moisture in spongy tissues. Above them, taller growths — not trees but columns of stacked leaves, each leaf a shallow cup that faces upward to catch the drifting humidity. And above them, the highest layer, a tangled ceiling of vine-like growths that never touch the ground, suspended on the columns below, their leaves narrow and silver-gray, their surfaces beaded with a perpetual film of condensation that never drips.

Nothing here relies on rain. Nothing here has ever seen rain. The plants drink the air. They are lush in the way that a sponge is lush — saturated, dense, dripping with moisture that never falls. The colors are wrong for a desert: deep greens, bruised purples, the pale yellow of new growth. The smell is wrong too — not dry and dusty but wet and green and faintly sweet, like the inside of a greenhouse, like a compost heap, like the breath of something alive.

The basin is small. You can walk its width in an hour, maybe less. It is a pocket, a single contained feature, a micro-environment hidden within the vast worn plateau. And yet it is full. The density is almost overwhelming — every surface is occupied, every gap is filled. The creepers grow over each other, the columns press close, the vines tangle. There is no bare ground visible anywhere. Even the stone walls of the basin are furred with growth, the pale rock only showing through in patches where something has died and not yet been replaced. The air itself feels crowded, thick with spores and pollen and the tiny drifting insects that fill every cubic meter.

And the sound. The sound is constant and layered and loud. It is not the roar of a city or the crash of water. It is the sound of life — a dense, overlapping chorus of clicks and whirs and low resonant hums. The plants themselves are part of it: the hollow stems of the mid-layer growths act as resonance chambers, and the warm air moving through them produces a steady, breathy drone, like a distant organ playing a single note. Over that drone, the fauna.

The fauna is everywhere. It is the dominant presence here, more than the plants, more than the stone. There are insects in the air, thick as dust, their wings catching the dim light in flickers of iridescence. There are creatures in the vegetation — small, many-legged things that move through the layers with a rustle like dry leaves, though nothing here is dry. There are larger things too, glimpsed rather than seen: a shape that might be a lizard but moves like a sloth, its skin the same pale green as the leaves it clings to; a flying thing with wide, membranous wings that beat slowly, heavily, like a heart; a chorus of voices from the ceiling vines, high and rhythmic, not birds but something else, something that clicks and trills and answers itself in rounds. The sound is not chaotic. It has structure. It has patterns that repeat, call and response, a conversation that has been going on for a very long time.

The town is in the center of the basin, and you almost miss it. It is not built in any way you would recognize. There are no walls of stone, no roofs of tile. The structures are grown — or rather, they are the vegetation itself, shaped and trained and woven. The columns of stacked leaves have been bent and braided into arches. The ceiling vines have been pulled down and tied into platforms, suspended above the ground on living supports. The creepers have been encouraged to spread across these platforms, forming floors that are soft and springy and warm. There are no straight lines. Everything curves. Everything is alive and still growing, the walls thickening slowly, the floors settling, the arches bending under their own weight. The town is not maintained so much as negotiated with.

The people here are few — you see maybe a dozen, moving through the layers with a practiced ease. They are small and dark-skinned, their clothing woven from the silver leaves of the ceiling vines, their tools of bone and hard wood and knapped stone. They carry narrow blades for pruning, curved hooks for pulling down vines, shallow bowls for collecting the condensation that beads on the highest leaves. They do not speak much. The chorus of the fauna fills the silence. When they do speak, it is in low tones, and the plants seem to lean toward them, the hollow stems turning slightly, the leaves cupping toward the sound. The town is not separate from the basin. It is one more layer of growth, one more set of chambers and passages and living surfaces, indistinguishable from the rest except that it was shaped.

The rules of this world are simple: nothing falls, everything flows. Water moves through the air, not through the ground. The plants take it from the air, the animals take it from the plants, the people take it from the bowls they set out under the leaves. Nothing is wasted. When something dies — a leaf, a creature, a person — it is not buried. It is laid on the creepers, and the creepers grow over it, and in time it becomes part of the mat, part of the layer, part of the slow accumulation of life that fills this basin. The stone beneath is ancient, worn down to almost nothing, but the growth on top is always new. The basin is a pocket of active life embedded in a landscape that has otherwise settled into its final form. It is a last flicker of motion in a world that has stopped moving.

You stand at the edge of the town, and the air presses against you, warm and wet and alive. The drone of the hollow stems fills your chest. The chorus of clicks and trills rises and falls around you. The light is dim and milky and comes from everywhere. The ground beneath your feet is soft and springy and slightly warm. You are in a hidden place, a pocket place, a place that has been here for a very long time and will be here for a very long time still. It is loud and crowded and lush and dry. It is the oldest kind of new.
