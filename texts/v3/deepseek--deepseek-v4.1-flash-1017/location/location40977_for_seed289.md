The place is a geode the size of a cathedral, but it was not made of stone. It is a cavity in the world, a pocket of impossible brightness and stagnant air, and it is still being born.

To reach it, you must crawl down through a fissure that smells of wet chalk and old lightning. The passage is tight, scraping shoulders, and condensation beads on the raw, unformed rock—rock that feels less like a solid and more like a thought not yet finished. The walls are warm and faintly slick, and they hum. It is a low, constant thrum, the sound of a world under construction, a frequency you feel in your teeth before you hear it.

Then the fissure opens, and the light hits you.

It is not the light of a sun or a fire. It is a sourceless, pervasive luminescence, a cold white glare that leaches all colour from the air. It emanates from the very substance of the cavern, a place of impossible, packed density. There is no "floor" and "ceiling" in the way you understand them. The space is filled.

It is filled with a lattice of crystalline threads, a billion billion filaments of translucent, glassy material that crisscross the void in a structure so dense it is almost a solid. It is a forest of glass, a frozen web of light. Each filament is wet, coated in a perpetual film of condensation that never drips. The entire structure is weeping, but the tears never fall; they just shimmer, clinging to the glass. This is the water: not a river or a rain, but a saturation, a world held in a state of perpetual, breathless damp.

The air is thick with the sound. The hum of the rock is the bass note, but over it plays a cacophony of clicks, chimes, and resonant tones. The glass filaments vibrate, and where they touch, they ring like a thousand tiny, untuned bells. The sound is a constant, shimmering music of stress and tension, the sound of a structure holding itself in a state of perpetual, precarious balance. There are other sounds, too: a wet, sucking pop from somewhere deep in the lattice; the high, keening whine of a filament slowly, imperceptibly fracturing; the rustle of something moving.

For it is not empty. The density is life. The fauna of this place are small, scuttling things that have evolved to navigate the glass. There are beetle-like creatures with carapaces of the same translucent material, their internal organs glowing faintly through their shells. They move with a delicate, tap-tapping gait, their feet finding purchase on the slick filaments. Moth-like things, their wings little more than sheets of gossamer, drift through the glare, their bodies so thin they are almost invisible. They do not fly so much as they float, carried on the constant, minute currents of air disturbed by the vibrations. They feed on the condensation, on the mineral taste of the weeping glass.

And all of it—the crawling, the floating, the chiming, the weeping—is a frozen moment. This is a world in a state of absolute equilibrium. Nothing changes. The light does not flicker. The hum does not waver from its key. A droplet of water on a filament has been poised on the verge of falling for an age, held by a surface tension that will never break. The scuttling beetle has been mid-stride for a century. This is not a place where time passes; it is a place where time *is*, a single, perfect, terrifying instant, stretched out forever.

The only sign of anything else, of a world beyond this one, are the faint, scattered marks. Down near the bottom of the cavity, where the lattice is fused into a more solid mass, you can see them. A series of perfect, geometric carvings on a thick glass trunk. A spiral. A row of dots. A shape that might be a crude, stylised hand. They are not tools, not structures, but marks—the faint, deliberate graffiti of someone who was here, and who is now gone. They are a question with no answer, left in the most permanent, unchanging place imaginable.

You stand on a relatively thick shelf of the glass-stuff, and the light is so bright it has no shadows. The damp is a cold second skin. The sound is a physical pressure against your eardrums. The air tastes of ozone and wet stone. You are a single, alien cell in a vast, living, static crystal. You are the only thing in this perfect, densely packed, bright, and loud world that is changing, simply by being there, by breathing, by looking, a temporary and fleeting flaw in a masterpiece that will never be finished, because it is always, and has always been, just-coming-into-being.
