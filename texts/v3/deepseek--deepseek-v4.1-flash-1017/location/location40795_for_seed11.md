The place is called the Aria Vast, and it is a highland so ancient it has forgotten how to be a mountain. It is less a peak and more a continent of stone, worn down by a hundred million years of wind into a series of rounded, undulating shoulders and broad, shallow valleys. The bedrock is a pale, warm-toned granite, soft-looking in the perpetual, wind-diffused light. Ridges of harder quartzite stick out like the vertebrae of some colossal, long-dead creature, but most of the elevation is a gentle, deceptive roll. You can walk for a day and feel you've gone nowhere, yet the air grows thinner, the sky deepens from a soft blue to a profound, bruised violet, and the world below recedes into a hazy memory.

Water is not a constant here; it's an event. The air is thick with a damp warmth, a perpetual humidity that clings to your skin and condenses on the ancient stone in the cool hours before dawn. The ground is a patchwork of textures: dry, sun-warmed rock that's rough as pumice, and damp, springy cushions of moss like saturated sponges underfoot. Rain doesn't fall so much as it is torn from the sky in brief, violent sheets, the clouds above a turbulent, churning sea of grey and white. Afterward, the water doesn't gather in lakes but vanishes into the stone, leaving behind fleeting, jewel-like pools in shallow depressions, their surfaces steaming faintly in the warm air. The whole landscape seems to breathe moisture, a slow inhale and exhale that leaves everything slick and glistening before the wind scours it dry again.

And the wind. The sound is the defining feature of the Aria Vast. It's not a noise; it's a physical presence, a deafening, all-consuming roar that is the world's constant soundtrack. It screams through the quartzite ridges, hisses across the mossy plains, and moans in the hollows. It never ceases; it only changes its tune. In its quieter moments, it’s a low, resonant hum that you feel in your teeth, a vibration that seems to come from the earth itself. But when it surges, it’s a chaotic cacophony, a turbulent, roaring god that flattens the vegetation and makes speech impossible. It is a storm-state that never ends, a hyperactive, chaotic energy that scours and polishes the world, a constant, restless creation and destruction.

Life here is lush but low. There are no forests, only vast, rolling carpets of vegetation that have learned to bend and cling. Great mats of tough, fibrous heather in shades of bronze and rust cover the hills. Between the stones grow clinging vines with leathery leaves and clusters of small, pale flowers that open only in the fleeting calm between gusts. There are fleshy, water-filled mosses and a type of tough, woody grass that whispers and rattles in the wind. The flora is dense on the ground but vertically stunted, a thick, resilient skin over the ancient rock. Fauna is rare and fleeting. You might see a flash of iridescent blue as a ground-dwelling bird, all legs and streamlined feathers, scampers for cover, or hear the high, clear chime of its call cutting through the wind for a single, startling moment. The insects are mostly wingless, scuttling things that live deep in the heather. The Vast is empty of large life, and this only amplifies its sense of vast, open space, the feeling of being a single, solitary point in a world of immense, turbulent sound and space.

There are marks of a past. Scattered across the landscape are structures so old they seem grown from the rock itself. They are low, circular enclosures of dry-stone masonry, their walls no more than knee-high, half-buried in heather and soil. Some look like the foundations of simple huts, others are just long, meandering lines of stone that follow the contours of the hills for no discernible purpose. There are no artifacts of metal, only chipped stone tools and fragments of coarse, hand-formed pottery. The people who built them are gone, their world eroded away, leaving only these faint, primitive echoes. The structures feel less like ruins and more like the final, settled state of a place that has almost finished its slow return to dust.

The light is normal, but it feels strange. The perpetual wind carries a fine dust, a pale-grey powder of ancient stone that diffuses the sunlight, making shadows soft and edges blurry. The sun is a bright, hazy disc, and the sky is a constant, shifting drama of racing clouds. The only things that move quickly are the clouds and the wind itself. A human in this landscape is a slow, intrusive thing, a temporary anomaly in a place of immense age, deafening sound, and turbulent, chaotic energy. The Aria Vast doesn't need you. It simply is.
