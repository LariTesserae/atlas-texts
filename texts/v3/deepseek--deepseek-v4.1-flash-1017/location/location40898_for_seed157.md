The approach is the first strange thing: the path up the mountain simply stops being a path, and the rock closes in. You climb a seam in the cliff, hands on cold wet stone, and then you step through into a hollow that should not be there—a small pocket of land caught in the mountain's ribs, no bigger than a chapel, and yet it is full.

Full to the brim. There is almost no bare rock to step on. The floor of the hollow is a saturated mat of roots and moss, impossibly thick, giving underfoot like a soaked mattress, and from it rises a density of green life that seems to press back against you: ferns with fronds so thick they overlap into a solid ceiling at chest height, pale liverworts scaling every surface in overlapping plates, clubs of moss and strange thick-stemmed plants with leaves like wet leather. The air is cold and heavy, so full of water that it condenses on your skin. Everything drips. The walls weep. There is a sound of water but it is a small, constant sound—not a rush, only the endless slow seep and the occasional heavy drop from a frond. It has been dripping like this for a very long time.

This place is old and has settled. The erosion is not violent; it is the patient work of ten thousand years of water finding its way down, and now it has found its shape. The walls are rounded and dark and streaked with mineral sheen; the floor is a cup that holds water just below the surface everywhere; the hollow has reached a kind of completion. Nothing about it wants to change. It is balanced.

The light is almost nothing. Filtered down through levels of canopy and rock is a dim brown-green murk, enough to see shapes but not colors. What you mistake at first for glowing fungus turns out to be patches of pale growth that hold a faint luminescence, and they are everywhere, so the hollow has its own dull light, like a room lit by many dying bulbs. It is not bright. It is just enough to show you how much is here.

And there is so much here. The fauna is overwhelming and everywhere. Small wet things move on every surface—pale salamander-like creatures the length of your finger, sliding between the liverwort plates; clouds of midges that do not bite but hang in the still air in dense columns; beetles with soft bodies that crawl over your boots. Things live inside the moss. Things live in the standing water. Something larger moves at the back of the hollow, a slow heavy shifting in the undergrowth, and you cannot see it and it does not come closer. It is simply there, one more occupant in a place that is fully occupied. The density is nearly unbearable. There is no empty space. Every surface is a habitat. The air itself is a habitat. It is like standing inside a lung.

And yet it is quiet. The creatures here do not call. The salamanders are silent, the beetles are silent, the midges make no whine. What sound there is comes only from water and the occasional soft shifting of wet leaves. It is the quiet of a place where sound has been given up as a bad habit. It is the quiet of saturation.

You stand there in the cold dark wet and understand that this pocket has been in this exact state for longer than you can imagine. The water seeps at the same rate. The moss grows and dies and is replaced without changing the shape of the mat. The creatures are born and eaten and replaced without changing the population. The hollow found its equilibrium and holds it, a small, dark, packed, weeping room in the mountain, full of life that has nowhere to go and does not want to. You are the only unstable thing in it, and you feel it—the place would prefer you to be still, to sink into the wet mat, to become one more occupant of the density. You leave the way you came, through the seam in the rock, and behind you the water goes on dripping and the pale lights go on glowing and nothing changes at all.
