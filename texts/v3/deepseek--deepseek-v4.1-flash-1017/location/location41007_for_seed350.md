The air here is a solid, warm thing. It presses in on the eardrums and carries the sound of a great, ceaseless labour. This is the Warm Engine, a place where the world’s own machinery has broken its casing and runs on, exposed and screaming.

The landscape is a shallow, submerged basin, a vast coastal shelf that has forgotten how to be dry. The water is not the clear blue of an ocean, but a thick, warm soup, the colour of milky tea and just as opaque. It is dense with life—pollen, spores, the microscopic debris of a million plants—so thick that it feels less like a liquid and more like a medium you must part with your body. The scale is regional; you could journey for days across this soup and never leave it. The elevation is almost nil. There are no hills to climb, only a feeling of profound, watery flatness, occasionally broken by the monolithic shapes of the Engine’s remnants.

And what vegetation. Lush is too simple a word. It is a hyper-abundance, a riot of growth that has learned to thrive in this deafening, fluid world. The plants are the color of bruised metal and wounded flesh—deep greens, coppery reds, and the phosphorescent blues of things feeding on decay. They grow in towering, coral-like thickets, their trunks soft and porous, their leaves not leaves but membranes that undulate with the water's pulse. Some are solitary, balloon-like sacs the size of houses, tethered to the muck by thick cables of root, their interiors filled with a lighter-than-water gas. They drift and swing with the constant, churning currents. Everything is slick, every surface coated in a warm, slippery film of algae. The vegetation grows fast, dies fast, and its decay is part of the water's density, its smell a thick, sweet, rotting perfume.

The sound is the first thing that truly unmakes you. It is not just loud; it is a total, physical presence. The primary note is a low, bone-shaking thrum, the sound of a trillion gallons of water moving with purpose, a deep basso profundo that vibrates in your chest. Layered over this is theEngine itself. The landscape is littered with scattered industrial remnants—a single, skeletal cooling tower half-swallowed by a strangler vine, a field of rusting pipes that erupt from the water like metallic geysers, a vast, tilted slab of concrete that might have been a floor, now furred with moss and red lichen. These are the sources of the higher noises: the hiss of scalding steam venting through a cracked valve, the high-pitched, frantic shriek of a pump that has been running for a thousand years and will never stop, the intermittent, world-ending CLANG of some colossal, unseen piston striking metal. The sound is a chaotic, arrhythmic symphony of function without purpose, a deafening industrial heartbeat.

The technological remnants are not just relics; they are active geology. A broken threshing machine, its gears fused into a single mass of verdigris, still tries to turn, emitting a grinding groan that's felt more than heard. A thick, electrical cable, sheathed in rotting rubber, hums with a terrifying, sizzling energy, its end dangling in the water, creating a constant, boiling plume of steam and a nimbus of wild, blue light. These artifacts are the closest thing to animal life. There are no birds, no fish, no insects. The only movement comes from the plants, the shifting water, and the tremors of the Engine itself. It is a world of flora and machine, with a strange, symbiotic relationship between them. Vines grow through the vents of an engine block, venting steam through their hollow stems; a turbine, choked with weed, turns slowly, listlessly, like a beast caught in a trap.

The light is dim, a perpetual, submerged twilight. The sun is a pale, diffuse memory, a lighter smear on the churning, murky ceiling of the sky. The sky itself is a phenomenon—a churning, oily layer of cloud that seems to be the surface of the water from below, a "wall of rain" that doesn't fall so much as it simply *exists*, a permanent, oppressive ceiling. The true light comes from the landscape itself. Bioluminescent fungi erupt in constellations on the rotting hulks of the machinery. The undulating plant membranes pulse with a soft, internal glow, the color of a dying ember. The sparks from the exposed cables throw brief, violent flashes of actinic white light across the scene.

And the weirdness is the water. It isn't just a medium; it's a mind. It eddies in patterns that feel like thought. The turbulence is not random. It is chaotic change, yes—the water is constantly swirling, boiling, creating new channels and collapsing old ones—but it feels *intentional*. It is a Solaris-like creation and destruction, a hyperactive, anthill energy. You might see a shape in the mist thrown up by a vent: the outline of a door, a staircase, a figure you almost recognize. The rules of the world are not physics, but a kind of dreaming logic. The Engine’s chaotic output—heat, sound, electrical discharge—is the engine of this dream. It creates local anomalies: a patch of water that is suddenly ice-cold; a pocket of air that smells of ozone and machine oil; a place where sound is perfectly, unnervingly cancelled out, replaced by a hum that feels like a memory.

You find a single, scattered structure that offers a kind of shelter: a concrete bunker, half-sunk in the mire, its doorway a dark rectangle. Inside, it is dry, warm, and silent. You can still feel the deep thrum of the world through the floor, but the deafening chaos is muffled. Through the doorway, the landscape is a churning, beautiful nightmare of green, rust, and radiant light. It is a world in its turbulent youth, freshly emerged from some catastrophe, its sharp features not yet softened by time, its ecology a riotous, improbable mix of sprouting jungle and dying machine, all of it breathing, all of it screaming, all of it alive.
