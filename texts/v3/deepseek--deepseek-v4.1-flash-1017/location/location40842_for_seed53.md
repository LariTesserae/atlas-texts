Nine hours a day the light is enormous. At this height it has no atmosphere to soften it in — the sky goes up from the horizon through a pale straw color and then straight into a blue that is nearly black, and the sun sits in it like a chip of something too bright to look at. Shadows are not dark, they are blue, and there are a great many of them, because the ground is not flat.

The place is a shelf: forty paces at its widest, twenty at its narrowest, kicked out from the lee side of a summit ridge the way a shelf of snow sometimes sits on a branch. Below it the mountain drops away into a sea of cloud that has not lifted in the memory of the stone. Above it there is more mountain, and then nothing but air. It is a small place. You can cross it in a minute and you will not, because crossing it takes twenty, and every step has to be chosen.

The shelf is not empty. It is crowded.

What fills it are pipes — that is the only word — a couple of hundred of them, packed close enough that their crusts almost touch, standing from the size of a fist to the height of a standing person and every size between. They are mineral through and through: fine concentric sleeves of wind-blown dust and carbonate and pale gray salt, laid down one skin at a time over a span no one could guess, each sleeve a season of somebody's weather. They are hollow. Warm air comes out of them, faintly, steadily, the breath of some long fissure inside the mountain, and because the air is warm and damp and the world is not, it condenses the moment it leaves the lip: a bead of water, a film of rime, a crust, a skin, an increment of growth. Each pipe is a slow fountain running upward into winter, building itself out of its own exhalation.

Insulated inside that crust is a core of ice. It never fully melts. On a still afternoon you can see the sun go into the walls of the thinner ones and come out the other side, dull amber, and the whole field becomes a low thicket of lit lamps. The tall ones lean — they have been leaning for centuries, drifting half a millimeter a year toward wherever the sunlight is strongest, and every one of them leans the same way, so the whole crowd inclines like wheat in a wind nobody can feel.

The moisture shows everywhere. Each pipe sits in its own dark ring of wet stone. In the first hour after dawn the whole shelf ticks and mutters with meltwater: drips, threads, small hands' worth of water sliding down the rounded rock and pooling in the hollows and gone by noon. Around the pipes the bedrock is pocked and scalloped and worn into bowls and pedestals, brown-black where the water runs, and the rock itself is old and rounded and settled, cut by long wind into shapes that look finished. Nothing here is new. Everything here is being maintained.

Nothing grows. Nothing has ever grown. There is no lichen on the stone, no green anywhere, no insect in the wet. The only processes are the mineral ones, and they are busy: accretion, melt, frost-splitting, the periodic collapse of a pipe into its own rubble, dust moving in, a new crust starting on the stump. A struck pipe does not grow back in a lifetime. If you break one to see inside it you have made a mark that will outlast your language. People are careful here, and always have been.

They came, though. At the field's edge, where the shelf meets the ridge, there is a crescent of stacked stones, knee-high, one side open to the lee; behind it a flat slab worn smooth in the middle and green-gray with handling; a scatter of river cobbles carried up from far below, each one chipped in two or three places and then set down; four stakes of some dense brown wood, rotted to the thickness of a thumb; a ring of small cairns, waist-high at most, threading off through the pipes and down toward where the couloir must be, though the couloir is under cloud by ten. There is no metal. There is no writing. The wall's stones have sunk into the mossless ground the way old teeth sink into a jaw, and the whole assembly has the settled look of things that have stopped being a camp and become part of the weathering.

It is quiet. The vents hiss, very low, a sound like the inside of a shell held at arm's length. The ice ticks. Water falls onto stone from a height of half a meter and makes a small clear note and is not repeated. Now and then a chip of crust lets go somewhere out in the field and you hear it land and skitter. The wind is a huge presence just over the ridge and it does not come here; you hear it going by like a crowd in the next street. Once in a long while the cloud sea belches up a slow white arm that feels its way over the lip, wets everything with a skin of water, and is gone, and the sun comes back and starts drinking it.

The warmth is the strangest part. Stand among the pipes and the air around your hands is a few degrees kinder than the air at your face. There is a smell in it, wet chalk and something like struck flint, and it is not a smell you get anywhere else. Put your ear against a hollow one and you hear the mountain exchanging air with the sky. The whole field is breathing, and it has been breathing a very long time, and it will go on being here after the cairns have gone under and the slab has cracked, growing a skin at a time, leaning into the light.
