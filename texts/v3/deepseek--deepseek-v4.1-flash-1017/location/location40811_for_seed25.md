The world here is defined by its sound. It is a physical pressure, a constant, layered wall of noise that begins in the marrow and radiates outward. There is no silence to contrast it against, only the deafening, ceaseless chorus of a place that is impossibly, relentlessly alive. To stand here is to be inside a waterfall of noise, a deluge of clicks, whirrs, bellows, and screeches that never breaks.

The terrain is a vast, low basin of cool, dark rock, worn into a settled, ancient state. It is a landscape of erosion without vegetation, sculpted by a process not of wind and rain, but of life itself. The ground is not bare stone but a deep, porous crust, a spongy accumulation of cast-off shells, husks, and the mineralized remains of a billion generations, all cemented together by a thin, intermittent slime. This crust is the foundation of the world, and it is alive. Its surface is riddled with pores and chimneys, the entrances to a subterranean labyrinth that is the true domain of the overwhelming fauna.

Water is present only as a pervasive, cool dampness. It condenses on every surface, beading on the knobby, textured ground and trickling into the countless fissures. Here and there, the crust gives way to shallow, stagnant sumps where a thicker, organic-rich slurry collects. These sumps are the oases of this world, the centers of frantic, dense activity. The air itself is thick with moisture, a perpetual, gentle drizzle that is less rain and more a slow, continuous exhalation from the ground.

In the absolute darkness, the inhabitants of this place do not see; they feel, they taste, they echolocate. The air is a storm of sound and vibration, the primary medium of existence. The fauna is a nightmare of density and variety. They are everywhere, layered upon each other, packed into every crevice. Blind, soft-bodied crawlers with a dozen legs pick their way across the crust, their antennae twitching. Pale, whip-thin things like animated roots dangle from the ceilings of the larger vents, snatching at the smaller creatures that scurry beneath them. Vast, slow-moving sacs of membrane, bloated with gas, drift through the thicker air of the sumps, their passage a low, resonant hum. The most dominant sound is a percussive, rhythmic tapping, a million tiny hammers striking the stone in a complex, evolving code that seems to be the very language of the world.

The rules of this world are simple: moisture and heat are the only currency. The great mineralized crust is the producer, drawing some subtle energy from the planet's core, and everything else is a consumer in a vast, intricate food web. To survive is to find a sump or a warm thermal vent; to die is to be unceremoniously absorbed back into the crust, another layer in the foundation. There is no light, no green, no peace. The world is a single, dynamic, closed system of biological force, a place of strange, damp, and deafening life where the only measure of time is the frantic, ceaseless rhythm of a billion things living and dying in the dark.
