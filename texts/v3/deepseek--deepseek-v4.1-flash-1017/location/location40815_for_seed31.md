The place is a high plateau of wet, black stone, young and sharp-edged, where the world has not yet had time to soften. Ridges and pinnacles stand out with the crispness of a fresh fracture, their surfaces slick and dark from constant moisture. The rock is not ancient and worn; it is new, recently emerged from some deep pressure, and the water that sheathes it seems to be still in the act of shaping it, though so slowly that nothing appears to change from one year to the next. The air is warm and heavy, saturated to the point of breathlessness, and everything is beaded with condensation. The stone weeps. Not in dramatic torrents, but in a pervasive, silent exudation: thin films of water creep down every incline, gather in the hollows of the rock, and slip away through narrow, sharp-cut channels. The whole landscape glistens, a dark, wet mirror under a sky of normal, diffuse light that seems to press down through the humid air. The elevation is high enough that the horizon is a distant, hazy curve, and the plateau extends outward in a vast, open expanse, a region of interconnected ridges and shallow, water-filled basins. The scale is regional; you can see for a long way, but the view is broken by the sharp, new-made topography.

The vegetation is lush but not abundant. It is a low, clinging growth, a dense mat of mosses and ferns that have found purchase in the cracks and on the wetter slopes. There are shrubs with broad, dark leaves that drip constantly, and small, pale flowers that seem to bloom and fade in the perpetual damp. The green is a deep, saturated color, almost black in the shadows, and it covers perhaps half the stone, leaving the rest bare and shining. The plants are scattered, not continuous; there are wide gaps of naked, wet rock between the patches of growth. It is a landscape of oases, each cluster of vegetation isolated by stretches of slick, dark stone. There are no trees; the exposure and the thin, waterlogged soil do not permit them.

The settlement is built into this wet, sharp world. It is a town, but a strange, quiet one. The buildings are of stone, gathered from the plateau itself and stacked without mortar, the walls thick and low, their roofs flat and pitched to shed the constant water. They are clustered in the shallow basins and along the gentler ridges, wherever a patch of ground is flat enough to build on. The architecture is primitive and manual; there is no metal, only stone, wood from the shrubs (cut and dried with difficulty), and rope made from fibrous plants. The structures are layered and complex, built up over generations, with narrow, winding passages between them, stairways cut into the rock, and channels for water that run through the town, carrying the runoff from the higher slopes. The town is organized but not dense; there is much space between the buildings, and many are half-ruined or abandoned, their walls now part of the water's path. It is a place of quiet, persistent labor, where the people have learned to live with the wet and the stone.

The atmosphere is one of profound stillness. The air is warm and thick, and sound does not carry. There is the soft, constant sound of water: the drip from the roofs, the trickle in the channels, the faint hiss of rain that falls often but lightly. But beyond that, there is almost silence. The people move quietly, their voices low, their tools simple and their work slow. There are no animals. No birds in the sky, no insects in the air, no fish in the pools. The only life is the vegetation and the people. The world is empty of other presences. The stillness is not peaceful; it is a held breath, a waiting. Nothing changes. The water drips, the moss grows, the people build and repair, and the stone remains sharp and new. It is an equilibrium, a frozen moment in a young landscape, where the forces that made it are still present but inactive, and the world is caught in a long, wet, quiet pause.
