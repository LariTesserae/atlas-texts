# The Kettle

You come over a lip of soft red stone and the whole of it is below you at once — an oval basin perhaps forty meters across, no more, whose walls have been rounded by water until they are almost comfortable to look at. It is like looking down into a cupped hand. The bottom of the hand is green and it is breathing.

The basin is old and settled into itself. The stone has gone the color of wet brick and the strata have softened into curves. There is no raw edge anywhere. Even the places where the wall has slumped are upholstered in moss so thick it looks poured. This is a form that finished arriving a long time ago and has been sitting in the warm ever since, being gradually refined.

## The plant

Vegetation does not grow here so much as occupy. There is no ground visible and no air visible below the canopy line. From the rim you see a low roof of leaves — broad, waxy, overlapping like roof tiles, chest-high in places and shoulder-high in others, unbroken except where the pump house pushes through. Underneath that roof is a volume of plant so thoroughly packed that it behaves like a substance. Stems thick as a wrist, stems thin as wire, aerial roots hanging in curtains, epiphytes growing on epiphytes, vines that have found a seam and filled it and then found another. There is no gap to put a hand into that is not already full.

It is warm to the touch. All of it. The leaves are blood-warm, the stems warmer, and if you press your palm flat against a big root it feels like the flank of something asleep. The warmth comes from below and from the machinery, and the plants have arranged themselves to harvest it — they lean in toward the pump house, they crowd the pipes, they grow in a density gradient that peaks at the boiler and thins only slightly toward the rim.

They move. Not much, not fast, but on a cycle. Every ninety seconds or so the whole mass lifts slightly — a few centimeters, thousands of leaves at once — and settles again with a sound like a held breath released. Down in the middle you can see the roof of leaves rise and fall against the pump house wall, rise and fall, patient as a sleeper.

## The works

The pump house is the only building and it is barely a building. Brick, two stories originally, roof gone, three walls standing and the fourth fallen inward and colonized. A flywheel stands in the middle of it, taller than a person, still turning — very slowly, a quarter turn per surge, its rim furred with mineral crust and its spokes sleeved in vine. Pipes leave it in four directions and go down into the plant mass, and the plant mass has swallowed them. You can trace a pipe by the ridge of roots that follows it and then the ridge closes over and the pipe is gone and you know it continues because the roots are warmer there.

The boiler is a squat iron cylinder, half-sunk, its rivet seams opened by root growth that has entered it and made itself at home. It is hot. The fire in it is still a fire — you can hear it, a low roar under everything else — but nobody has fed it in a very long time. A sap line has grown into the fuel intake, a fat translucent root that pulses and drips a thin resin into the firebox at a rate the fire seems to expect. The plants feed the machine that warms the plants. The loop is closed and has been closed for decades, and the whole basin is the shape of that loop.

Elsewhere: a footbridge of rusted grate crossing the central pool, half-swallowed, still crossable if you trust it. A valve wheel grown through by a stem so you cannot turn it. A ladder bolted to the wall going up to nothing. The foundations of two other structures, reduced to rectangles of brick under moss. Faint marks — this was a works once, a draining operation, someone's small industry. Now it is a warm organism with a worked skeleton inside it.

## Water and air

The water table is at ankle height nearly everywhere. The central pool is knee-deep, warm, faintly mineral, and it does not stagnate because the pump keeps it moving — a slow circulation, in at one edge, out at another, through the root mass, filtered by it. The water comes out of the leaves. Toward evening, hundreds of leaf tips weep, and the dripping is one of the loudest things here.

The air is saturated. You are wet within a minute of climbing down, and it is not rain, it is simply that the air has no room left for vapor. It condenses on your arms, on the pipes, on the underside of every leaf, and falls. The rock weeps. The brick weeps. There are drip-points everywhere and each has worn its own small hollow.

## The sound

You raise your voice here without meaning to. The fire roars low. The flywheel groans and the groan travels up the pipes and comes out of the plant mass as a shifting rustle. The boiler surges every ninety seconds with a bass note you feel in the sternum, and in the trough between surges there is the dripping — thousands of separate ticks and plops, dense as static — and the hiss of steam escaping through a leaf-lung somewhere, and the wet creak of stems swelling. It is a working noise. It is the noise of a factory that has been persuaded to be an animal.

## What lives here

Almost nothing but the plants. A pale isopod, blind, lives in the warm runoff channels and comes out in numbers when the boiler surges, and that is the whole of the animal life worth naming. There is a fly that breeds in the steam and hangs in the wet air in small drifting clouds near the pump house. Nothing larger. No birds come down into the basin. It is too loud, too warm, too full.

## The rule of it

The place runs on two clocks. The fast one is ninety seconds — surge, lift, settle, drip. The slow one is the season, or the decade: the boiler crusting, the pipes closing, the roots thickening by a millimeter a year, the pool silting, the walls slumping a little further toward a shape they will reach long after anyone is watching. Nothing here is in a hurry and nothing here is idle.

Stand on the rim before you climb down and the strangeness of it is clear in a way it will not be once you are inside. It looks like a garden that was left alone with a machine, and the two of them came to an understanding.
