# The Choir Vault

There is no outside here, only the vault, and the vault is the whole of the world.

It is a room — that is the honest word — though "room" implies walls you could walk past and a door you could leave by. The Choir Vault is a cavity roughly the size of a threshing floor, pressed into the interior of a mass that extends in every direction farther than anyone has measured, because the only measuring tool is the eye and the eye meets woven fiber everywhere it turns. The cavity is not carved. It was *left*, the way a potter leaves the hollow of a bowl, and then it was furnished, endlessly, over a span of time so long that the furnishings have gone soft and gray at their edges and settled into one another like old cushions.

Every surface is made. There is no stone, no soil, no raw ground beneath the feet — the floor is a matting of bundled stems, pale as oat straw, compressed by their own long weight into something like felt over boards. The walls are braided: cords of plant matter, thumb-thick, twisted and re-twisted into cables, the cables knotted into panels, the panels lashed to ribs that arch up and over and close the ceiling. The material is dry. It has always been dry. It has the color of paper, of old rope, of the inside of a wasp's nest, and it is cool to the touch the way shade is cool — not cold, just absent of heat, holding its temperature the way a cellar holds its dark.

The density is what overwhelms. There is no air here that is not occupied. Every span between ribs is packed with made things — spindles, pods, hanging bundles, capsules of woven rind, dried gourds strung on twine, knots within knots, little cradles of splint that hold nothing, shelves of pleached canes crowded with objects whose purpose has been forgotten by everyone including the objects. You cannot see through the walls; you cannot see the wall, only the accumulation in front of it. The only open volume is the narrow column of standing room at the center, and even that is strung across at head-height with guy-lines of waxed grass, taut and humming. To move is to brush against a hundred presences. Everything is adjacent. Everything is touching. The place is a sentence with no spaces in it.

And it is *bright*. This is the first thing that unsettles a visitor: the light has no source you can find. The fibers themselves are the source — or rather, they are translucent, and the vast made mass around the cavity passes light through its own substance as if the whole structure were a lantern with a fire somewhere deep in it, so the walls glow a pale, even, honeyed white. There are no shadows with edges, only a general softness where objects overlap. The brightness does not vary. It is not daylight; daylight changes. It is a light like the light in a sealed jar of honey held to a window — the same at every hour because there are no hours.

And it is *loud*. The vault sings. This, too, is a property of the construction: the guy-lines and braided cables are strung under tension, and the tension is held in balance by the living things — the few living things — that remain. In the seams, in the cracks between panels, in the chinks where one bundle rests on another, grow thin pale shoots: runners of a creeping vine, no thicker than wire, that have rooted in the packed fiber and spend their whole existence pulling. They pull inward. The structure pushes outward. The result is a standing tension, and the tension has a voice — a continuous chord, low and sustained, that fills the cavity from every direction at once. It is not wind; there is no wind. It is the sound of the whole made thing being held in place by the grip of its few green tenants, and because the grip never tightens and never loosens, the chord never changes. It has one note with many overtones, a drone like a bowed string heard from inside the instrument. It is loud enough that speech would be difficult and silence impossible. You do not get used to it. You merely stop noticing that you haven't.

The vine is the only vegetation, and it is sparse — a lace of it at the base of the walls, a few runners climbing the ribs, a puff of pale lichen on a cradle of splint. Nothing flowers. Nothing fruits. The vines do not seem to grow so much as *hold*, and it is possible that they are the builders, or the descendants of the builders, or the only surviving part of a construction process that was never a process at all but a slow mutual arrangement between plants that grip and fibers that yield. There are no animals. There are no insects in the gourds, no mites in the matting, no burrowers in the felt. The vault is empty of everything but the made and the holding.

And nothing happens. That is the deepest strangeness of it, deeper than the sourceless light or the sourceless chord. The Choir Vault is in equilibrium — not the restless equilibrium of a thing maintained by constant effort, but a settled one, a balance that was reached long ago and has not been seriously disturbed since. The fibers have weathered into their own state; the knots have gone soft; the runners have found their tension; the light and the sound are fixed points. There is no water to seep, no frost to split, no rot because there is no damp. The erosion here is not decay but *settling* — the slow, finished accommodation of a hundred thousand made objects to one another, like a sleeper who has finally found the position. It will not change tomorrow. It did not change yesterday. The "yesterday" is not a meaningful unit here; there is only the chord, holding.

A visitor stands in the narrow column at the center, brushing guy-lines, in the honeyed light that comes from nowhere, in the loud and changeless chord, surrounded on all sides by the packed and patient abundance of a construction that no one remembers making and no one tends and that has no reason to ever come apart — and understands, after a while, that they are the only dynamic thing in the vault, the only gradient, the only change, and that the vault is waiting, politely and indefinitely, for them to leave so that it can be still again.
