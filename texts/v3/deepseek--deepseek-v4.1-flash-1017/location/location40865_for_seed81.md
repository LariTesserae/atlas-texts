# The Sinterworks

You come up onto it from below, and the first thing is the warmth on your face, and the second is that the air is thicker than air should be at this height. The plateau is a shoulder of dark bedrock — not quite basalt, something older and more streaked, veined with pale mineral seams that catch the little light there is — and it tilts, gently, everywhere, so that nothing is quite level and every surface you might walk on has been cut or worn flat in small patches, like the land's own memory of being stood on.

It is high here. The sky is a lid of grey-violet that never fully opens, a smear of sun somewhere behind it that gives you direction but no shadow worth the name. What light there is comes from the ground as much as the sky: the stone is warm. Not hot to the touch, not at first — warm the way a stone is warm when something has been lying on it, except nothing has, and the warmth doesn't fade. It comes up from below, from the slow fever of the rock beneath, and where the seams are widest you can see the heat's work in the air itself: a shivering column, a distortion, a place where the horizon won't sit still.

## The sweating

Nothing grows here. There is no green, no lichen, no rust-coloured scurf of anything alive. The surfaces are mineral and they are wet, and that is the whole of it.

The water does not fall as weather, mostly. It comes up. The plateau exhales — through fissures, through vent-cracks widened by centuries of their own breath, through the old drill-holes and the newer ones — and the vapour is hot and heavy and smells of iron and something sharper, like a struck flint, and it condenses on everything cool enough to hold it. It beads on the undersides of stone ledges. It gathers on pipes and runs down them in slow threads. It sheets the floors, collects in every hollow that was ever worn into the rock, and each of those hollows is a shallow pool the colour of weak tea, or of nothing at all, with a ring of pale crust around its rim where the water has been and left its load behind.

The rain, when it comes — and it comes every few days, sudden and warm and heavy — is almost a relief, because at least it is coming down, and you can hear where it lands. It doesn't cool anything. It just adds itself to the general wetness and then stops, and the steam comes back up through the puddles to meet it.

## The works

The building is everywhere here and the people are not.

It's a town in the way that a clock is a town: dense, organised, layered, every part addressing every other part, and almost none of it for standing inside. There are stacks — not many, and none of them smoking hard, just breathing a thin heat-shimmer — and condenser sheds with their louvres half-open, and long low halls of riveted plate that house engines you can hear but not see. There are pipes. There are so many pipes. They come out of the rock in bundles, run along the ground on squat feet, elbow up the faces of buildings, cross the open ground overhead in spans worn pale by condensation dripping from their low points, and every one of them is beaded and dripping and ticking with the small sounds of metal cooling a degree at a time. In places the pipework has been redone and redone, so you find a fat old main with three generations of thinner line strapped along its belly, and the rock beneath worn into a gutter by two hundred years of the same drip in the same place.

Between the structures there is enormous emptiness. You can walk for ten minutes between one shed and the next and pass nothing but ledge, puddle, pipe, the pale crust flourishing in a seep. The town is not crowded; it is *spread*, and the spread is mostly unwalked. Some of the halls you look into are the size of market squares and hold one machine and a lot of clean wet floor and a lamp on a long cable, swinging slightly, lighting a circle of nothing. The population, whatever it is, is not large and does not fill its own architecture. You'll see a figure far off, moving between buildings with a lantern, and then not see anyone for an hour.

## The crop

Here is what the place is for.

The vapour carries minerals up out of the deep rock — salts, silicates, things that crystallise only reluctantly and only on the right surface at the right warmth. The Sinterworks is a machine for giving them the right surface. The whole town is a set of angled plates and baffles and hanging lattices, arranged by temperature and slope and how much drip they receive, and on them the minerals bloom. Slowly. A crust a year thick in the best places. Pale green fan-crystals furring a copper plate until it looks upholstered. Amber-coloured spires in a runnel that is three degrees warmer than the one beside it, and in that one, nothing but a glassy skin. White cauliflower nodules on the underside of a beam, growing downward, away from a light they've never had.

Tenders walk the lattices with a hooked rod and a lamp, turning plates, scraping one surface clean so another can start, breaking off a crop of crystal into a sack. It looks like gardening and it is gardening, in a way that makes the word strange. Nothing here is alive, and yet the place is under cultivation — hedged, pruned, weeded, harvested, the whole plateau a field of chemistry coaxed into rows.

## The rules of the world

- **Water arrives from below.** It upwells as vapour, condenses on anything cooler, and returns. Pools are always forming and always draining into the cracks. The water table is the air.
- **The rock is warm and stays warm.** Nothing dries. Cloth stays damp. Metal is always sweating. You do not sweat, exactly, because the air won't take it — you just become wet, and stay that way.
- **Growth is mineral.** If something appears to spread, fur, crust, or colonise, it is crystal, and it is following a temperature gradient. Do not mistake it for life. It will not respond to you except by growing where you stand, if you stand long enough and warm enough.
- **Vents open and close.** The plateau's breath is not steady. Some days a fissure you crossed yesterday is a warm dry groove; some days it hisses and will not let you near. The works adjust — valves turned by hand, louvres opened, a whole run of lines shut down and another brought up — and the town's activity is mostly this: a slow constant argument with the ground about where the vapour should go.

## Sound and the few others

Mostly quiet. The base layer is drip — not one drip but thousands, at every pitch, a soft irregular rain that never stops and never resolves into rhythm. Under it the low hum of the engines in the long halls, felt more than heard, and the sigh of a vent, and the tick of pipe metal. Once every few minutes something knocks, far off, in a way that could be machinery and could be stone settling. Voices carry strangely: you can hear two tenders talking a hundred metres away and not make out a word, because the air is so wet and so still.

Few animals, and none of them bold. Small blind things the workers call sump-crawlers live in the warm runoff — pale, flat, about the length of a finger, feeling their way along pipe undersides for the crust. They are the only predators and the only prey, and they are slow. Birds come over rarely, high, and do not land; there is nothing here for them but wet stone and their own reflection in a puddle, and they seem to know it. Once in a long while something larger crosses the plateau's edge at dusk, and the tenders will tell you what it was and disagree about it.

## Out past the town

The works thin out and the bedrock goes on. It tilts away in great shallow planes, streaked and glossy and beaded, marked with the old courses of water that have found their comfortable channels and will keep them. There are hollows out there big enough to be called lakes and shallow enough to walk across, their bottoms crunching with a crust that has been forming since before the town. Fissures breathe. A warm mist stands in the low ground in the dim hours and thins by whatever passes for noon.

It is not empty because it is barren. It is empty because almost nothing has any reason to be there, and the things that do — the vapour, the crust, the slow tilt of water into stone — are not company. You could walk out for an hour and find a single footprint, your own, filling with condensation behind you.

And above it all the lid of grey-violet, and the smear of sun, and the ground warm underfoot, breathing its slow breath up into the wet air, and the whole plateau quietly crystallising, a millimetre at a time, in the dark.
