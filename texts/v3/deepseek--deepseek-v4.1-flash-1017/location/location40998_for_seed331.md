# The Kettle

A shallow bowl in the last of the hills, a few hundred paces across, that you can walk end to end before your tea goes cold. The rim is low and rounded and soft with moss — no rock face, no cliff, just ground that has been worn down so long it has forgotten how to be steep. Beyond the rim the hills go on, equally tired, equally low, and then the haze takes them.

The floor of the bowl sits ankle-deep in water in most places. It is a warm water, blood-warm, and it does not sit still: it seeps. It creeps up through the moss in beads, gathers in the low pans, drains away through channels that have shifted a finger's width overnight. After the afternoon rain — and there is almost always an afternoon rain, brief and warm and soundless in its falling — the whole floor becomes a mirror in pieces, and the light comes up off it in a way that makes the air feel lit from beneath. Between the pans, the ground is a wet mat of liverwort and knee-high fern and pale grass, and it takes your footprint and holds it, slowly filling, so that a day later you can find where you stood by the little wells your feet left behind.

The vegetation is not a thicket. It comes in islands. Two great trees stand in the water, old and low-branched and so wide that their roots have become walls of root and knuckle, each one its own small country of shade. Around them, scattered: a reed bed here, a fern hollow there, wide bare pans of wet clay between, and it is the emptiness that makes the lushness legible — you see each clump as a thing, not as a crowd.

Everything is ancient in the way of a hand worn smooth. The stone is rinded and pitted, the edges of the pools are rounded down to nothing, the hills have settled into their final shapes and will not change much now except by imperceptible degrees. This is not a place in its making. It is a place that finished making itself a long time ago and has been quietly, comfortably ageing ever since. Nothing here is sharp. There is almost nothing left to erode.

And yet it is busy. It is more alive than anywhere you have been.

The fauna is overwhelming — not in sound, but in presence. The pools are thick with small things: not fish, exactly, but a kind of translucent swimmer, finger-length, that moves in soft clouds and turns as one and grazes the algae in visible scallops. The moss is full of insects, glassy and slow in the warmth, hanging in the air in columns above the water so dense you walk through them and they part and close behind you. Small mammals — many of them, of several kinds, some hopping, some slinking — move between the islands of vegetation, crossing the open pans quickly and with a practiced wariness, because something hunts them; you will see the hunter only as a change in the light on the water, or a disturbance in the reeds. Birds come down to drink in the late afternoon, dozens of kinds, none of them calling. There are moths out in the daylight, which should not be, and they are the most numerous thing of all, pale and broad-winged and utterly silent.

Because the sound is nearly nothing. That is the strangeness of it. A place this crowded should roar, and it whispers. The warm wet air swallows noise — it comes back at you flattened, the edges taken off. The reeds move without rustling. The birds open their beaks and nothing much comes out. You hear your own breathing. You hear the water seeping, a sound like a held breath let out very slowly, all the time, everywhere. Once in a while a low hum, felt more than heard, from somewhere under the moss.

That hum is the other thing.

Scattered across the floor, half-sunk and mostly overgrown, are marks of a making. A single structure on the eastern rim — a squat, low shouldered thing of a pale material that is neither stone nor metal and does not weather — with a doorless opening that admits the light and shows, inside, a floor of the same material, dry. It is warm in there. It is always warm in there. Faint lines run out from it under the moss, and out from other places too, and out from nothing in particular: a threadwork in the soil, finer than root, that you can find by feel if you press your palm flat and wait. It answers. Not language — a small pulse, a shifting of the warmth, water moving somewhere it was not moving before. The channels that shift overnight shift because of it. The water is kept at its level. The pools are kept warm. Something under this tired old bowl is still doing a job, patiently, with no one to do it for, and has been doing it long enough to be mistaken for geology.

The light is bright and diffuse and constant through the day — the humid air a lens, the wet floor a second sun. Colors come up out of the ground: the green of the moss is almost luminous, the clay pans are a pale gold, the moths are the color of milk. At dusk the light goes rose and stays rose a long time before it dims, and the hum under the moss gets very slightly stronger, and the whole kettle seems to settle, the way a sleeping animal settles, the water still seeping, the moths still going, the invisible hunter still out there somewhere in the reeds, and no sound at all but your own breath and the slow breathing of the ground.
