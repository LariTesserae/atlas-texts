# The Kettle

The sound arrives before the light does, and long before you can see anything at all. It comes through the water as pressure first — a low turning note, thirty cycles or so, that you feel in the sternum before you hear it. Then the rest resolves out of it: a crackle at the edges, like frying, which is cavitation coming off something large and moving slowly; a ticking, thousands of small ticks, not quite in rhythm; and under everything a hiss, steady, the sound of an intake drawing.

The water is cold. Not the cold of ice, but the cold of a basement in spring — cool enough that your hands go vague after a while, cool enough that the warm places announce themselves. It is very slightly green and very slightly turbid, and it tastes of iron and lime. There is no surface above you. There is a dome, and the dome is the ceiling, and above the dome there is more water, and above that, according to a stencil on a plate near the entry lock, half eaten by corrosion, there was once air. Nobody here remembers air. It is a rumor the building tells about itself.

The hall is a cathedral, and it was built to be one, in the way that engine halls are: a cylinder forty meters tall, ribbed, with a gallery running around it at two levels and stairs spiraling up the wall in a long slow helix. Down the center, in a ring, stand the columns — but they are not columns. They are pipe stacks: downcomers and risers and valve towers, flanged, bolted, braced, twelve of them, rising from the deck to the dome and vanishing into it. Between them there is a great deal of water and very little else. That is the first thing the place teaches you. It is large, and it is mostly empty, and the emptiness is not a failure of the design. The emptiness is where the water goes.

Everything here is made. There is no rock under the deck, no silt on it that a river put there. The floor is a machine deck, perforated plate over channels, and it is buried to the ankle in a fine grey flour — corroded metal, shed scale, precipitated lime, the slow dander of the machinery — and if you sifted that flour you would not find one grain that had not been manufactured or excreted by something manufactured. The walls are poured and formed. The bolts are bolts. Even the sediment is a product.

And all of it is old. Not abandoned — old. There is a difference, and the Kettle is the difference. The water has been moving through this hall for a very long time, and it has finished with it. Every edge that could be rounded has been rounded. The flanges have gone soft, the bolt heads are nubs, the stair treads are worn into shallow dishes, the gratings are worn down to wire. Where the current runs fast, metal has been planed away to a smooth hydrodynamic profile, like a stone in a river. Where the current lingers, mineral has built up instead, in crusts and lumps and slow fat collars around every pipe. So the hall has two kinds of age on it at once: the polished kind and the furred kind. Nothing in here is on its way to becoming something else. It has arrived. It will be like this for a long while yet.

The light is dim and it is made, too. A few lamp housings survive along the gallery, thick glass, pressure-rated, and their glass has been filmed over by algae so the light comes out greenish and in shafts, and the shafts are full of drifting matter. One lamp flickers on a long slow cycle, brightening over a minute and dimming over a minute, and the animals have learned it; the flicker is a kind of clock. Another is dead. Between the lamps there is a great deal of dark, and the dark is where the sound does the work that light would do elsewhere.

Because here, sound is sight. You can hear the twelve columns. Each one has a rind of filter-feeders crusted on it, thumbnail-sized, opening and closing in the current, and the crust ticks. The ticks come back off the dome a half-second late and slightly changed, and by the returning ticks you can tell how far the dome is, and where the nearest stack stands, and whether something large has moved between you and it. The hall is loud. The impeller at the center — five blades, each the size of a car, worn to the shape of river stones — turns once every forty seconds, and every forty seconds a pressure wave crosses the hall and every soft-bodied thing in it flexes and recovers. The blade tips crackle. The intake hisses. The relays in the wall cabinets tick and clack, still switching, still sequencing, though what they sequence has not needed sequencing for a century.

The intake draws from the dome and the impeller pushes it down the sumps and the return comes back up the outer ring, so the water in the hall is always moving, slowly, in a closed loop, down at the center and up at the walls. It is not a current you would notice standing still, but it is a current, and everything here is oriented to it. The pale ribbons growing in the outfall are bent downstream. The grazers swim into it when they travel and sideways to it when they feed. Nothing drifts free for long. If you stop moving, the loop will have an opinion about where you go.

The life is abundant and it is scattered. This is not a crowding. You can swim a long way between presences. A shoal of twelve small pale fish is a crowd, and they move from stack to stack in loose order, grazing the algae films, of which there are few — a fur on the lit glass, the ribbons in the outfall, one small pale meadow of them in the corner where the flickering lamp has been burning for a very long time, which is the only dense green in the place. The rest of the abundance is small and it is on the surfaces: the ticking crust, the grazers, the scavengers working the flour on the deck, the things that live in the warm housings — because the motors are a degree or two warmer than the water, and there is one bearing housing near the north gallery where a species lives that lives nowhere else, a knot of pale limbs around a warm cylinder, and they have been there so long that the housing has grown a lip of mineral to shelter them. Heat is a resource here. The warmest few square meters are the most crowded.

Something large passes through every few minutes: a blind grazer the size of a person, slow, humming a note a fifth below the impeller so that the two sounds beat against each other and the whole hall throbs. It never hurries and nothing harries it. The hunter is thin and it does not stalk — it waits where the machinery is loudest and lets the noise cover its approach, and it takes the small pale fish when they cross the dark between two lamps. That is the whole of the violence here, and it is not much, and it is over quickly. The ticks resume.

The weather is the valves. Every so often — there is no day to measure it by, only the lamps and the impeller — a valve cycles somewhere below and a pulse of colder water comes up through the deck, and everything in the hall turns to face it, the crust opening wide, the grazers lifting, the ribbons flattening, and then it passes and the hall settles back into its long hum. That is the storm. That is the season.

Beyond the hall there are pressure doors, and the doors are shut, and there is a corridor behind them, and beyond the corridor more of the same or nothing, and it has been a long time since anyone opened one. The machinery does not need anyone. Little crawlers work the rails and the flanges, scraping and oiling, keeping the loop going, indifferent to the fact that the loop no longer accomplishes anything except itself. They maintain the hall the way the hall maintains them: without intention, and very well.

It is not a sad place. It is a finished place, which is different. The water is cold, the columns tick, the great worn impeller comes around and pushes its pressure through you, and the blind thing hums, and the flickering lamp brightens, and dims, and brightens.
