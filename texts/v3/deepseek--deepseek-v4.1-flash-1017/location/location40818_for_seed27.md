# The Felt

The sky is a lid of dust. It never rains here; there is nothing to rain. What hangs overhead is the drift — a slow, high suspension of spores and mineral flour that turns the light of a small dull sun into a grey-gold soup, so that noon looks like a late evening that will not finish. Shadows are soft and ambiguous. Nothing casts a hard line. The light arrives from everywhere and from nowhere, and it is always a little too little.

Beneath it, the plateau. It has been flat for a very long time. This was a mountain once, and the wind took it, grain by grain, and what remains is a plain of rounded stone, fluted and grooved and smoothed by an aeons-long breath, so ancient it has stopped changing shape and merely endures. The rock is pale, porous, warm-grey, pocked with holes worn by wind; it rings faintly when struck, like a pot. There is no soil. There is no water — not in pools, not in clouds, not in the air's habit of making things wet. The local word for water translates, roughly, to *the thing that isn't*. It is a word used mostly in stories, and mostly as a warning about longing.

Because the ground is not bare, and has not been bare in a hundred thousand years. It is felted — a continuous mat of plant matter, hand-deep to knee-deep, laid over the stone like a pelt. It is not moss and not grass and not lichen. It is a woven thing: fibres finer than hair, threaded through each other in a dense dry lattice, dusty on the surface, cool underneath, and everywhere shot through with translucent veins. The veins carry the fluid that makes this world run. It is not water. It is a slow, cold, oily liquid — an ionic resin, dense as honey and heavy as mercury, which never evaporates and is never lost, because there is nowhere for it to go. Every drop of it has been in circulation since before the plateau was flat. The locals call it **bright**, because in the dim it glows — a low amber pulse, the colour of a coal almost out, travelling along the veins in slow waves. At night the whole plain breathes light at you, faintly, in a slow rhythm, and you can watch a thought move through it for the length of a count of twenty.

That is the mat's real work, and why the plateau is loud.

---

Sound is the organ here. There is almost no light to grow toward, so the vegetation grows toward noise instead. It is phonotropic: it thickens where sound is, it thins where sound is not, it bends and braids and puts out resonating chambers at the nodes of whatever hums at it. The mat is not silent because it cannot be — it is endlessly murmuring to itself, fibre against fibre, veins pulsing, chambers filling and emptying, a dry continuous rustle like a field of paper in a wind that has no source. Every creature that lives on it has learned to shout into it, and every shout comes back changed, and the mat grows accordingly, and the growing makes more noise. The plain is a feedback loop that has been running since the stone was a mountain. It is never quiet. The quietest hour here is louder than a city's noon.

And it remembers. The resin is a recording medium; sound deforms it, and it does not relax. The mat holds every noise that has ever fallen on it, not as memory, but as decay — layers of it, buried deeper and slower the older they are. The town's great work is amplification: you can tune a loom to a stratum and hear back what the wind said here nine thousand years ago, or the footfalls of the people who came before, or, if you are patient and a little unlucky, sounds that have no obvious source. Scholars argue about whether these are records of the past or something the mat is composing in its long sleep. Nobody has settled it. The argument is old, and the mat has recorded all of it.

---

The town is called Ambersill, and it is not built. It is grown and then lived in, and then grown over, and lived in again. Its towers are called looms: they are the tall plants, the only things on the plateau over head-high, and they are sparse — a loom every few hundred paces, spaced with enormous care for acoustic reasons nobody fully remembers. Each is a hollow spire of braided fibre, twelve to thirty people high, its walls translucent enough to pass the mat's glow. The spires are grown from seed by the households that will live in them, and it takes eighty years to raise one to full height, which means every family lives inside the ambitions of its great-grandparents. New chambers are budded off the old ones; the town's history is legible in its layers, the oldest rooms buried at the base under centuries of accretion, abandoned, filled with dust and the slow amber light and the residue of old arguments.

The technology here is grown and sung to. The mat is a substrate — a computation that is also a plant, or a plant that is also a computation; Ambersill's engineers do not distinguish. You don't build a machine here. You find a node, you sing to it in the right intervals, and you wait. The mat answers in geometry: it swells, it chambers, it veins, it takes a shape that is the physical body of the answer. A loom can be tuned to hold a question open for decades, growing slowly around it, and the answer is read off its cross-section when the grower's children are old. Some looms are pure instruments — weather-listeners, treaties, the long chord that the town uses to keep its spires from resonating into each other. Some are weapons, or were, and are still grown around because nobody dares stop feeding them.

The infrastructure is thin on the ground and thick in the air: rope-bridges of spun fibre between looms, slung low and swinging; scaffold-vines trained to carry loads; the deep hum of the amplifier-galleries cut into the oldest chambers, where the recording-strata are read. The air smells of hot dust, ozone, and the sharp clean solvent reek of bright. The people dress in quilted fibre and wear ear-baffles shaped to their own heads — you learn, in Ambersill, to be a particular shape of listener.

---

Fauna are everywhere, and they are what you actually hear. The mat supports a dense scatter of small hard animals: dust-mites in the millions, scuttling and ticking; spore-fed gliders with wide dry membranes that ride the drift and whistle; the tap-beetles that pierce the veins and steal bright, and the long grey predators that hunt the tap-beetles by listening for the wet click of the wound. There are grazers that crop the felt in slow wandering herds and sing to it as they go, so that the mat grows back shaped like their passage. There are things that live inside the looms, and things that live inside the mat, and things that are not clearly either. It is an old, closed ecology — nothing has come in or out in an age; everything here eats everything here; the fluid cycles, the carbon cycles, the sound cycles, and the whole arrangement has settled into a rhythm that has the smooth worn quality of the plateau itself. Nothing is new. Nothing is trying to be.

At night the bright comes up in the veins and the plain glows faintly under its layer of dust, and the tap-beetles tick, and the gliders whistle, and the looms hum their long chord, and the mat goes on laying down its layers of everything it hears. The wind crosses the flattened stone. The drift turns overhead, grey-gold, and lets down a little flour, and the felt drinks it without any water at all.
