# The Loft

**Position.** You reach it from below, which takes days, and the last of those days is spent in cloud that never becomes rain. The massif is a stub — a mountain that has been eroding for longer than mountains are usually given, worn by wind and frost and something slower until every peak and pinnacle has been planed away and what is left is a stump of rock a few hundred meters across and, by any measure that matters, enormously high. Its sides are fluted and rounded like the shoulders of something very old asleep. You do not climb a peak to get here. You climb the smoothed remains of one, and then you step onto a summit that is nearly flat.

**Terrain.** The top is a shallow kettle, a bowl a few hundred meters across with a lip of wind-carved stone around it. Erosion here is not ongoing so much as finished; the stone has settled into the shape it will wear for a very long time. Everything is rounded. Nothing has a sharp edge. The bowl's floor is terraced — not by geology, by hands, over centuries — into shallow steps no higher than a knee, and each step is packed with growing things.

**No water.** This is the thing you notice last, because it is the thing that is missing and everything seems to be thriving anyway. There are no streams, no pools, no dew in the cups of leaves, nothing that runs. The air stands thick and warm and heavy with suspended moisture, so fine it never gathers — it settles as a film on cold stone and is drunk directly from the surface by everything that lives here. The plants have no roots worth the name for water; their leaves are waxy, dusted, dimpled with pores that open onto the vapor and take it in whole. The animals lick stone. The people press cloth against the shaded sides of rocks overnight and wring it into their mouths at dawn. To spill is a small crime. Nobody has ever seen a river, and the word for river has drifted, in use, to mean *a thing that is imagined.*

**Vegetation.** Lush is not a word you would have guessed at this height, on this stone, with no water to speak of, and yet the terraces are close-packed and green to the knee, to the chest in places — low, dense, wind-shorn stuff with fat pads and soft grey-green blades and a great deal of moss. The moss is the foundation of everything: it holds the soil, which is not soil but a centuries-deep mat of its own dead, and it wicks vapor out of the air with a patience that looks like stillness. There are shrubs grown into the lee of every stone. There are two or three trees, old and short and twisted flat by wind, and they are each individually known and named, and there are rules about them.

**Settlement.** People live here. Not many — a few hundred in a place the size of a cathedral floor, packed shoulder to shoulder and stacked, because the terraces carry walkways and the walkways carry roofs and the roofs carry more walkways. It is dense the way a hive is dense and it has the same logic: everything close to everything, because the outside of the bowl is wind and the drop. The construction is stone and stone and stone, dry-laid, no mortar, corbel and lintel, with everything else — doors, screens, ladders, rain-catchers that catch nothing, rope, tool hafts — cut and shaped by hand from shrub-wood and moss-fiber and bone. There is a little metal, soft and scavenged, hammered cold. It is a town in the sense that a town is an organized answer to a place; it is not large.

**Fauna.** The bowl is loud with animals. They are small and everywhere and many — insects in the moss by the thousand per square meter, things like crickets and things like beetles and things without a good comparison, all of them calling; colonies of flying things that nest in the corbels and the hollow lintels; crawlers under the walkways; a kind of furry slow climber the size of a hand that lives in the trees and is not eaten, by custom. There are birds, or bird-shaped things, that ride the updraft along the lip and scream. Density is the rule: presences per volume, near-touching, layered — you are never alone in the bowl and never out of earshot of something alive.

**Sound.** It is loud. The bowl is a resonator. The stone itself hums, a low note that rises and falls with the day's warmth and is not a sound anyone here finds strange — it is the sound of the place being the place. Over that, the animals, layered and constant. Over that, the wind working the lip, which is a fluting roar when it comes. People talk over all of it without noticing, and their speech has grown clipped and hard-consonanted to survive the noise.

**Light and air.** Dim. The bowl sits most days under a lid of high overcast, and the sun comes through as a bright smear that never resolves. Midday is a grey-gold dusk. The colors are all muted and close in value, moss-green on stone-grey on lichen-yellow. At night the overcast holds the town's own small lights down close over it like a lid on a pot.

**Rules of the world.** The stone is warm — heat comes up through the floor of the bowl from somewhere deep and has done so for as long as anyone has counted, which is the reason for the warmth, the reason for the moss, the reason the vapor gathers here at all rather than blowing past. The cycle is slow and patient: the warmth breathes out over a day, the vapor thickens and thins with it, the moss opens and closes, the hum rises and falls, the animals call and go quiet and call. Nothing here is in a hurry. There is a rhythm but no seasons worth the name; nobody remembers a year being much different from the last.

**Time.** The whole place has the quality of a thing that reached its final form and stayed. The mountain eroded to a stump and stopped. The town was built and rebuilt and built again on the same terraces until the walking surfaces are worn into shallow troughs and the door-sills are dished by feet. The moss is deep. The trees are old. Change happens, but you would have to stay a very long while to see it, and the people here measure things in *the year the upper walk gave way* and *before the last good hum*, and they are not being poetic. That is simply the resolution at which time is legible.

You stand on the lip at what passes for evening, warm stone underfoot, green to the ankle in every direction, the hum coming up through your bones, no water anywhere, everything drinking the air, and the bowl below you dense and loud and old — and it is hard not to feel that you have arrived not at a height but at an ending, and that it is thriving.
