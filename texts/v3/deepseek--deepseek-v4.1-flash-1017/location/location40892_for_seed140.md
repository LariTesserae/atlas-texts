# The Kettle

There is a mountain that has almost stopped being a mountain.

You come up through cloud for a long time — a warm cloud, the kind that clings — and then the cloud does not end, it merely becomes the ground. The summit is a broad shallow dome of stone, worn down over an unimaginable tenure to something like the crown of a skull, and it is only as large as a cathedral floor, or a hamlet, or a good-sized grove: a place you could walk across in twenty minutes if you could see. You cannot see. The dark here is not the dark of night, which has direction and promise, but the dark of being inside something — a closed, warm, breathing interior, the dark of the inside of a mouth.

The stone underfoot is old in a way that is hard to hold in the mind. It has been rained on for so long that the rain has won. Edges have gone. There are no cliffs left, only the suggestion of a slope. The rock is soft as chalk in places, and where you put your hand down it comes away damp and gritty and smells of iron and old cellars. It is warm. Everything here is warm. The warmth comes up through the floor rather than down from above, so the whole place feels like the flank of a sleeping animal, and you find yourself walking carefully, as if not to wake it.

**Water.** Water is everywhere and it does not fall — it condenses. The air is at the exact temperature where it gives up its water onto any surface slightly cooler than itself, which means that every stone, every leaf, every rail and remnant up here is beaded, dripping, furred with moisture. Springs come up through the moss in slow pulses, not jets but wellings, and each one has made itself a shallow basin of travertine, a rim of cream-colored stone built up over centuries the way a shell is built. The basins overflow into one another by hairline channels. The whole summit is a system of shallow cups spilling into shallow cups, and the sound of it is a constant small conversation of water finding the next level down. There are pools the size of a bath and pools the size of a coin. Some are hot enough to steam; the steam does not rise far before it becomes part of the general wetness. You can put your hand in one and feel a heat that comes from a kilometer below you, from a body of rock still holding the planet's earliest warmth, and it is a strange thing to be warmed by something so old and so far down while standing in the dark on the roof of the world.

**Green.** The plants do not care about the dark. They are pale — bone-white, urine-yellow, the color of things kept in a cupboard — and they are thick. Moss in cushions you sink into to the ankle. Ferns that unroll slowly and feel along the rock with the patience of something that has never once been in a hurry. There are mats of soft growth that are not quite moss and not quite anything else, which peel up in sheets and leave a clean wet stone beneath. Because there is no light, there is no competition for light, and so the vegetation grows in a way that is almost obscene in its ease — not reaching, not straining, just *thickening*, spreading sideways, filling every gap, climbing over the ruins and over itself. It smells green and sweet and faintly of ammonia. Where you step, it exhales.

Some of the larger growths are the size of a person and hollow, and if you put your ear to them you hear the slow gurgle of water moving through their interiors, up and around and out. They are plumbing as much as they are plant. They filter the air for you. They make it breathable. Everything here is in the business of moving water from one place to another, and the vegetation is simply the softest part of that machinery.

**The loudness.** You would think a dark, warm, wet, empty place would be silent. It is not. The sound is enormous, and it comes from small things.

The fauna is abundant and it is everywhere and it is nearly all of it invisible. Insects in the moss in numbers that make the ground feel alive underfoot — a continuous fine shrilling, a thousand small saws. Frogs in every basin, and their calls are the dominant note: a rising metallic *tink* that repeats and repeats and is answered from three directions, so the whole summit is a slow argument of frogs. Something larger moves in the ferns at intervals — you never see it, you only hear the ferns close behind it, and it is unhurried. There are things in the pools that surface and take the air with a wet smack. There are things that fly, and you know them by the pressure of air on your cheek and by a papery ticking that stops abruptly when they land on you.

And underneath all of it, the rock itself is loud. The dome rings. When the water shifts in a channel, the stone carries the sound down and around and gives it back changed. There is a note in this place — a low, wavering, almost vocal hum that comes out of the warm rock and the moving water together — and the fauna have built their calls around it, so that the whole summit sounds like an orchestra tuning to a drone that has been sounding for a very long time. The loudness is not noise. It is a dense, layered, ongoing *occupation*. You are not alone here and you are never given the impression that you are.

The emptiness is the strange part, then. There is almost nothing per volume. The space between one creature and the next is vast; the summit holds perhaps a few hundred kilograms of animal life spread across all that warm wet dark, and the rest is moss and water and air. You can walk for minutes without touching anything but moss. The density of sound disguises a tremendous hollowness. It is a place crowded with voices and nearly vacant of bodies.

**What was built.** Scattered across the dome, half-swallowed, are marks of something that was here.

They are small and they are few and they do not add up to a settlement. A ring of nine posts, waist-high, of a material that is neither metal nor stone and is warm to the touch and slightly too smooth — worn, but worn in a way that suggests they were never sharp. The moss has climbed them to the shoulder. Between them, the ground is flat and clean and the moss will not grow there, and if you stand in the middle of the ring the frogs go quiet. That is the whole of it: the frogs go quiet, and you can hear your own blood, and then after a while they start again. Something in the ring is still doing something. It is not doing it loudly or quickly. It is doing it at the pace of the springs building their rims.

Elsewhere: a single low structure, roofless, the walls a lattice so fine you can put your hand through it and feel nothing but warmth — a mesh of a hundred thousand threads of something that has not corroded and will not, holding a shape that has no structural reason to still be standing. There are three of these across the summit, in a line, and the line points at nothing you can find in the dark. There are objects on the ground — smooth ovals, rods, a thing like a folded hand — that respond to being picked up by becoming slightly warmer, and then, after a while, slightly cooler, as though they had considered something and decided against it. They do not do anything else. They have been considering for a long time.

The tech here is quiet and slow and it does not want anything from you. It is not derelict; dereliction implies abandonment, and this feels more like maintenance — as if the things are still being kept, by something, at a timescale that makes the springs look hasty. The lattice walls keep the rain off nothing. The posts keep a boundary around a circle of clean ground. The ovals warm and cool. There is a sense — and this may be the strangeness in the air, the ammonia and the warmth — that if you came back in a thousand years you would find the moss at exactly the same height on the posts, and the frogs would go quiet when you stood in the ring, and nothing else would have changed at all.

**The rules of the world.** This is a place in its final form, and the final form is: *slow*. Nothing here is arriving or leaving. The mountain is worn to a dome and the dome is in equilibrium with the rain. The springs build their rims at a rate that a human lifetime cannot measure, and the rims are the only thing that grows quickly. The vegetation has stopped competing and simply fills. The fauna is abundant because abundance costs nothing here; the warm wet dark is a place where life is cheap and easy and there is no season to survive. Nothing is being selected for. Nothing is in a hurry to become anything else. The whole summit is a system that has found its resting state and is resting in it, and it will rest in it for as long as the warmth comes up from below.

The one thing that moves is the water. Water is the only thing here with ambition. It condenses, it gathers, it finds the hairline channel, it goes over the rim into the next basin and the next, and eventually it goes off the edge of the dome and down into the cloud, and it does not come back — not for a long time, not until it has been around the world and up through the sky and back into the warm wet air above the summit, where it will condense on a stone and begin again. That circuit is the only clock.

**Atmosphere.** Standing here, in the dark, in the warm, with the water ticking from leaf to leaf and the frogs arguing and the rock humming under it all, you have the feeling of being *inside* something that is very old and entirely indifferent and not at all hostile. It is not a frightening dark. It is a dark like a closed eye. The air is thick enough to feel on the back of your hand, and it tastes of iron and green and warm stone. You cannot see your own feet. You can see nothing at all, and yet the place is so densely *present* — so loud, so wet, so warm, so thoroughly occupied — that the darkness stops reading as absence and starts reading as a material, a soft black fleece laid over everything.

And the smallness of it is the last strangeness: a cathedral floor of ancient rock, high above the world, holding its warmth and its water and its noise and its few quiet artifacts, resting. If you walked off the edge you would fall a long way through warm cloud before you saw anything. But you would not want to. The interesting thing is here, in the dark, at the pace of a spring building a rim, in a place that has finished and is content to have finished and will go on being finished, loudly, for a very long time.
