The sound is what you notice first. It is not a single sound but a continuous, layered roar, as if the mountain itself is a vast instrument playing a chord that never resolves. It is the wind, screaming through the needle-thin gaps of the Whistling Stair, a natural formation of black, glassy rock that spirals up for a thousand feet. It is the deeper, resonant hum of the Vent, a miles-wide caldera whose bottom is lost in a churning sea of cloud and whose rim constantly exhales a low, contrabass thrum that you feel in your teeth and bones. And it is the percussion: the sharp *crack* of frost splitting stone, the distant, thunderous groan of a glacier shifting its weight, the constant, dry clatter of loose scree skittering down impossibly long slopes.

The air is cold, a deep, permanent chill that has nothing to do with the season and everything to do with altitude. It tastes of fractured minerals and ancient, clean ice. Yet the world is damp. It is a paradox held in the thin air. The moisture does not fall as rain but exists as a constant, swirling condensation. It clings to every surface as a fine sheen of rime, gathers in the lee of boulders to form tiny, temporary pools, and flows in silent, silver threads down the faces of the rock. This is a land of intermittent rivers that appear and vanish, of waterfalls that are more mist than water, of fog that pours over the high ridges like a slow, silent tide.

You are standing on a shoulder of the Whistling Stair, looking out across the open, broken expanse of the High Basin. The scale is immense, the emptiness profound. The terrain is a chaotic sculpture garden of wind and frost. Great shards of obsidian-like rock, thrown up from some ancient, impossible tectonic event, lean at precarious angles. The ground is a mosaic of naked stone, shattered slate, and ice. There is no soil, only the memory of it in the dust that powders the rock. Vegetation is a stubborn, minimal thing: dark, crusty lichen splotching the rocks in patterns that look like forgotten runes, and the occasional tuft of a pale, wiry grass that grows in the deepest, most sheltered crevices. It is a world of primary colors: the black of the rock, the white of the ice and cloud, the grey of the sky.

The light is a perpetual, dim twilight. The sun is a pale, hazy coin seen through a veil of high, perpetual overcast, casting no shadows, only a soft, directional gradient of grey. This diffuse light makes the world feel flat, then suddenly, terrifyingly deep when a gust of wind tears a hole in the cloud and a shaft of cold, brilliant light illuminates the distant, fanged peak of a mountain you hadn't even perceived. These moments are brief and leave you feeling more exposed, more alone.

Life is rare, and its presence is a surprise. A small, blind, armored creature—like a pillbug the size of your thumb—scuttles across a rock face, leaving a clean trail through the rime. You see its kind only occasionally, a flicker of movement in a place that should be dead. They feed on the lichen, on the microscopic life that clings to the frost. There are no birds, no large animals. The only other living thing is the "ghost moss," a phosphorescent fungus that grows in the absolute darkness of the deep crevasses. It emits a faint, cold blue-green light, a spectral glow that can be seen from a distance, like the eyes of buried things. To see it, you must venture into a place where the wind's roar is muffled to a whisper and the only sound is the slow drip of water.

The rules of this world are strange and unwritten. The sound, for instance, cannot be trusted. The Whistling Stair is known to mimic voices, to replay fragments of conversations held on its upper reaches hours later, distorted and woven into the wind's own song. A shout can be swallowed by a gust and returned as a whisper from a direction you know to be empty. The echo has a life of its own. Here, a long-standing calm is not peaceful; it is a held breath. The balance is a tightrope walk, and the mountain is always leaning.

Faint, scattered marks of something else exist. A low wall of fitted stones, almost completely reclaimed by frost, runs for a hundred yards along a ridge before ending in nothing. A single, slender arch of the same glassy black rock stands at the basin's edge, perfectly balanced, its purpose unknowable. It is not a ruin; it is a feature, as natural and inexplicable as the Vent itself. These are not the marks of builders who left, but of a world that has always been in the process of settling, of finding its own, long-existed state of equilibrium. Nothing changes here, not in any way that a human lifetime can measure. The rivers run their intermittent courses, the wind sings its endless, unresolved chord, and the mountain simply *is*.

You feel the damp cold seep through your clothes, and the roar of the place vibrates in your chest. You are a temporary flicker of warmth and noise in a colossal, silent, and screaming emptiness. The place is not hostile, it is simply indifferent, holding you in its vast, hollow space like a single, insignificant note in its eternal, complex chord.
