# Omber

The first thing is that there is no ground. You come up the switchback road onto the shoulder of the basin and you see it laid out below like the inside of a clock that someone left open in the rain: a valley entirely of decks. Iron and concrete and riveted plate, tier upon tier, eleven and twelve levels deep in the middle where the land sags, each level roofed by the one above and floored by the one below, and every surface colonized. The natural rock of the mountainside shows only at the rim, a wet grey lip, and even that is stitched with retaining walls and culverts and the stumps of old piers. Everything below the rim is made.

It is cool here, and it is wet. There is a permanent soft weather in the basin — the clouds come over the ridge, hit the warm breath of the stacks, and stall, and the air is always just on the point of rain without quite committing. Water condenses on every cool surface: the undersides of the great plate roofs bead and drip in continuous thin lines, so that beneath any large span there is a curtain of fine rain that isn't rain, a ticking on the moss. The moss is the other thing. It has taken the whole valley. Not in patches — as a skin, a pelt, green-black and grey and a pale acid green where the steam pipes run hot, growing three fingers thick on the lee sides of every wall, hanging in soft beards from the cable trays, filling the seams of the paving decks so the ironwork shows through it like bone through gum. In the drainage channels it grows as a bright slime that the water combs flat. Ferns have found the verticals — they sprout from the gaps where the expansion joints have opened, they hang from the flanges of the I-beams in long feathered cages, and their fronds are all turned the same way, downstream, wherever the drip is strongest. There are terraces of planted things, too, pale and leggy under the lamps: beds of something like chard, ranks of vines trained along the catenary wires, a whole slope of low scrub alder that has rooted in a collapsed roof two levels down and become, over forty years, a small forest with a floor of its own.

Under all of it, the Works runs. It runs quietly, which is the strange part, and the quiet is not peace. The engines are everywhere — pumps, presses, the deep cylinders of the great trompes drawing their air down the wet shafts, belt drives threading up through the floors like roots — and all of it is muffled by the moss and the wet and the acoustics of a valley built of baffles. What you hear is a low persistent hum, a breathing, and then, near at hand, the small sounds: a chain taking up slack, water going down a chute, a hoist ticking over, someone's boots on a ladder. But the valley is turbulent. You feel it as vibration through the deck more than you hear it. Things are being moved constantly — crates, spars, drums of something, whole sections of railing being unbolted and carried off and bolted somewhere else — and the structure itself is in a state of perpetual small adjustment: old spans shored, new spans laid over them, a crane turning at the far end of the valley all night and all day bringing up girders to replace girders that have gone soft with rust. It is an anthill in slow motion, and it never stops, and nobody seems to be in charge of it. The work simply continues, shift after shift, the way the water continues.

The light is dim and even, a grey-green daylight filtered through cloud and steam and the tall stacks that vent white into the mist at every level. Lamps burn at all hours along the walkways — sodium, mostly, a muddy orange that stains the moss and makes the wet iron shine — so the valley has no real day, only a slow brightening and dimming behind the overcast, and the lamps always on anyway. Shadows are soft. Distances are hard to judge; the far terraces go grey and then vanish into the condensing air, and you get the impression, standing on a bridge deck, that the Works extends much further than the basin allows, that it is a town and a factory and a drainage system and a garden all built into each other and there is no edge to it that isn't another edge.

Animals are few. There are beetles in the moss and pale things in the cisterns, and eels in the lower sumps that the men will not eat, and once or twice a day a heron — thin, grey, patient — stands on the parapet of the highest deck and waits for whatever it is herons wait for here, then leaves. Gulls come over the ridge in bad weather and do not stay. The rest of the fauna is people, and the people are packed in: twelve thousand of them, on the terraces, in the stacked rooms behind the workshop walls, on the stairs, on the roofs of the lower decks (which are, of course, the floors of the upper ones), so that at any hour you can look across the valley and see a hundred small figures in motion on a hundred different levels, none of them idle, all of them going somewhere with something.

The rule of the place is water. Water is not fought here and it is not drained away; it is directed. Every roof is a gutter, every gutter a channel, every channel leads by some route to a cistern or a sump or a wheel, and the whole valley is plumbed so that the condensation of a thousand surfaces becomes, by the time it reaches the bottom, a working head. The lowest level is not floor but reservoir, and below the reservoir is old, and nobody goes there. Keep to the uphill side of the drip lines. Do not stand under a long span in cold weather; the bead-line will find your collar in a minute and your boots in ten. Repair is not a project here, it is the local weather — something is always being taken apart and something always being put back, and the two are the same activity, and if you stand still too long on a catwalk you will find yourself gently moved along, because you are, structurally, in the way.

And it is thoroughly ordinary. Nothing in Omber violates anything. The water runs downhill, the iron rusts, the moss grows on the wet side, the engines turn because someone feeds them, and the someone is fed in turn by the terraces of chard. It is only that the whole of it — the valley, the eleven decks, the forest in the collapsed roof, the crane turning in the mist, the whistling of a man on a stair you can't see — has been going so long, in its own wet and patient and ceaseless way, that it has stopped being a place anyone built and become a place that simply is, and is maintaining itself, and would go on doing so perfectly well without you, and will.
