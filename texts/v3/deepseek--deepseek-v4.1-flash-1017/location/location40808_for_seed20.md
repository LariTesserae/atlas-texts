# The Kettle

You come up the south ridge and the rock is still broken glass — fresh fractures, no soil in the joints, edges that cut through a boot sole if you're careless. This is a young mountain. It hasn't been rained on long enough to be rounded. Everything here has the look of something set down recently and not yet settled, and the ridgeline is still going up, a few millimeters a year, which you can't feel but can see in the way the fractures have opened along the crest like a loaf splitting.

Then the air turns warm and wet, and wrong, because you are high enough that it should be cold, and it isn't. It's the warmth of a mouth. It gets into your clothes and stays.

The Kettle is over the next lip: a small sea, four hundred paces across at its widest, sitting in a basin it has only just finished making. There are other names for it, depending on who you ask and how much they liked being there — the Cup, the Warm Mouth, the Boil — but the people who named it that way didn't stay, so the names sit loose on it. You can walk the whole rim in twenty minutes. That's the scale of the thing. A room you could shout across, if shouting worked.

The water is not rainwater. It comes up from below, forced through a fracture the mountain opened in itself, and it leaves upward as steam, and it is coming and going so fast that the surface has never once been still. It is blood-warm, a degree or two above your skin, milky with minerals, and where the upwelling is strongest it stands in a dome a hand high, glassy and shaking, before it collapses and starts again. Everything down there is in a state of being made and unmade. New pale crusts of sinter form along the edges overnight and are gone by afternoon. Small islands appear — a meter of grey sponge-stone, steaming — and are swallowed. The shape of the shoreline is not the same twice. Do not make a map. Maps of the Kettle are a joke people tell.

Nothing here has been eroded yet. That's the strange thing about a place with this much water in it: the water is too new, and it's too busy leaving, and the rock is too hard and too recently broken. The rim is all clean angle and splinter. No beach, no pebbles, no smoothing. You cut your hands on the shore.

**The sound.**

It is deafening, and it is the first fact of the place, and everything else is arranged around it.

There are at least five things making noise and they do not agree. The upwelling roars from underneath — a thick, sub-aqueous thunder that comes up through the soles of your feet before it comes through your ears. The steam leaves through a dozen fissures in the north rim, and the fissures are different widths, so they shriek at different pitches, a chord that is not a chord, a set of flutes played by something with no sense of tune. The surface slaps and chops at the sharp rock. The wind over the lip is a continuous hiss. And under all of it, below hearing, there's a low throb that is the mountain itself, water and rock arguing in the dark, and it comes up through your sternum and makes your teeth feel loose.

You cannot talk here. Not "it's hard to talk" — you can shape the words and they leave your mouth and are simply deleted, half a meter from your face. You cannot hear your own footsteps. You put your hand flat on the rock and feel the vibration and that's the only way to know you're walking. After an hour your thoughts get thin and pointed, pared down to the immediate, the way they do in a storm.

And then, without warning, a pocket of silence will drift past — a volume of true nothing, a meter or two across, riding the steam like a bubble in glass. Step into one and the roar is not muffled but *absent*, cut clean, and you hear the blood in your ears and your own breath and someone saying a word you don't know. Then it's gone and the noise comes back like a wall falling.

**Light.**

There is no direct sun here. The steam stands over the basin in a permanent column and turns the daylight into a flat, dim, warm grey — dusk at noon, dusk that never resolves into evening. The far rim is always slightly hazy. Colors reduce to about four: the pink-grey of the wet rock, the milky green of the water, the white of the steam, the darker white of the crusts.

The water glows. Not much — a pale luminosity that hangs in it, drifting in the churn, brightest at the boil. Something suspended in it, a bloom of something, single cells by the billion, and it's the reason the place is legible at all. At night the Kettle is a low lamp in the shape of a sea, breathing light, and the steam above it carries a little of the glow up into the dark.

**The living.**

Not much, and what there is, is strange.

At the waterline where the spray falls there's a scalded mat of something plant-like, olive and rust, that gets cooked back each time the level rises and creeps out again when it drops. It never gets more than a hand's width from the edge. It's the only vegetation on the summit, and it smells like a hot iron.

In the water, in the column of the boil, there are animals the length of a finger, blind and pale, with a faint lattice of light along their sides. They ride the upwelling. They let it throw them to the surface and then they swim down and do it again. Nobody knows what they eat. When the boil pauses — and it does pause, for a second or two, unpredictably — they hang suspended in the water, all of them, motionless, waiting, and then the column takes them again.

On the rocks, one bird. Small, brown, unremarkable. It nests inside the loudest fissure on the north rim, in the full scream of the steam, and this is not an accident: nothing that hunts by ear can hear anything in there, and the bird is safe in the one place on the mountain where being quiet doesn't matter. You will see it go in and out and never hear it once. Its call, if it has one, has never been recorded, because there's nowhere on the rim you could stand to hear it.

**The marks.**

Somebody built here. Not much, and not for long.

Around the south lip there's a ring of stones, hand-stacked, half-sunk in new crust — about twenty paces across, regular enough to be deliberate, old enough that the sinter has grown up over the bottom courses and is slowly eating it. Inside the ring, the rock is worn smooth in a circle, as if people stood there a great deal.

On the east lip, a platform: split wood, lashed with cord, still standing, still level, made from timber that doesn't grow at this elevation, so it was carried up. On the platform, a shallow depression worn into the wood, and nothing else.

And along the inside of the rim, stakes driven into the fissures — a dozen of them, aligned, at intervals; they hum. Whatever they were for, the person who set them chose the fissures with the best pitch. There's no metal anywhere on this mountain. No tool marks on the stakes except the ones a blade makes. Whoever it was came up, worked, and left, and the mountain is already unmaking their work at the rate of a millimeter a year.

**The rules.**

Locals — the ones who come up seasonally and don't stay — will tell you three things, and they'll tell you them by gesture, because you can't hear a warning up there.

Sound is substance. The loudest thing is the most real. Things that go quiet lose their edges; you can't say where they are, and after a while you can't find them at all.

Distance is measured in noise, not space. You can see the far rim clearly and you cannot walk to it in any predictable time. The route exists and it moves. The sea is four hundred paces across and sometimes it is a morning's walk across, and the people who tried to prove otherwise are the reason the ring of stones is worn smooth.

The surface lies. Look down into the water and the reflection shows a calm sky with a sun in it, which is not the sky above you. Sometimes it shows the rim and the rim is crowded, and you look up and you are alone on the mountain. Pull your hand out of the water and the hollow stays — a dent in the surface, a hand-shaped absence, filling slowly, and it will still be there a minute later.

And: from inside the basin you can hear nothing of the world outside. No wind from the valleys, no birds from below, nothing. The Kettle is a hole in the sound of the earth. Stand in it long enough and the outside gets very slightly less real, and you begin to feel a homesickness for a place you can still see from the rim.

You leave the way you came, down the sharp south ridge, and the cold comes back into the air about three hundred paces below the lip — sudden, thin, clean, and it feels like being handed something you'd forgotten you were carrying.
