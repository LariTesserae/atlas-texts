# The Basin of the Weeping Mountain

## I. The Descent

The water is warm and does not move much. It presses against you from all sides, and if you are not careful about which way you float, you will not know which way is up. The top of the chamber — if it is a top — glows with a soft amber haze, a light that filters through meters of standing water and pours down over everything like syrup. The bottom is farther; I cannot see it. The water is not clear exactly, but it is not clouded either. It holds the light and carries it, and you can see perhaps twenty meters in any direction before the haze swallows the rest.

You are inside a mountain. Not a natural one — or rather, perhaps it was natural once, before its heart was hollowed and filled. The walls curve away from you on every side, rising in segments of smooth ceramic pipe, each one perhaps two meters across, fitted together like the segments of an enormous worm. They are green — not moss green, though the moss is part of it — a green of glaze and minerals, ribbed and jointed and sealed with some dark resin that has held for centuries. The pipes breach the surface somewhere above. You can see the wetter gleam of a few of them where the water ends and whatever air there is begins; maybe that is where the light comes from.

## II. The Weirs and the Industry

The pipes branch. They branch and rebranch until the branching is the space itself. There is no wall behind the water, no rock you could put your hand against, because the rock was long since wrapped in pipes and struts and cable and then wrapped again. In some places the systems have been bundled so tight you could mistake them for a wall, but the seams and valves give them away. Copper lines thread through the ceramic. Valves the size of doorways stand open and dripping and have been open for a very long time. A ladder of iron rungs, bolted to something, descends past you into the green.

Above — there is an above, though the top is not a surface you can reach — the pipes converge on a weir. Water spills over it in a thin, even curtain, a waterfall like a sheet of glass, and it makes no sound. It makes almost no sound. If you hold your breath (if you are holding your breath) you can hear the click and tick of the machinery: valves and pumps and whatever deeper engines, turning slowly, patiently, moving water through the mountain as if this were all quite normal, as if the mountain were a heart and this were its circulation. The sound is very quiet, and it comes from everywhere, and it never changes.

## III. The Lung

The vegetation does not grow *on* the pipes. It grows *out of* them. Moss and liverwort close the joints, so that the segments are held tight in green. Fiddleheads uncurl from the mouths of half-open valves. Ferns, enormous ones, grow upside down from the undersides of the weirs, their fronds hanging into the water and swaying only when you touch them. A plant I cannot name — a dark, rubbery rosette with spines along every edge — has colonized the warm surfaces of the engines, and flushes of red growth climb the copper lines to the points where the copper is hottest.

Everywhere is packed. There is no place to put your hand without finding a leaf or a frond or the furred green of moss or a copper line warm against your palm. Roots have found every gap. You see them — pale threads, then cords, then the thick woody cables of something that has grown to fill a pipe and then burst it, so that the ceramic lies in shards, glazed on one side and green on the other. The place has no room left for anything empty. Even the water is full; if you open your eyes underwater you can see the seeds and the drifting leaves and the floating threads of algae, all of them going nowhere, suspended.

## IV. The Water Itself

The water is warm. It is warmer than you expect, and it stays warm as you go down; this is a heated place, heated from below, heated by the engines. Near the weirs you can feel the gradient — a warm sheet of outflow, a cooler slip of something recycled. It tastes of iron. It tastes of salt and of the particular mouthfeel of water that has run over copper and ceramic and the furred green of moss for its whole long passage.

It is not moving very fast, and it moves without direction, a slow breathing rather than a current. Things drift. You find the occasional screw, a shard of glass, a leaf. For a long time the water seems to be the same water, as though it had nowhere to go.

## V. The Silence

There's no life. No darting things in the green, no mouths or eyes or wings. The moss has not been grazed. The ferns have not been browsed. You go looking for a sign that something living has been here, and there is nothing, only the growth and the machinery and the water. It's very quiet. The tick of a pump, the hush of the sheet of water over the weir, and then a long space of nothing. Not the silence of a dead place. The silence of a place that works. A place like a room you have walked into at the wrong time, so empty it feels like someone has just left.

---

Far below you, the pipes continue. The light does not. The machinery is down there — the engines, warm, turning. The green goes down with them, and the moss, and the quiet. It would take time to reach it. You would have plenty of time. The mountain turns, patient, and moves its water around, and the growth closes back over every seam, and nothing hurries. The place has held the same shape for longer than anyone remembers, and it will hold it a while yet.
