# The Slow Foundry

There is no sun here, and no memory of one. The sky is a lid of its own vapor, high and unbroken, lit from nowhere in particular — a dim pearl-grey that never brightens past the edge of dusk and never quite goes black. It is warm. That is the first surprise. Under a sky that looks like a closed cellar, the air is the temperature of a hand held near a stove, and it smells of hot stone, iron, and wet chalk.

The land is a plateau the size of a continent, and it is tilted very slightly, so that everything about it tilts too: the fog, the runoff, the long shallow basins where water collects. You can walk for days and the ground will not change character. It rings faintly underfoot, a dry hollow note, as though the crust were a shell and the shell were not thick. In places it cracks and the crack exhales.

Because beneath this plain, something is running.

Not built. Nothing here is built; there is no wall, no post, no mark of a hand. But the deep rock is stacked in cylinders — columnar, hexagon-fluted, some of them ten paces across and taller than a house, leaning like books on a shelf that has begun to sag. They emerge from the ground at angles, half-sunk, weathered at the shoulders, furred with salts. Between each cylinder and its socket there is a gap, and the gap is not still. It widens by a finger's width and closes again, over about forty heartbeats. Each closure pushes out a soft bolus of steam, hot and sulfurous and alive with fine dust. Somewhere under the whole plateau, heat is being made and something is being lifted, and the columns are what that lifting has made of the stone over a span of time that has no useful number.

The sound of it is almost nothing. This is a quiet place. But the quiet is not empty — it is the quiet of a large room where someone in another room is working. A drip. A tick, as a flake of crust cools and contracts: tick — pause — tick. A low groan, felt in the teeth more than heard, when a seam slips. Wind crossing the terraces, and the wind makes no rustle, because there is nothing here to rustle.

Water comes in the reluctant way. It does not rain so much as condense: at night the whole plain goes slick with a fine film, and every hollow will hold a skin of it by morning. Now and then a genuine rain arrives, warm and brief and strange, falling out of a fog rather than a cloud, and after it the ground exhales visibly for an hour. The runoff gathers in the long basins and the tilted troughs, and there it stays, warm and mineral-cloudy, ringed with white salt crust and a rime of yellow-white sinter that builds a millimeter a century. The pools are the color of weak tea and they smell of eggs and lime. Some of them are hot enough to hurt. Most are not.

Vegetation keeps to the margins, and it is thin — a mat here, a thread there, never a green cover. Rust-colored moss in the seams where the steam condenses and the rock stays damp. Pale wiry grass along the runoff channels, cropped to nubs. In the wet cracks, stands of something jointed and grey-green, waist-high, that draws up the mineral water and deposits a crust along its own stem until it looks calcified, and perhaps is. Away from the water and the seams, the plain is bare: black crust, salt, dust, and the long slow tilt of the light that is not light.

And yet the place teems. It is full of animals — not in crowds, but everywhere, thinly, distributed like the vegetation is distributed, one creature to a great deal of room. Mites and flies in every steam plume, hundreds of thousands of them, each plume with its own population and its own color. Crabs the size of a thumbnail in the warm pools, translucent, their guts visible. Crabs the size of a plate in the cooler ones. Things like crickets that rasp, briefly, when the seam closes. Pale blind fish in the basins, and in one basin, by some accident of isolation, a fish that has given up on eyes entirely and navigates by the taste of the water.

There are larger animals. A grazer, knee-high, hump-backed, hairless, that stands in the steam plumes with its mouth open and its throat working, filtering the warm air for the dust that lives in it — it lives on the foundry's exhalations, which is to say it lives on the flies and the mineral flakes both. It moves in ones and twos across a hundred kilometers of crust and never seems to hurry. Something bigger hunts it, and leaves tracks in the salt that fill with condensation by morning and are gone by the following night. The big thing is rarely seen. You know it mostly by the way the small animals go quiet along a line, and by a smell, and by the pause in the ticking.

The rules of the place are simple and are not negotiable.

The ground moves, so you learn to stand on the columns and not in the seams. The seams will take a foot. Not out of malice; they simply close.

Heat is the map. Warmth means breathable air; the plumes that sting the eyes and set you coughing are the ones feeding off a bad fissure, and the animals will show you which is which by where they are not.

The dark is real, so everything is done by warmth and by sound and by the faint living lights — the glow of the flies in their plumes, the pale luminous smear of the pool-crusts, the dull red of a fissure at its hottest. There is enough to see by. There is not enough to see far.

And the place is old enough to be in balance with itself. The terraces have settled into their basins. The columns lean as far as they will lean. The sinter builds and the wind takes it and the sinter builds again. The hot water has been coming up long enough to have carved its own soft chimneys and to have filled them in again. Erosion here is not a force acting on the land; it is the land's habit.

It is a factory with nothing in it that was made. That is the strangest thing about it and the thing you stop noticing last: that for all the pistons and the flues and the venting and the slow production of terraces and heat and dust and living things, no one built it, no one runs it, and no one is coming. It is simply stone, behaving the way stone behaves when it is warm and deep and has a great deal of time.

Walk out onto the plain at the dim hour that is the closest thing to morning. The seams are breathing. The flies are rising in their columns of warm vapor, each column a small city. The fish are at the surface of the tea-colored pool, taking the condensation. Somewhere out past the limits of seeing, the big animal is standing still, and the small animals know exactly where. Tick. Pause. Tick. The air is soft and warm and smells of iron. Nothing here needs you. It will go on closing and opening, closing and opening, long after you have found your way back off the plateau, and it will not have registered that you were there at all.
