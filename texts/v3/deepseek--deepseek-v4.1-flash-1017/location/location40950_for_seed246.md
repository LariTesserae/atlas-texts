# The Croon

High up, past where trees give up, there is a broad shallow bowl of pale stone worn almost to level — the flattened skull of a mountain that was once something else. It is old in the way that a riverbed is old: not ruined but *finished*, everything on it having reached the shape it will keep. The ridges around the bowl are not peaks anymore, just soft grey lips a few hundred feet high, rubbed down by a wetness that comes and goes across millennia. Snow falls here, but thinly, and does not stay. What stays is water in its middle forms: mist, seep, condensation beading on stone, and hundreds of small clear pools that sit in the hollows of the worn rock like coins left in a bowl.

Down in the bowl, the stone is not bare. It is furred — mosses in deep maroon and a green so dark it reads as black, lichen in pale rosettes the size of dinner plates, and a low wiry scrub that grows in cushions and smells of resin and cold iron when you crush it. Everything is wet. Everything is soft underfoot. The lushness is out of proportion to the place: this is not a generous climate, but the water never quite leaves, and so life has settled in thick and patient and small.

## The town

And there is a town. That is the thing you notice second, after the wet, and it takes a while to understand that it *is* a town, because nothing here is built the way you would expect. No walls stand. No beams. Instead, the bowl is scattered with hollow mounds — several hundred of them, some the size of a fist, some the size of a chapel — grown rather than assembled, each one a shell of layered mineral and hardened organic matter, banded like a cut tree, each with a single opening facing the low side of the slope. They are not arranged in streets. They are arranged in *drainage*: every mound sits where water wants to go, and each one passes the water through itself, in at the mouth and out through pores in the base, so the whole settlement is one slow plumbing system, a filter built out of architecture.

The bands in the shells say they were laid down in generations, by colonies of something that no longer lives here — small shelled things that built around themselves in the shallows and died and had their successors build on the rubble of them. Nothing in the mounds is a tool. There is no metal, no fire-scar, no cut mark. But there is intention in the way the water is handled, and there is a long, patient intelligence in the fact that the mounds have been shaped by use, generation on generation, the way a path is shaped by feet.

## The emptiness

Almost nothing lives in the town now. You can walk it in an hour — the bowl is small, you can see the whole of it from the rim — and in that hour you might see a single grey animal the size of a cat moving between the mounds, unhurried, unafraid. That is the whole population. There is space in the settlement for thousands of the things that built it and there are, at any given moment, a handful of presences. The mounds stand open-mouthed and empty, like a room of cups set out for a party that ended before you arrived.

## The sound

But it is not quiet. This is the strange thing, and it is the thing you will remember. The mounds are hollow, and their mouths face downslope, and the wind that crosses the bowl — which crosses it almost always, because there is nothing left to break it — enters the mouths and finds the pores and the inner chambers and *sings*. Each mound has its own pitch, set by its size and the thickness of its walls and how much water stands inside it, and because the mounds are all shaped a little differently the settlement is a chord, not a note. It is not a howl. It is closer to a room full of people humming, or a hive, or a very distant choir that never resolves. On a still day it drops to a breathy murmur. When weather comes up over the rim, the whole bowl rises into a low bronze roar, and the small mounds whistle over the top of it, and the pools shiver in rings, and the sound bounces off the soft grey lips of the ridge and comes back.

Underneath the wind there are the other sounds, and they are constant, because this is a wet place and water is always doing something: the drip from the mound-mouths, the trickle and gurgle of the drainage channels, the creak of ice forming in a pool at dusk and breaking at noon, the resinous crackle of the scrub when frost gets into it.

## Weather, and the way the place moves

The bowl has its own weather because of its shape and its height. Mist forms inside the rim at night and does not leave until the sun is well up, so mornings are grey and close and every surface is beaded; by afternoon the mist has burned off the stone but hangs in the low channels, and the light — ordinary, unremarkable light, the light of any overcast day — goes flat and even and shadowless, which makes the mounds look drawn rather than standing. Rain comes in thin persistent curtains from the west, sometimes for days, and then the drainage channels run full and the singing changes pitch as the chambers fill. The water is what moves here, and the moss, and the slow winter creep of the frost line up and down the slopes, and the one grey animal going about its business among the empty shells.

At the end of the day the light goes long and low and the mouths of the mounds glow with it, briefly, and the choir of them is at its loudest, and if you stand on the rim with the mist coming up around your knees you will hear the whole finished, ancient, waterlogged, half-abandoned town humming to itself, all of its small architecture full of wind, patiently waiting out the next few thousand years.
