# The Settled Plain

There is no water here, and no memory of water. The sky is a warm bright nothing — not blue, not white, but the color light goes when it has passed through a great deal of very fine dust and come out the other side patient and diffuse. It glows rather than shines. There is no sun you can point to; the whole vault is lit, evenly, from a source smeared across the ceiling of the world, and it does not move much. Day and night are a matter of degree, a slow dimming and re-brightening, more like breathing than like turning.

The ground is flat the way very old things are flat. It runs out to a horizon that is close only because the air is thick with suspended matter, and beyond that horizon it runs on, and on, and there is no edge anyone has found. Underfoot is not soil and not rock. It is laminate — layer upon layer upon layer of manufactured substance, thin as paper, laid down over an unthinkable span and then worn, over a span more unthinkable still, into a plain. The topmost layers have gone to dust, a pale warm grit the color of weak tea, and the grit lies loose and shallow over the still-coherent strata beneath. Where the wind has scoured it clear you can see the structure: faint terraces, faint grids, faint tessellations the size of rooms and the size of fields, each slightly proud of its neighbor, all their edges rounded by ages of abrasion until the whole surface reads as a single soft quilt of near-flat panels, tilted a degree this way and a degree that, catching the diffuse light in long lazy gradients.

It is warm. Not hot — warm the way a stone wall is warm at dusk, having held the day. The warmth comes up through the soles of your feet and it comes down from the luminous vault and the air between is body-temperature and still. It smells of nothing much: hot mineral, faintly sweet, faintly like the inside of an old machine that has run a very long time without complaint.

The density is what unsettles newcomers. Everything here is layered, packed, near-touching. The air is not empty; it holds a fine constant suspension of shed laminate, motes that drift and settle and are lifted again, so that the light is always slightly grained and distances soften. Below the surface, the strata are crowded — every cubic meter of the world's depth is full of structure, channel, cell, archive. The plain is not a floor over a void. It is the top of a book a thousand kilometers thick, and the book is still being read.

Because it is still running. That is the thing to understand. This is not a ruin. Nothing here has failed. The machinery went down so deep and became so small and so patient that it does not need to be tended, and it repairs itself at a pace too slow to watch — a seam closing over a decade, a channel clearing over a century. The erosion is not decay; it is the world settling into the shape it will keep. The winds that scour the grit are the world's own weather, thermal cells rising off the warm plain and sinking again, and they carry the dust in long slow rivers you can see by the way the light thickens. Sometimes the dust lifts higher and the horizon closes to a few hundred paces and the air goes gold and humming with static, and then it settles, and the plain is pale again.

Sound is almost absent, and what sound there is, is small. The grit hisses when the wind moves it. The laminate ticks as it warms and cools — a scatter of tiny reports, here and gone, like rain on a distant roof. Under everything, if you lie down and put your ear to the ground, there is a very low continuous something, not quite a hum, more a sense of enormous slow circulation, the sound of a system doing its work at a temperature barely above the ambient. It is the quietest loud thing imaginable.

Life is sparse and strange and mostly does not look like life. In the shallow scours where grit collects, things grow: low mats and fans of a pale fibrous stuff, neither plant nor fungus nor quite machine, that send fine threads down into the laminate and persuade the strata beneath them to extend, to thicken, to build a little more of themselves. They are slow. A fan the width of your hand may be older than your language. They cluster where the warmth is strongest and the dust is deepest, and between clusters there is nothing but grit and panel for kilometers. The vegetation of this world is a scattering of pale tufts across a quilt, and it is enough.

Animals are rarer. There are things that move — small, low, many-legged, the color of the ground, that come out in the dim hours and cross the panels on errands of their own, following thermal gradients the way other creatures follow water. There are a few larger ones, seen perhaps once in a season, that move so slowly they seem to be part of the terrain until they are not. Nothing here is in a hurry, because there is nothing to hurry toward. The predator-prey of this place is a matter of patience and of heat: who can wait, who can hold still, who can find the warm seam first.

And there is a rule to it, which you feel before you can state. The world is finished. Not dead — finished, the way a well-made thing is finished. It has been worn into its final form and it holds that form, and everything in it, the mats and the motes and the slow animals and the deep patient machinery, is keeping to that form. You may walk here. You may watch the dust rivers and the ticking panels and the pale fans growing a millimeter a century. But you are the newest thing on the plain by an enormous margin, and the plain will outlast you without noticing, and it will still be warm, and still be bright, and still be quietly, thoroughly, entirely itself.
