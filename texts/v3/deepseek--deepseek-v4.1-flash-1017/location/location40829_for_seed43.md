# The Kettle

You come to it through reeds, and the reeds are the only thing about it that isn't made. The Kettle is a mound in the warm marsh — low, wide, and green on top — and the reeds grow up its flanks because the flanks are wet clay that has been wet clay for a very long time. There is no door. There is a slope of rammed earth, slick with algae, that goes down between two matted walls of reed-bundle, and you follow it because the sound comes up out of it: not one sound, but a wet, layered, argumentative din that gets louder the closer you get, the way a hive gets louder when you put your ear to the trunk.

Inside, the mound is hollow, and everything inside is built. There is no rock. There is no natural cave. The walls are clay daub over a lattice of split reed and bone, and where the lattice has shown through — and it shows through everywhere, in patches, worn open by forty or fifty generations of shoulders and hands — you can see that the wall is a sandwich: daub, reed, daub, old daub, older daub, and at the bottom, near the floor, a band of packed shell and potsherd that someone put there before anyone's grandmother was born. The floor is laid brick, small and warped, tilted so that all the water in the world runs to the low chamber at the north end. The ceilings are vaults of woven cane over ribs of drifted timber, and they sag in the middle the way a basket sags, and they are hung with everything: netting, dried fish, gourds, lamps, sleeping slings, bundles of root-cord, children's toys made of knucklebone. You cannot stand upright in most of the Kettle. You walk bent, and the place walks with you.

It is warm. It is warmer inside than out, always, and it smells of steam and yeast and wet clay and the ammoniac sweetness of the animal pens, and it is *damp* the way a mouth is damp. Water is everywhere and none of it is a river. It comes down through the roof-vault in threads, through the reed matting that covers the mound, and it gathers in the low chamber in a shallow pool the color of tea, and it is lifted out in gourd-buckets and poured over the terraces, and it condenses on the ceilings at night and drips — plink, plink, plink, but never in rhythm, because the vaults are all different depths and the drips fall into pots placed under the worst of them, and the pots are tuned, more or less, so that the dripping is not noise but a kind of loose, wandering music that the children learn to keep time to before they learn to walk. The air is thick enough to drink. Light comes down the light-shafts — six of them, reed-lined, angled like chimneys, capped with scraped bladder-skin that is translucent and yellow-green — and the shafts lay down bars of warm green-gold light, and in those bars the air is visibly full: gnats, dust, spores, steam. Where the light doesn't reach, there are lamps: shallow clay boats of fish oil with floating wicks, set into niches worn into the wall by other lamps, and the niches are black with the soot of centuries and the soot has a shape, a sort of glossy petal-shape, that everyone in the Kettle knows without looking at.

**The terraces.** This is the strange engine of the place. The Kettle is small — you could walk its whole length in a hundred and fifty paces — but it is built in layers, and the layers are shelves: stepped beds of clay and compost that rise from the pool at the north to the roof at the south, and on them grow the Kettle's thin, pale, determined vegetation. Not much of it. Sparse — that's the truth of it. Mushrooms in the dark shelves, grey and pink and one kind that glows faintly and is used for reading; cress and water-mint along the pool; a leggy, light-starved vine that someone brought in a long time ago and that has never quite given up, trained along the ceiling ribs so it hangs down into the light-shafts like a curtain of green thread. Everything edible that is not grown here is brought in, or caught. The terraces are the whole agricultural base and they are barely enough, and everyone knows it, and this is why the Kettle is loud: because it is full, and it is hot, and it is close, and it is *alive*, and life in a small hot crowded place makes a great deal of noise.

**The sound.** It is not chaos; it is a density. Water: the drips, the trickle down the north wall, the slosh of buckets, the pool. Animals: the pens are in the second chamber, and there are the marsh-pigs — small, bristled, bad-tempered, six or seven of them — and the eel-tanks cut into the floor, and the birds, which are not kept but which come, a dozen kinds, in and out of the shafts all day, and which have learned that the Kettle means food and have made it their own, and whose calls ricochet off the vaults and multiply. The plaster beetles, which are everywhere, in every wall, making a dry continuous rustle like rain on thatch. Frogs, who have colonized the pool and the drip-pots and the wet corners and who sing at night with the particular brainless persistence of frogs. And the people: the Kettle holds perhaps forty, and they are not quiet people, and the architecture does not permit privacy, and so there is always someone talking, someone calling down a shaft, someone hammering a bone pin into a sagging rib, someone singing the drip-song, someone arguing about the terraces, which is the great argument of the Kettle and has been for two hundred years. The dead are kept in the walls. This is not a figure of speech. The wall-niches hold them, wrapped in reed and sealed with daub, and the daub is repainted with a handprint each generation, and there are so many handprints now that the lower walls look like a sediment of hands.

**The rules.** The Kettle is a place that has settled into itself. Nothing here is straight. The bricks are laid crooked because the floor was never flat; the vaults sag because the ribs have crept; the passages are polished smooth and slightly too narrow because a hundred and fifty years of shoulders have worn them narrower and smoother in the same motion. Every repair is visible and none of them match. The daub is patched with daub of a different color, so that the walls are mottled like a hide. When a rib gives, it is sistered with a new one lashed alongside, and the lashing is always gut-thread or root-cord, never metal, because metal in the Kettle is small and precious and comes from elsewhere: a knife-blade, a fish-hook, a single iron nail that is the Kettle's most valuable object and is used for everything and is kept in a gourd. There is no wheel. There is a ladder, and a rope, and a windlass made of a hollow log, and that is the whole of the machinery. The people are fish-catchers and reed-cutters and marsh-pig-keepers and builders, and they build with their hands and their teeth and their knees, and they have been doing it here so long that the building has become a form of inheritance: a wall is not finished, it is *kept*.

**The strangeness**, which is small, and which no one remarks on: the Kettle is growing. Not building — growing. The mound's outer skin of reed and clay thickens by a finger's width a year, laid down by the same patient labor that mends the inside, and the light-shafts have had to be extended upward twice in living memory. The marsh is silting. The Kettle is rising to keep its mouth above the water. It will not last forever and everyone knows this too, the way they know the terraces are barely enough, and the knowledge sits in the noise and gets argued with and forgotten and re-argued, and meanwhile the pool rises a little in the wet season and falls in the dry, the frogs sing, the birds come and go through the yellow-green shafts, the beetle-rustle goes on in every wall, the pigs complain, the vine hangs down into the light, and the drips fall into the tuned pots, wandering, keeping their loose time.
