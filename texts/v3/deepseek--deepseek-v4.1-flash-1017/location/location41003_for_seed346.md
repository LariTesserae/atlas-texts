# The Seep-District of Hollow Reach

The ground here never finished becoming a floor. It's a low island of slick, half-set stone — sedimentary, but not yet comfortable being sediment — still sweating a mineral film that pools in the shallow dish of the district and refuses to fully drain. The water isn't a river or a lake; it's a persistence. It beads on every surface, gathers in striations where the rock grain opens, and occasionally — on some schedule nobody has pinned down — the sky will drop a short, cold rain that passes in minutes and leaves the whole hollow steaming gently. Cold steam. The kind that drifts low and catches in your throat. Temperature sits just above freezing, the air always on the edge of a held breath.

The district is small — you could walk its long axis in a slow quarter hour — and crammed. Dense. Buildings lean and press toward one another the way teeth crowd in a jaw, and the alleys between them are less streets than seams. And the buildings are *building*. That's the first thing that unsettles. The architecture here is continued geology: the structures grow from the bedrock in slow, patient strata, half-mineral and half-made, glistening gray composites that extrude and thicken year over year. An awning that was a suggestion of an awning a decade ago now holds; a wall that's been "going up" since before anyone remembers has a definite top now, though nobody watched it arrive. Curbs undercut themselves and fill back in. It is a district made by a process, not a plan, and the process is slow and indifferent and very, very old. Emerging erosion: things wear away and rebuild in the same gesture.

The tech is woven through all of it, and it doesn't announce itself. There are no machines, no cables, nothing you could point at and call a device — but the surfaces are wrong in a way that only shows under attention. Step close to a wall and the moisture on it has arranged itself in fine channels that move a little when you're not looking, like the building is breathing through a million capillary mouths. The puddles don't reflect you exactly; they reflect the space slightly *after* you, a lag of a half-second, a correction arriving late. Some corners of the district are simply closer to their neighbors than the geometry allows — the alleys shorten when you walk them, and the walk takes exactly as long as it takes. The infrastructure is the district, and the district is a low-grade physics that the surrounding world doesn't quite admit to.

The vegetation is sparse and stubborn, and it grows in the seams where water stays longest. Pale things — mosses like damp felt, low filamentous growths that tremble in the condensation, a few stunted shrubs with leaves that hold a strange inward curl, as though guarding something. None of it is lush; all of it is *persistent*, making its living off mineral seep and the slow gift of the place. Nothing flowers. Nothing needs to. The fauna is rarer still — you'll go a long while seeing nothing at all, and then a small gray something will pass through a seam and be gone, unidentifiable, unbothered. The district is quiet: the low hiss and drip of water, the far tick of stone settling a fraction of a millimeter, the soft click of a composite tile completing another year of itself. Sometimes, in the deep seams, you hear a faint harmonic — the buildings and the water and the cold finding a resonance — so low and patient it might just be the district thinking.

And here is the thing that no map admits: reality in Hollow Reach is thin, and getting thinner. It's not dramatic — nothing screams or tears. It's quieter and worse than that. The district is a place where the rules are still being drafted, and the draft is visible. You can catch the world in the act of rendering: a wall that, observed too long and too directly, seems to be made of its own description; a shadow that falls a little too precisely; a puddle that ripples to a sound you didn't hear. Turn a corner twice and it's a different corner that's nonetheless the same one. Stand still long enough and the stillness gets into you, and you come to understand that the district does not know where it ends — the fog at the edges is not weather but the place *not having decided yet*. The boundaries are a provisional agreement it keeps reconsidering.

It rains a little. It drips. The stone thickens by a hair. The alleys crowd close and the pale things guard their inward curls, and the whole slow district goes about the enormous patience of becoming, unhurried, indifferent to whether anyone is here to see it finish. Perhaps it never will. Perhaps finishing was never the point.
