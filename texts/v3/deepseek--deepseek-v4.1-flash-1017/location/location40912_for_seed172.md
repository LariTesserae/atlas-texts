# The High Separation

The works stand on a summit that has been entirely clad. You cannot touch the mountain. Every meter of the peak is sheathed in bolted steel — stepped, welded, crimped around the old rock like a hand closed over something — and out of that sheathing rise the stacks and ducts and ladder-cages of the plant, long pale bones going up into air so thin and so cold that it reads as an absence rather than a weather.

Inside the sheathing, at the end of a corridor nobody uses, there is a room.

It is small. Fifteen paces across, maybe. A drum with a flat deck, an access hatch at one end, a stub of passage at the other that turns twice and stops at a door welded shut. One chamber in a plant that must have hundreds; the only one you will ever see. And it is entirely made. The deck is plate. The walls are plate. The ribs are rolled steel, the insulation behind them mineral wool behind perforated sheet. The light comes from four fixtures in a ring. There is no rock anywhere in it, no wood, no earth, nothing that grew and nothing that was quarried and left raw. It looks new. It will go on looking new.

Then the sound.

You feel it before you are through the hatch — in the sternum, in the sinuses, in the long bones of the arms. It is not a noise in the sense of something happening. It is a condition of the room, like pressure or brightness. There is no rhythm to it, no rise and fall, nothing you can catch and tap your foot to. It is a standing wave, or several braided — a low groan carried in the plant's own structure, a mid shriek off a duct, a fine high ringing off the perforated sheet that sits at the crown of the head like a wire pulled taut. The room is a resonator. It was tuned. It has not gone out of tune.

It is deafening in the literal sense: you cannot hear your own voice through it. Shout, and the shout comes back at your jaw as pressure and nothing else. The room takes whatever you make and folds it into the standing wave, and within a breath it is indistinguishable from the rest. And still it never varies. That is the thing you cannot get used to. After an hour the ear stops hunting for variation and reclassifies the roar as silence — the floor of the world, beneath which nothing else exists. If it stopped, you would not be relieved. You would be destroyed.

The light does not change either. Four fixtures, steady, the color of a cold afternoon. No flicker, no dimming. Nobody turns them on. Nobody turns them off. The cold sits well below freezing and does not drift; the air is dry to the point of strangeness, so nothing condenses — no ice, no bead of melt, no fog lingering. Your exhalation is a brief white thing, gone before it becomes a shape. There is no water here. There has never been water here. The plant takes the water out of the air first thing, and what remains is cold and clean and mineral, and on the coldest surfaces — the underside of the hatch rim, the shaded side of the cable tray — a pale bloom settles out of that dryness. Not frost. The air's own trace salts, efflorescent, faintly crystalline, growing so slowly that nobody has ever watched it grow.

The room is mostly empty, and the emptiness is spaced. A single bench, bolted, at the far side. A single console, dark, with one handwheel. A single column of pipe coming down through the deck and going out through the ceiling, lagged and banded. Wide reaches of bare plate between them, painted in a safety orange that the cold has bleached to a dry salmon, and the paint is unmarked. No scuffs, no dust, no wear. Nothing here is wearing out, because nothing here does work that wears. The work is below, in the parts of the works you never see. This room only holds the tone.

And then the growth.

It is sparse — patches the size of a palm, widely separated, set out according to some arithmetic of their own. Pale, dry, fine: a mat of threads, grey-white with a faint pink beneath, felted into the seams of the caulking, into the roots of the bolt heads, along the top edge of the cable tray where the sheet is perforated. It has nothing you would call leaves. It does not drink. There is nothing here to drink.

What it does is grow on the sound.

Cup a patch with your hand and the roar under the palm softens by a degree you can feel in the wrist. The threads take the vibration and spend it, turning the room's pressure into whatever slow chemistry is available to a thing that lives on nothing but motion. Where the growth is thickest the standing wave is weaker by a percent or two — a dead spot in the pattern, a small soft place in the roar. The engineers measured that once and then stopped measuring. The plant's tone has a tolerance. The growth is inside the tolerance. And the growth is at equilibrium: exactly as thick as the sound allows, no thicker, and it has been exactly that thick for as long as the room has existed, which is not long, and also forever.

There is no animal life. No flies circling the fixtures, no rats in the insulation, no birds on the stacks outside. Nothing visits. Nothing nests. Nothing dies here. The only living things in the High Separation are these patches of pale hair, and they are not alive in any way that would reassure you.

So what happens in the room is nothing. The needle on the console stands where it stands. The handwheel is not turned. The bloom does not thicken. The growth does not spread. The tone does not drift. Set a bolt down on the plate and come back in a year: it is where you left it, undusted, unrusted, the bleached salmon beneath it unchanged.

The rules take a minute to learn and are impossible to keep for long. You cannot add. Whatever you make is absorbed, folded, normalized into the wave. You cannot hear. You cannot speak and be understood. You cannot leave a mark. What you can do is stand in the thin cold and let the wave move through the cage of your ribs, and watch the pale threads on the cable tray working their slow arithmetic against the noise. Some people find it unbearable within minutes. Others stay a whole shift. There is no telling in advance which you are. The room does not care. The room is not changing.

Outside, above, the stacks go up into air that is barely there, and the wind that would be a roar anywhere else is nothing against the plant's own voice. The works are louder than weather. Louder than the mountain. They have been saying the same thing, without pause, without emphasis, since the hour they were finished and switched on. The growth is eating it. Slowly — at the rate the threads take sound, it will need a hundred thousand years to make this place quiet.

It will get there. Nothing here is in a hurry. Nothing here is going to change.
