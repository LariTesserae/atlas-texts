The place is a high hollow, a cup of land caught in the shoulder of a mountain. It is small — you could walk its longest dimension in a quarter hour — but its smallness is dense with detail, so that standing in the middle you feel enclosed by a world that has forgotten to be large. The scale is a room, a grove, a single held breath.

The ground is never dry. Water stands in the low folds of the hollow in flat, dark pools the color of steeped tea, and it seeps in a thousand smaller ways: beading on the undersides of leaves, glistening on exposed roots, running in thin films over the pale, rounded stones. The stones themselves are old and settled, worn smooth by long centuries of trickling, and they fit together like the knuckles of a sleeping hand. Between them, the soil is a black, fragrant muck, rich with the decomposition of countless generations of leaves. The air holds water too, so that distance is smeared and softened, and the far rim of the hollow — a low, mossy lip of rock — seems always just on the verge of dissolving into the sky.

Vegetation is everywhere, but it is not a thicket. It is a lushness with space in it, a scattering of fern and moss and flowering things that have found their own arrangements. Great ferns arch over the pools, their fronds unrolling like slow green questions. Moss cloaks every stone, every fallen branch, every inch of the hollow's floor, so that the ground is a single soft, spongy carpet, green shading into green. There are flowers — small, pale, nodding things — that grow in ones and twos, never in masses, as if they have agreed among themselves to keep their distance. A few stunted trees, twisted by wind and time, lean over the pools, their bark furred with lichen in gray and gold. Nothing here is straight. Everything leans, curves, finds a way around.

The temperature is warm and heavy, the warmth of a greenhouse or a summer evening after rain. It is the warmth that makes things grow and rot at the same speed, and the air is thick with the smell of wet earth and green sap and the faint sweetness of things blooming and fading at once. Light falls through the mist in soft columns, shifting as the high clouds move, so that the hollow is never quite the same from one moment to the next. The light is ordinary — not dim, not bright — but the mist makes it feel borrowed, filtered through layers of air and water.

Sound is constant and layered, a low roar that resolves into parts if you listen. Water is the base of it: trickling, dripping, the soft plash of a frog entering a pool, the tiny hiss of a rivulet finding a new path over stone. Wind moves through the ferns with a dry, papery rustle that is almost a whisper but never quite goes quiet. And there are other sounds, harder to place — a faint, rhythmic knocking from somewhere in the moss, like a very small hammer on very soft wood; the occasional deep, resonant note that seems to come from the ground itself, a sound like a plucked string heard through water. These sounds are not loud in the way of thunder, but they are loud in the way of a room full of people all talking at once, a constant, living noise that fills the hollow and leaves no silence.

Animal life is rare and shy. You might see a single frog, a bright impossible green, sitting on a stone for an hour before it moves. Insects are present but few — a dragonfly with wings like stained glass, a beetle with a shell of hammered copper. There are no large animals. The place is too small, too enclosed. But the signs of them are everywhere: tracks in the mud, the neat hollows where something slept, the bones of a small bird picked clean and half-sunk in moss. They pass through, these animals, and then they are gone.

The rules of this world are simple and slow. Water moves downhill, always, but it moves with infinite patience, finding the least resistance, pooling where it must, wearing the stone by fractions of a hair's breadth per century. Growth is slow, too — the ferns unfurl over weeks, the moss creeps an inch a year, the trees add rings so thin you would need a lens to count them. Nothing here happens quickly except the rain, which can come sudden and hard, drumming on the fern-fronds, raising the pools an inch in an hour, and then passing, leaving the air washed and the light sharper for a little while. The hollow is in balance: the water that falls is the water that drains, the leaves that fall are the leaves that rot, the creatures that come are the creatures that go. It has been this way for a long time, and it will be this way for a long time yet.

But there is a strangeness here, a quality that resists naming. The air is too breathable, the water too sweet. The light falls in ways that seem to follow their own rules, brightening a single fern while leaving its neighbor in shadow. The sounds have a rhythm that is almost regular, almost a language, and if you sit still long enough you begin to feel that the hollow is not just a place but a process, a slow, patient thing that is doing something you cannot quite see — tending, perhaps, or waiting. The stones are warm to the touch even when the air is cool. The moss grows in spirals. And on certain afternoons, when the mist thickens and the light goes gold, you can hear, very faint, a sound like a bell, far away and deep underground, ringing once and then stopping, as if it had been waiting for you to listen.
