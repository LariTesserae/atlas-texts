# The Weir-Room

You reach it by crawling. There is a passage of dressed stone, a little wider than a shoulder, worn glassy at the corners where a hundred thousand shoulders have not worn it — where water has, running the same curve for so long the corners have gone smooth as river cobble. The passage is warm and dripping. It slopes down. Then it opens.

The room is small. Nine paces across, low-ceilinged, and entirely made — there is no natural surface anywhere in it, no soil, no bedrock, nothing that was not cut and fitted and bolted. But you would not know that at first, because everything has been ground down and grown over into one continuous thing. The floor is stone and iron plate, and the iron plate has been worn into soft mounds like a riverbed, and the joints between stones have filled with a fine pale silt that is not silt — it is the dust of the building itself, ground from its own walls, settled and packed and colonized. The walls rise three meters and are furred with moss to the height of the waterline and beyond, a green velvet that swallows the corners and makes the room feel rounder than it is.

Everything is wet. The air condenses on the ceiling and falls, not in drips but in a fine constant rain that is too light to feel as rain — you simply become wet, gradually, everywhere. The walls weep. There are channels cut into the stone for weeping, and they are full, and they overflow, and the overflow has worn new channels beside the old ones, and those are full too. The room is a basin and a lung.

And the sound.

At the far end, under an arch that is itself half-swallowed by moss, an iron sluice stands open. Water comes through it — not a torrent, a steady enormous pressure, a column thick as a thigh, falling into a channel that runs the room's length and out beneath the floor. The channel is old. The water has eaten it into a curve, and the curve has been there so long that the moss grows down into it in curtains, and the water hisses through the curtains with a roar that fills the room completely. There is nothing else to hear and no gap in it. It is not the roar of a thing happening; it is the roar of a thing that has been happening. Deafening, and steady as a held note. Your ears adjust to it in the way ears adjust to the sea, and then you hear the small things inside it: the tick of the condensation hitting iron, the low sub-bass hum of the pipework somewhere behind the wall, a sound like a kettle three rooms away that never boils.

The machinery is all around you and mostly invisible. Pipes emerge from the wall at chest height and dive into the floor; brass valves with their wheels half-eaten to green lace; a pressure gauge whose face is a milky cataract and whose needle has not moved in living memory, because the pressure has not moved. It is an industrial room, a working room, and it is working — that is the strangest thing. Nothing here is broken. Everything here is simply finished. The weir-room is at the end of its life and the end of its life is identical to its middle: the same water, the same roar, the same level. It has settled into its final form the way a stone settles into a streambed. There is no gradient left in it. Nothing is filling and nothing is draining. The room's equilibrium is so complete that it has become a kind of weather.

The plants know this. They are lush — absurdly so — not because the room is generous but because the room is *constant*, and constancy is what a plant wants. Liverworts sheet the walls in overlapping scales. Ferns hang from the pipework with their fronds in the falling water, splayed and unmoving, though the water pushes through them constantly; they have grown into the shape of the flow and the flow has grown into the shape of them. In the corners, where the condensation is heaviest, there are pale things that might be fungi or might be roots, webbing the joins of the stones, and they are warm to the touch because the room is warm, blood-warm, the water carrying heat from some engine further in that no one has seen in an age.

Almost nothing lives here that moves. A few snails, small and dark, patrolling the wet stone on errands of their own. A single kind of pale crustacean in the channel, blind, thin as a thread. That is the fauna, and it is enough, because the room does not need much. The emptiness is not a lack; it is the room's actual character. Between the ferns and the valves and the falling water there is a great deal of nothing — soft green hollow space, empty air you could stand in for an hour and touch nothing. The room is a container for its own sound.

You crouch by the channel and put your hand into the water. It is warm and moving and it does not change. Behind you the passage pulls a thin cold breath from somewhere, and the condensation falls, and the sluice roars its one held note, and the moss grows a fraction of nothing into the shape it has already chosen. You could come back in a lifetime and find the gauge at the same reading, the water at the same height, the fern bent at the same angle, the sound exactly as loud. It is a room that has arrived. It is the oldest stable thing you will ever stand inside, and it is the size of a room, and it is working.
