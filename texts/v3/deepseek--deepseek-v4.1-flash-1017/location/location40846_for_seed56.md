You hear it before the crack lets you through — a held note, low and rounded, so constant that at first you take it for the absence of sound. The stream you followed down has cut a slot through the rock, and the slot is warm at the edges, and then it opens, and the note resolves into a chord.

The chamber is small. You could cross it in fifteen paces; a tall person would stoop. But “small” is the wrong measure, because the room is not empty and the room is not a room. It is full — densely, patiently, entirely full — of a pale wet mass that has no bare surface anywhere to rest the eye. The floor is not a floor. It is a folded terrain of growth, layered like a map left out in the rain, and the growth climbs the walls in sheets and hangs from the ceiling in slack curtains that stir when the air moves, which it does, slowly, because the chamber breathes.

Above, where you expect stone, there is ice. The ceiling is a lid of glacier, maybe two meters thick at the center and thinning toward the edges, and it glows — a dim, underwater blue, the color of light that has come a long way through something solid. It is not bright. It is barely light at all. But after a few minutes your eyes adjust and the room assembles itself out of the dark: the blue lid, the drip, the mat going pale green and bone and wax-amber where it meets the stone.

The stone is where the story is. Along the lower walls, where the growth has not yet finished with it, you can see the transformation. Raw rock, gray and weeping, still rough from whatever fracture opened it. Then a band where the rock has gone translucent — not dissolved, not crumbled, but *changed*, its grain reordered into something waxy and faintly warm. Then tissue. The frontier creeps. Perhaps a millimeter a year. The plant does not break the stone; it negotiates with it, disassembling the lattice ion by ion and setting it down again in a new arrangement, packing the walls tighter as it goes, and leaving no rubble behind. There is no rubble anywhere. Nothing here is debris. Everything is either food, or body, or in the process of becoming one of the two.

Everything is wet. Water stands in the folds of the mat in shallow lenses that tremble when you step. Water runs down the walls in films too thin to see except where they catch the blue. Water falls from the ice — not a downpour, a *rhythm*, each drop taking its own long interval to swell and let go, so the chamber is never silent and never regular. The air is saturated; your breath goes out of you as a visible thing and hangs there. And the water is cold enough that it should be ice, and mostly is not, because the chamber is warm in its middle. Not warm like summer. Warm like a body. The heart of the mat sits a few degrees above freezing, and the gradient from there to the ice is steep and strange: you can put your hand in the growth and feel blood-temperature, then move it a forearm’s length toward the wall and feel the cold come up through the skin.

The warmth is not free. It is exhaust. The mat is doing something, and the doing is what heats it.

Because the growth is not quite a plant the way a plant is a plant. It has no green. Nothing here photosynthesizes, because nothing here sees the sun. It feeds on stone and water and the slow chemistry of its own decay, and it is built out of a tissue that is less like wood and more like a very cold, very slow crystal — a lattice that can hold a state across the whole mass at once. In the cold, its chemistry is sluggish enough that its own noise doesn’t drown it out. The whole chamber is one connected thing, and the whole thing is thinking, and the thinking is the note.

The sound is the reason the room is shaped the way it is. The note has structure: a low fundamental you feel in the sternum, a fifth above it, a shimmer of overtones that come and go as the ice above shifts its weight. The walls take the note and give it back. The chamber is being tuned. Somewhere in the growth there is a curve that is not random — a throat, a bowl, an ear — and it is not finished, and the plant is slowly, millimeter by millimeter, making it rounder. When the ice groans overhead the note bends and the whole mat shivers in phase, a slow wave of faint light running through it from the far wall to the near, taking minutes to make the circuit. The light is not reflection. The tissue makes it. It is dim and cold and it pulses like something asleep.

The loudness is the strange part. Nothing here moves quickly. The growth is patient to the point of geology; the transformation front is a crawl; a new curtain of growth takes a human lifetime to hang. And yet the room is loud — not painful, but full, a sound you have to raise your voice over, a sound that gets into your teeth. It is the loudness of many slow things agreeing. The drip. The tick and creak of the ice lid settling. The low chord. And underneath it, a fine continuous crackle, like a fire heard through a wall, which is the sound of root tips working mineral bonds at the scale where matter is grainy. It is the noise of the room being built. It never stops, because the room is never finished.

Life is everywhere in it. The mat is not one thing but a city: pale things the length of your thumb moving in loose herds over the folds, eyeless, wet, grazing the soft edges where the tissue is newest; borers that leave clean round holes and plug them behind themselves; drifting fibers in the air that are not seeds and not insects but something in between, moving with a purpose too slow to see except over minutes, gathering where the note is strongest. In the standing water there are things that flatten themselves against the bottom and pulse. Everything is small. Nothing is bigger than a hand. But there are so many of them, layered through the growth and the water and the air, that the room feels inhabited the way a hive feels inhabited — densely, deliberately, with the sense that each presence has a job.

Some of them do. That is the other strangeness. Certain of the small animals are not separate creatures at all; they are buds, grown by the mat, loosed into the chamber to carry water up a fold or prune a dying curtain or move a grain of dissolved stone from one wall to another. They work for a season, or a decade, and then they are reabsorbed. You can tell which ones by the way they do not scatter when you approach. They are not afraid of you. They are not interested in you. They are the room’s hands.

There are rules here, and they are not the rules of the surface. Nothing is built. There is no tool, no mark, no artifact anywhere in the chamber — and if you leave one, it will not last. Set down a metal thing and the water films will find it, and the growth will gather around it, and in a year or ten it will be feedstock. The room does not tolerate the made. It only tolerates the grown and the growing and the eaten. Water behaves oddly, too: it runs in films and lenses and stays liquid well below the temperature where it should stiffen, until it touches certain faces of raw stone — cold teeth, the growth calls them, if it calls anything anything — and there it flashes to ice all at once with a small sharp report that is part of the chorus. The ice on those faces is clear and hard and full of trapped blue.

And time is uneven. The growth is slow, but the thinking is not. Stand still long enough and you begin to feel the mismatch — the minutes-long pulse of light, the millimeter-per-year frontier, and beneath both of them something running very fast, the note flickering at a rate too fine to hear, the room taking in the drip and the ice-creak and the crackle of its own roots and *sorting* them, shaping itself around what it learns. It is building an ear. Not a metaphor: a curve of tissue that will catch and hold a sound, and it has been rounding that curve for a few thousand years and will be rounding it for a few thousand more. What it is listening for, or whether it is listening at all, or whether the listening is the same act as the growing — the chamber does not say. It only says the note, and holds it, and goes on.

You will leave the way you came, up the warm slot, into the cold. The sound will follow you a long way. And behind you, in the wet and the blue and the patient crackle, the room will close the water you tracked in, and take the warmth of your standing there, and go on tuning itself in the dark, unfinished, embryonic, loud.
