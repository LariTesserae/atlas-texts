# The Hollow of the Weeping Works

You come upon it the way water does—slowly, then all at once—descending through a seam in the hills where the path turns to running mud and the air grows thick as a held breath. The Hollow is not visible from above; it announces itself first as sound and only then as a place.

## The Shape of the Land

The Hollow is a bowl within a bowl, a small settlement wedged into a natural depression perhaps half a kilometer across, so that you see the whole of it at once from the rim and feel you could hold it in your hands. It is not deep, this bowl—the rim is gentle, grown over with creeping ferns and fat-leaved shrubs that spill toward the center—but it is deep enough that the water that gathers here never quite leaves. It seeps down through the terraced soil, collects in the lowest quarter where buildings stand on stilts of green-black stone, and pools in the shafts and sunken courtyards that pock the settlement like the pores of a sponge.

The ground is soft everywhere. Mud is a given, ankle-deep in the alleys, but the builders here learned long ago to work with it: the foundations are floating rafts of timber and rubble, the streets are slatted walkways that shift and creak underfoot, and the ground floors of every building are open to the floods, designed for it, with drainage channels carved into the stone and iron grates that hum with the sound of moving water at all hours.

The walls are stone—dark, water-stained, veined with rust and with the green of a hundred years of moss. They rise two, three, sometimes four stories, leaning into one another across the narrow lanes so that you can touch both sides of the street with outstretched arms. Balconies overlap; roofs of copper gone verdigris and weeping an unceasing bead of condensation crowd the sky. It never quite rains, but the air is so wet that water runs down every surface as though it did. The buildings weep. It is the first thing you notice, and the last thing you forget: every wall, every pipe, every eaves is wet, and the water gathers, and it falls, and it falls, and the sound of it is everywhere.

## The Works

At the center of the Hollow, occupying its lowest and wettest quarter, the Weeping Works stand. You might call them a factory, if a factory could be a place where the earth itself is the engine. The Works were built to draw power from the slow seep of water through the Hollow's soil—they harness the pressure, the constant, patient gradient of a landscape that never stops draining—and they have run for so long, so quietly, that no one now remembers what they were first built to make.

What they do now is this: they process the water. The water comes down and the Works catch it, filter it through beds of gravel and iron filings and crushed shell, and they move it, and they heat it, and they let it go again. Iron pipes the circumference of a man's torso weave through every street in the Hollow, buried and half-buried, exposed and bolted and sweating. They groan. They tick. They carry the water in a vast slow circulation, a bloodstream of the settlement, and around the clock they speak in the language of metal and pressure: a constant low thunder of flow, punctuated by knocks and gurgles and the occasional great shuddering slam of a valve that makes the ground tremble and the birds all startle from the rooftops.

The Works have a smell. Steam and rust, wet stone and hot iron, the sharp clean minerals of water freshly drawn from underground, and underneath it all a faint organic sourness, a hint of the deep and living soil the water has passed through. The steam is constant. The chimneys do not smoke; they breathe. They exhale white plumes into the wet air, and those plumes join the mist and the low cloud and become the ceiling of the Hollow, so that even at noon the light is dim and diffuse—everything seen through a veil.

## The Sound

The Hollow is loud, but not with voices. The people here speak quietly, if at all; they have the habit of those who live among machinery, leaning close to hear, speaking in the shorthand of workers. What fills the air is the place itself:

- The endless falling of water—dripping from roofs, running down walls, trickling into grates, pooling, overflowing, taking the long slow fall from the upper terraces to the lower pools. It is not one sound but a thousand, a chorus of small water sounds that never stops.
- The pipes, which have their own voices: the deep hum of the big mains, the high thin singing of the small ones in the upper houses, the arrhythmic clanging of the pressure reliefs, the slurp and gulp of the settling tanks.
- The engines of the Works themselves—not steam engines, not quite, but water turbines and screw pumps and something older, something the first builders sunk into the bedrock: a great slow-turning mechanism whose tremor passes up through the ground and into the bones. You feel it in your teeth. You feel it when you lie down. Everyone who lives here has a tremor to their handwriting; they are born with it.
- The music of overflow: the terraced spillways that carry water from one level of the Hollow to the next, half-carved into the rock, half-tiled with glazed ceramic in colors faded to bruisey greens and yellows, so that the water comes down not just sounding but looking like something, a blurred and glittering sheet seen through steam.
- Bells. The Works bells—great cracked brass things hung in the chimneys—strike the hours, but the hours here are the hours of the water's cycle, not the sun's, and they do not align with any clock you know. They are marking the pulse of the Works: the fill and the drain, the pressure and the release. The bells are loud and flat and slightly wrong in their tuning, and they fall across the Hollow in waves, and the people set their days by them as surely as they set them by the fear of flood.

## The Green

The vegetation is lush, absurdly so, fed by constant moisture and the warmth of the Works. It grows where it is not wanted and where no one could want it: in the gutters, on the walls, across the faces of buildings so thoroughly that many walls are more moss than stone. The moss here is thick as carpet, varied in color—grave-greens, sallow yellows, deep near-black—and it holds water, and it lets it go, and it keeps the walls always green and always dripping.

Ferns sprout from cracks in the masonry, high up, unfurling in the steam. Vines hang from the balconies, heavy with moisture, and they trail across the lanes so that you part them like curtains. In the flooded courtyards, rushes and reeds grow, and the people harvest them for thatch and basketry, and they are always cutting them back and the reeds are always coming back, faster than they can be cut.

There are trees, too—a few, planted in great stone tubs where the water pools and can be controlled. They are broad-leaved and pale, their leaves dripping, their bark furred with lichen, and they grow slowly, patiently, in the dim air, leaning toward the steam-vents where it is warm. Their roots have broken the tubs and gone down into the saturated ground, and they are part of the drainage now, part of the structure. The biggest of them grows from the roof of the Works' main hall, a tree that was there before the hall was built and has absorbed it, its roots cracking through the roof in a slow process that everyone agrees will someday be a problem and that everyone has agreed about for generations. The water from its leaves falls in a constant fine rain onto the copper below, and the copper beneath it is greenest of all.

## The People and the Work

No animals live in the Hollow. This is a thing you notice only after a while, and then cannot unnotice. No dogs, no rats, no birds—not for lack of food or shelter, but because the place is loud and wet and metallic in a way that is wrong for animals, and the people have simply made it their own without them. What moves in the alleys is people, and tools, and water. What fills the niches where sparrows should be are small shrines of copper wire and glazed tile, offerings to the works, little icons of valves and pumps. The people touch them as they pass. It is not clear to what, exactly, they pray.

They dress in waxed cloth and oilskin, colors chosen to hide the mud, and they wear wooden clogs with iron rims, and the sound of clogs on the slatted walkways is the human music of the place, a clatter that joins the water sound. They work the Works in shifts, but the shifts are not fixed, because the Works run on the water and the water runs on the season and the season is not clockwork and the pressure comes when it comes. They are mechanically minded, these people, and their hands are always damp and always a little black with grease and filings. They are careful with one another: there are not so many of them that they can afford carelessness. A hundred? Two hundred? The Hollow holds them all and holds their history, the tiers of building rising and falling with the water's rise and fall, each generation built atop the last, each generation of structures visible as a different pattern of stone or tile, so that the walls are their chronology.

The wealth of the place is water and the command of water, and this is what the Works trade in, in a small way—processed water, filtered and heated and moved to places beyond the Hollow that have need of it, carried out in iron tanks on carts with spring suspension that groan away up the one long road out of the bowl. It is not a large trade. The Hollow is a small place, and it makes no pretense of being more than it is.

## The Upper Terraces and the Rim

The bowl's rim is the dry margin, where the water that gathers in the Hollow has not yet gathered and the growth is something other than the moss and ferns below. Here the terraces rise in gentle steps, the walls low, the sky—if you can call the gray and shifting ceiling of cloud a sky—more visible. The people come up here to hang laundry, to smoke, to look down at the place where they live and see it whole. The light is better here, though "better" is a relative thing in the Hollow; it never reaches full brightness.

The rim also holds the old waterworks, the ruins of the first attempts to tame the seep, abandoned and overgrown, their walls collapsed into the earth and their pipes burst and rusted to lace. These ruins are quiet (or as quiet as anything in the Hollow gets) and the moss is thickest there, and the people do not go there often, though they sometimes climb up at the turning of the great cycle, when the Works marks its slow years, to leave small offerings and to be alone with the water.

## The Air and the Light

The atmosphere is a constant soft gray, dim and warm, saturated with steam and the fine mist that rises off the pools. The light is diffuse and shadowless; there are no hard edges anywhere in the Hollow. Colors are muted, water-darkened, but not sad—the verdigris of the copper, the moss-greens, the wet stone the color of old slate, the glazed tiles of the spillways the color of riverbed pebbles. Everything is a little indistinct, seen through the veil of the air, and the distances are less than they seem. You cannot see the far end of the Hollow from where you stand, though it is close; the steam and the humidity hide it. The air is warm and heavy and smells of iron and rain and the green breath of growing things. Breathing it is like drinking something.

## The Rules of the World Here

The water falls and rises and the Works breathes and the people work. The cycles are slow—a great flood every few years, a long dry season that is never quite dry, a rhythm of pressure and release that governs all that they do. When the pressure builds, the valves sing and the bells ring and the people go up the terraces and wait, and the water comes down and through the Works and out, and the landscape is washed clean again, and the moss grows back, and the people come down and rebuild what needs rebuilding and clean the filters and set the valves and wait.

There is no drama here, or rather, the drama is the slow one: the patience of water and machinery and a place that has settled into its own way of being. The Hollow is not a place that changes quickly. It is a place that has been doing what it does for a long time, and it will be doing it for a long time yet, and if you stay you will come to feel the slowness in yourself, the way the tremor of the Works gets into your bones, the way you begin to set your days by the bells and the flow and the patient conversation of the water and the iron.

The place is not unfriendly. It is just busy with its own slow business, and it will let you stand at the rim and listen to it weep and sing and clatter, and it will let you feel small and warm and damp and watched over by something that is not watching you, and after a while you will find you have begun, without deciding to, to smell the water for its changes, and to listen for the valves, and to know the hollows of the place as something that holds you, and to think of it as a place you might, perhaps, stay.
