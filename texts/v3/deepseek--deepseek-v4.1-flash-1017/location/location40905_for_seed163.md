The sound arrives before the place does. It is a roar with no single source — a thousand waterfalls, or the inside of a shell the size of a valley, or wind combed through a vast and unseen harp. You climb toward it through cloud, and the cloud is not weather but the place itself, the medium you breathe.

The Shatterlands are high, wet, and unfinished. This is young country: the ridges are still raw-edged, the gorges still cutting themselves deeper each year, the rock still remembering it was recently something else. Nothing here has settled into the softness of age. Instead the water and the green have simply overtaken the rawness, flooding every fracture before it could heal.

Water is everywhere, and it is not decoration — it is the engine. It hangs in the air as a cold, permanent bright fog that beads on every surface and drips from every edge. It shears off the cliffs in white curtains and gathers in the hollows as black, still pools. Rivulets thread the rock like veins, some steaming faintly where they cross warm stone, others cold enough to ache. The ground, where you can find it, is a sponge of moss and root that squelches and releases with each step. Rain does not arrive in storms so much as in long patient sheets that last for days; when it lifts, the light comes through whiter and more glaring than before, and every leaf throws back a lens-flare of droplets. This is a land seen through wet glass, always.

And the green. The green is total. It is not that plants grow here — it is that the landscape *is* plant, and the rock and water are merely what the plant has been given to work with. Ferns the height of houses unfurl their fronds in slow motion; hanging gardens cascade from every ledge, roots woven into roots; the cliffs wear living pelts of moss and vine that bloom in colors of rust and emerald and pale silver. The great trunks are almost incidental — what you notice is the lattice, the canopy, the sheer architecture of chlorophyll. It fills the air, the space, the sightlines. Even the fog feels green.

The temperatures are cool, and the coolness is a held breath — never cold enough to still the water, never warm enough to let you forget it. You move through the day within a few degrees of shivering.

Scale opens when the mist parts. This is not a valley but a region: ridge beyond ridge, gorge beyond gorge, each one a variation on the same wet and green theme, receding into more cloud. But the presences are scattered. Between the great fern groves and the fern-choked spires there are long, quiet stretches — bare wet stone, a single mossy boulder, a wide grey nothing where the water runs and no plant has yet taken hold. Emptiness is a real material here, balanced against the abundance.

Markings: here and there, a structure. A rusted lattice tower, half-swallowed, still humming something low. A shed roof barely visible under a mantle of vine, a pipe running out of it into the mist. A broken walkway of grated metal spanning a gap, one end drooping into the white. They are sparse, these marks — you might walk half a day and pass three. But they are industrial, and they are still working, or still failing, in ways you can hear. The thrum under the roar, the occasional shudder of machinery somewhere out of sight, the grinding complaint of a pump that has been running for a very long time.

The light is the other overwhelming thing. It comes from everywhere, diffused by fog, so that bright is not a direction but a condition. Shadows are soft, fugitive things. Highlights bead and glare. You squint even on overcast days. At the edges of the day, the white light turns faintly golden and the whole wet world glows from within.

Fauna is rare — a bird's call might be the only animal sound in hours, though the air hums with insects you rarely see. The sound, though, is the constant. The roar is not background; it is a pressure, a presence, a thing that makes your teeth ache and your ears strain and your voice feel small. You learn to read it: the roar near the big falls, the hiss near the steaming fissures, the slow drip-tick in the deep fern shade that nonetheless fills your skull.

And it is all happening slowly. The rock is still emerging; the water is still deciding its channels; the plant is still reaching. But you cannot see it happen. This is a landscape being built in geological patience, one leaf-fall and one drip at a time, and it will look almost exactly like this when you are gone.

A place of bright wet green, unfinished, roaring, and mostly empty. It asks nothing of you but to stand still and listen.
