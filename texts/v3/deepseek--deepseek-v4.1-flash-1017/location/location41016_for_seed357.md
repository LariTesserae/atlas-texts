## The High Weir

The High Weir is a made landscape with no makers left in it. It sits high — a broad, irregular shelf of green construction above the cloud line — and yet it is hot, hot as a hayloft, hot as the inside of a compost heap. The altitude would be cold elsewhere. Here the plant mass holds heat; the wet floors steam; the air feels breathed-on.

There is no original ground. If you dig, you do not find soil over rock. You find root cable, pith, fused leaf-litter, and a springy membrane of living mat; beneath that, more plant, warm and wet; beneath that, hollow chambers where water booms and runs. The place is built entirely, but not with tools. No metal, no glass, no carved stone, no rope that was twisted by hands. The builders are the plants themselves, and they build by grafting, swelling, tensioning, leaking, and growing into one another. Where two cut surfaces touch, they fuse. Where water collects, the tissue swells and makes a cistern. Where heat rises, stems extend into chimneys and arches. It is architecture without technology — a city grown by its own materials.

The terrain rises in terraces and sharp new scarps. It is young: nothing has been rounded yet. Edges are raw, joints are fresh, split stems still show their wet inner fibers. The whole region looks extruded recently, as if the plant mass heaved itself up out of the cloud and has not finished settling. Tall towers of bundled stalks stand far apart across shallow wet plazas. Between them are long, empty intervals of glistening mat and warm standing water — the density is sparse, balanced with emptiness. You can walk for a day across the Weir and pass only a handful of great plant structures, each separated from the next by a silence of wet distance.

Everything visible is vegetation: load-bearing trunks grown into arches; root cables under tension; thorn-rebar; roofs of overlapping leaves; bladder cisterns; floors of interlaced stem. Some surfaces are green and growing; some are brown and structural; some are pale and swollen with water. Nothing is dead in the way wood is dead — even the brown parts are still part of the metabolism, still creaking and taking water. The place smells of cut stems, hot sap, wet cellulose, and rain striking warm leaves.

Water is constant. It condenses on every surface and runs in threads down the plant walls. It collects in the low seams between terraces and lies near the surface, trembling when something grows beneath it. Rain comes in hot bursts; steam rises after; the air stays heavy. The walls weep. The floors are slick. Pools are warm to the touch. In the hollow stems, water falls from one chamber to another with a sound like tuned drums.

The light is ordinary — bright, diffuse, the flat white of a hot overcast — but it is broken everywhere by leaf and stem into moving green shade. Nothing is gloomy. The place is too wet and too active for gloom.

And it is loud. Not with voices. There are no animals here, not even insects. The sound is water and plant and air: water thundering into hollow galleries, wind sawing through leaf roofs, stems creaking under load, joints popping as they fuse, cisterns overflowing, whole terraces shifting with a low grinding sigh. It is the noise of a foundry made of wet wood and living fiber.

The rules of the world are simple and strange. Growth is construction. A cut is a joint waiting to happen. Water is the medium of everything — carried, stored, pumped, released. The plants pollinate by rain and concussion; seeds lodge in wet seams and swell into new buttresses. There is no animal life to graze or browse or carry. The vegetation is total: substrate, architecture, machinery, and inhabitant all at once.

It is also unstable. The Weir is turbulent in the way a storm is turbulent, except the storm is made of growth. New terraces rise in a season; old ones split and collapse into the water; floods scour channels through the mat; entire towers lean, fuse with neighbors, and become bridges. The place has the energy of a bazaar — canopies opening, stalls of leaf unfurling, passages closing — but no crowd. It is an anthill without ants, a city that is continually renovating itself though no one has ever lived in it.

You hear the High Weir before you see it: a roar of water and friction coming from above the cloud. Then the cloud thins and there it is — sparse, high, hot, wet, green, and busy with its own construction. A made environment with no machines. A plant city with no citizens. A young landscape that has already built itself and is already tearing itself down.
