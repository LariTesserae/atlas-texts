# The Ringing Shelf

There is no water here, and the fact asserts itself before anything else — not as absence but as pressure, a dryness so total it registers like altitude sickness. The ground is a high plateau of laminated salt and sintered mineral, terraced at intervals of perhaps half a kilometer, each terrace a step down of maybe forty meters, the risers fluted and pale, the treads flat and crusted with a rime of something that is not frost. The air is hot. It is hot in the way of a foundry's exhaust, dry and bright and *smelling of nothing*, because there is nothing to smell — no rot, no sap, no soil. Only mineral. Occasionally a gust carries a burnt-ceramic tang, and when it does, it comes from the east, where the terraces break down into a run of low ridges that hum.

The plateau stands very high. The horizon is close and the sky above it is a hard, thin, overexposed white-blue that it hurts to look at directly, and at night the stars are too many and too sharp, and the salt underfoot gives them back doubled, so that for a moment you cannot tell which field is above. Distances lie here. A ridge that looks an hour away is four. A ridge that looks four hours away is an hour, or is not there at all, or was there when you looked and is not when you look again. The heat wobbles the middle distance into a vertical smear, and inside the smear shapes stand that are certainly standing and are certainly not standing.

**The sound.** This is the thing you notice second and never stop noticing. The whole shelf rings. It is not a loud noise in the sense of a crowd or a machine shop; it is loud in the sense of being *everywhere and inescapable*, a low composite drone made of thousands of overlapping tones, some so low they are felt in the jaw rather than heard, some high and thin as wire. The terraces are the instrument. Each riser is a different thickness, a different mineral layering, and each holds a slightly different pitch, and the whole shelf bows and shivers under the thermal gradient of day against night and settles back — slowly, over hours, in a patient slide of expansion and contraction. Walk from one terrace to the next and the note under your feet changes, like stepping your weight across the keys of an organ the size of a county. In the late afternoon, when the cooling front moves across from the west, the terraces crack softly, one after another in a traveling line, and the sound of it runs ahead of the cooling like a slow applause that takes twenty minutes to pass.

And beneath the ringing there is the other sound: the engines.

**The engines are not built.** This is the hard thing to accept, and it takes days to accept it. The shelf is industrial — it is full of heat, of pressure, of circulated material, of repeating cycles that convert gradient into motion, of byproducts and slags and exhausted reagents — and yet no one built any of it and nothing has been constructed. There are no walls, no stacks, no pipes. Instead there are *organs* of the landscape: places where a thermal gradient has organized itself into a circulation. You will come over a rise and find a bowl, two hundred meters across, its floor a whorl of banded mineral, and the bowl is breathing — drawing the hot air of the low terraces down through a ring of narrow vents and lifting it back up the center in a slow column of shimmer, and in that circulation the mineral is being separated, band by band, layer by layer, a refining process as slow as a glacier and as patient as rust. Fine white powder collects on the upwind lip. Downwind, the refined salts have been deposited in a fan, and the fan is thicker than you are tall, and it is growing the way a delta grows, one afternoon's dustfall at a time. Nobody did this. The gradient did it. It has been doing it — the erosion of the terraces says so, the settled, weathered, wholly-in-its-own-state arrangement of the place says so — for a very long time, long enough that the whorl is old and calm and has stopped changing its behavior, and now only recurs.

There are dozens of these. Some are chemical, some are thermal, some appear to be electrical: I found a shallow crack between two terraces lined with a dark vitreous glaze, and it gave a stiff little shock to the knuckle, and the air above it had the flat stink of ozone. The crack ran for a good kilometer, and it ran with regularity, dot-dash, dot-dash. I do not think it was carrying information. But it was carrying *something*, in the way that a clock carries something.

**Almost nothing lives here, and yet the fauna are overwhelming.** Both are true and the truth of it is in what the fauna *are*. They are not animals in the sense of bodies that come and go and eat and die. They are vast, thin, distributed, and they arrive as weather. The first time, you think it is a heat shimmer or a trick of the salt-flat horizon. A shimmer stands up — tall, thirty meters, made of stacked rings of dust turning slowly in place, and it moves along the terrace edge without disturbing anything, and the seeds of it are small things: fine grit with a slow torsion to it, the grains turning about each other in loose chains, and the chains finding each other, and cohering, and where they cohere they hold a shape. Billions of them, spread across half the shelf. A single one of these things is indistinguishable from dust. Together they are a *creature*, or the word is not right but nothing else is, and it crosses the shelf in the afternoon and by evening the wind has taken it apart, and it is dust again — actual dust, ordinary dust, and the ordinary dust is turning, faintly, in place.

They come with the day's heat. Every day. They are far apart, each one, hundreds of meters of empty ringing salt between them, so that you walk through a world that seems empty and are, the whole time, inside something. They crisscross and their crossing points are where the high thin tones come from, I am nearly sure of it. Their passing is what keeps the shelf clean, keeps the salt scoured and bright and free of dust except for that which they themselves keep making. They are the weather of this place. It is not a metaphor to say that the heat has a body.

And there are others, less daily. Once, at the far edge where the terraces give out, I watched a process that took four hours: a long low line of ridges, dark against the white, and along the line, slowly, the ridges lifted and laid themselves down again, lifted and laid down, one after another, in sequence, each of them thirty meters of stone moving ten or twelve centimeters, and the sound it made was a grinding that came up through my feet rather than reaching my ears. It did not advance. It did not retreat. It was turning over, the way a sleeper turns, and after four hours it had not finished and might not ever finish, and the light went gold and then red and then the false dusk that comes when the sun is below the rim but the sky still fires the salt, and I left it still turning over, and I do not know what it was.

**The rules here are few and they are not reliable.** The world breaks down in specific, repeatable ways. Directions in the middle distance are not conserved; I have walked toward a ridge for an hour and found it no nearer, and turned to go back and found the camp behind me. Objects left in the open are sometimes found where they were left and sometimes found a half-kilometer downwind with no trace of having moved. There is a class of shadow on the empty terraces that does not correspond to any object casting it — a long, thin, blurred shadow lying flat on the salt at midday when the sun is overhead, and it thins and widens and thins as though something overhead were passing, and nothing is overhead. Twice I have heard my own footsteps arrive slightly before I took them, a small impudent doubling that is not an echo in any acoustically respectable sense; the ringing is already so complex that the ear has no idea what is echo and what is forewarning, and this, I think, is the point. The place is loud enough and strange enough that you cannot trust the base case. The instrument and the music have become the same thing.

But the breakdown is slow. That is the mercy of it. The false shadows last minutes. The mislaid directions correct themselves by evening. The ringing shelf is what it is and it is not going to become something else before you can look away; it has been settled in this form for a span you could not measure with the terraces' own slow cracking, and the whole of the weirdness is *stable* weirdness, patient, at its own equilibrium, running its refineries and its daily weather-creatures and its long turning-over stones with no haste whatsoever. It has not changed its behavior in a very long time. It will not change it for you.

Stand still, then. The heat is on the back of your neck. The white ground runs out to nothing in every direction, and the nothing is terraced, and the terraces are singing, each to each, in a chord that does not resolve and does not need to. The dust is turning, faintly, in place. Somewhere east of you a bowl is breathing and refining a band of yellow mineral into a band of white. Something you will never see whole has just come apart and gone back to being grains of grit, and the grit is still holding the shape of what it was, for a moment, before the wind. The shadow with nothing above it slides across the salt and thins. And the note under your feet changes, very slightly, as the terrace cools.
