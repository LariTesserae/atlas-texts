# The Galleries of Khent

You emerge into darkness and immediately understand you are inside something—the weight of enclosure presses at the edge of your awareness. Your eyes adjust to find that darkness is not absolute. There is a faint luminescence, greenish and cold, emanating from the walls themselves.

The walls are vegetation.

Not moss-covered, not lined with creeping ivy. The walls *are* plant material—a dense, woody matrix that has calcified and hardened into something between bark and stone. The texture is ancient beyond reckoning; there are no growth rings visible, just a smoothed, grain-crossed surface that feels simultaneously organic and mineral. When you touch it, it doesn't yield. It has settled into its final form millions of years ago, the cells compressed and sealed into something that will not rot, will not decay, will not change.

The galleries stretch in both directions—low ceilings no more than twice your height, corridors that widen and narrow without clear geometry, chambers that open into other chambers. The architecture is not built but *grown*, or rather, it is the fossil record of growing, arrested at some point so distant that the distinction between construction and geology has become meaningless. Branching passages fork and rejoin like the veins of a leaf, but at the scale of human habitation. You are walking through the interior of something that was once alive.

The luminescence comes from the walls themselves—a phosphorescent bloom within the compressed plant matter. It glows in faint, pulsing waves that follow no visible pattern. Sometimes the green brightens to a sickly jade; sometimes it nearly vanishes, leaving you in a green-tinted twilight. The light is cold. It casts no shadows because it seems to emanate from depth rather than source, as though the walls are glowing from inside their own structure.

The air is cool—not cold, but crisp in a way that suggests the absence of warmth rather than the presence of cold. There is no moisture. The atmosphere is dry in a way that makes your throat tighten after a few minutes. This place has never known water, or knew it so long ago that every molecule evaporated and never returned. The dryness is ancient.

And yet it is not empty.

Hanging from the ceiling in vast, irregular sheets are curtains of *something*—plant matter again, but different. Dried, papery, almost translucent where the faint light passes through them. These are not cobwebs; they are too substantial, too deliberate. They hang in loose waves, like fabric or like the dried strips of bark that peel from dead trees, but they cover nearly every surface. They are the interior of the place continuing its slow existence without you. They move with air currents you cannot feel—a gentle, perpetual flutter that creates a sound.

The sound is not loud in any conventional sense, but it is *pervasive*. It is the sound of a thousand tiny vibrations—the dried sheets moving against themselves, the subtle shifts of air through countless crevices, the occasional crack or settlement of the ancient wood-stone as it contracts in the cool darkness. It is a layered whisper, a susurrus that has been continuous for eons, patient and inevitable. Sometimes it intensifies; sometimes it recedes. There is a rhythm to it, but the cycle is slow—changes across hours or days, not minutes. The hum of a world moving at geological pace.

The floor is covered in a fine dust—not sand, not soil. Decomposition product. The slow breakdown of the hanging sheets as they dry and fragment and fall. The dust is so fine it barely disturbs when you walk through it, just swirls gently in your wake. It smells of nothing. Scent requires volatiles, and nothing volatile survives this dryness.

The galleries go deeper. You have not seen the extent of them—they curve away into darkness in both directions, and some passages descend in shallow slopes, always low, always claustrophobic, always architectured by a living process you cannot quite imagine. The vegetative walls continue unchanged. The sheets hang. The faint green glow persists.

There are no creatures here. No insects, no fungal networks, no life that moves or feeds. Yet the place is not dead—it is in a state beyond death, a final equilibrium that has persisted so long it has become the natural state of things. The vegetation is not growing. It has no capacity to grow. But it is also not decaying. It has found a form stable enough to endure in this airless, waterless, lightless depth.

This is the end state. This is the place where all process slows and stops and becomes architecture.

You walk deeper. The sound continues its patient susurration. The walls glow their sickly phosphorescent green. The dust swirls with no purpose behind it. The air is cool and dry and tastes of minerals. 

And somewhere in the darkness ahead, deeper still, the galleries continue.
