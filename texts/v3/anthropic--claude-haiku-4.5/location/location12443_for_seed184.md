# The Silt Bazaar

The water here is not transparent but *inhabited*—a brown-gold soup so thick it barely moves. You wade through it rather than swim, your hands pushing dense suspension. The depth above is unmeasurable; you stopped looking for the surface three turns ago. The pressure sits on your shoulders like old weight, familiar now.

This is a market that has sunk and stayed sunk.

The structures around you are brick-and-iron, barnacled but intact. Warehouse walls rise in layered tiers, their foundations long since surrendered to the sediment. Wooden scaffolding—reinforced, ancient, still load-bearing—splices between buildings in a web that someone, decades or centuries ago, decided was permanent. It holds. The wood is so thoroughly waterlogged it has become stone-adjacent, dark and heavy and utterly still. Rope traces paths between posts. Copper piping, green with verdigris, feeds into nothing visible, or perhaps feeds everything; you can't tell where the system begins or if it still operates.

The air—if you can call it that—is barely air. It's the thin breath-space at the surface of the silt, a membrane where the water meets a thin pocket of old atmosphere. You keep your head in this skin, where it smells of rust and algae and something mineral that tastes like metal on your tongue. The moisture enters everything: your lungs, your clothes, your thoughts.

Light comes from sources you don't fully understand. Not sunlight. Perhaps it never was. There are chambers within the warehouses where bioluminescence has taken root—clusters of fungi that glow faint blue-green, cultivated or perhaps just tolerated by whoever still tends them. The glow is so weak it deepens the shadows more than it fills them. Most of what you see is suggestion, outline, the memory of shape. Your eyes work harder than they should.

The buildings are not uniform. They were built in layers, eras layered like geological strata. The oldest structures—pre-flood, you think, because they have different angles, different proportions—sit foundation-deep. Above them, later construction: brick-built shops and processing facilities, their purpose marked by faded lettering still barely readable on the walls. *Textile? Processing House 4.* *Grain.* *Mechanized Something-or-Other.* The lettering stops being legible about thirty years back, you estimate from the growth pattern of the verdigris.

The newest buildings are the strangest. They were built *as* the water was here, adapted to it from the start. Grates instead of walls in some places. Channels built into the architecture. What you might call windows if they weren't completely open to the medium. Whoever still maintains this place has learned to metabolize the water rather than resist it.

And there is still maintenance. That's what unsettles you most.

The machines are everywhere—small, mostly, scaled to human operation. Pump systems thick with corrosion still clack and shudder on slow cycles, moving water between reservoirs, filtering the silt into something nearly transparent in sealed glass chambers. You watch the cycle: push, settle, filter, deposit. It takes hours for the silt to separate. Hours. The machines work at their patient industrial pace, indifferent to their own drowning. Pulleys creak as they turn. Belts—rubber long gone, now some kind of leather or treated plant-fiber—squeal very faintly. The sound is so quiet you're not sure it's real. Your ears might be inventing it.

The vegetation is dense but strange. It doesn't grow *up.* It grows *out,* in tangles and mats that cling to the structures. Vines like muscular wrists wrap the piping. Creepers dense as fur cover entire warehouse walls, their leaves small and fleshy and a sickly yellow-green. Some kind of moss carpets the lower levels—the things you wade through. It's warm and soft and you don't want to think too hard about what's living in it. There are flowers, tiny things, pale and almost translucent, blooming from the vines in sequences that might be timed to something you don't perceive. Blooms appear, hold for days, vanish. A cycle that is extremely slow, extremely patient.

The water is warm. That's disorienting—you expected cold, the density of drowning. Instead it's blood-temperature, thick as syrup, and the warmth makes the slowness worse. Nothing wants to move fast in warmth like this. Everything sedates toward entropy.

Fish are rare. You've seen three—pale, eyeless or nearly so, shapes that made more sense as feeling than as sight. They move rarely, hang suspended in the water as if resting, as if the effort of living here has convinced them that stillness is the only strategy worth pursuing. Something larger moved once in your peripheral vision—a shadow with weight—but you didn't turn to follow it. Best not.

The silence is almost unbroken. The machines work without fanfare. Water displacement is a soft thing. Every now and then—hours apart—you hear something metallic shift, a chain adjust, a weight settle. These sounds carry strangely through the silt, arriving without direction, from everywhere and nowhere.

And there is a presence. Not visible. Not quite tangible. But someone is maintaining the pumps. Someone has been pruning the vegetation, carefully, surgically, keeping it from completely occluding the pathways. Someone has hung the glowing fungi in chambers in specific patterns—and if you stare long enough at those patterns, you start to see a logic, a grid, a record of something being counted or tracked. 

You have not seen this person.

The whole market is in perfect balance: waterlogged but functional, overgrown but navigable, ancient but still operational. It has achieved a state and settled into it. Nothing here is dying rapidly. Nothing is growing wildly. It's in stasis the way a terrarium is in stasis—a perfect, self-maintaining pocket of a world that has already ended and has become comfortable with that ending.

The tunnels between buildings branch in patterns that make sense once you stop looking for surface logic. After a while, you realize you're not trying to escape.
