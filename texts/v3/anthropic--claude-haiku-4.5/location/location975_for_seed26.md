# The Undercity of Viadem

You emerge into a space that is not quite a cavern—it has been *made* into one, or perhaps unmade from one. The ceiling stretches high above, lost in shadows and the pale condensation that hangs like gauze. It is coffered concrete, sectioned by massive I-beams that run the length of what you now understand is a valley, though the term feels wrong for something so deliberately structured. The beams drip. Water collects in the channels between panels, creating a geography of shallow metal gutters that feed into wider, open canals that hum with slow current.

The floor itself is a grid—not quite finished, or perhaps intentionally left in this state of incompletion. Corrugated metal pathways, worn slick, cross between stations that appear to be industrial in purpose but stand mostly dormant. Hydro-presses sit silent but not resting; they are *waiting*, their pistons beaded with moisture. Conveyor belts, laid out in sprawling patterns across the floor, stretch into the darkness in multiple directions without apparent destination. Some still move, driven by mechanisms somewhere beyond sight—their whir and mechanical gasp forms a baseline rhythm under everything else.

The temperature is brittle. Your breath clouds immediately. The air moves slightly, circulating through ventilation shafts, carrying the smell of rust, mineral-laden water, and something faintly chemical—not unpleasant, but industrial in a way that your lungs register as *not meant for breathing*. Yet you must breathe it.

Water is everywhere, but controlled, channeled. The architects of this place anticipated it, invited it even. Precipitation—and you realize now that you can *hear* it, faint and regular, a tapping from vents somewhere above—drains into designated paths. But the humidity it creates doesn't leave; it pools in the air itself. Droplets form on surfaces that should be dry. The metal sweats continuously. There are sections where standing water has accumulated in shallow basins, catching the dim light like mirrors, except the light here is so scarce that these pools are barely distinguishable from the floor around them.

The light comes in patches, unevenly distributed. Industrial lamps—mercury vapor, you think, though half seem to have failed—cast pools of greenish-white glow at intervals so far apart that the spaces between them feel substantial, like separate rooms. The darkness between these pools is not absolute; a phosphorescent glow comes from certain machinery—instrument panels that have never been turned off, or perhaps have been off for so long they've developed their own strange bioluminescence. In some sections, the shadows are so complete that you must move by touch and sound, navigating by the echo of your footsteps off metal and the irregular drip of water.

Sound dominates. The mechanical breath never stops: pumps cycling slowly, metal expanding and contracting as temperature fluctuates slightly across the hours, the constant liquid whisper of water finding its levels. Occasionally something shifts—a bolt settling, a beam groaning under unseen stress—and the sound carries far in this enclosed space, arriving from directions that seem impossible. There's a rhythm to it, almost biological, like listening to the breathing and digestion of something vast and dormant.

Vegetation here exists but feels transgressive. Where moisture collects longest, thin mosses have claimed territory, painting the metal in patches of grey-green. Certain walls—if they can be called that, more like the scored sides of the chambers—have developed a fine film of algae in colors that shouldn't exist: mauve, dusty rose, almost bioluminescent in their own right. These growths are sparse, scattered, as if the ecosystem hasn't yet decided whether this place is invitable or merely barely-tolerable. A few fungi have established themselves in cracks, white and fragile-looking, extending feathery fruiting bodies into the stagnant air. The plants don't thrive here; they *persist*, and there's something unsettling in their patient, creeping presence—the slow biological insistence that even this artificial void will eventually be reclaimed.

The fauna is rarer still. You see no large animals. But there are signs: tracks in the dust that accumulates on horizontal surfaces, following the pathways as if creatures understand the grid as readily as the human engineers who built it. Small things move in your peripheral vision—rats, probably, or something rat-sized—adapted to the cold, the darkness, the relentless machinery. You hear them sometimes, their scratching overlaid with the dripping, indistinguishable until your mind sorts the sounds. There is an ecology here, but it is thin, almost theoretical, as if predator and prey are both operating on minimum reserves.

The scale of the place unfolds as you move. What seemed like a single chamber reveals itself, gradually, to be one section of something far larger. The floor slopes almost imperceptibly in one direction, and following it reveals more complexes of machinery, more branching pathways, more sections of the grid extending into a dimness so profound it might as well be infinite. You sense other levels above and below through acoustic clues: sounds that echo from shafts, the feel of air pressure differences. This is not a cave. This is a *facility*, but its function has become obscure—perhaps deliberately obscured, perhaps genuinely forgotten.

There is evidence of active processes, but they are strange and slow. Some machinery still cycles on automated schedules you can infer from the patterns of sound and light. Pumps drive water through systems you can trace by the sound of flow in hidden pipes. Temperature fluctuates in cycles that might span hours or days; you feel it as a gradual deepening and easing of the cold. Something is still *running*, even if no one is operating it, and hasn't been for a time long enough that moss has begun to grow over the control panels.

The weirdness lies in the tension: this is entirely artificial, every surface *made*, and yet it has begun to be colonized by natural processes that find purchase in every imperfection. The order and the decay are contemporary. The machinery suggests industrial purpose, but the purposes are not evident. The human scale—the size of the pathways, the height of the platforms—seems designed for beings who are no longer here, if they ever were. You sense that you are *inside* something massive, something that extends beyond your ability to fully perceive, and that the space you occupy is just one small section of a much vaster, half-articulate system.

Above, the concrete ceiling stretches on, scored and stained, weeping its constant water, and you don't know if it goes up forever or if there is sunlight somewhere beyond it—a surface, a sky, an open world—or whether this *is* the world now, entirely subterranean, entirely made, running its slow, mechanical, half-alive cycles in the perpetual dimness, waiting for purposes you cannot guess at, or cannot yet comprehend.
