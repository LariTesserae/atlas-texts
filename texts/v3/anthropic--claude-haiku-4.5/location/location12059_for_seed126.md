# The Lattice

The first thing that strikes you is the silence—not the absence of sound, but its *texture*. What little noise exists arrives muffled, as though filtered through layers of something softer than air. A faint grinding, perhaps. Or the settling of enormous structures. It's hard to tell if it's happening around you or inside your skull.

The landscape is not a landscape in any traditional sense. It is a *construction*, vast and engineered, yet so ancient that the distinction between artifact and geology has dissolved. What you walk on is not quite ground. It's a lattice—a honeycomb of interlocking geometric forms, their edges worn smooth by time beyond geological measure. The material is not rock or metal or any single substance you can name. It has the translucence of certain ceramics in some places, the density of obsidian in others, and in still others, a quality like frozen resin or time-calcified polymer. Where the sun hits it—and the sun is bright here, achingly bright, filtered through an atmosphere that seems both thin and heavily laden with particulate matter—the surfaces glow with a deep amber warmth.

The scale defeats easy comprehension. You stand in what might be called a valley, except that "valley" implies natural origins. This depression runs for horizons in all directions, between walls of the same latticed material that rise to heights that blur into haze. The walls are not sheer; they are themselves porous, punctured with apertures, channels, and dead ends. Some of these openings are vast—a structure the size of a cathedral could pass through them. Others are so small they'd reject a fingertip. The entire system appears to be a single coherent structure, yet so weathered by eons that it reads as almost organic. Baroque, even. The geometry was precise once; now it is *settled*. The sharp edges have softened. Edges have begun to fray.

There is almost no vegetation, but what little exists speaks to the age and alien nature of this place. Near the lowest points of the lattice—where any moisture might theoretically accumulate—thin films of something moss-like grow. It isn't green. It's a color between rust and copper, thin as dust, and it seems to exist in a state of near-complete dormancy. You could run your finger through it and feel almost nothing. These growths are spaced out across enormous areas, and they grow in the deepest recesses, where the lattice is most densely layered.

The temperature is warm, almost pleasant—around 35 degrees Celsius, steady, unchanging. There is no diurnal cycle here, or if there is, it operates on a scale of days or weeks that you cannot perceive. The warmth seems to emanate from within the lattice itself, a slow, ancient heat-death of some vast system, cooling at a pace measurable only in geological time. It creates a gentle shimmer over the surfaces in the brightest light, turning the most distant visible structures into mirages.

The atmosphere has weight. It tastes faintly metallic—as though you're breathing air that has been recycled through mineral-laden systems for so long that it's taken on the signature of its circulation. The sky above is pale, not quite white, not quite yellow. Clouds don't exist here; instead, there are variations in opacity, regions where the air becomes denser or more particulate. These form slow patterns, shifting over what might be hours, creating a sense of weather without water, wind without discernible gusts. The brightness is diffuse, coming from all directions at once, as though the entire sky is a source of illumination.

You encounter few signs of biological life. Once, you see something move—a small form, possibly insectile, possibly not, navigating a ridge of latticed material three hundred meters distant. It moves with mechanical precision, following channels as though they were pre-established pathways. You never see it again.

The density of structure around you is overwhelming. Every meter of space is occupied by the lattice in some form. Even what appears to be open air is threaded with thin geometric frameworks, creating perspectives that loop back on themselves if you stare too long. The engineers who built this—if "engineers" is the right word for beings who could construct something on this scale—understood compression intimately. Vast volumes of space are somehow accommodated within the interlocking geometry. Walk into certain apertures and the interior space seems to multiply. The lattice appears to be a *mechanism*, though for what purpose, time has rendered opaque.

At certain intervals, you notice where the lattice has been repaired. These sections are newer—though "newer" here means perhaps only five or six million years old—and the material is sharper, less weathered. The repairs follow no consistent pattern. Some are meticulous. Others appear hasty. What necessitated repairs on such a time scale? What mechanism still functions here, still requires maintenance?

The erosion is not uniform. Deep channels have been worn into the material by processes you cannot identify—not water, not wind, not chemical weathering in any conventional sense. These channels are remarkably geometric, suggesting not entropy but rather a form of *directed dissolution*. Something here is slowly consuming the lattice, eating through it in patterns that are almost purposeful. The channels are warm to the touch, slightly warmer than the surrounding material, and they glow faintly when you examine them closely. Inside these channels, you can sometimes see residue—a thin, grainy substance that resembles sand made from crystallized light.

The rules of this place assert themselves subtly. Orientation becomes difficult. Directions that feel equidistant based on visual information may take vastly different times to traverse. Some sections of the lattice are easier to navigate; others seem to actively resist movement, as though friction increases in zones of higher density. You begin to suspect that the structure has *properties*—that it doesn't merely exist in space but rather defines the space within itself. Distance here might be a variable, responsive to something you don't perceive.

Sound, when it occurs, travels strangely. A sound made at what feels like close range might arrive muffled and delayed, as though it's passing through sealed chambers. The grinding noise—if that's what it is—seems to come from all directions simultaneously, or from no direction, or from far beneath the surface. It's a frequency that sits just at the edge of hearing, low enough that you feel it through your bones as much as hear it through your ears. Eventually, you stop listening for it and start feeling for it instead. It becomes a kind of tactile sensation, a subtle vibration in the material beneath your feet that might be geological, or might be the lattice still performing some incomprehensible function.

There are moments—rare moments—when you see evidence of what this place might once have been: structured, purposeful, alive in some fashion. You find sections where the lattice is arranged with obvious intentionality, where apertures align in ways that suggest windows or vents. You discover nodes—spherical chambers perhaps three meters in diameter, transparent, empty. What once occupied them? What flowed through the channels? The lattice keeps its secrets. It has had an incomprehensibly long time to forget.

The landscape breathes, very slowly. The grinding sound becomes, over extended observation, less of a sound and more of a rhythm. The entire structure seems to shift minutely—subsidence, you think, the slow settling of an ancient architecture toward its final equilibrium. Nothing collapses; nothing fails. This is a machine in its final act, performing maintenance on itself through processes that have become so slow, so vast, that they barely register as movement at all.

The brightness persists. There are no shadows here—not true shadows. Instead, there are regions of slightly lesser light, places where the dense lattice blocks portions of the all-pervading illumination. These create a sense of depth, of interior spaces folded within the structure, but they offer no relief from the brightness. Your eyes adjust. Eventually, you see in this light the way others might see in darkness—by learning to read the subtle variations, the almost-imperceptible gradations of luminescence that reveal the architecture's true extent.

It occurs to you, standing here, that you might never leave. The lattice is too vast, its patterns too complex, its geometry too willing to present false exits and corridors that loop back on themselves. But you're not afraid. There's something reassuring about a place this stable, this settled, this utterly indifferent to your presence. You are the dynamic element here—the brief, warm flicker of biological process in a system that operates on scales of millions of years. The lattice will endure. It is enduring. It has always been enduring, and it always will be.

The warmth envelops you. The silence holds. The vast structure of this place—this made thing, this artifact of purposes lost to time—settles deeper into its own ancient equilibrium, and you settle with it.
