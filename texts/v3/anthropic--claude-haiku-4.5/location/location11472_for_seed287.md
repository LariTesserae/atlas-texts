# The Cistern Gardens

You enter through a rusted inspection hatch—the kind that hasn't been properly sealed in decades. The air hits first: thick, warm, almost syrupy with humidity. It tastes of iron and something vegetable, something that's been decomposing slowly and will continue to decompose slowly, perhaps forever.

The ceiling is concrete, maybe thirty feet up, stained in brown archipelagos where water has wept through for years. The seepage is constant but measured. Every few seconds you hear a soft *tick*—droplets finding their way down through hairline fractures, hitting stone or pooling in the dense vegetation below. The sound accumulates. Tick. Tick. Tick. It becomes a slow rhythm, almost rhythmic enough to predict, but not quite. Sometimes there's a pause. Sometimes two drops fall so close together they blur into one sound.

The floor isn't a floor anymore. It's a mat—several mats, layers of them. Moss has colonized the concrete, thick and spongy, threaded through with roots that shouldn't exist down here but do. The vegetation is strangest in its *density without wildness*. There are no trees, nothing vertical enough to block your path, but what grows here grows in suffocating profusion. Creeping figs with leaves the size of spoons. Some kind of fern that repeats its fronds in almost mechanical patterns. Vines that might have been decorative once, back when someone maintained this place, now so ancient and established they've become a structural element themselves, binding the walls together.

The heat makes it all move slowly. Not that anything moves—the fauna has never arrived here, or if it did, it left—but the vegetation seems to respire at a quarter-speed. You realize you're watching it metabolize. The moisture cycle here is closed and perfect: water seeps down from above, the plants transpire it back up, it hits the cold concrete ceiling and condenses, and starts again. This loop has been running for so long that the ecosystem has forgotten there was ever an outside.

Along the west wall, industrial bones: the remains of a water treatment system. A pump housing, now a sculpture of corrosion. Thick pipes that once carried water in programmed patterns, now they just weep. A control panel with switches still on its faceplate, their labels long waterlogged into illegibility. Someone stopped maintaining this years ago—decades, maybe. The machines didn't fail so much as *stop mattering*. The vegetation grew around them, through them. Now they're just landscape features, warm to the touch from some residual heat source deep below.

The sound here is layered. The dripping. The slow *hiss* of something condensing or evaporating—you can't quite tell which, and it might be both, simultaneously, in different zones. An occasional groan from the pipes, metal expanding or contracting in the stable heat. And underneath it all, something electronic: a faint hum. A pump somewhere still running, or maybe just the echoes of an old electrical system that never fully powered down. It's quiet enough that you notice it only when you stop thinking about the dripping.

The light is the worst part. There are a few bulbs—industrial fixtures bolted to the ceiling, their plastic cages clouded with condensation and dust. They're maybe forty percent of their original brightness, yellow-green with age. They cast no real shadows. Instead, everything exists in a murky half-light, details emerging and receding. The moisture in the air acts like a scattering medium, so distances don't work right. Something ten feet away might look fifty. The far end of the cistern garden simply fades into indistinct gloom.

The space is small—maybe the size of a large warehouse basement, or a small cathedral nave. You can walk its length in a few minutes, but the density of vegetation makes the distance feel negotiable, uncertain. The walls are lined with stone blocks, each one slightly different in shade and weathering pattern, visible evidence of construction that happened decades or perhaps centuries ago. The masonry is settled into itself, at rest. Water has worn channels into some blocks, smooth grooves that suggest the same trickle has run the same path for years. The erosion here is complete and patient—it's not *happening* so much as *evident*, a state that's been reached and maintained.

The strangest thing is how alive it is without anything living in the animal sense. The entire environment feels animate, sentient even—not in any threatening way, but as though the plants and water and air have achieved some kind of slow, ponderous consciousness together. The way the moss glows faintly in the dim light. The way certain vines seem to reach toward the barely-functional ceiling lights, still remembering photosynthesis even down here, where photons are precious and rare. The mathematical precision of the fern fronds, repeating their formula endlessly, as if solving some problem that has no answer but requires constant work anyway.

And the heat. Constant, patient, pervasive. It slows your thinking. The moisture means your skin never dries. You're always on the edge of sweating, always cool-but-not, in a state of equilibrium that makes time feel strange.

This is a place of deep stasis. Nothing is dying here. Nothing is quite living, either. It's a system that found a balance and committed to it completely, years ago, and has been following the same script ever since. The dripping, the growing, the slow chemistry of rot and regeneration and decay and growth, all at the same glacial pace.

The hatch is still open above you. You could leave whenever you wanted.

You don't, yet.
