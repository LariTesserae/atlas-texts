# The Settled Flats

The air arrives before anything else—thick, mineral-laden, warm enough that breathing feels like drinking. Water clings to everything, not as rain or mist but as a constant condensation that beads on every surface, every angle. The ground is damp without being mud. Everything feels like it's slowly weeping.

You stand on what was once a mountain range, though the peaks have worn down to a flat plateau that extends to every horizon. The stone beneath your feet is layered—deep reds and grays and ochres, banded in horizontal strata that speak of incomprehensible ages. Each layer is exposed at different elevations across the plateau, creating a subtle topography of worn benches and gentle scarps, as if a vast hand had sanded the world smooth over millennia and left only the gentlest undulations.

There is no vegetation here. There never was, or there was so long ago that the minerals have forgotten it. Instead, the world is alive with *animals*—overwhelming in their presence, their density, their sheer impossible numbers. They swarm across the stone in layers, and it takes a moment to understand what you're seeing.

The surfaces are carpeted in creatures that might be insects or might be something that hasn't evolved a name. They are small, translucent, moving in coordinated ripples across the damp rock. Their bodies reflect the light strangely—refracting it like living glass. Millions of them, maybe billions, arranged in patterns that feel almost purposeful. They congregate around moisture seeps, those constant weeping points where water emerges from deep within the plateau and immediately re-evaporates into the warm air. Where they gather, the stone takes on a faint shine, almost like they're polishing it.

But there are larger things too.

Above, creatures the size of dogs—or perhaps they're the size of houses, the perspective is difficult—move with deliberate slowness across the exposed cliff faces. They appear to be semi-geological themselves: their shells or skin or carapaces are fused with or composed entirely of the local stone, making them difficult to distinguish from the rock until they move. They graze (if that is the word) on the smaller creatures, or perhaps they're filtering something from the air itself. Their movements are patient, generational in their slowness. You watch one shift position by perhaps a meter over the course of many minutes.

The settlement layers over and around all of this.

Industrial structures rise from the plateau in ordered ranks: refineries built of riveted iron and copper, their surfaces stained by centuries of seepage. Factory blocks constructed from the local stone, their exteriors already beginning to merge with the landscape—a slow architectural dissolution in progress. Pipes and conduits run in every direction, corroded but functional, carrying water (which here is valuable, harvested from those weeping seeps) and processed materials that generate the constant, patient industrial hum that forms the baseline sound of the place.

The buildings are layered vertically too, built in stacked tiers that follow the contours of the old geology. They seem to have been added to incrementally, over such a long time that each section represents a different era of construction technology, a different understanding of how to build in this place. The oldest sections—built with a kind of rammed-earth masonry—are already indistinguishable from the natural stone. The newer sections, perhaps only three or four centuries old, show clear signs of intentional construction.

Between the structures, the creatures continue their work. The small ones—those translucent swarms—seem to shelter in the factory buildings, using them as vast scaffolds. The larger, stone-fused animals move more slowly between the industrial zones. There's an understanding here, an equilibrium that has settled into place: the humans (you catch glimpses of them rarely, in protective suits, moving through pneumatic tubes between buildings) have built a civilization that the native fauna simply incorporates into their existence. The creatures are not hostile. They are indifferent, and they are patient.

Water management is everywhere, the central concern of this place. The constant condensation means that mold and mineral deposits accumulate; the industrial processes have adapted to this reality. Channels cut into the stone guide water toward collection pools. Industrial pumps operate on slow cycles, designed to run for decades without servicing. Steam rises from vents where the warmed water escapes. The air smells of rust and minerals and something organic and ancient, a scent that doesn't quite fit into any category.

The light is normal—not dim, not bright, just steady. The plateau's elevation means that there's always some weather presence, but it's slow weather. A wind that carries moisture and warmth, moving the same direction on most days. Clouds that form and persist rather than passing through. The sky, when visible between the industrial haze, shows a color that's neither blue nor gray but something between, as if the light itself has been diluted by the suspended minerals in the air.

Sound is the constant industrial drone, yes, but beneath it is something else: the soft rustling of countless small creatures moving against stone, the patient grinding of machinery that's been running since before living memory, the periodic release of steam through valves, the deep creaking of settling metal as the structures gradually merge with their geological foundation. And underneath all of it, barely perceptible, the sound of water moving through stone—slow percolation, seepage, the planet itself weeping.

Time moves differently here. The erosion you can observe is not the rapid wear of wind and water, but the slow merger of construction with geology. The settlement has existed long enough that the distinction between what was built and what was found is beginning to blur. New industrial structures are being constructed even now, but it's not clear if this civilization is growing or simply being slowly absorbed. The change happens across seasons that might last for decades. The machines operate in rhythms that align more with stone-time than human-time.

The fauna outnumbers the human inhabitants by some incalculable margin. But there's no predation in the traditional sense. Everything here feeds on the slow metabolism of the place itself—the minerals, the water, the patient conversion of the plateau's resources into life. The humans have found their niche, and the animals have adjusted around them. It's an equilibrium that feels as if it could persist indefinitely, or which could collapse without warning if any element shifts. There is no way to tell which.
