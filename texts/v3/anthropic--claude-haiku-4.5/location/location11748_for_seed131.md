# The Conservatory

You are standing in a single room—but the word room does nothing. It is a constructed enclosure, roughly thirty meters long and half that wide, its dimensions dictated not by human proportion but by the machinery that fills it.

The floor is terrazzo, deeply stained. Water runs constantly across it in shallow channels cut into the surface, a network of grooves that guide condensation, seepage, and deliberate release from above. The water is warm—blood-warm, slightly sulfurous. It pools nowhere; the floor slopes so gradually you cannot see it slope, but the water moves always toward the eastern wall where it vanishes into a grate, only to be pumped back up through a system of copper pipes that run visibly along the northern edge. The pipes hammer with a rhythm that has no variation: *clang-whoosh-clang-whoosh*, the sound of a single massive pump that has run this same cycle since before anyone living can remember. The rhythm is so steady it stops being noise and becomes instead the room's heartbeat—you feel it through your shoes, through the humid air itself.

The walls were built of concrete, but time and water have done something to them. They weep constantly. Not leaking—weeping. Moisture emerges from the pores of the material itself, running in rivulets that have carved permanent channels, leaving mineral deposits like frozen tears. The concrete is no longer the color of concrete. It is ochre and rust and the pale green of something that has grown very slowly over decades in permanent dampness. Lichens, you think, though they are not quite lichens. They are something the industrial air and the eternal moisture and whatever is in that sulfurous water have cultivated together.

Vegetation fills every vertical surface.

Not plants, exactly—not in any pure form. The distinction has blurred. There are vines, certainly: thick-stemmed things with leaves the size of your hand, deep green and glossy, condensation beading on their waxy surfaces. But they are threaded through with tubes and trellises of industrial metal, and the metal has been colonized. Moss clings to steel. Something flowering—small purple blooms, obscene and delicate—grows from the joints where pipes meet the walls. Hanging from the ceiling (which you cannot quite see through the moisture and the tangle of growth) are chains of plant material hanging so thick and dense that they seem almost architectural: walls within the wall, creating pockets of deeper green, deeper humidity.

The density is deceiving. For all the growth, the space is fundamentally empty. Hollow. The vines and flowering things create the *illusion* of abundance, but between them is vast open air. The room could hold hundreds of people and still feel sparse. What you are seeing is life in extreme, slow-motion proliferation—not filling the space so much as reaching through it, exploring it, stretching across it like fingers learning the shape of something vast and cold.

The machinery does not shut down. The pump continues its three-second cycle, and somewhere—you cannot locate it precisely—there is the grinding of something older, something that might be a generator or a compressor or a turbine. It is not loud so much as *present*, a white noise that has burrowed into the room's fundamental frequency. Periodically the grinding pauses and a valve opens somewhere—a sharp, pneumatic *hisss*—and then resumes. The light flickers slightly when this happens. The lights are industrial: panels set into the ceiling, sallow and steady, casting everything in a faint yellow-green that might be the actual color of the air or might be the color of the light. You cannot separate them.

The light never changes. Neither does the temperature. The warm water, the humid air, the dense growth—these are not states the room is moving through. They are states it *has assumed*. Nothing is arriving or departing. Nothing is growing noticeably or dying. The moss is neither spreading nor receding. The vines are neither longer nor shorter than they were decades ago. The water that condenses on the eastern wall is the same water that has been cycling for longer than the room has been sealed.

You have been here for an hour and everything is exactly as it was. You will be here in an hour and it will still be this way.

An animal moves. You catch it at the edge of your vision: something small, reptilian, damp-skinned. A salamander, perhaps, or something that learned to be one. It moves through the vines without seeming to disturb them. You do not see it again.

The air smells of rust and growing things and something mineral. Something ancient. The smell does not change.

The room does not age. It has aged completely and settled into its final configuration. The erosion is total—everything sharp has been rounded, everything new has been incorporated into the old patterns. The concrete weeps because that is what it has learned to do, forever. The machinery runs because it is not a machine that can stop; the stopping of it would be catastrophic in ways no one remembers. The vegetation grows because the light and the water and the air are perfectly calibrated to growth without change. Nothing wins. Nothing dies. Nothing arrives new.

You are breathing warm, wet air that tastes of copper and chlorophyll.

The room continues its work—what work, no one now recalls. But the pump's rhythm says it must continue, and the vines say nothing, and the light remains steady, and you are one of the few things that has ever entered here that will someday leave.

It is the oldest place you have ever been, and also the most artificial.
