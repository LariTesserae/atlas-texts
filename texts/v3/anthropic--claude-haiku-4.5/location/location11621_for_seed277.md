# The Corroded Plateau

You stand at roughly eight thousand feet, though the elevation feels wrong—the air pressure doesn't match what your lungs expect. The plateau extends in all directions until it dissolves into a haze of its own making, a vast tableland easily five hundred miles across, maybe more. Perspective fails here. A distant ridge might be ten miles away or a hundred.

The ground is ochre and rust-red, weathered into corrugations that run in long, parallel lines like a record of some ancient wind that never stopped. These grooves are deep enough to hide in—you could walk into one and lose the horizon. The stone beneath is sandstone primarily, but shot through with seams of something darker, almost ferrous, that oxidizes at its edges into burgundy stains. The rocks are old. Genuinely old. Not in a romantic way. In a way that makes you feel the weight of time pressing down from above.

Sparse vegetation clings to existence: low desert shrubs with silvery-gray leaves, their needles drawn tight against the warmth. Bunch grasses cluster in the bottoms of those corrugated grooves where moisture collects. There's a particular species of low, sprawling tree—something between a juniper and something that hasn't got a name—that grows in scattered formations. These trees are gnarled, their trunks twisted as if wrung by hands, their limbs reaching sideways rather than up. They've learned not to fight the wind. Between them: empty space. So much empty space. A person could walk for an hour and see only five or six other plants.

But the emptiness isn't silent.

---

The water comes irregularly.

Every three to five days, clouds build on the western horizon. Not rain clouds—something heavier, more architectural. They arrive with a sound like machinery warming up. The rain, when it falls, is brief and violent, dropping two or three inches in twenty minutes. It doesn't soak in evenly. Instead, it channels into those ancient grooves, becoming temporary streams that flow for a few hours before evaporating or sinking into deeper stone fissures. There are places where seepage has created small wetlands, almost hidden—depressions where the water collects and stays, creating microclimates. Moss grows there. Lichens. A type of small, dark frog with a call like a rusted hinge.

The air itself holds moisture most mornings. A heavy dew condenses on the silvery leaves, and if you look at the corrugations at dawn, you can see them glistening, almost wet, before the temperature climbs past eighty degrees and the moisture vanishes. It never truly rains at night, but the air is *damp* in that deep, geological sense—the plateau's subsurface is soaked, and that water rises through capillary action, through invisible cracks, to kiss the surface each dawn.

---

The warmth is constant. Even at elevation, even with the early morning chill that touches the forties, by noon the temperature hovers around seventy-eight to eighty-two degrees. It's the warmth of high altitude where the sun's rays aren't filtered by as much atmosphere—direct, almost metallic in quality. Your skin sunburns here faster than it should. The stone radiates heat back after sunset, creating a nocturnal thermal layer that makes the nights less cold than you'd expect. The wind is warm too, when it comes. It arrives from the south in the afternoons, carrying the scent of something distant—distant earth, distant heat, distant rain.

---

The strange thing—the thing that takes days to notice—is the sound of engines.

They're not close. The sound carries across the plateau in ways that make distance meaningless. Sometimes you hear them from the north, sometimes from the west, sometimes from below—or seeming to come from below, from beneath the plateau itself. They're distant, mechanical, steady, the sound of turbines or pumps or some kind of industrial extraction happening at scales you can't see. The sound comes and goes with the wind. Sometimes it's just at the threshold of perception, a vibration in the stone beneath your feet rather than something in the air. Sometimes it swells, a low diesel roar or an electric hum that makes your teeth itch.

There are no visible structures. No refineries, no factories, no smoke stacks. You walk for days and find no evidence of machinery. But the sound never stops. The equipment is elsewhere—somewhere beyond the horizon, or integrated into the plateau itself in ways that remain hidden.

---

Life here is abundant, though you'd never guess it at first glance.

The insects are relentless. Locusts, the color of stone, emerge from the grasses in clouds when disturbed. There are beetles—vast beetles, some the size of your fist—that process the scattered deadwood, their carapaces a industrial blue-black. Flies, countless flies, hovering over the small patches of moisture. Spiders in the corrugations, their webs spanning the gaps between stones, each web filled with the debris of insect life.

The larger fauna appears rarely but surely. Creatures adapted to sparse vegetation: goats or something like them, sure-footed and sparse themselves, that you'll spot on the ridgelines at distance. A type of hare with enormous ears that appear in the early morning, feeding on the silvery brush. Birds—hawks, certainly, and smaller species that you hear more than see, their calls cutting through the mechanical hum of those distant engines. Once, you saw something larger moving across the plateau at dusk: four-legged, bulking, possibly feline. It was far enough away that you could have been wrong. The plateau doesn't invite certainty.

There are predator-prey relationships happening constantly at scales you're not quite equipped to observe. Movement in the brush. Fresh kill marks on stones. The occasional feather, still damp, near one of the wetland depressions. Life eating life, as it does, but doing it quietly.

---

The erosion here is ancient and settled. The plateau isn't being worn away—not visibly. It's reached a kind of equilibrium. The wind has shaped it, yes, into those long grooves, but no new gouges appear. The rain runs off in predictable channels that haven't changed, you suspect, in thousands of years. The stone has weathered to its final form: stable, deeply fissured, colored by oxidation, but not crumbling. It's a landscape that has stopped changing rapidly and entered a long, slow stasis. Even the corrugations seem almost intentional, like the landscape was carved and then left to settle into itself.

Yet there's activity everywhere if you pay attention—the kind that operates on multiple timescales at once. The daily cycle: cool dawn, warm afternoon, slow evening cooling, cold night. The rain cycle: the irregular storms that reshape the temporary water flow. The biological pace: the insects' constant work, the predators' cycles, the slow growth of those twisted trees. The sound of engines waxing and waning with the wind. Multiple processes layering over each other, none of them fast, none of them still.

---

The light here is strange.

There's almost no artificial light—no city glow on any horizon, no distant lights at night. The darkness is genuine. When clouds cover the moon, the plateau becomes featureless, a black void. Your eyes adjust and adjust and still find nothing. But during the day, the light is harsh and white, bouncing off stone, creating shadows that are sharp enough to cut. The sun is a relentless presence, moving across the sky in a fixed, predictable arc, leaving you temporarily without shadow at noon. In the hour before sunset, everything turns red—not romantically red, but an oxidized, industrial red, as if the stone itself is bleeding.

At night, under clear skies, the stars are overwhelming. More stars than sky. The Milky Way casts a genuine shadow. You can almost see the rotation of the galaxy. But this brightness, paradoxically, makes the darkness between the stars feel absolute—it's a darkness that goes down forever.

---

And still, across all of this—the sparse vegetation, the warmth, the moisture that comes and goes, the steady hum of invisible machinery, the abundant hidden life, the vast empty corrugated stone—there is an absence of anything made. No path, no structure, no sign that human intention has touched this place. Only the engines, audible but never visible, as if the industrial world exists parallel to this one, bleeding through at the edges.

The plateau is complete without you. It will remain complete after you leave.
