# The Sunken Cities of the Plateau

You stand on a shelf of land so vast that its far edges blur into cloud. The plateau extends beyond visual certainty in all directions—your eye cannot find its end, only the gradual dissolution of atmosphere. This height is real: the air carries the thin, metallic quality of elevation, cool enough that breath becomes visible when the mist clears, which isn't often.

The ground beneath your feet is stone paved in places—not by intention, but by the simple fact of age. Sections of exposed bedrock, worn to butter-smooth planes by millennia of moisture, create accidental flagstones between areas of deeper substrate. The rock is gray-veined, studded with what might be fossils or embedded minerals. It weeps constantly. Not rain—or not only rain. The stone itself seems to sweat. Water beads from hairline fractures and trickles downward in threads so fine they're barely visible until afternoon light catches them, transforming the ground into a vast, slow web of silver.

This is a place that has settled into its drainage. Long ago—so long the timeline folds into geology—water found its routes here, carved its channels, established its pooling-places. Now the system is perfected. Shallow depressions darker than surrounding stone hold still water, their surfaces trembling with the life of microorganisms invisible at this distance. The air above them shimmers faintly. You can smell the deepness of it: mineral, neither stale nor fresh, a smell like the interior of earth.

But the water has built something else here. The sparse vegetation—low, slow-growing, adapted to cold and wet—has accumulated in the depressions and along the seeping channels. Moss, primarily. Thick carpets of it, electric green under the cloud-filtered light. The moss is so dense in places that it forms cushions, soft enough that your footstep disappears into it without sound. It has been accumulating for so long that it has begun to build structure of its own: small hillocks, ridges following the grain of water-flow beneath it, territories of different species creating visible textures and color-gradations from near-black to a bright, almost luminous green. Where the moss is oldest, it has compressed itself into something almost soil-like, dark and crumbly at depth.

The scale of silence here is peculiar. Not absence of sound—the landscape generates sound constantly, but in a register below attention. Water drips. The drop-hits register only when you focus on them: *plink, plink, plink*—separated by intervals long enough that no rhythm emerges. Sometimes the sound comes from close by; sometimes it arrives from so distant that you realize again how large this space is. A low murmur moves through the moss-covered ground: subsurface water, probably. Occasionally wind aloft touches the upper air and makes a sound like something breathing, but it doesn't reach the ground level, not really. It only deepens the quiet of where you are.

This was a city. Or rather: cities, plural, layered, built and rebuilt by something that isn't here anymore.

The structures are visible now that you've been here long enough to let your eyes adjust. They rise from the moss—not dramatically, but unmistakably. Walls of fitted stone, some of them still bearing the marks of precise mason-work, now colonized by lichen. Archways, their curves still geometrically true, frame views of further platforms and descending levels. The buildings are half-submerged in moss and the accumulated damp of ten thousand rains. In some places, the stone has fractured from the freeze-thaw cycle, edges rounded and softened. In others, it remains sharp, the angle of a cut still readable. The city has been here so long that it has achieved a kind of permanence—not unchanging, but no longer actively crumbling. It rests in its own erosion, balanced.

The architecture follows the fall of the land. The plateau isn't flat; it descends in long, imperceptible slopes that only become obvious across vast distances. The city-structures have been built and rebuilt following these slopes—terraces, really, stacked levels, each platform feeding water to the one below. There's an intentionality to it that suggests the builders understood the hydrology of this place intimately. They worked with the wetness, not against it. Their channels and gutters and deliberate depressions became the city's veins.

Now those systems work on their own. Water that entered through ancient openings finds its way through the stone, emerges at predetermined spots, moves downslope in patterns that have been reinforced by centuries of repetition. The city has become a machine for processing water through its own body.

The fauna here is minimal—sparse, as the parameters suggested, and rare. You might wait hours and see nothing. Then a movement catches your eye: a small lizard, albino or nearly so, disappearing into the moss. These creatures are adapted to the cool, the damp, the absence of much sun. They're slow. They move only when necessary. You catch them sometimes drinking from the stone pools, their thin bodies bent at angles that seem unnecessarily delicate. Once you see one of the great water-spiders that live in the deepest pools—a creature the size of your fist, legs clear as glass, hunting things too small to observe. The sound of its movement is the softest whisper.

Birds are rarer still. Once or twice you hear them—high, thin cries from the depths of the mist. You never see them clearly. They might be carrion birds, or water-feeders, or something adapted only to this specific altitude. The sound carries a quality of strangeness simply because it breaks the deep quiet.

The temperature never changes much. Cool in the morning. Cool in the afternoon. Cool at night. Your body adjusts. The stone stays cool to the touch always, even in the rare moments of direct sun. The moisture in the air—constant, nearly visible—keeps any heat from accumulating. This is a place where warmth cannot establish itself. The climate of the plateau seems to have its own physics, its own rules. Weather moves across it like something conscious. You can see storms arriving from days away—visible as changes in the quality of mist, shifts in what angle the light falls from. When the storm comes, it comes thoroughly. Rain that seems less like falling water than like the air itself becoming heavier. It passes. The ground drinks it all. The moss grows greener in the days after.

Time moves differently here. Individual days feel distinct, but years collapse into something unmeasurable. The city's builders—whatever they were, whenever they lived—have been gone long enough that the question stops mattering. The structures they left have achieved a kind of equilibrium with the place. They're not being reclaimed. They're not decaying. They're simply part of the landscape now, as fundamental as the moss, as the weeping stone, as the slow downslope of the plateau toward edges you'll never see.

The air tastes of minerals and silence. Your own breathing is the loudest sound in the entire vast space.
