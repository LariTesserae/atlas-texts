# The Tarn of Settling Things

Water first. Not a lake—something older, a depression where the land has given up and pooled itself with the patience of deep time. The tarn sits maybe two meters below the surrounding terrain, its surface neither quite still nor moving, breathing with a slowness that takes hours to notice. The water is clear to a depth of perhaps three meters before the accumulated sediment of ten thousand years dissolves it into pearl-gray unknowing. At the margins, the shore doesn't exist as a line but as a gradient: firm ground gives way to springy moss-thick earth, then to matted vegetation that floats on water so shallow your boot sinks through botanical fiber into genuine mire. Everything here is damp. Not raining—hasn't rained in weeks—but the air has condensed against itself so thoroughly that moisture beads on every surface. Spider webs jewel themselves with it. The rocks weep.

Those rocks are why the place persists. Smooth and river-rounded, they jut from the mire at intervals, having been polished by water and time into something almost sculptural—not carved by intent, but worn down to an archetypal form that speaks of completion. The largest is perhaps three meters across, its surface ancient enough to hold lichen colonies measured in geological time. Smaller ones dot the shallow water like the vertebrae of something immense that has settled into the earth and given up the pretense of anatomy. They are warm to the touch, heated by the bright sky.

The vegetation doesn't crowd; it flourishes in deliberate pacing. Sedges and marsh-reeds grow in clumps, each colony separated from its neighbor by open water or bare moss. But *within* each clump, the growth is fierce—tall grasses with feathered tops, flowering spike-plants with blooms in deep purples and bitter yellows, ferns whose fronds have achieved a complexity that suggests they're still solving problems from the Carboniferous period. Closer to the water's edge, the plants become amphibious: half-submerged flowering things with roots that seem to photosynthesize as much as absorb. The vegetation doesn't crowd because there's no competition; there's only cooperation, a green parliament that has reached consensus on exactly how much of the landscape should be empty.

Yet the emptiness is not empty.

The animals own the silence. They are present in such overwhelming quantity and variety that the place feels less like habitat and more like a demonstration of what vertebrate and invertebrate form can achieve in a single cubic kilometer. Not visible all at once—that would be chaos—but revealed in layers. The insect hum is the baseline: not the loud keen of summer insects, but a vast, modulated susurration, as though the air itself has developed the capacity to murmur. Dragonflies in iridescent populations hunt above the water in patterns too complex to follow. Their colors are wrong in ways that suggest their wings bend light according to rules current optics doesn't fully account for. They loop in three-dimensional geometries that shouldn't be possible at those velocities. They don't make much sound.

Amphibians emerge in the warmer hour just before evening: frogs of at least four distinct species, their calls sufficiently separated in frequency that they don't jam one another, instead creating a kind of aquatic choir that sounds almost like distant machinery—like the tarn is a vast, patient device in process of some calculation. The smaller frogs cling to reeds. The larger ones float half-submerged, their gold-rimmed eyes just above the waterline, unblinking, eternal.

Birds. The density is modest but the variety is staggering. A heron haunts the far shallows, its patience absolute, its movements so slow they seem to be a form of meditation. Smaller birds—perhaps warblers, perhaps something that hasn't been catalogued—flit between reed and emergent stone, their calls thin and musical and somehow already nostalgic, as though they're singing about a place they're currently in, mourning it as they inhabit it. In the deeper sedge-clusters, ground-nesters move with invisible purpose. There is an ecology here that functions without apparent urgency, as though time has become a resource in such abundance that haste would be wasteful.

The smallest creatures are overwhelming in aggregate. Beetles, bugs, the creatures too small to name in the casual taxonomy of the visible—they are *here* in what must be millions. Their collective breathing creates a texture in the air that isn't quite sound, more like a shadow of vibration, a knowledge that you're not alone in the immediate vicinity of your epidermis.

The human structure—and there is only one—sits on the largest rock, a small platform or shelter that might have been built three hundred years ago or three thousand. It's difficult to determine. The material is neither stone nor wood but something intermediate, something that has achieved a kind of parity with the ecosystem around it rather than resistance to it. Lichen colonies bloom across it like a gentle conquest. The structure is small enough to be a shelter for perhaps two people in the sitting position, open on all sides, a roof of interlaced material that might once have been metal but has become something closer to crystalline chitin in texture. There are no walls. There is no door. 

No one is there now, hasn't been for decades at minimum. Yet the structure feels less abandoned than *prepared*—as though it awaits the inevitable return of something that left knowing it would come back, in its own time.

The light here is bright but soft, filtered through a constant haze of moisture and biological activity. The sun never seems to strike directly but instead to illuminate from all directions at once, turning the entire landscape into a kind of gentle overexposure, everything slightly bleached, all shadows suggested rather than cast. The water glows. Even the shadows glow.

Time moves differently here. You become aware of this slowly. The wind—when it moves at all—carries warmth rather than direction. The temperature holds at a pleasant warm, neither heating nor cooling, as though the place has found its equilibrium and sees no reason to depart from it. The erosion is complete. The landscape has worn itself down to its final form: a depression, water, ancient stones, vegetation in its mature ecological balance, an overwhelming biological presence distributed across space with the precision of a formula that has been solved. The damp air, the soft light, the almost-silence—these are not in process of becoming something else. They are what remains when becoming itself runs out of ambition.

The birds know. They sing anyway.
