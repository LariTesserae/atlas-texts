# The Glistening Crags

You stand at something like the edge of the world, though what lies beyond refuses to resolve. The elevation here is absolute—you are far above, far enough that the air tastes mineral and thin, far enough that the distant lowlands blur into a white haze that might be cloud or might be light behaving incorrectly. The mountains around you are not mountains in the sense of earth piled high. They are *extrusions*—vast geometric facets of what looks like obsidian or flawed glass, black and charcoal and deep purples, rising in angles that seem too sharp, too intentional. Their surfaces are scored with regular striations, as though something with tremendous patience and no regard for organic form carved them with a tool.

Water is everywhere, but wrong.

It does not pool. Instead it *emerges*—from fissures in the stone, from the very air itself as condensation that runs downward in constant, patient streams. The rocks weep. They have been weeping for so long that channels have worn into them, and these channels now run perpetually, collecting in hollows, creating small lakes of remarkable clarity. The water is ice-cold. When you touch it, your hand goes numb within seconds. Yet it does not freeze. The temperature hovers in that hostile range where nothing can properly live, where water remains liquid only through its own stubbornness.

The light here is too much. The sky is a pale, almost white blue—the blue of thin altitude—and the sun is sharp. But the real source of brightness comes from the stone itself. Every polished surface reflects. Every facet catches and multiplies the light until the landscape becomes a prismatic assault, a geometry of illumination. Shadows are knife-edged and black. In the shadow-spaces between rocks, the darkness is absolute. You can see perhaps fifty meters in any direction before the glare becomes too intense to parse.

There is no vegetation. Not the absence of green—the *absence of life-as-you-understand-it*. 

And yet the landscape is not dead. Something else inhabits it.

In the cracks and crevices of the stone, there exists a kind of *presence*—thin, filamentous, almost luminous. At first you think it might be mineral deposits or crystal structures, but when the light catches them at certain angles, they seem to move with purpose. They do not grow in the way plants grow. Instead they *branch*, *reticulate*, *refract* across the stone surfaces in patterns that suggest neither plant nor animal taxonomy. They might be fungal. They might be something that predates the distinction. They absorb and redirect light. In the deepest crevices, they glow faintly—a phosphorescent silver-grey that becomes visible only when the sun's direct reflection cannot reach.

The density of these filaments increases around the water-channels. Wherever condensation runs, wherever the small lakes gather, the presence becomes thicker, more opulent, more complicated in its geometry. It is as if the mineral-life feeds on both moisture and light, or perhaps converts one into the other. The boundary between stone and these organisms is so blurred that you begin to wonder if they are separate at all—if the rock itself has become something else entirely, or was always meant to be this way.

The sound is constant and strange.

Water runs—a sound like thin rain, like distant traffic, like static in the frequencies below hearing. The water-streams create a kind of white noise that fills the space, and within it you can sometimes distinguish individual notes: the dripping from a ledge three hundred meters away, the trickle into a pool behind you, the barely-audible rush of something moving through channels too narrow to see. But there is another sound beneath this. A kind of *resonance*. When you pay attention, you realize the stone itself is humming—not with sound exactly, but with a vibration that your bones register more clearly than your ears. It is a tone that wavers at the edge of perception, sometimes almost subsonic, sometimes rising to a high frequency that makes your teeth ache. The filaments might be producing it. The stone might be. The water might be conducting it through channels in the rock like it conducts current through a medium.

The structures are few and far between.

There are marks of human presence, but they are old—old enough that you cannot tell if they are very old or impossibly old. Stone cairns rise at intervals along the high places, built from the same obsidian-like material, stacked with deliberate precision. They are weathered not by time, exactly, but by the same patient, geometric erosion that shapes the landscape itself. Some are intact. Others have collapsed into their own logic, becoming new rock formations that blend indistinguishably with the natural crags. There is a structure—if it can be called that—near what might be a natural shelter: a wall built with tool-marks visible on each stone, crude and purposeful, constructed with early methods. It blocks nothing. It protects nothing. It simply stands, its purpose eroded beyond retrieval. The mortar between stones is something like clay, something like dried water—it has become part of the rock now, or the rock has reclaimed it.

There is no sign of habitation. No indication that anyone has been here in any timescale that matters. And yet the structures suggest they *meant* to be here, at one point, for some reason their tools could achieve but their purposes could not articulate.

The erosion here exists in a strange state: settled, established, ancient. The landscape gives the impression of having reached an equilibrium—these specific angles, these specific channel-patterns, this precise distribution of water-flow—as if the mountains have found their true form and now maintain it. Yet things *are* changing. The erosion is not halted but slowed, distributed, balanced against time in a way that makes change nearly invisible at human scales. A rock-face that has taken ten thousand years to achieve its current curve might take another ten thousand to curve further. The water-channels deepen, but so slowly that you cannot watch it happen. The filamentous organisms spread and contract with seasonal variations that might take decades to complete a single cycle.

You notice, as your eyes adjust to the light, that the whole landscape has a kind of *pull* to it. 

Not physical magnetism, though your compass might lie here. Something in the geometry, in the angle of the stones, in the way the light refracts and the water runs, seems to *draw* your attention toward higher elevations, toward the deeper crevices, toward places you cannot quite see. It is as though the landscape is incomplete at the periphery of perception—as though it only fully *is* where you are looking directly at it, and warps or dissolves into something else in your peripheral vision. When you try to map it mentally, the map refuses to hold shape. You can remember the immediate features around you with clarity. Describe them, locate them in relation to one another. But the broader geography—how the valleys relate to the peaks, which direction the slope truly descends, what lies beyond that wall of glare—becomes slippery, uncertain, subject to revision.

Is it the light playing tricks? The altitude affecting your thinking? Or is the landscape itself *unstable in its larger geography*, fixed only locally, breaking apart into uncertainty at greater scales?

The cold intensifies as you stand here longer. Not a descent of temperature, but a *deepening* of cold—as though cold itself is a dimension this place has depth in. Your breath becomes visible. The tips of the water-streams begin to develop thin crusts of ice, though that ice never seems to solidify completely. It hovers in a state of becoming-frozen, caught between states. The humming in the stone intensifies. The filaments glow brighter. The light begins to take on qualities that have no names.

You are not sure how long you can stay before the landscape *changes the rules* on what staying means.
