# The Beneath-Holds

You descend through a natural chimney where root-light fades to grey. The stone is newborn—sharp-edged, barely weathered. Your palms cut on edges still acquiring their smoothness. No one has been here long enough to dull this place.

The cavern floor opens into a vast system, but "system" is generous. It's more honest to call it an antechamber to an antechamber: a series of shallow lakes separated by low shelves of limestone, each shelf raw and porous like a sponge that's been torn rather than shaped. Water pools in depressions but doesn't move. It hasn't learned the word flow. Where it sits, condensation beads on the ceiling in slow, patient accumulation—small stones growing slick, the air thick and still with the smell of stone-dust and minerals.

The caverns extend. You sense it in the way sound doesn't die but echoes flatly, suggesting volume without end. A mile down here might resolve into five miles. The stone gives no hints.

But the noise—

Everything alive here screams.

Bats. Thousands upon thousands. Their voices fill the cavern in a layer like smoke, a constant high chittering that doesn't rise or fall but simply *is*, a permanent frequency of the place. Occasionally one passes so close you feel the displacement of air. They hunt something. Or many somethings.

The water holds small translucent creatures—crustaceans, perhaps, pale as bone-fragments. They boil across the shallow pools in twitching masses, their carapaces catching what little light reaches down here. There are also fish: eyeless, translucent, moving with an eerie purposefulness through water that seems too shallow for their activity. Yet they find depth somehow. The water shimmers with them.

Along the stone shelves: insects of uncommon size. Centipedes as long as your forearm, segmented backs catching what luminescence exists, moving with patient inevitability across the raw stone. Spiders have strung webs in the crevices—not the delicate geometries of surface spiders, but geometric walls, dense and repaired constantly, catching whatever drifts through the still air. You see small bones caught in some of them.

The vegetation is almost an afterthought. Where seepage has created thin soil, pale root-like growths emerge—not quite fungi, not quite plant. They don't photosynthesize; they're strategies for existing in the absence of light. Translucent, fibrous, they thread across damp stone like veins in marble. Occasionally they flower—or something like flowering—in small nodules that might be reproductive or might simply be the way they bulge when fed by the water that passes through them. These growths feel new too, as though the entire ecosystem is still learning how to exist here.

The temperature is constant and cool—maybe 8 degrees Celsius, never varying. It's the cold of stone that goes deep, that remembers the season above only as a distant rumor.

The water itself is cold enough that if you stayed in it long, your fingers would go numb. But it barely rises to mid-calf at its deepest pools. The shallow water moves nothing. Leaves from above—the rare leaf that's blown into the chimney—sit on the surface exactly where they landed, waiting to decompose at their own pace, which seems to be centuries.

The cavern system doesn't feel finished. The erosion that created it seems to have stopped abruptly, as if the water that carved it paused mid-thought. The stone walls show fresh breakage, clean striations. Some shelves look like they might collapse tomorrow or might stand for ten thousand years. There's no way to know. Geological youth and geological stability are not the same thing here.

And everything—*everything*—is loud. The bats alone make conversation impossible. The animals churning in the water add clicks and chittering. The occasional drip of condensation creates small echoing plunks that repeat off curved stone and return distorted. Even your breathing seems to add to the general noise, as though the cavern is orchestrating all sound, including yours.

Yet nothing changes. The pools remain the same depth. The bats hunt in the same patterns they've always hunted. The pale creatures boil in the water with the same turbulence they had an hour ago, or will have tomorrow. No rain reaches here to swell the lakes. No seasons alter anything. The temperature is a constant promise: this will not shift.

You are standing in an equilibrium so profound it feels like it might be older than time, even though—by every sign—it was born yesterday.
