# The Undergarden

You enter through a shaft—industrial, bolted, descended by a cage that groans downward through centuries of cultivated silence. The air changes at threshold, becomes textured. Cool. Not cold: precisely 9 degrees Celsius, maintained by the massive circulation tunnels that run beneath the city above like root systems in reverse.

The floor, when you step onto it, is not floor. It is compressed mycelium—woven fungal matter treated with something chemical, something that keeps it from breaking down, from returning. It holds footprints like damp earth but is firm enough for rail-lines and the weight of wheeled carts. The carts are here, along the main thoroughfare, pulled by nothing you can see at first. Then you notice: the vegetable fibers braided into traces, the bioluminescent fungi embedded in their harnesses, pulsing a soft green every three seconds. They move at a steady pace, seemingly autonomous, following grooves worn into the mycelium substrate over decades.

The vegetation is not arrangement—it is the architecture itself.

The walls rise in columns of fused plant matter: living wood that has been trained upward and laterally into support structures, their bark still breathing slightly, still conducting fluids. But woven *through* this woody lattice is an entire secondary growth—vining things with leaves of different morphologies, some paper-thin, some succulent, some thorned in ways that serve no obvious predator. The vines have been deliberately trained along the wood columns, plaited, sometimes twisted into patterns that suggest intention rather than chaos. Here and there, where a column meets a junction, the vines have been sheared or allowed to develop into dense masses—living walls that partition the space into districts.

Light comes from above, not the sun. The ceiling (far above, barely visible in the cool, breathable air) glows with a pallid luminescence—panels of something organic, something cultivated. Perhaps bacterial colonies, perhaps engineered algae. It gives the entire space a uniform twilight, neither bright nor dim, a kind of eternal afternoon that has no beginning or end. Shadows exist but are gentle. Color is muted—the greens are dark, almost slate-tinged. The mycelium is pale tan. Industrial metal (rust-spotted, oiled, well-maintained) of the infrastructure shows as intervals of worn grey against the living world.

The scale opens *outward*. You are in a chamber, yes, but the chamber extends—you can see, perhaps two kilometers ahead, the wall of the place curves slightly, suggesting immensity. To your left and right, the built structures begin. 

Warehouses of woven plant fiber, walls that breathe, roofs that are themselves gardens of specialized fungi. Windows—yes, actual windows—filled with panes of something organic and translucent, yellowed like aged paper. Behind them, you sense movement, activity. The buildings are not dense. There is space between them: corridors where air circulates, plazas where the mycelium floor opens into shallow pools of standing liquid—not water exactly, but a nutrient solution, clear and cool, that reflects the overhead glow in long, still mirrors.

The vegetation is not wild. It is managed with an obsessive care that speaks of generations of cultivation. 

Somewhere in the middle distance, a structure of significant size: a factory or processing plant, its walls a hybrid of treated wood and riveted steel. Steam—faint, cool steam—rises from vents along its length. The building is connected to others via bridges made of the same trained-plant material, a network of pathways that suggests a town, a city, an organized human presence attempting to do something with this impossible garden.

But the density is wrong for a proper city. There are spaces. Large spaces. Between warehouses, the mycelium extends bare, or nearly bare—only scattered low-growing fungi, some of them bioluminescent in shades of blue and sickly green. These spaces feel intentional. Buffer zones. Or perhaps what remains of the original substrate before human intervention reshaped it into infrastructure.

The life here—you begin to notice it—is not absent.

Small things move through the undergarden. Creatures that seem evolved (or designed, or selected) for this environment: arthropods of various scales, some no larger than a fingernail, others the size of a forearm. Segmented. Many-legged. A few have adapted to the plant matter so thoroughly they are difficult to distinguish from it—chitinous exoskeletons in patterns that mimic leaf-veining, that echo the coloring of the fungi. You see one the size of a cat, its segments articulated in ways that allow it to climb the mycelium pillars with no visible effort, its antennae extended and gently quivering. It is not aggressive. It seems to be performing some function—tending, perhaps, or feeding on the lower growth.

Birds exist here too, though they are strange. Adapted to the low-light, the cool: their plumage is dark and iridescent in ways that make them nearly invisible until they move. You hear them more than see them—soft chirps, almost subsonic, a communication that seems tied to the life-cycle of the fungal ceiling above. One lands on a mycelium pillar ahead of you. It is smaller than you expected, and its beak is not for seeds but for something softer—fungal fruiting bodies, perhaps, or the nutritious slime-trails that certain fungi produce.

The sound level is fundamentally low. No machinery that thunders. No crowds. But it is not silent.

There is a persistent hum—electrical, perhaps, or the vast circulation of air through channels you cannot see. There is the occasional creak of the trained-plant structure, responding to pressures and stresses, wood adjusting to load. Every six seconds, an automated pump somewhere closes with a soft *thunk*, regulating the nutrient flow. The fungi themselves produce sound at the edge of hearing—a kind of cellular whisper, an ultrasonic clicking that your inner ear catches as presence rather than noise. The arthropods are quiet, but their passage across the mycelium creates a soft susurrus, a rustling of many small movements.

Voices—human voices—emerge distantly from one of the larger structures. Someone calling out measurements. Others responding. The sounds are calm, unhurried. This is a place where work happens at a biological pace, aligned with the rhythms of growth and decay.

The strangeness sits beneath all of this like an undercurrent.

You become aware that the vegetation is *too* ordered. The trained columns are geometric enough to be artificial, yet they have not been carved or built in the conventional sense. They have been grown. Directed. The fungi colonizing the walls are not randomly distributed—they form patterns, pale circles against darker wood, that suggest cultivation at the microscopic level. Someone is tending this place with precision. Someone is *farming* it.

And then there is the question of the water, or rather, its absence. In an environment this saturated with life, with fungi, with growth—where is the water? The mycelium should be damp. The plants should be dewy. Yet everything here has a faint desiccation, a crispness. The air is cool but not humid. The vines, though clearly alive, have a slightly papery texture to their leaves. The nutrient solution in the pools below is not water but something else—something engineered, something that sustains without allowing the familiar wetness of a normal garden. 

It is as if the entire ecosystem has been rewritten to function *without* water, or with a minimal, controlled, artificial proxy. The fungi are not the moisture-lovers of the surface world. They have been selectively bred, perhaps over generations, to thrive in this cool, arid environment. The plants are not as they were. They are adapted. Modified. They are what remains of the surface biota after it has been stripped down, compressed, and reengineered into something that can exist indefinitely in a sealed chamber beneath a city.

The geology beneath the mycelium substrate is raw. Where the floor has been disturbed for repairs or maintenance, you glimpse the layer beneath: stone that is fractured, newly broken, looking as though it was split only months ago. The mycelium treatment has not yet completely sealed these breaks. There is something about the roughness of it that suggests this place is still forming, still being built, still emerging from an underworld that was only recently accessed. The cavern itself may be natural (limestone, perhaps, or volcanic), but the human intervention is so recent, so active, that the entire space feels like an act of construction in real-time.

You walk further. The scale begins to make sense.

This is not a single cavern but a network of connected chambers, visible as a series of gradually rising and falling sections, each with its own cluster of structures, its own microclimate regulation (you notice the temperature variation slightly as you move—some areas are a degree or two cooler, others fractionally warmer, suggesting zoned environmental control). The total expanse must be kilometers across. The population here—you estimate, based on the size of the structures, the extent of the infrastructure—must number in the thousands, perhaps tens of thousands. An entire underground city. A town. A civilization.

And it is functional. There is no sense of abandonment or failure here. The structures are maintained. The mycelium pathways are groomed. The fungal ceiling glows with consistency. The automated carts move along their grooves on schedule. Whatever experiment or necessity drove the creation of this place, it has succeeded. It is *working*.

But the emptiness between the built structures—the sparse distribution of density—suggests that this place was designed for more, or that it is newly populated, recently resettled. The infrastructure has capacity for growth. The mycelium can be expanded. New columns can be trained. The fungal networks can be extended. This is a place that is actively becoming itself, still under construction, still in the process of emergence.

The activity is constant but unhurried. Movement. Growth. Adjustment. Slow, relentless. The biological pace that underlies everything here is not frantic but inexorable. Fungi are extending their filaments through the substrate. Vines are growing along the columns, adding bulk, adding structure. The creatures—the arthropods and birds—are performing their functions, feeding, reproducing, maintaining the lower trophic levels. The humans, in their structures, are monitoring, adjusting, managing the slow dance of an ecosystem that exists beneath a world, separate from sun and rain and the familiar biota of the surface.

And it is strange because it should not work. It should not be sustainable. It should not be possible.

Yet it persists. It functions. It is, in its way, beautiful—this garden without water, this city of cultivated strangeness, this underworld that blooms in the cool darkness beneath a civilization that may or may not know what grows beneath its foundations.

The air, when you breathe it, tastes faintly of earth and something chemical, something processed. There is a life-smell to it, but distant, muted, as if the nose itself has been adapted to expect this and no longer register it as unusual.

You move deeper into the undergarden. The work continues. The light remains constant. The cool persists. The vegetation surrounds you—always present, never overwhelming, a green-grey embrace of engineered life that has learned, over generations of intentional cultivation, to exist without the one thing every plant is supposed to need.

The strangeness, you realize, is not strangeness at all to those who live here. It is simply *how things are*.
