# The Sealed Galleries

Below the final crust, the air moves in currents that have not changed course in forty thousand years. You stand in a vast hollow that opens downward—a buried valley, really, though "valley" suggests too much variety. The floor stretches for miles in all directions before the walls of older stone rise up and close the sky away.

There is no water. But the stone itself breathes moisture. The walls weep—not flowing, but perpetually damp, minerals dissolved and redeposited in slow cycles that have glazed the rock in sequences of pale crust, each layer as thin as eyelash. Where your light catches these glazes, they gleam with a wetness that isn't water at all but something closer to lacquer, to the dried residue of geological patience. The air tastes mineral. Your throat grows coated within minutes.

The vegetation here is impossible. It should not exist—yet it does, in magnificent, unbothered profusion.

The plants have adapted so completely to this sealed world that they barely resemble anything that grows above. Tendrils of pale coral-like growth spiral out from the stone itself, some of it rooted, some of it something else entirely—perhaps the boundary between plant and chemical accident has grown irrelevant at this depth. They are lush, yes, but a lushness of texture rather than color: soft pinks, bone-whites, the deep lavenders of bruises, all of it cool-seeming even when you touch it and find it warm. Some growths are frilly, almost delicate. Others are fleshy and swollen like organs exposed. They climb the walls, pool in the depressions of the ancient stone, cluster so thickly in certain hollows that they form something almost like forests—but forests where the trees are yielding, almost soft, where you could push through them without resistance.

The temperature is what keeps you alive here. The sealed galleries maintain a perfect, perpetual cool—around fifty degrees in your estimation. It's the cold of deep earth, of places that the sun has forgotten. This temperate blandness pervades everything. Nothing is uncomfortable; nothing is alive in the urgent sense. The cold preserves everything in a kind of suspension.

Light comes from above, through cracks so ancient and so settled that they have become permanent features of the world. Thin shafts descend at angles that never change. Dust hangs in them—dust that has been falling in those same shafts for millennia, creating visible pillars of slow descent. Some of these shafts are no wider than your hand. Others open into pools of murky illumination where the pale vegetation seems almost to glow, having evolved to drink from even this thin archive of sunlight. Most of the galleries exist in a permanent dusk, where your eyes adjust and then stop adjusting, where the stone in the distance blurs into suggestion. Phosphorescence would be a word, if you wanted to be poetic about the way some of the deeper vegetation holds a faint internal luminescence—though it's probably just the angle, probably just your eyes learning new ways to see.

The sound is what disturbs you.

It is not loud in the sense of violent volume, but it is *present*—a constant, layered murmur that fills the galleries like water filling a cup. It originates everywhere and nowhere. There is the dripping—not regular, not rhythmic, but perpetual—of condensation falling on stone and pooling vegetation. There is a deep, geological creaking, the sound of stone remembering its weight, of pressure slowly shifting across millions of years of settlement. There is a rustling, almost like wind, though there is no wind; it's the movement of the pale fronds, responding to air currents so subtle that you cannot feel them directly, only see their effect in this susurrus of growth against growth. And beneath it all, a distant rushing—water somewhere far below, or something like water, moving through channels so deep and so old that its sound has become a constant, almost a silence of its own.

The fauna here is abundant in ways that contradict your intuition.

Creatures move through the vegetation—most of them small, most of them pale like the plants, as if color itself had become unnecessary in this world. There are things with too many joints that navigate the vertical spaces with unsettling grace. There are clusters of smaller organisms that might be individual creatures or might be colonies; the distinction seems unimportant to them. Some are sessile, rooted to the plant-growth, filtering something from the air that seems perfectly clear to you. Others move in slow, determined patterns, and you suspect they have been following those patterns for longer than human civilizations have existed above. They do not seem aware of you. They do not seem aware of much. They simply *are*, engaged in processes so gradual that they might as well be geological themselves.

The settlement was built here—or perhaps grew here—at some point so distant that its original purpose has become indecipherable. Stone structures rise from the valley floor at intervals: not dense, not crowded, but clearly intentional. They are arranged in patterns that suggest function, though that function is now illegible. Some are towers, if towers is a word for structures that taper so gradually that you cannot determine where they actually end. Some are more like wells or shafts sunk into the floor itself, as if mining for something that was here. Some are geometric, all straight edges, while others curve in ways that seem to follow no natural form—yet they seem perfectly at home here, as if they've always been part of this place's geology.

These structures are not maintained. They are not abandoned, exactly, but rather settled—absorbed into the slow logic of the caves. The vegetation has woven itself through them. The stone of which they were made has developed the same glaze-weeping as the walls. It is impossible to determine which structures are recent (within the last thousand years) and which predate the vegetation itself. They exist in a kind of temporal blur, all of them equally aged.

There is no technology here. No tools, no devices, no infrastructure of the kind that requires maintenance. Whatever logic of organization the settlement maintains is biological, is structural only insofar as stone is structural. Channels run between the buildings that might be roads or might simply be the paths that water (or water-like substances) carve naturally. Some channels flow downward; some seem to run level for miles before sloping gently deeper.

The air, sealed as it is, has developed its own ecology. Spores drift in visible clouds during certain slow-seasons. The taste of mineral changes slightly based on where you stand, as if different sections of the caves maintain different atmospheric compositions. In some hollows, the air itself seems to shimmer slightly, warmed by decomposition happening deeper still, processes that operate at a pace so slow that they barely register as heat.

Everything here is in conversation with everything else, in a dialogue that has been occurring, uninterrupted and unrushed, for longer than anything above ground has existed. The sealed galleries have settled into their final form—not static, not truly, but changing only along paths that were established so long ago that change itself has become indistinguishable from permanence. The creatures here will not evolve further. The structures will not age more dramatically. The vegetation will continue its slow spread, but its spread follows contours that were determined eons ago, and it will continue along those same contours until the stone itself finally decides to shift again—which might take another forty thousand years.

You are inside the world's lungs, listening to its endless, patient breathing.
