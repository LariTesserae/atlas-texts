# The Bog-Works of Keth'lin

You step onto boards that were ancient when your grandparents were children. They're slick. Not from recent rain—though it rains here constantly, a mist that never quite becomes drops, never quite becomes air—but from the permanent weeping of the structure itself. The planks are fitted with obsessive care, mortise and peg and rabbit-joint, all of it handwork, all of it slowly surrendering to the water it was built to withstand.

The boards form a narrow maze. They're the *only* thing you walk on. Beyond their edges, the bog begins immediately—not gradually, but as a definite threshold. You can reach down and touch the boundary: solid timber, then absence. The space between planks is intentional; water flows beneath them in channels cut deeper than your arm is long, visible through the gaps as dark, moving glass. It's *loud*. The water doesn't rush but rather mumbles constantly, a collective murmur of ten thousand small flows, each with its own pitch. The sound is conversational, almost intimate, but never silent.

The bog itself is open water. Not quite. It's difficult to say what it *is*. The surface appears solid from a distance—a uniform pale grey-green, almost luminescent—but as you approach, you understand it's a mat of living things. Moss. Algae films. Plants with names that wouldn't make sense in sunlight. The mat is thin enough in places that you can see water moving beneath it, shapes moving, and thick enough in others that small animals walk across it without sinking. You've seen deer-like creatures do this in the early mornings, their joints moving at angles that suggest they're not quite jointed the way yours are.

The platform *works*. That's the strange thing. It's not maintained by any visible hand—you've seen no one—but it *maintains itself*. Fresh boards appear in the gaps where old ones have rotted through. Not sprouted. Appeared. As if the structure has the ambition to persist and produces what it needs. The people who built this are gone, probably, or gone most of the time. But the system they created has become a thing unto itself, a semi-living bureaucracy of wood and craft and intention.

The vegetation on the boards themselves is almost nil. A few lichens, encouraged and cultivated in subtle grooves, prized perhaps for their grip-surface in wet conditions. But nothing grows *on* the structure. The forest—if you can call it that—exists only in the mat, only in the water, only *beneath* the boards. The visible world is stripped down. Wood, water, sky.

The sky is always low. The mist catches at about the height of a standing person, so your world contracts to a cylinder perhaps thirty feet tall and a hundred feet wide. The paths spiral within this space, creating small clearings, each one bounded on all sides by the grey nothing of fog-light. Within each clearing, the boards form geometric patterns—spirals, grids, nested rectangles—as if the original builders couldn't resist giving their platform a hidden architecture, a system that would only be apparent from a height you'll never achieve.

The animals live in layers. The water itself is thick with them. You see fins, sometimes, breaching the living mat. You hear them—constant chittering, clicking, sounds like wood on wood and sounds like nothing you can name. At night, they surface. The bioluminescence is green-white, cold, and so abundant that you can read by it. Thousands of small lights in the water, each one the lamp of something living, something eating, something signaling to something else. The communication is intense, almost frantic. The bog is not at rest. It never rests.

In the water-mat itself, on its surface and just beneath, smaller things move. Insects with wings that beat in registers too high to hear clearly, though you feel them as a kind of pressure against your ears. Arthropods of unclear taxonomies, their carapaces catching the dim light in iridescent flashes. Some of them are large. You saw something the size of a cat yesterday, moving with the efficiency of a hunting animal, its limbs too many and jointed like a ruler. It ignored you entirely.

The birds are sparse. That's the one absence that feels intentional. You've seen three in the three weeks you've been here. Large ones. Patient. They hunt the fog-layer, diving into the mist with a precision that suggests the fog is less opaque to them than it is to you. Their calls are profound and slow, sounds that seem to take several seconds to express a single note.

The temperature is always the same. Cool without being cold. Your breath never quite mists, never quite stays clear. It becomes part of the ambient humidity the moment it leaves your lungs. Sweat doesn't evaporate; it gathers on your skin like a second moisture, an internal mist. You don't get cold, exactly. You become *wet in your bones*. Everything does. The wood never truly dries. The tools people brought here—you found some, abandoned near the spiral-clearings—are all starting to rust despite being ancient. The metal itself seems to weep, to surrender its hardness to the water-heavy air.

The boards underfoot are worn to a shine in the center of each path, but the shine isn't from many feet. The wear-pattern suggests maybe two or three people, moving in this system for years. Decades, perhaps. The grooves of their passage are carved deep enough that walking in them is inevitable. You don't choose the path. The path chooses you. It's worn to your gait already, whoever you are.

Sometimes, in the fog-clearings at dusk, the air itself seems to move without wind. It ripples. The mist becomes visible as a thing with texture, nearly solid. The animals sound frantic at these moments. Whatever communication they maintain through the dark water and the mat becomes urgent, overlapping, almost violent. Then it passes. The fog settles. The murmur returns to its baseline.

The structure itself has been here longer than the bog, you think. Or the bog has grown around it, accepted it, integrated it. There's no clear sense of which came first. The oldest boards, the ones at the center of the maze, are blackened with age and something else—some staining that might be mold or might be oxidation or might be the bog itself, slowly painting the structure in the colors of the water. Beneath them, you can see water moving. The boards flex with it, subtle, giving. They don't break. Whatever wood this is, it has a patience that matches the bog's patience. They're the same age now. They're the same thing, almost. The structure and the land have made a treaty.

There's a quality to being here that resembles being held. Not constrained. Held. The paths guide you but gently. The water is always present but never threatening—not openly. The animals are abundant but indifferent. The light is insufficient but consistent. You can see exactly as far as you need to see, no farther. It's strange. It's ancient. It's entirely constructed. And it moves at the pace of water, the pace of rot, the pace of birds' calls and creatures' signals, a tempo so slow it almost feels like stillness, like rest, like the world finally being allowed to settle into the shape it was always meant to take.
