# The Condensation Halls

Water moves here not as rain or rivers, but as a constant breathing. The air is saturated—thick with moisture that collects on everything it touches. You understand this place through dampness first, before your eyes adjust to the dark.

The walls are built. Every wall. The ground beneath your feet is constructed stone—uneven, fitted by hand without mortar, the gaps wide enough that moisture seeps through and collects in pockets along the floor. The ceiling, thirty feet up, is also stone, also fitted, but cracked in places where the weight of something—age, water pressure, the earth above—has begun its slow work of deformation. This is a city that has never seen sky, or has forgotten it.

The darkness is near-total. Whatever light exists comes from gathered dampness itself: certain mineral deposits in the stone walls—olivine, perhaps, or something stranger—that faintly luminescence when water passes across them. This glow is weak, greenish, inconsistent. Your eyes never quite adjust. Shadows move in the spaces between the light.

The air is loud.

Not with voices. With dripping. With the constant percussion of water finding its way downward. Thousands of drops, each one echoing in a slightly different pitch as it falls from different heights, strikes different surfaces. Some drops land on stone and vanish into the porous gaps. Others land in standing pools—shallow reservoirs that have accumulated in the depressions of the floor. The sound is layered: a high insistent tapping from the heights, a heavier *plop-plop* from the pools, and underneath it all, a low rushing susurrus that might be water moving through deeper channels, or might be the city itself breathing.

Your breath makes sound here too. Even stillness is loud.

The vegetation parameter reads 0.2. There is no plant life, but the absence is not simple sterility. The walls support a different form of growth. Fungal colonies—pale, thread-like, almost translucent—spread across the stone in branching patterns. They follow the moisture, and the moisture follows the geometry of the construction. The fungal networks are so fine they're barely visible except in the greenish glow, where they appear as shadow-patterns, lace-work, the blueprints of some invisible architecture. They don't grow visibly as you watch. But over seasons—or what passes for seasons in this damp unchanging environment—they advance incrementally, breaking down the stone grain by grain. The walls are being consumed and held together simultaneously by these threadlike things.

The scale is regional. The halls extend for miles, you understand this instinctively. You can't see it—the darkness forbids clear sight beyond fifty feet—but you can feel it in the way sound propagates, the way the air moves subtly in directions that suggest vast neighboring chambers. This is not a small place. It is a network. A labyrinth of constructed passages, some wide enough for three people to walk abreast, others narrowing to crawl-spaces where the ceiling presses down and water drips directly onto your shoulders.

The density is high. Not because there are many people—fauna is rare here, at 1.4/3. But because the space itself is densely packed with features: support pillars set close together, some of them cracked and leaning against their neighbors; alcoves carved into walls at irregular intervals (what were they for? storage? altars? the answer has been forgotten); channels cut into the floor to direct the water's flow in particular directions, though the channels clog and overflow, creating new paths. Horizontal wooden beams cross some passages at chest height—ancient, waterlogged, no longer quite rigid, they bow under their own weight. Ropes, also ancient, hang from some of these beams. For what? Climbing? Securing cargo that no longer arrives? The ropes are slick with algae or fungal growth. They move slightly in the air currents. Touching one would soak your hand in mineral-thick water.

The few creatures that inhabit this place are adapted to near-darkness and perpetual damp. You might glimpse them: pallid crustaceans the size of your hand, with long feelers and no visible eyes, clicking softly as they move across the stone. Blind fish in the pools, their skin translucent enough that you can see the faint movement of their organs. A bird once, perhaps—something vestigial, with diminished wings and skin instead of feathers—but it is extremely rare. Most life here is simpler: bacteria, perhaps, working in the waterlogged spaces; microorganisms turning the slow chemistry of stone and moisture into something else. The ecosystem is minimal but present, a thin persistence.

Temperature is cool—around 50 degrees Fahrenheit, constant, unchanging. No sun reaches here to cause diurnal variation. No seasonal shift. The cold and dampness together create an environment where time moves differently. Objects don't decay rapidly. They absorb moisture and become part of the stone, slowly. The fungal threads work slowly. The water seeps slowly. Everything is in motion, but the motion is patient, geologic, indifferent to human scales of urgency.

The technology that built this place was primitive—hand-tool primitive. The stone blocks are dressed but not perfectly fitted. The work is visible: chisel marks on many surfaces, variations in the cut that suggest individual hands, different levels of skill. No mortar means water exploits every gap, and over time the gaps have widened. The fitted-dry construction is both clever and failing. Someone understood hydrology well enough to cut the channels, to angle certain floors to encourage drainage, but the knowledge has been lost or the design has been overwhelmed by the relentless seepage of water into places it was never meant to go.

The place is fully constructed. There is no natural substrate. The ground beneath the stone channels is also built—compacted clay, perhaps, or another layer of stone below it. The world here is artificial, self-contained, cut off from whatever natural landscape might exist above or beyond it. You might wonder if it was always like this, or if this was once a natural cave system that was later engineered. The evidence is ambiguous. The stone is too regularly fitted in most places to be natural, but too unevenly fitted in others. Perhaps both are true. Perhaps the builders adapted existing geology, expanded it, imposed order on it without fully erasing what was there.

The weirdness is not extreme—at 1.7/3, it's muted, integrated—but it accumulates. The phosphorescence in the stone is unexplained. The fungal patterns, when you look at them over time, seem to follow geometries that almost but don't quite resemble writing or mathematics. Occasionally you find objects that don't match the primitiveness of the construction: a smooth sphere of unknown material, too regular to be natural; a section of wall where the stone has been polished to mirror-smoothness and then deliberately damaged, as if someone was trying to conceal something. Time has done its work on these anomalies—they're covered in fungal film, crusted with mineral deposits, dark with damp—but they're there, evidence of something the builders knew that the current inhabitant does not.

The dynamic is active. The water is always moving, always finding new paths. The pools drain and refill on some rhythm you haven't yet learned. Occasionally, the sound of a distant collapse—a section of weakened wall finally surrendering to the combination of water and weight and fungal erosion. The sound carries for miles through the stone, a deep groan that takes seconds to fade. These collapses create new passages, reroute the water, alter the acoustic landscape. The dripping pattern changes. New fungal colonies move into the disturbed stone. Small tremors sometimes pass through the floor, so subtle you feel them more as changes in air pressure than as actual motion.

Life here moves at multiple paces simultaneously. The water moves quickly—you can hear it, see it flowing in the channels, watch it drip. But the deep processes—the slow dissolution of stone, the expansion of the fungal networks, the structural failure of the ancient construction—these move on timescales of years or decades. You are caught between these rhythms, aware of both, unable to match either.

The darkness is the constant fact. Even as your eyes adjust, the adjustment is minimal. The luminescent minerals provide not light but the *concept* of light, enough to prevent absolute blindness but not enough to see clearly. Colors don't exist here except as shades of the same greenish-grey. Edges are soft. Distances are uncertain. Your perception of the space is always somewhat dreamlike, constructed as much from sound and touch and intuition as from sight.

This is a place where something was built, something was maintained, something was lost. It continues to exist, but it is slowly transforming into something else. Not ruins—ruins imply a defined end to habitation. This is still inhabited, still active, still slowly being worked on by the forces that created it and the forces that are now reclaiming it. The construction and the deconstruction are happening simultaneously, indistinguishable from each other, partners in an ancient and ongoing negotiation.
