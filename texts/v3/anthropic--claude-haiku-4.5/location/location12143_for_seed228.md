# The Cistern

You descend through a shaft—natural fissure or engineered, impossible to tell now—and the air thickens around you. Humid. Warm. Not summer-warm; something older in the warmth, as if the stone itself remembers heat from depths.

The walls weep.

Not dramatically. Not in sheets. But continuously, persistently—a thousand thin seeps that have been running for so long they've worn the basalt into soft velvet. Your hand against it is damp, almost slick. The seepage patterns form pale mineral trails, calcium deposits building their own branching language down the rock face. Some trails are fresh and wet; others are ancient, calcified into white scars. All of them are present at once, a palimpsest of drainage written in stone.

The floor is perhaps thirty meters across—a sunken chamber or caldera that belongs more to liquid than air. Water pools exist here, but calling them "pools" is like calling an ocean a puddle. They're shallow, no more than shin-deep where you can see the bottom, but they *exist*. They don't evaporate entirely. They sit with the patience of things that have been here longer than anyone remembering has been alive.

The stone beneath is layered, stratified. Light gray, darker gray, almost black in places where something has stained it—oxidation, or something the water brought. The stone has been worn into soft undulations by millennia of moisture, by the patient work of dissolution. Nothing is sharp. Everything curves.

Around the perimeter, maybe two meters up the curved wall, you see the structures.

Not buildings. Not quite. A structure suggests intent, completion, occupancy. These are more like... *marks*. Arrangements. The faint geometries of something built and then either abandoned or absorbed into the natural state so thoroughly you can barely distinguish construction from erosion. Metal—impossible in a space this wet, and yet—catches the light. Oxidized to a deep rust-orange, yes, but metal nonetheless. Framework of something. Platforms, or scaffolding that calcified into permanence. They don't serve a purpose you can identify now. They're too weathered, too integrated. Perhaps they were never meant to be read as functional. Perhaps they were always meant to dissolve into this.

The light comes from *everywhere and nowhere*.

There is no sun source. There is no clear lamp or luminescent organism. Instead, the stone itself seems to glow—a bioluminescent quality that shouldn't exist in stone, but does. The light is bright enough to see clearly, enough to read texture and shadow, but sourceless. It's a bright twilight, a perpetual dusk-without-darkness. The glow is slightly blue-white, concentrating around the areas of highest moisture, as if the light comes from the water itself, or from something in the water too small to see, something engineered to survive here across centuries, still working, still glowing. It makes the weeping walls luminous. The mineral deposits shine like veins of something precious.

The air moves.

Not wind—there's no wind in a sealed chamber—but the air *churns*. Thermal currents, you'd say at first, until you notice the pattern is *wrong*. It's not smooth. It's turbulent, almost chaotic. Warm air rises in one corner, spirals, creates vortices that move the moisture around in visible patterns. Sometimes the currents create small whirlpools in the water pools themselves, spinning slowly, steadily, without any external energy. The water should settle. It doesn't. It moves like something is stirring it from below, or like the water itself is responding to forces you don't have language for.

This creates sound.

The turbulence creates a constant low *roar*—not loud in bursts, but sustained. A groaning, hushing, almost musical quality. The water's movement adds to it: dripping from the walls, yes, but also the whisper of shallow water being moved by unseen currents, small waves crossing the pool surfaces. The air itself seems to hum. It's not unpleasant. It's the sound of a place that's *alive* in some sense, that's constantly working, constantly in conversation with itself.

There is almost nothing *in* the water. One creature—or perhaps once every few days—you might see something break the surface. Not fish. The shape is wrong. Segmented. It moves with purpose but no apparent method. It doesn't flee from light or sound. It seems indifferent to your presence. It sinks again. You won't see it again for weeks, maybe, and when you do it might be the same creature or a different one. There's no way to count.

The vegetation is sparse, and wrong.

Near the pools, sometimes you find growth: filamentous algae, yes, but also something else. Structures that are almost crystalline, almost organic. They grow slowly from the mineral-rich water, forming geometries that are too regular to be entirely natural, too complex to have been engineered by human hands. They're some third thing. They shimmer in the blue-white light. When you touch them they're neither soft nor hard—they're something between, a texture that doesn't map onto your tactile categories. Some are barely visible. Some reach half a meter tall. They don't interfere with movement through the chamber. They seem to occupy the space *around* you rather than blocking it.

The chamber isn't sealed, you realize. Not entirely.

At the far end, there are openings. Fissures leading deeper. The water that weeps down the walls must come from *somewhere*—from above, yes, but how far above? From the sides? There are no visible springs, but there are channels cut (or eroded, or worn, or *made* somehow) that feed the pools. And the water in the pools must drain somewhere. You can't see an outlet. But the water level stays constant. The system is balanced. The system is old. The system is still working.

The air smells like stone, minerals, something almost metallic, something almost sweet. It's not unpleasant. It's the smell of deep places, of geology made present, of water that's been cycling through rock for longer than you can imagine.

Time moves oddly here.

You're not sure if you've been here for hours or days. The light doesn't change. The sounds don't cycle. You don't get noticeably hungrier or thirstier, though you're not sure why. Maybe the water has properties. Maybe the air itself feeds something in you. Maybe time just works differently at this depth, in this stable, turbulent, ancient, ever-present state.

The chamber holds you.

It's not hostile. It's not welcoming. It simply *is*, and because you're in it, you *are too*. You move through it as a small presence in a small space that's nonetheless vast, filled with emptiness and the soft grinding work of water, light, air, and something you can't quite name.

Around the metal structures—the built things, the traces—you sometimes find objects.

Not many. But scattered, spaced far apart, they sit on the stone or half-buried in mineral deposit: tools, maybe, or pieces of tools. Made of material you can't identify. Smooth. Cold even in the warm air. Designed for a purpose that makes sense for maybe one tenth of a second before the logic dissolves and you're back to not understanding.

There are no other people.

But you're not certain there have never been people. The structures suggest *someone*, sometime. Maybe they're gone. Maybe they adapted. Maybe they became part of the circulation, the turbulent cycle, and you're simply not recognizing them as present.

The chamber continues. The water weeps. The air churns. The light glows from sources that don't exist. And in the center of it all, small and vast and balanced and turbulent, the cistern holds its breath—or breathes continuously, and you're simply not equipped to hear the rhythm.
