# The Coven-Marsh of Threen

You enter it between two fingers of lodgepole pine—not grand, but old—and the ground gives under you immediately. Not mud. Not quite. The earth here is a living felt, made of moss-hummocks and the interlaced root-crowns of things that died when your grandparents' grandparents were young. The mat holds you. Your boots sink two inches and stop.

The air tastes of decay and growth happening in the same breath. It's cold—the kind of cold that lives here year-round, pressed up from water that never quite dries, that seeps through everything. Frost-ferns have begun to mottle the north side of every plant-stem, even though the season isn't quite there yet. When you exhale, your breath lingers visible for longer than it should.

The vegetation doesn't leave room for much else. It doesn't *pile*—that would be forest-floor disorder. Instead it *builds*. The moss has shaped itself into a gentle architecture: low domes, knee-high, that flow around larger presences like water around stones. Sundews cluster in flat rosettes on the wettest hummocks, their tentacles beaded with morning-dew or afternoon-condensation—there's always moisture here. The sphagnum beneath them is pale, almost white, and when you touch it the water releases like squeezing a sponge. Sweetgale and bog-laurel form the skeleton: not tall, never tall, but densely present, their stems dark red and jointed, their leaves small and efficient.

But here's the strangeness: there are *structures* made of the plants themselves. Not grown. Built.

Low walls of cut reeds, bound with a fiber that might be inner bark or might be something else, run in fractured lines through the marsh. They're recent-looking—the cut-ends still pale—but already colonized: moss creeping over them, small ferns unfurling from cracks. The walls don't enclose anything obvious. They create angles. Passages. They divide the open water-channels into smaller pools that seem almost intentional, almost ceremonial in their geometry.

There are bridges too. Saplings—thin, supple, no more than finger-thick—laid across the deepest water-gaps, lashed at the joints with the same binding-fiber. The lashing is tight, precise. Hand-work. The bridges are old enough that liverwort grows across them in a thick carpet, but new enough that you wouldn't trust them with your full weight unless you were small, or used to living here.

The water underneath is black.

Not dark brown or tea-colored as marsh-water usually is. Black. You can see perhaps four inches down—silt, peat, the compressed matter of centuries—and then nothing. The black reflects nothing. It seems to absorb light. Occasionally you hear it: a soft splash that nothing you can see has made. Small ripples expanding from the black to the moss-edges. Once, something large shifted beneath, and the water-surface bulged and subsided. You didn't see what. You're not sure you wanted to.

The silence is almost total.

No birdsong. This is the strangest part—the absence of the small sounds that usually inhabit marsh-edges. No insect hum. The cold keeps most of them away or dormant. But there are *other* sounds, and they're sporadic and very quiet. The water moving when nothing moves it. Small splashes at the edges of your hearing. Once, a sound like clicking—like dry bones in a pouch—from somewhere in the vegetation to your north. It lasted three seconds and didn't repeat.

But the *fauna*.

They're everywhere.

Not visible, mostly. But present with an intensity that makes your skin feel too thin. The water is alive with small dark shapes. Aquatic insects, you tell yourself, but they move with a coordination that insects rarely show. Schools. Organized. When you look directly at them they scatter into the weeds. The moss itself seems to shift if you hold your attention on it too long—not moving, exactly, but as if something underneath is breathing it upward. Shrimp-like things no bigger than your thumbnail live in the sphagnum; you find their carapaces, iridescent, when you press into the moss with your fingers. There's bird-scat everywhere despite the absence of birds.

And larger presences. You feel them. Not seen, but felt—weight-shifts in the water, the sudden stillness that means a vertebrate has paused to regard you. The reeds part sometimes without wind. Once, something that might have been a head, slate-colored, appeared at the water-line, considered you, and disappeared with a slowness that seemed deliberate.

The settlement—if you can call it that—has no people visible. The structures (the wall, the bridges, a low wooden platform sunk into the moss near what might be the center) are *inhabited*, though. There are the subtle signs: a collection of shells arranged in a line along one bridge. Strips of bark peeled from the laurel-stems and laid out in a pattern. Marks cut into a standing dead pine—marks that might be tally or might be language. The whole thing smells of use: human sweat and effort, though old, and something else. Something that might be ritual-making, the accumulated scent of repetition and intention.

The place is *young*, though. Recently made. The ground-erosion shows no depth—the walls haven't had time to settle into the earth, the water hasn't redirected itself around new obstacles. It's still introducing itself, still finding its shape. But that makes it stranger, not easier. A young place that feels inhabited by something that knows exactly what it's doing.

The light is dim. The sky—when you can see it above the vegetation—is the color of lead. It might be dawn or dusk or perpetual afternoon. The shadows under the hummocks are very dark. The pale moss seems to hold what light there is and release it slowly, like bioluminescence, though you know this is impossible. Yet the marsh glows faintly in the dimness, especially near the black water.

You could stay here. The platforms suggest people do. There's something almost restful about it once you stop expecting warmth or clarity or the usual rules of forest-ecology. The cold is consistent. The moisture is constant. The vegetation supports you perfectly. The water doesn't touch you unless you step into it, and even then—you suspect—only if it decides to.

But you become aware, gradually, that you're not moving *through* the place. The passages that seemed random now appear to be leading you in a spiral, slowly inward. The dark water is getting closer. The walls that seemed fractured and ancient are actually forming a pattern, one you're walking into rather than past.

And the splashing sounds are coming from inside the spiral now, not beyond it.

They're gathering. Or they were always gathered, and you're only now reaching their depth.
