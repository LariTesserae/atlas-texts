# The Ascending Mire

The light here is relentless. It comes not from a single sun but from the entire sky, which has gone pale and luminous—not cloud-covered, but bleached, as though the atmosphere itself has become phosphorescent. Nothing casts a shadow. Everything is visible at once: each plant, each stone, the thin mist rising from the ground in thin ribbons that never quite dissipate. The brightness is cool. It doesn't warm.

The water is everywhere and nowhere.

It sits just below the surface—you feel it in the sponge of the ground beneath your feet, in the way your boots sink slightly, in the perpetual sheen of moisture that coats every leaf and stem. The moss is thick here, moss layered upon moss, each stratum a different texture. Some is velvet-soft and yielding. Lower down, it becomes wiry, almost fibrous, bound together by root systems so fine they're nearly invisible. The ground doesn't squelch so much as exhale when you step on it. The air itself is wet—not quite rain, not quite mist, but a permanent suspended moisture that makes breathing feel substantial, like pulling water through your lungs.

Here and there, actual water emerges: seeps rather than streams, places where the water has decided to show itself. These gather in shallow depressions lined with sphagnum and sedge, their surfaces skin-thin, dark brown from tannins. They don't move. They have the patience of things that have all the time in the world.

The vegetation is the architecture.

What was once terrain has become irrelevant. The true geography is written in plants. Dwarf birches lean at impossible angles, their pale bark flaking like old paint, their roots splayed across the surface searching for any drier ground. But there's no drier ground—the roots have simply learned to spread wide, to distribute themselves. Between them grows a dense mat of berry bushes: crowberry, cloudberry, the occasional lingonberry with its dark leaves and translucent fruit. These form a interlocking carpet, waist-high in places, ankle-high in others. Push through them and you disturb clouds of moisture that had accumulated in the hollows between branches.

The understory is a wall of sedge and cotton grass—tall, fine-leaved things that wave slightly though there's almost no wind. Their stems are pale, almost bone-white in the brightness. Mixed through are liverworts and small ferns, and beneath those, a spongy layer of decaying plant matter that may or may not still be called soil. It's all becoming the same thing: plant-as-substrate, substrate-as-plant. The boundary has dissolved.

The elevation is noticeable but not dramatic. You're high enough that the air tastes thin and cool. High enough that the vegetation has surrendered its ambitions toward trees—only the birches attempt height, and they fail gracefully, spreading sideways. The landscape extends upward in gentle increments, almost imperceptible. What would be a journey of hours on flatter ground might take a day here, the slope so gradual that your legs don't know they're climbing until you look back and realize the valley has swallowed itself into distance.

And it is *distant*.

The landscape sprawls. From any given point you can see across an enormous, flat-textured expanse—rolling tundra-like, but denser, more alive than tundra. The vegetation continues to the horizon, uniform in its basic texture but endlessly varied in its microtopography. Occasional outcrops of raw stone push through, their surfaces still jagged, not yet worn smooth by anything. These are young features, freshly revealed. Around them the plants work with slow, patient determination, beginning the process of colonization. Lichen paints orange and white patterns across the exposed faces. Tiny seedlings find purchase in fissures. These rocks feel like they're being gently reabsorbed, unmade, returned to the plant-world from which they briefly emerged.

The industrial presence is faint but unmistakable.

A single peat extraction facility sits perhaps two kilometers to the northeast, visible as a low geometric intrusion—metal and wood scaffolding, weathered but functional. It doesn't roar. The machinery operates at a patient pace, removing layers of accumulated plant matter with slow hydraulic motion, processing them into neat blocks stacked in dying pyramids. The sound it makes is a *thump-hiss*, regular as a heartbeat, audible across the entire landscape. *Thump-hiss.* It's muffled by distance and wet air, but it never truly disappears. There's also the occasional groan of corrugated metal when the wind picks up (which it doesn't, often). And beneath that, the sound of the machinery's circulation pumps, a lower register hum that seems to come from the earth itself.

A narrow rail line cuts through the vegetation northwest, barely elevated, laid directly atop the moss and sedge. The rails are green with oxidation. A narrow-gauge electric line—decrepit but maintained—runs beside it. Occasionally there's the electrical hum when current passes, a thin whine that travels far in the wet air. A single corrugated shelter sits at the railhead, its corrugations running vertically, its paint the color of old rust. Inside (visible through a gap in the door, which doesn't quite close) are coiled cables, a hand pump, mechanical tools. A single desk. Someone works here, though rarely.

The machinery moves slowly. Everything here moves slowly. The peat extraction advances by centimeters per week. The decay that feeds it happened over centuries. The railway carries loads only occasionally—perhaps twice a month. Trucks arrive even less frequently, their engines straining against the soft ground, wheels sinking despite the metal track-plates laid beneath them. Once they leave, the plants begin immediately to reclaim the tire-marks. By the time the next truck comes, the devastation is already half-healed.

The ecology is sparse in animal life but dense in process.

You won't see many creatures. Perhaps a ptarmigan in the distance, still in its white winter plumage despite the season's change (the seasons hardly exist here; it's always the same cool brightness, the same moisture, the same slow turn). Perhaps an insect—a mosquito, though they're rare—or the small brown birds that nest in the dwarf birch. But mostly the place is still. The silence would be absolute if not for the constant sound of the extraction machinery. It creates a strange acoustic environment: wherever you stand, you hear both vast quiet and that regular, patient *thump-hiss*.

The fungi are the true animals here, in a sense. They're everywhere and invisible. The decay layer they work through is their kingdom. The air carries their spores. When the humidity reaches its apex in the early morning (or perhaps always—it's hard to know when "early morning" is in the perpetual brightness), you can almost smell their work: an earthy, complex odor that's neither pleasant nor unpleasant but deeply informative, like reading a book in scent. Death and birth, simultaneously.

The strangeness, if any, is subtle.

It's in the quality of the light—that directionless brightness that makes the landscape feel both exposed and somehow intimate. It's in the absolute evenness of plant coverage, the way no single spot stands out as special, all individualism flattened into a democratic green-brown. It's in the sensation that the place is not finished, that it's still deciding what it is. The raw rocks feel like mistakes, like the landscape hasn't yet figured out whether they should be here. The extraction facility feels temporary, despite being decades old—something that could be removed, though it probably won't be.

There's also something uncanny about the sound field. The *thump-hiss* is loud enough to dominate, yet far enough away that no other sounds can quite fill the space it creates. If the machinery stopped, the silence would be profound—the kind of silence that has been waiting underneath sound, patient, for its turn. But the machinery doesn't stop. It *thump-hisses* on, patient as the moss, patient as the slow climb of elevation, patient as the water seeping through the substrate-that-is-not-quite-soil.

The place has no sharp boundaries. At the limits of vision, the landscape doesn't end so much as continue into haze—not fog exactly, but an atmosphere so full of suspended moisture that differentiation ceases. There are no far mountains. Just the gradual dissolution of features into brightness, into the pale sky, into a elsewhere that exists in the landscape's geometry but not in any visible way.

The temperature hovers just at the edge of comfortable. Cool enough to keep you alert, warm enough that you won't shiver if you stand still. But standing still here feels wrong. The urge is to move, slowly, through the deep vegetation, feeling the wet ground beneath your feet shift slightly with each step, hearing the *thump-hiss* follow you, unable to escape or approach it. It's patient work, walking here. It teaches patience. The place is teaching it constantly.

And beneath everything—felt rather than heard—is the sense of accumulation. This place is building itself, very slowly, into something. The peat layer deepens by millimeters per year. The plants inch their way across raw stone. The water table rises imperceptibly. The machinery processes and processes, converting the old growth into materials for elsewhere. But the place produces faster than it's extracted. The momentum, subtle as it is, is toward more plant, more wet, more of this slow-motion emergence. The landscape is not finished. Perhaps it never will be.
