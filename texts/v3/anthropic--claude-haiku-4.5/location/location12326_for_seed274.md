# The Basalt Pools

The air tastes of minerals before you see the place—a warm, iron-thick taste that coats the mouth. Then the light changes. You've descended into a depression that doesn't announce itself as such; the ground simply becomes unreliable underfoot, sloping away at angles that feel both gentle and wrong.

The pools are what define this space. Not water pooled in a conventional sense, but *saturation*—everywhere, the basalt weeps. The black stone, ancient beyond local reckoning, is not dry anywhere. Water rises through it as if the earth's breath is constant and slow. The rock face shows a thousand vertical seeps, each one leaving its own pale ghost-track where minerals have leached and concentrated. These tracks create patterns that almost read as script, but never quite resolve.

The pools themselves are shallow and interconnected—some only finger-deep, others reaching your knee—separated by ridges of stone worn smooth at their crests from centuries of being crossed by the same paths. The water in them is perfectly clear, but brownish with dissolved iron. It's warm. Warm enough that steam rises from the deeper pools in the early hours, making the air hang thick and close. The warmth isn't comforting; it's vital, animal-hot, like touching something alive.

There is no soil here. No true earth. The valley floor—and it is a valley, though low and bounded—is entirely stone and water. Black basalt, mostly, but also: ochre deposits where mineral-rich waters have evaporated in the shallower depressions, leaving rings like tree-rings that mark wet seasons and dry ones. These deposits are thick with salts and oxides. When you walk across them, your footprints darken momentarily where your warmth activates the damp before the prints dry.

The air itself is thick—not with smoke, but with moisture. Humidity enough that you taste it, that it condenses on any cool surface, that everything here exists in a perpetual state of being slowly dissolved and slowly reconstituted. The sky, when visible through the haze, is pale and washed-out. The sun is a disk without edges.

Life, if it exists, is not visible to the ordinary eye.

But the space is not empty.

There are structures. Very simple ones. A low stone platform built from stacked basalt blocks, its purpose obscure—perhaps a sleeping place, perhaps a shrine, perhaps only shelter from the water. Its edges are rounded by time or by intention; you cannot quite tell. Near it, a series of cairns, small and deliberate, each one perhaps three feet high. They're spaced at irregular intervals, as if marking paths that are no longer followed, or never were obvious. The spacing suggests intention over accident, but it's a faint intention—the structures don't dominate the space. They rest within it, accepted like stones that happened to gather.

There's a fire-pit too, or what remains of one: a rough circle of blackened stones, the interior crusted with ash that hasn't been replenished in a long time. Long enough that moisture has begun working on it, crumbling the ash into paste. Whoever last used it used it with simple tools—no metal, or none that's visible now. The stones weren't precisely fitted; they were chosen and arranged with a kind of casual care.

The sound environment is near-silence, but not empty silence. Water, everywhere, creates a ambient susurrus—the sound of seepage, of tiny movements, of condensation collecting on rock. It's too faint to hear directly; instead you become aware of it as an absence of sound's opposite. In certain moments, a single larger drop will gather and fall into a pool, sending a small ring outward. This happens infrequently, randomly, but enough times that you begin listening for it. The sound is the place's only movement.

The light is dim not because the sun is far away, but because it's filtered through moisture and dust and the light-absorbing black of the basalt. Everything appears slightly grayscale. Colors are muted. The ochre deposits seem more brown than orange. The water has no gleam; it reflects nothing.

The temperature never seems to change. Warm—constant warm—like a long breath held. No wind circulates through the valley. The air stagnates in a comfortable way. You could sleep here without cover. You might sleep here and wake not knowing if an hour or a day had passed.

Nothing has happened here for a very long time. Nothing is happening. And yet the place is saturated—*filled*—with some kind of presence. Perhaps it's only the density of water, of mineral deposits, of stones worn into their final forms. Perhaps it's only the sense of equilibrium so complete that change has become unimaginable. The built things—the cairns, the platform, the fire-pit—seem less like intrusions than like the place's own slow attempt to remember that other things once moved through it.

There is a boundary to this space. You can feel it. Behind you, the terrain begins to rise slightly. Ahead—you can't quite see it, but you sense it—the depression becomes deeper, the water-saturation increases. This pocket is stable, settled, in the middle range of something. It will not flood. It will not drain. It has found its balance and stopped there.

The weirdness would be if something *changed*.
