# The Dry Gardens of Keth

You emerge into a place that doesn't quite breathe—or breathes only in the spaces between moments.

The ground beneath you is scored and fissured, hard-baked clay the color of old bone, split into irregular polygons like a vast mosaic left to cure in a furnace. There's no soil. The clay drinks nothing and gives nothing back. Yet somehow, improbably, *life* has made a cathedral here.

The vegetation erupts in violent contradiction. Towering stems—some thick as a man's wrist, others thin as reeds—rise in tangled profusion, their surfaces sun-darkened to deep rust and maroon. They're not quite trees, not quite grasses. Their leaves, where they exist, are small and waxy, silvered underneath, arranged in patterns that maximize surface area while minimizing water loss. Everything is optimized for theft: stealing moisture from air too dry to hold it, wringing life from a world that has sworn to give none.

The stems creak and groan constantly—a deep wooden complaint that underlies everything. The sound is *overwhelming*. There is no silence here, only layers of noise stacked like sediment. The stems flex in a wind you can barely feel, and they sing. Not beautifully. Harshly. A dry, rasping susurration that builds and swells, dies and returns, never quite reaching crescendo because it never fully descends. The air itself seems to vibrate with complaint.

But louder still—and this is what first strikes you as you arrive—is the *grinding*. Mechanical, purposeful, organized. It comes from the built structures nestled throughout the garden.

The architecture emerges gradually as your eyes adjust. It's woven *into* the vegetation rather than imposed upon it. Low stone buildings—grey limestone fitted without mortar, fitted so precisely that a blade won't pass between the blocks—sit nestled in clearings between the plant stems like stones in a river. The buildings are small, intimate. Some are barely taller than a man. All of them are ancient; their corners are worn smooth, and lichen grows in precise patterns along their seams, evidence of centuries of slow accumulation.

But these structures are not mere ruins. They are operational. Active. Alive with purpose.

Each building has an aperture facing the prevailing wind, and set into that aperture is a simple turbine—wooden vanes carved by hand, rotating in the constant, arid air. The rotation drives a mechanism inside each structure, and through stone channels carved into the ground itself, these mechanisms power mills and presses. You can see the results: great wooden screws, turned by hand-cranks and mechanical advantage, driving downward into the clay. The grinding sound is the sound of these presses working, crushing and compacting the clay itself, reshaping the ground inch by inch.

It is work that will never be finished.

The *purpose* becomes clearer as you move deeper into the garden. The presses are laying down new clay in patterns—thickened borders, raised platforms, subtle channels that guide what little moisture does exist. They are terraforming, but with the grain of the world rather than against it. The gardens have been shaped this way for so long that the distinction between natural and constructed has dissolved. The settlements and the vegetation and the clay have achieved something like equilibrium.

Temperature-wise, the place is warm—not punishing, but persistent. The sunlight is bright and unfiltered, harsh enough that shadows are crisp and deep. The sky is pale blue, nearly bleached by its own brightness. Everything glares. There is no softness to the light here.

Creatures are few. You might see them if you watch long enough: small, pale reptiles, nearly the color of the clay, darting between stems. Once, something larger moves between the buildings—a creature with too many legs, translucent in the brightness, seeming to absorb rather than reflect the light. It vanishes before you can determine its true form or size. The animals here are thin, efficient, minimal. They exist in the margins of this barely-habitable place.

The people, when you encounter them, are equally sparse. They move between the buildings with purposeful economy, and their skin holds the color of old clay. They speak rarely, and when they do, their voices are subsumed by the grinding, the creaking, the wind-song of the stems. They are not unfriendly—they nod, they gesture—but conversation seems foreign here. Action is the language.

What strikes you most profoundly is the *strangeness* of the whole arrangement. This should not work. The parameters are all wrong: too dry for this much vegetation, too sparse for this much density of construction, too small a scale to support a true settlement. And yet it persists. It has persisted. The weathered surfaces of the buildings, the deep grooves worn into the clay by centuries of foot traffic, the self-sufficiency encoded into every carved channel and fitted stone—all of it speaks to a civilization that has made peace with impossible conditions and simply *remained*.

The grinding continues. The stems creak. The wind carries no moisture, yet the gardens endure. In the distance, beyond the limits of the settlement, the vegetation grows sparser, and you can see the true desert beyond—a void of brown and white and the pale color of heat shimmer. It's not far. It's always close. The garden keeps it at bay through sheer stubborn design and the relentless labor of mechanisms that never stop, that were built centuries ago and continue their work as though they might, through persistence alone, hold back the encroaching void forever.

You realize, slowly, that you are standing in the mechanical respiration of a world that chose not to die.
