# The Scarp of Hollow Mirrors

You emerge onto a ledge perhaps two hundred meters across—a natural platform of pale stone that juts from a mountainside so steep the world falls away behind you without compromise. The elevation is genuine here. The air thins noticeably; breathing requires deliberation. Your lungs know they are high.

The stone beneath your feet is a weathered limestone, its surface carved into a thousand shallow basins by something other than conventional rain. Water doesn't fall here—it *condenses*. The cold is precise, surgical, around −8°C even during what passes for daylight. But the air is damp. Perpetually damp. The basins collect this moisture as mist settles, as frost sublimes and refreezes, creating a strange economy of water that never quite accumulates into rivulets or pools, yet never fully leaves either.

These basins are the first thing you notice. Thousands of them. They range from fingernail-sized to depressions large enough to lie in, and they've been here long enough—centuries, maybe longer—that their edges have softened into their current form. Each one catches light differently depending on angle. Each one is a small, shallow mirror. In the bright sunlight (and it is bright up here, the kind of bright that comes from snow-reflection and high altitude and minimal atmosphere to filter), the ledge becomes a constellation of moving highlights. Walk ten meters and the entire landscape rearranges itself around you.

The brightness is almost troubling. The sun sits high in a sky that is nearly colorless—not blue, exactly, but the blue of something that has been bleached. There are no clouds. There may be no clouds ever, up here. The light casts no soft halos; everything is edged, outlined, absolute.

The moisture creates a secondary phenomenon: a thin, constant glaze. Not ice, not quite. Rather, a film of frozen condensate that reforms continuously in the coldest hours, sublimes away in the brighter moments, condenses anew as evening approaches. The stone is always faintly slick. Your footsteps don't sound normal here—they're muffled, slightly adhesive, as though you're walking on something that wants to hold you momentarily before releasing you again.

This adhesion has shaped the architecture of the place. Scattered across the ledge are perhaps fifteen structures, all small, all ancient. They are not built *onto* the stone so much as *into* it. Long-dead craftspeople carved shallow alcoves—not rooms, not quite, but recesses—into the southern face of a rise at the ledge's far end. The recess faces the brightest part of the sky. Some alcoves are only deep enough to stand in; others penetrate five meters back into the mountain. The entrances are less than two meters high. No doors remain, if doors ever existed. No artifacts litter the floors save pebbles that have rolled in from outside.

The stone of these alcoves is darker than the surrounding pale limestone—stained, perhaps, or simply older, exposed to different weathering. It's possible these structures are thousands of years old. The edges of the alcove mouths have rounded significantly, wearing away from repeated freeze-thaw, from the constant work of the condensing damp. They look less like architectural statements and more like the place itself is slowly erasing the idea of them, smoothing them back into the mountain.

The silence here is almost physical. You notice it because of the occasional sounds that puncture it: a distant crack, sharp as a rifle shot, from deep within the mountain. Ice expanding and contracting. A low, sustained hum that may be wind moving through the alcoves or may be the mountain's own acoustic property—a frequency it produces when conditions align. The mist sometimes moves. It doesn't blow, exactly. It shifts. Redistributes. Breathes. The hum correlates with these movements, suggesting the mountain itself is ventilating, exhaling cold dry air from deep chambers.

The ledge extends in both directions along the mountainside, narrowing as it goes, until it becomes impassable or simply peters out into a smooth face. Behind you—upslope—the mountain becomes unstable: loose scree that would slide if disturbed, a landscape that hasn't settled. Ahead and below, the drop is absolute. You're on a shelf carved by something patient, by water that had time.

There is no evidence of current human habitation. No smoke, no tools, no bones. But the alcoves were built by *someone*, and something about their positioning—always facing the brightest sky, always slightly sheltered—suggests purposefulness. A community, perhaps, that extracted something from the damp cold. Salt, maybe. The mineral residue left by endless evaporation and recondensation. The basins themselves could be collectors, though for what isn't immediately clear.

The place has the feeling of being *finished*. Not abandoned—finished. As though it arrived at its final form centuries ago and has since been maintaining that form through small, constant adjustments: the frost working imperceptibly at stone, the mist redistributing, the hum continuing its single note. The erosion here is not the dramatic erosion of a young landscape; it's the gentle, thorough erosion of something that has already become its own ideal version of itself.

The light keeps changing fractionally as you stand there—not because clouds pass, but because the angle of the sun shifts infinitesimally, and with it, the constellation of mirror-basins resets itself. Each reconfiguration is subtle. You could miss it if you looked away.

At the far end of the ledge, where the alcoves cluster most densely, something catches light with a different quality than the limestone. Metal, perhaps. Oxidized into something orange and fragile. It lies partially buried in a drift of pale dust—mineral dust, not organic. Too small to identify from this distance. Too weathered to mean much anyway.

The cold reaches through your layers. Your breath becomes visible vapor that the damp catches and holds momentarily before letting it disperse. You're standing on something that was built to last, in a place that was built to endure, and the mountain is still working at both, with infinite patience, with a process so slow that it might as well be eternal.

The hum continues. The basins reflect. The frost reforms, grain by grain, in the perpetual twilight of the alcove mouths.
