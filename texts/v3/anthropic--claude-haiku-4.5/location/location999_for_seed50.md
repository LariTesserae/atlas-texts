# The Throat

You arrive in the pause between waves—that moment when the ocean wall has risen to its apogee and has not yet fallen. The air itself is water becoming air, saturated so completely that breathing feels like swallowing. Every inhalation leaves a film on your teeth. Your clothes don't get wet; they integrate, merge into the surrounding humidity like watercolor bleeding into paper.

The sound is immense. Not the sound of water moving—something older than that. It's the noise of pressure differentials collapsing and rebuilding, a resonant bellowing that seems to originate below you and above you simultaneously. When the next wave falls (and you know one will fall, and soon), the sound will intensify into something that bends light. For now it sits at the frequency where your bones ache in sympathy, where you feel heard by the place.

You are standing—or are you floating inches above standing?—on a platform of raw stone. Not worn, not finished. The rock looks punched outward from the earth yesterday. Its surface is sharp, fractured, architectural in its randomness: faces and angles that haven't yet decided what they are. Lichen hasn't found it yet. Water has touched it, yes, but hasn't claimed it. The mineral gleam is still confused, uncertain whether it will accept oxidation.

This high altitude shouldn't exist here. The air pressure reads wrong—too thin, too thin, yet too saturated. Around you, at intervals that seem random but perhaps aren't, stand stone teeth: formations barely emerged, barely differentiated from the platform itself. They look like they might still be growing, or might be only halfway through dissolving. No way to tell which direction they're moving.

The light is strange not because it's absent but because it's *multiple*. Sunlight falls from above in neat shafts—normal enough—but also seems to rise from the churning water below, diffracted and strange, as if the liquid itself is luminous. The two sources don't quite align. Where they overlap, colors shift: not refraction exactly, but something like the light is being digested and reconstituted.

Vegetation doesn't grow here, not in any traditional sense. But there are threads. Gossamer things, almost invisible, suspended between stone teeth—or are they anchored in them? You can't approach close enough to determine. They move against the prevailing wind, or with a wind you cannot feel. Their color is the color of exhausted copper, or old bone. There may be only three of them in your visual range. There may be hundreds; the saturation makes distance unreliable.

Life, sparse, moves in the margins. You see it only in interruption: a fish-thing sliding vertically through the humid air, or through the boundary between air and water (is there one?), leaving no wake. Its eyes catch light as they pass. It's gone before you can categorize it. The space it occupied feels violated afterward, as if it was never meant to pass through that particular cubic foot of atmosphere.

The sound changes. The deafening hum shifts down a half-octave and something mechanical enters the pattern—or something that resembles mechanics. A grinding, rhythmic, like stone on stone but intelligent. From deeper in the platform, perhaps. Or coming closer. Or both. The other sound—the wind-pressure-collapse sound—doesn't stop; it only braids together with this new frequency. The combined effect is almost musical, almost intentional.

There is a structure. You notice it because it *fails* to be geological. A corner. A single stone fitted against another, made of the same emergent rock but shaped. Small—the scale of a closed hand, a child's building. One corner. Just the one. The mortar between the stones is still wet. Still being worked. You don't see who does the work, but when you look away and look back, the seam has deepened slightly, or the stones have settled a millimeter closer.

The weather is changing. The wall of water to your right (or is it all around you?) is darkening. The waves, when they come—and they come constantly, in overlapping sets—are beginning to carry texture: not foam, but something granular, particulate. Sand, maybe, or stone so fine it becomes powder, or something generated by the waves themselves. Each impact reshapes the raw stone platform minutely. You can see the dust rising and falling, caught in the light-sandwich above.

The temperature maintains itself in cool precision. Not cold, precisely—more like the absence of warmth, a void where heat should logically be. Your breath clouds. The moisture on your skin doesn't evaporate; it deepens. You're being slowly absorbed into the landscape's own saturation.

There is a sense of *becoming* here, pervasive and active. Not slow geological time. Something faster. Something that's building and destroying simultaneously, where erosion and deposition happen in the same moment. The raw stone suggests newness, but the worn platform beneath your feet suggests age, and both are present without contradiction.

When the next wall falls—and you can feel it gathering, the pressure building again above—the sound will become the world. The humidity will increase beyond current measurement. Something deeper might become visible. For now, the place holds itself in turbulent suspension, in the throat of something vast that has not yet chosen whether to swallow or scream.

You are very small. The place is aware of your smallness. It's not hostile; it's simply indifferent in a way that feels like attention.
