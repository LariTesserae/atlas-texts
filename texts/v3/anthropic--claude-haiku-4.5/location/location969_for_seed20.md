# The Throat

You arrive at the sound first—before sight resolves, before the body understands where it is, the noise enters through every aperture. A roaring that is also a singing that is also a grinding, continuous and pitched just wrong enough that your sternum vibrates. It never stops. It never even wavers.

The light resolves in fragments. You're in a ravine, but not one that followed water downward—this one *is* water, or was, or is becoming. The walls are pale stone, recently fractured, their surfaces sharp enough to draw blood from a careless palm. They rise perhaps forty feet on either side, and the space between is narrow—cathedral-narrow, the kind of stone throat where sound doesn't dissipate; it accumulates.

What was once a riverbed has become something else. The stone floor is slick but not wet in any ordinary sense. It's tacky, slightly warm beneath your feet, and it moves. Barely—centimeters per minute, you'd guess—but it moves, a slow muscular shifting that reminds the brainstem of things it doesn't want to remember. The color is wrong: a pale mauve-grey that seems to generate its own faint bioluminescence, enough to see by, though the light dies just beyond arm's reach and the ravine's upper reaches fade into absolute black.

The walls aren't still either.

They pulse. The stone breathes in a rhythm that doesn't match any respiration you'd recognize. Sometimes the rhythm accelerates—a dozen shallow pulses in quick succession—before settling back into that slow, geological throb. When it accelerates, fragments crack away from the walls. They're sharp, these fragments, and they don't fall so much as drift, suspended for moments in the warm air before settling onto the moving stone below with soft, wet sounds that you feel more than hear beneath the constant roar.

The sound. Let's address it directly: it's not one thing. Underneath everything is a low frequency vibration you feel in your molars, in your inner ear—the sound of stone under unimaginable pressure, or perhaps the sound of stone giving way, grain by grain. Layered over that is a shriek, almost musical, that rises and falls in pitch without pattern. And woven through both is a rhythmic grinding, wet and organic, like massive jaws working, but patient, methodical. The three sounds don't harmonize. They create interference patterns that move through the ravine like weather.

Life is sparse, but it exists.

There are things on the walls. At first you mistake them for marks in the stone—streaks, perhaps, or mineral deposits—but they move. Slowly. They look almost crystalline, almost plant-like, though the category feels wrong. Some are the color of old teeth. Others fade into translucence toward their edges. They're sparse enough that you can see most of the stone around them, but they're distributed with a kind of intentionality, clustered in certain zones as if they're mining something, or waiting for something. When you approach one, it retracts slightly into the stone, then—after a delay—extends again. It doesn't flee. It doesn't attack. It acknowledges you.

You see three birds, if birds is the right word. They're skeletal, translucent at the wingtips, and they move through the ravine's air in stuttering, impossible trajectories—accelerating where they should slow, hanging suspended where physics says they shouldn't. Their cries are distinct even above the roar, a sound like glass breaking underwater. They don't perch. They don't rest. They flow through the space as if they're part of the sound itself.

Then there are the structures—or what remains of them.

One is clearly intentional, though its purpose has become secondary to its dissolution. It's built into the wall itself, a small alcove maybe six feet deep, walled with what looks like interlocked bone or petrified wood, now pale and warped. The threshold is sharp-edged, fresh, as if the stone only recently decided to expose this place. Inside, there are arrangements: patterns made of small stones, or teeth, or something between the two. They're orderly enough to suggest thought, sparse enough that your eye can trace the gaps between them. The patterns don't repeat. They seem to be *calculating* something, slowly, across the dark floor.

The other structure—you almost miss it because it's barely there—is a platform, no more than two feet high, standing alone on the moving stone floor. It's made of something that isn't stone, isn't wood, isn't anything your taxonomy knows. It's slightly warm to the touch. Slightly damp. It has a faint pulse of its own, not synchronized with the walls. Something was built on this platform once. Something small. The marks suggest it was removed—or *extracted*—recently. Very recently. The indentation still holds a faint warmth.

The air itself is wrong.

It's warm, humid, but not like a tropical climate. It's the warmth of something internal, something that's been held close to a living core. When you exhale, your breath becomes visible for a moment—not as cold vapor but as something darker, a shadow-breath that seems to linger longer than it should before dispersing into the surrounding air. The humidity doesn't feel like moisture so much as like saturation—the air is full of *something*, something that makes each breath slightly substantial, slightly resistant.

The weirdness sits in your perception like a stone in a shoe.

The ravine sometimes seems like it's rotating around you while you stand still. The walls are definitely not parallel, though your eye keeps trying to make them so. The distance to the far wall seems to change depending on where you look—closer in the center of your vision, farther at the periphery. When you look directly up, toward where the top of the ravine should be, there's a moment of vertigo; the stone seems to extend downward even as you look up, a spatial paradox that resolves only when you look away.

Time is uncertain here. You've been in the ravine for what feels like minutes, but your body suggests hours. Or has it been seconds? The roar doesn't provide landmarks. The pulsing doesn't measure. Only the slow shifts of the floor, the gradual accumulation of stone fragments, the patient retraction and extension of those crystalline things on the walls—only these suggest that time is passing at all.

And it's *changing*.

As you watch, the roar shifts in pitch. The walls accelerate their pulse. The fragments coming loose from the stone are larger now, and they settle faster onto the floor. The platform in the center seems to be sinking, micron by micron, into the living stone. The birds—you've lost track of them. Were there three? You thought there were three. Now you see two. Or perhaps they've merged into a single, larger shape that moves with that same impossible geometry.

The breath-shadow you exhale seems to linger longer now, almost accumulating in the air.

You understand, with a clarity that arrives without warning, that the ravine is *aware* of you. Not conscious in any way you'd recognize. But aware. Responsive. The architecture of this place—the walls, the stone floor, the slow throb of whatever this is—it's adjusting to your presence the way an eye adjusts to light. And the adjustment is subtle, but it's accelerating.

The roar deepens. The walls press slightly closer. The crystalline things on the stone begin to glow, faintly, their pale light creating shadows that point in directions that don't correspond to any single light source. Multiple shadows. Contradictory shadows. Your shadow multiplied, scattered, reassembled in configurations your mind struggles to parse.

This is a young place. Fresh. Recently formed. Or perhaps it's ancient beyond reckoning, and it simply doesn't wear time the way other places do. The sharp edges suggest newness, but the authority of the roar, the settled certainty of the floor's slow throb—these suggest something that has existed forever in this configuration, even if the configuration itself shifts.

You are small here. Not insignificant—you're too novel for that, too strange—but small. One small acoustic anomaly in a ravine that is learning, very slowly and very surely, how to exist.

The roar continues. It will continue. It has always continued.

And somewhere in the dark above, in the spaces you cannot quite perceive, the ravine is still forming, still discovering itself, still grinding toward whatever geometry it's meant to achieve.
