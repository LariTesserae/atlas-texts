# The Terai of Ascending Voices

You enter through mist that clings to your skin like a second membrane. The air here is not transparent—it's a living thing, wet and deliberate, condensing on every surface. Stone sweats. Leaves drip though no rain has fallen in hours. The sky, where visible through the canopy, is a bleached white that never quite resolves into cloud or clarity.

The elevation rises in tiers. Not gradually. The land seems to have decided on its shape long ago and held it—a sequence of plateaus ascending toward mountains you cannot see, only feel as a pressure in the air, a weight that presses down even as the ground climbs. Stone outcroppings break through the dense vegetation like ribs through thin skin, weathered smooth by centuries of moisture, their surfaces furred with moss so thick it feels like fur when you touch it.

The vegetation is strangling itself with its own abundance. Vines have become architecture here, their woody stems thick as thighs, creating a second canopy suspended above the ferns and creeping plants below. Leaves overlap leaves overlap leaves—a near-total saturation of green, though the green itself is not uniform. It ranges from the silver-backed undersides of high fronds to the near-black of the deepest understory where light has forgotten the way down. Some plants glow faintly, a phosphorescence that seems to have nothing to do with bioluminescence and everything to do with the light being trapped inside them, unable to escape.

The ground is soft. Not marshy—there's no open water, no standing pools—but spongy, made of centuries of decomposition held in compression. Your footsteps disappear into it. The vegetation grows directly from this rot-and-richness, its roots visible on the surface, tangled and exposed, suggesting the soil is already full to bursting.

And the sound.

The sound is not background. It is the place's primary feature, its defining characteristic. There is an acoustic phenomenon here—a resonance in the valley, or in the rock itself, or in the very moisture of the air—that amplifies and distorts what should be quiet. Dripping water becomes a cacophony. The settling of leaves is a roar. Your own breathing echoes back at you from multiple directions, as though the landscape is breathing in rhythm with you, slightly out of sync, creating a dissonant harmony.

But beneath the ambient noise-magnification, there is something else.

Voices.

Not human. Not quite animal. A sound like singing that emerges from no clear source—sometimes it seems to come from the stone itself, sometimes from the depths of the vegetation, sometimes from the warm, wet air. It's layered, polyphonic, a chorus that maintains constant pitch and intensity. It has been happening for so long that the stone has worn itself into resonance chambers. The vegetation has evolved to transmit these frequencies. This sound is not changing. It never changes. It is in equilibrium with the landscape, part of the established order of things.

The temperature is oppressive—it hovers around body heat, maybe a degree or two above. Your sweat doesn't evaporate. It simply becomes part of the landscape's moisture. There's no relief, no variation. The heat is constant, absorbed and radiated by the wet stone, maintained by the density of vegetation that traps it close to the ground.

The town exists here, though "town" is the wrong word. *Settlement* is too orderly. What exists is *integration*—structures that have surrendered to the landscape's logic. Walls have become retaining surfaces for vines. Roofs have become gardens. The distinction between built and grown has eroded into insignificance. Stone foundations remain visible in places, but only because the vegetation has grown *through* them, not *onto* them, creating a lattice where structure and plant are indistinguishable.

There are no paths, exactly, but preferred routes—places where footsteps have worn the spongy ground slightly firmer, where the vegetation parts in familiar ways. The settlement extends across the multiple plateaus, connected by these erosion-paths and by the vines themselves, which have become a kind of network. You can follow them if you're willing to climb.

Nothing moves except the dripping, the settling, the occasional impossible sound that might be wind or might be the voices again. No animals. No birds. No insects despite the richness. It's as if they've found this place incompatible with their nature, or as if they've simply never existed here and the ecology has evolved without them.

The light, when it reaches this far down, is diffused and sourceless. It seems to come equally from all directions—from the glowing leaves, from the moisture itself, from some quality of the stone. There are no shadows, properly speaking. Instead, there are gradations of dimness, areas where less light has managed to accumulate.

And the whole thing is *stable*. Profoundly, almost aggressively stable. Nothing is changing. The settlement has reached some kind of equilibrium with the landscape, the vegetation with the moisture, the sound with the stone. This is not a young place, nor a dying one. It is a place that has achieved its final form and intends to maintain it forever.

The only unsettling thing—the true wrongness—is that equilibrium itself. In a landscape this wet, this warm, this verdant, there should be *change*. Things should rot and fall and be replaced. The voices should shift. The mist should clear sometimes. But they don't. It's as if the place has stopped cycling. It's in perfect stasis, and that stillness, combined with the endless amplified dripping and that polyphonic chorus with no visible source, creates an uncanny feeling that the settlement and the landscape have made some kind of agreement, one that keeps everything exactly as it is, forever.
