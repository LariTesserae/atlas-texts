# The Geothermal Plateau of Kethis

The light here is almost violent—not sunlight, but the bleached radiance of heated stone and mineral deposit, refracted through perpetual steam. The sky above is a pale, washed-out blue, nearly white at the horizon where the air hazed into shimmering columns of rising heat. The sun itself is an overexposed coin, difficult to look at directly.

You stand on a plateau roughly two kilometers above surrounding basins, though "above" feels like a nominal measurement in a place so fundamentally structured by what moves through it rather than by elevation. The ground is volcanic, but not recent—the black rock has settled into itself, acquired a patina. Obsidian catches the glare in scattered patches. The darker stone is threaded everywhere with mineral veins: sulfur deposits that stain the rock in sickly yellows and burnt oranges, silica formations that catch light like shattered glass, iron oxide that bleeds rust-red into the crevices.

Water is here. Everywhere, in a way. Not as rain—though the humidity sits on the skin like a second atmosphere, and morning clouds of condensation roll down from the high air—but as seepage. The ground weeps. Thermal springs emerge from fissures, running in shallow channels across the plateau, steaming even in the bright heat. These channels trace an intricate network, barely ankle-deep, warm to the touch. Where they collect in broader basins, the water takes on a milky turquoise tint from dissolved minerals. The smell is sharp: sulfurous, mineral-laden, ancient.

There is no plant life. This is not a desert with its sparse defiant vegetation. This is a genuinely hostile world to photosynthesis—the temperature, the chemical composition of the water, the saturation of the air with steam and gas. The ecosystem here has chosen a different path.

Instead: *life through chemistry*. The water that channels across the plateau hosts dense colonies of thermophilic bacteria and archaea, invisible to the eye but present in their billions in every warm stream. They are the foundation. Above them, barely visible, are *thermophilic nematodes* the width of a hair, their bodies transparent, threading through the mineral-rich water. In the larger pools, *extremophile crustaceans*—tiny shrimplike things with chitinous shells that seem almost translucent, existing in densities that cloud the water itself when disturbed. These creatures are abundant, prolific, and utterly strange; they should not exist, yet here they proliferate in the chemical energy of the vents.

Larger predators hunt them. *Thermophilic fish*, evolved over countless generations to tolerate water that would cook ordinary life—scaled things, no more than fifteen centimeters long, moving with a strange angular efficiency through the basins. Their eyes are reduced, almost vestigial; they navigate by detecting the subtle temperature and chemical gradients in the water. And above them, in the air itself, things that should not be called birds are present: *heat-adapted aerials*, lean-bodied and bronze-feathered, with eyes that seem too large. They hunt the insects—and yes, there are insects. Thermophilic flies and midges that breed in the shallow water, their larvae adapted to temperatures that would denature protein in less specialized organisms. The small predators move with a focused intensity, hunting in the noon heat that would kill ordinary life.

This is sparse abundance. The creatures are numerous *within their niche*, but the plateau itself is vast and empty. For every few square meters of water-channel where life congregates, there are dozens of square meters of bare, heat-radiating stone. The settlements have learned this geography.

The architecture is industrial and functional. *Geothermal extraction stations*—clusters of metal and piping, weathered to rust-orange, established in long-settled patterns at the major hot springs and vent zones. They are not recent. Corroded valves, patina-thick pipes, scaffolding that has existed long enough to develop its own mineral crust from the steam passing over it. These are production facilities: the heat is harvested, converted to energy. Cooling systems run water through radiators the size of sheds, pulling the thermal gradient to do work. Some facilities still operate; others have settled into a state of stasis, machinery frozen mid-motion by mineral accumulation.

Between the major stations: *linear infrastructure*. Insulated pipes running across kilometers of open plateau, carrying heated water to distant demand. Rail lines, oxidized, carrying ore or processed minerals down the gentle grade toward the lower valleys. The rails are not heavily trafficked—this is not a place of constant motion—but they are maintained, indicating ongoing, if intermittent, industrial use.

Smaller structures are scattered in a loose distributed pattern: worker settlements built on elevated ground above the flood-risk zones of the hot springs. Buildings of dark stone and corrugated metal, designed for functionality rather than aesthetics. Solar arrays would be pointless here; instead, heat exchangers and steam-capture systems dominate the architecture. Some structures are quite old, their purpose now ambiguous. A few show signs of recent habitation—ventilation pipes, recent metalwork repairs—while others are clearly abandoned, their doors secured with chains, windows reflecting the blank white sky.

The scale is unsettling. You can see for perhaps five or six kilometers in most directions before the plateau's edges dissolve into heat shimmer and atmospheric distortion. The terrain extends further, you know, descending into populated regions below, but up here it feels isolated—a specialized zone, an industrial lichen growing on the back of something vast and geological.

The silence is profound. Your footsteps on the rock make little sound. The creak of old metal from the abandoned facilities carries far in the still air, but there is no wind here at this elevation, or it moves so slowly it registers only as a change in humidity. The machinery that operates—a pump running somewhere distant, the hiss of steam from a pressure release—these sounds are absorbed by the heat-shimmer before they travel far. What you hear instead is the acute absence of birdsong, the lack of rustling undergrowth, the missing percussion of an ordinary ecosystem. There is only the occasional *click* of expanding or contracting metal as the sun moves, and the whisper of steam condensing, and the very subtle *ticking* of rock cooling in fractional increments.

The light never truly changes, even as the sun moves. The whiteness persists, the heat steady. The air tastes mineral, faintly metallic. Your lungs ache with each breath, adapted over time but never quite comfortable.

This is a place of process. The water moves through its channels, slowly, steadily, carrying minerals in solution. The bacteria multiply in exponential succession, consuming the chemical energy released by the Earth's interior. The predators hunt. The industrial machinery extracts and refines and moves raw materials downslope. Nothing about this place is finished. It is in active equilibrium—not static, not chaotic, but *balanced in motion*. 

And it is genuinely alien, even to those who have built settlements here. The life-forms that thrive are not recognized as kinfolk to the animals that live below. The landscape itself seems to operate under different rules, as though the normal ecology has been replaced by something chemical and fundamental, something that remembers an earlier Earth.
