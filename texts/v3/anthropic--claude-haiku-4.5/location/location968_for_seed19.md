# The Stillplains of Keth

The air tastes of rust and chalk. No wind moves it.

You stand on a plateau that extends to every horizon—not flat, but folded into itself with the patience of deep time. The ground rises and falls in folds so subtle and gradual that you can walk for hours before understanding you've climbed or descended at all. Everything here is worn. The bedrock itself seems exhausted, its sharp edges smoothed to curves by processes that stopped eons ago. The stone is a dull ochre-grey, cross-hatched with cracks that have held their same width since before anything lived here.

Temperature hovers at the border of comfortable—cool enough that breath leaves no vapor, warm enough that stillness feels like a choice rather than a mercy. But it never changes. Dawn brings no gradual warming. Dusk brings no chill. The thermometer of this place was set long ago and locked.

What little water exists exists wrong. In the deep creases between the folds, where drainage would make sense, instead there are *deposits*—seams of something crystalline and pale, not quite salt, not quite mineral, but clearly the ghost-residue of ancient seas that never returned. The crystals don't absorb light; they seem to *hold* it, luminous and faintly warm to the touch, but they don't flow or change. They are part of the settled order.

The vegetation respects this equilibrium. Low scrub appears in scattered patches—plants that look simultaneously bronze and grey, their leaves thin as paper, their roots likely running deeper than the eye can follow. They don't fight the terrain; they seem to *agree* with it. In some places, the same plant species occupies the same hollow for so far that you wonder if you're seeing one organism or a thousand clones at perfect intervals. The plants make almost no sound when you brush them. They crackle like ash, but quietly. The sound stops as soon as you're past.

But the fauna.

The fauna is *not* settled.

They move through the landscape in densities that should be impossible—creatures that seem to exist in a state of half-attention to the rules of nature as you understand them. The Listeners, the locals once called them: beings approximating roughly the size and shape of mountain goats, but their skin is transparent in places, showing networks of luminescence beneath. They don't eat the plants. They seem to drink from the crystal seams, their mouths opening sideways, their throats clicking in patterns that almost resolve into language. Dozens of them occupy any given fold of terrain, close enough that their flanks nearly touch, but they seem not to acknowledge each other at all.

And then there are the Climbers—smaller, more numerous, chitinous, moving in vertical lines up cliff-faces that appear to have zero gradient. They shouldn't be able to climb vertical nothing. Yet there they are, in their hundreds, in coordinated spirals, tracing paths through empty space. When you focus on one Climber directly, you can follow it. But when you try to track the pattern of all of them together, your eye loses coherence. The pattern *exists*—you can sense its intricate architecture—but it resists being held in a single human glance.

And then there are things without names, because the scattered villages (small stone structures, primitive, built into existing rock formations rather than onto them) have not yet decided what to call them. Flying things that leave no shadow. Burrowers that move through *air* as if it were soil. Something vast that lives in the folds, that you feel rather than see—a presence warm and regular as a sleeping heartbeat, something so old and so much at peace that it has become almost a feature of geology.

The people built here tentatively, in the way of those who know they are tolerated guests. Stone huts cluster in natural alcoves, with tools of bone and worked flint still sharp enough to cut. They keep few animals; the native fauna seems to prefer the people remain as witnesses rather than competitors. There's a communal structure where the records are kept—not written, not yet, but *impressed* into slate, patterns struck by hammers, repetitive and slow. No one seems to be working on them today. No one seems to be *anywhere*, though if you listen hard enough, you hear breathing that isn't yours, and the soft metallic click of the crystal deposits settling.

The light here is strange in that it is entirely ordinary. The sun (which rises and sets on exactly the same path, year after year, with perfect precision) simply illuminates everything equally. There are no long shadows. There is no chiaroscuro. Everything is visible, everything equally, and this absence of dramatic contrast somehow makes the landscape *stranger*, more fundamentally alien. In clearer light, you might be able to convince yourself of mystery. Here, in this even, unchanging luminescence, you see clearly that mystery has *settled into the ground itself*.

Sound, when it exists, is discrete and small. The click of crystal. The dry crackle of touching dead plants. The soft susurration of Listener breathing. And underneath it all—or perhaps *instead* of it, a kind of intentional silence that presses gently against your eardrums, a silence so complete it becomes an acoustic *presence*. You begin to understand that nothing here has needed to shout in longer than language has existed.

The Stillplains are in their final state—not dead, because the fauna contradicts death entirely, but *complete*. This is what reality looks like when it has finished becoming. The ground remembers being young, remembers the epoch of shaping and change, remembers when water mattered and wind carved canyons. Now it remembers only stability. And under the stability, under the fauna that defies easy physics, under the silence and the cool and the sparse careful vegetation—there is something else.

There is a *consensus*.

The landscape and everything in it have agreed to remain exactly as they are, for however long forever takes.
