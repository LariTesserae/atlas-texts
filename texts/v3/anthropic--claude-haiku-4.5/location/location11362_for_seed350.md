# The Deepfactory

The first thing you realize is that you cannot see the sky. This is not metaphorical. The sky has been replaced by water—or rather, you have fallen so far below it that the distinction no longer matters. Above, there is only the sensation of vast weight pressing down, and occasionally, the distant geometry of something manufactured cutting through the liquid dark.

You are standing—if standing is the right word—on what might be called ground, though it shifts. The foundation beneath your boots is freshly exposed rock, still sharp-edged, still bleeding minerals into the water around it. Sediment clouds billow upward in plumes so dense they become temporary geology. The erosion here is young. Fractured surfaces catch what little light penetrates, throwing fractals. Nothing has been worn smooth. Everything is still deciding what it will become.

The water itself tastes like machine oil and copper.

---

The vegetation is everywhere.

It erupts from every crevice, from the spaces between newly-fractured rock faces, from the discharge pipes and waste channels of things that were built here in the before-time. The plants are not what you expected. They move wrong. Their fronds are translucent and muscular, contracting and releasing in rhythms that don't match any photosynthetic calendar you know. Some are bioluminescent in the sickly spectrum—greens that look like industrial coolant, blues that pulse with the rhythm of electric hum. They tangle together so densely that in places you cannot see through more than a meter of growth. Not a forest. A mat. A living blockade that parts reluctantly before anything moving through it.

The temperature is warm—bathwater warm, the warmth of something metabolizing, something running at high efficiency. The water itself seems to generate heat. Thermal vents? Industrial discharge? Both? There are places where the plants thicken into almost solid walls, and the temperature spikes. You feel it in your lungs. You feel it in your teeth.

---

The scale reveals itself slowly.

At first, the landscape appears intimate—claustrophobic even. The vegetation, the suspended sediment, the dim green-blue light filtering through layers of growth, all conspire to make you feel as though you are moving through the interior of something enclosed, something stomach-like.

But then you notice: the rock formations you've been treating as boulders are actually cliffs. The sound that has been a background pressure in your ears for the entire time you've been here—that drilling, grinding, mechanical howl—is not coming from one source. It is coming from dozens. Hundreds. They are distributed across a region that extends far beyond where your light can reach. The grid of it becomes apparent: manufactured structures, massive and mostly submerged, laid out in what might have been ordered rows before the flooding, before the wild reclamation.

This is a valley. The valleys beyond are valleys too.

---

The density is suffocating.

You cannot move three meters without encountering an obstacle—a rusted pipe thick as a tree trunk, a tangle of growth so impenetrable it might as well be solid, a slab of wall that juts from the murk at an angle suggesting a building half-collapsed or still in the process of being erected. The water itself is thick with suspended matter. Particles float like sedated insects. You brush against them constantly. They feel waxy. They leave traces on your skin.

The structures are scattered only in the sense that they are not touching—but only barely. A factory hull. A section of catwalk, still intact, still recognizably industrial, grown over with luminescent vines. A chimney stack pointing up toward the water-ceiling, now serving as a kind of pillar for clustering growth. A tank, half-buried, half-collapsed. Valves and bolts and the geometric vocabulary of mechanical intention, all of it greening now, all of it being incorporated into something new.

---

The sound is unbearable.

It is the grinding of the industrial world continuing without supervision. Pumps run still—you can feel them in the water, the thrumming pulse of motors that should have seized years ago but haven't. Somewhere, steam is being released. Somewhere, metal is compressing against metal. The vegetation contributes its own sound: a wet, ceaseless friction, the sound of growth accelerated past anything natural, a susurration so constant and so near that it becomes the baseline against which all other sounds are measured. Dripping. The hiss of chemical reactions happening in the warm water. The occasional sharp crack of stressed metal giving way.

It is not quiet. It has never been quiet. The silence between sounds is not silence; it is only the moment when you stop being able to distinguish individual noise sources from the overall roar.

---

The light is failing.

Or has already failed. What little there is comes from the vegetation itself, from the water, from occasional electrical discharges visible through the tangle of growth further out—something still firing, somewhere down here, something still trying to run. The illumination is monochromatic and insufficient. There are no shadows exactly, only gradations of obscurity. Depth becomes a matter of inference. You know the cliff-side is there because the rock is solid under your feet when you step, because occasionally your hand brushes it. You know the structures extend further away because the sound does. You know because the landscape continues to insist on its own existence even as it refuses to show itself clearly.

The effect is nauseating: of being inside something vast while being blind to the vastness itself.

---

The reality here is not quite correct.

This is the first thing you notice, after the shock of the water, after the overwhelming density, after the sound settles into your bones. The rules are different. The vegetation does not behave like life. It grows in directions that suggest design—or suggest an intelligence that designs differently than you understand design. Some of the piping you encounter runs backwards, carrying flow upward through what should be gravity-fed systems. The temperature fluctuations are not gradient-smooth; they appear suddenly, localized, as if the world is testing different settings.

There are areas where the water itself seems to move against the current. Where the growth forms patterns too regular to be natural, too intricate to be mechanical salvage. Where the very concept of entropy seems to have been temporarily suspended.

And the structures—you begin to realize they were not all built in the same era, nor following the same logic. A piece of machinery will be recognizably early-industrial—1920s, maybe, salvage from some demolished factory—but it will be grafted directly onto something that looks like it was designed only last year, except designed by someone who had learned the principles of engineering only through dreams. Welded together. Integrated. Growing over each other.

Hybrid. Unfinished. Unstable.

---

The turbulence is constant.

The water is moving, always moving, though not in patterns you can track. Sediment clouds billow and reshape themselves. The vegetation sways in currents that shift direction without warning. You feel the current press against you from one side, then reverse, then shift again. Not violently—yet. But you sense the potential. You sense that the system is near some kind of threshold, that stability here is temporary, that things could collapse or rebuild or transform into something entirely different at any moment.

The structures creak. Some are obviously being compressed by the growth around them. Others appear to be being disassembled from within—you can see piping that has been re-routed, machinery that has been partially gutted and integrated into larger assemblies. It is not clear whether this is happening on a geological timescale or a rapid one. There are markings on some surfaces that might be recent. Other objects appear to have been here since before the flooding, but you can't be certain.

---

Everything here is young.

The flooding was recent. The erosion is still sharp. The growth is still establishing its patterns. The industrial infrastructure, though old in isolation, is new to being submerged, new to being reclaimed, new to being integrated into an ecosystem that does not behave like ecosystems are supposed to behave.

It feels as though you have arrived at a moment of transition. Not the transition from one state to another, but the moment when the choice is still being made. The valley is neither drowned factory nor living ecosystem. It is both at once, in violent negotiation.

---

There are no animals here.

Only plant-like things. Only machinery. Only the evidence of life in its strangest, most industrial, most contaminated forms. The absence of fauna is conspicuous—a void that the turbulent growth rushes to fill. You realize that whatever lived here before the water, whatever swam or crawled or flew, is gone. There is only the adaptation. Only the mutation. Only the green-blue bioluminescence pulsing through the murk like the workings of some vast, submarine mind.

And the sound. Always the sound. The droning insistence of a factory that forgot to stop running. The grinding of gears that have nothing left to grind. The cry of water and metal and living growth caught in some terrible, endless machine.

You breathe. The water tastes like copper and regret.
