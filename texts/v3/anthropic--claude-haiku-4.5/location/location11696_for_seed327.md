# The High Meadow of Tarn Goss

You stand at 4,200 meters, where the air thins and the world becomes itself—a space no wider than you could walk across in twenty minutes, hemmed by dark stone walls that rise sheer on three sides. The fourth side drops away into nothing but clouds.

The meadow is small, intimate almost, the way a temple is small because what matters doesn't require space. Grasses grow here in defiant profusion: sedges and alpine fescue, their blades a deep teal-green, wet-slick, matted where the small herd of mountain goats bed down between grazing. The vegetation is strange in its density at this altitude—too lush—and you understand after a moment why: this is where water lives.

Water everywhere. Not as a lake or stream, but as *presence*. The ground itself is the issue. Beneath the thin turf lies permafrost that hasn't fully thawed in three months; above it, melt-water finds no good exit down this high rocky cup, so it pools, seeps, weeps from the stone faces. There's a permanent trickle along the eastern wall, bright against dark basalt, moving so slowly you wouldn't see it move if you watched—but the rocks beneath gleam black-wet, colonized by thin-film algae that glows faintly green when light hits right. The air holds moisture like a held breath. On calm afternoons—rare here—it condenses on every surface: the stone, the grass, the single shepherd's shelter, the webs between the rocks.

Speaking of which: insects. 

The sound hits you before you fully process it. Not the wind—though the wind is there, steady and cold, cutting down through the gap to the west—but *life*. Mosquitoes in a cloud so thick they form a visible haze above the wettest ground. Smaller flies, countless, making a sound like distant machinery. Grasshoppers that leap in sudden sprays, each jump a small violence. And beneath it all, a deeper thrumming: the goats themselves, at least forty of them, their bells—crude copper rectangles hung from cord—striking each other as they move, creating a constant, almost musical chaos.

The goats are the lords here. They've worn paths into the turf that will not fade for decades. Their dung feeds the vegetation; their presence shapes everything. They're smaller than lowland goats, compact, their coats the grey-brown of weathered stone. They've never known humans well, and they regard you with indifference when you move. One doe is pregnant, visibly so, and three kids born this season—tiny, precise creatures—move with her.

The shepherd's shelter is made of stacked stone, five centuries old perhaps, its walls still upright though the roof is gone. A stone bench inside, a stone ring for fire outside. Simple. Functional. There are tools here, half-abandoned: a crook of ash wood, polished smooth by generations of hands; a clay vessel with a hairline crack, filled now with rainwater; a wool card, worn. These are not recent. You could be here in summer, you realize, among seasonal occupants who come only when the alpine grasses reach this green.

But the structure is *settling* into its place. This isn't new erosion. The walls align with the fall of the land as if they grew from it. Lichen covers the stones in orange, grey, and pale green—not the fast colonization of fresh stone, but the deep work of decades. The corners are rounded not by recent weathering but by the patient work of frost and thaw, repeated so many times the sharp edges have become memory.

The sounds of erosion are minimal here—the water is not rushing, not cutting. But you hear the *tick* of frost-crack sometimes, a small report like a gentle hammer blow. The rocks expanding and contracting with the daily temperature swing: cool now, this being alpine high summer, but freezing every night. The meadow lives in a slow, repeating rhythm of break and remake, so gradual it's indistinguishable from stillness.

Light here is clear and direct. The sun is bright even at this altitude, almost fierce, and the shadows it casts are sharp and black. But the air itself—that perpetual moisture—softens the edges slightly. Everything glows a little. The stone walls have a subtle luminescence from the water film on them. The grass, when light takes it right, seems to hold light within it.

You can see far from here when the mist isn't in. To the west, ridge after ridge of impossible peaks, most higher than where you stand, their snow still thick even now. To the east, the land descends in brutal switchbacks, eventually folding into valleys where actual forests exist, where the air is breathable without effort.

But here—here is something between worlds. Too high for ordinary life, yet *crowded* with it. Too wet for a true alpine plateau, yet stone-dry compared to the marshes below. The temperature hovers just at the edge of where human comfort ends: cool enough that you're always conscious of your breath, of the need to keep moving, of the fact that night will come and it will be cold. Perhaps freezing.

The rules of this place are patient ones. Nothing happens quickly except the wind and the insects. The goats have been here for longer than human memory. The rocks have been here for longer than goats. The water will be here until the mountain itself decides otherwise. Weather systems move in and out—you can see one approaching from the northwest, a shelf of grey cloud that will arrive in perhaps an hour, bringing rain, lowering visibility to thirty meters, turning this small clear space into something smaller and more intimate still.

The meadow is *alive*—actively so—but on a tempo that doesn't match your heartbeat. It moves in the turn of seasons, the rotation of grazing routes, the slow erosion of stone, the annual shift of snowline. To stay here is to feel yourself slowing down, or the world speeding up around you on a different clock entirely.

And it is utterly alone. One meadow, one herd, one shelter, one human if they choose to stay. The nearest other place is hours of descent away. The nearest other person is probably days. Here you are at the edge of *emptiness*—high enough that there is mostly nothing around you, just rock and ice and the enormous sky—and yet, in this small bounded place, there is *abundance*. Contradiction holding true. Both things at once.

The wind shifts. The insects pulse. A goat bells. The water trickles. The rocks endure.
