# The Terraced Reservoir

The place announces itself first as constraint—not sky, not ground, but threshold. You stand on a platform of laid copper mesh, and three meters above your head runs a continuous ceiling of water.

The water doesn't move like water should. It holds itself in a shallow, trembling lens against an invisible membrane. Surface tension magnified a thousand times, or pressure locked in stasis. You can see through it to the industrial sky beyond—diffused, chlorine-pale, the light reaching down in slow geometric rays through what might be a massive transparent tank or might be something weirder: perhaps the water is conscious of itself, holding its own boundaries. The rays shift across the mesh platform every two hours as something enormous turns somewhere above, and that is the only time here.

The platform itself is only fifty meters across—a perfect square. At its edges, the copper mesh gives way to vertical trellises of brushed steel, upon which grows everything.

The vegetation doesn't follow terrestrial logic. Orchids have rooted themselves perpendicular to gravity, their purple throats opening toward the copper mesh floor. Ferns with fronds the color of arterial blood climb the steel; their stems are thick as wrists and weeping a clear sap that pools in catches designed into the framework. Vines—you cannot identify their species—have braided themselves through and around the metal structures in perfect lattice patterns, as though they grew alongside the construction, as though the builders consulted the plants' geometry. Flowers bloom in sequence: first the pale morning glories (though there is no morning here), then the trumpet vines with blooms that smell of warm pennies, then night-blooming jasmine that perfumes the still air around 2 AM by some internal calendar only it remembers.

Nothing wilts. Nothing browns. The vegetation is *dense*—it fills all available space. There is no bare metal, no exposed mesh. Walk three steps and you brush wet leaves. Reach out and your fingers find either stem or flower. The lushness is absolute and ungenerous; it leaves you no room to move that isn't somehow cradled or touched by green life.

Temperature hovers around 28 degrees Celsius, steady. Humidity near perfect saturation—your breath condenses on the air even as you exhale. The warmth comes from below: the copper mesh platform is slightly hot. You can feel it through shoe-soles. Somewhere beneath, industrial heat-exchangers work at their patient cycles. The platform is the radiator. Everything else is the ecosystem that learned to love that warmth.

The structures themselves are new, yet somehow not. The copper shows no patina—it's been deliberately kept bright. The steel is unmarked by rust. But the design speaks of a place that has been running this way for long enough that its strangeness has calcified into routine. Catch-basins are positioned with mathematical precision. Drip-irrigation tubes (you think they're irrigation; they might be something else) run in parallel lines through the vegetation, weeping moisture at intervals. Pumps—three of them, visible on the platform's eastern edge, painted industrial green—hum at frequencies that sit just barely perceptible in the bones of your chest. They don't labor. They perform their function with mechanical patience, moving something between the water-ceiling above and the heat-floor below.

There is no sound, except:

The pumps. A soft percussion of dripping sap into the catch-basins. The occasional creak of the framework expanding minutely as temperature cycles—or that might be your imagination. The vast, submarine silence of the water-ceiling, which doesn't roar or surge but simply *is*, a heavy presence that sounds like the absence of sound.

No animals. You don't see birds or insects. You don't see anything that moves unless the plants count. But their movements are geological: the slow unfurling of a fern frond over six hours, the gradual rotation of a flower face to chase what little directional light filters through the water. You might live here for weeks before you registered that the jungle was reorganizing itself.

The rules of this place seem to be:

— *Water stays above. Heat stays below. Life fills the space between.*

— *Growth continues at one speed—the speed of the system, not of the organism.*

— *Nothing enters. Nothing leaves. The cycle completes itself here.*

— *Time is measured in hums and platform-rotations and the blooming calendar of orchids.*

As your eyes adjust, you notice the walls. The platform doesn't float in void—there are walls, but they're far enough back that you didn't see them at first. They're made of the same materials: copper and steel, mesh and frame, and they too are colonized entirely by the orchids and vines and unknown green things that have forgotten, perhaps, that they ever grew anywhere else.

The walls form a square chamber exactly one hundred meters on each side. A chamber the size of a cathedral, or the size of a warehouse, or the size of a small flooded city. The scale is hard to judge. The walls vanish into the cultivated darkness above where the vines grow dense enough to block the water-light.

Everything is made. Everything that could be green has been made green. Everything that could grow has been made to grow. The industrial processes hum and drip, patient, untiring, slow as geology, holding this small world in suspension between the water above and the heat below, where nothing dies and nothing is born and everything continues, forever, on the other side of the mesh.
