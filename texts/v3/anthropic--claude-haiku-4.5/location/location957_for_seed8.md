# The Worn Throat

The land here is so old it has given up on having features.

What you see stretches low and nearly featureless—a patchwork of shallow depressions filled with standing water, their surfaces stippled with the movement of rain that fell three days ago and will still be here in three days' time. The water doesn't flow anymore; it pools and settles, finding the lowest points in what was once terrain and staying there, patient. The ground between these shallow lakes is mud that's learning to be clay, compressed by a million years of its own weight. It's warm to touch, especially where the sun penetrates the haze that sits perpetually near the surface—that condensing breath that rises from water meeting warm air and never quite breaks free into sky.

The vegetation is resigned. Scattered across the mud are stunted sedges and something between a shrub and a weed—low things with thick, water-logged leaves, adapted not to thrive but to persist. They cluster around the margins of pools, their roots breathing into boggy soil. Between them: emptiness. Long sight lines over open mud. The scale of it registers slowly. It's not dramatic emptiness; it's the emptiness of a place that has no reason to be crowded. A few isolated willows, anciently gnarled, lean at angles that look permanent—not windblown, but settled into a lean the way a tired animal settles into sleep.

The water is everywhere and nowhere distinct. It's in the air, thick and warm, making breathing a wet thing. It condenses on the sparse plants, gathering in jewel-like droplets on sedge stems. The mud itself sweats when the day is hot. You can't tell where water ends and earth begins; they've negotiated a boundary so old that neither remembers fighting for it.

The sound is layered and never silent. Water drips—not urgently, but constantly—from plant to pool. Somewhere, the persistent croak of frogs, territorial and patient; they've been calling the same notes for centuries. When a breeze moves (and it does, irregularly, warm and moist), it moves through the sedges with a whispering that sounds like exhaustion. The sound has a thickness to it, dampened by humidity, muffled by water and vapor.

Life is here, but distributed. Not the dense hum of a tropical wetland, but the honest abundance of a place that supports things: insects hover and skim the pool surfaces—dragonflies in dull colors, iridescent with age. Wading birds appear at dawn and dusk, their long legs carrying them through the shallows with infinite patience. You see their three-toed tracks in the mud, filling slowly with water. There are fish in the pools—small, ancient-looking things that seem unchanged since the age when reptiles ruled. Amphibians chorus at night. Turtles surface to breathe. Everything moves slowly, everything has adapted to a landscape where urgency is obsolete.

The light here is never harsh. The haze filters the sun into something diffused and warm, creating no sharp shadows. It's midday-light that lasts most of the day, dimming into twilight gradually, without drama. At dawn, the mist is thickest, and the few trees become suggestions. In the afternoon, visibility stretches to where the horizon simply dissolves into atmosphere.

There is no horizon, exactly. The land continues, very gradually rising if you walk far enough—not into hills, but into the memory of hills, worn so completely that height has become a rumor. The curvature of the earth becomes visible if you stay long enough; the landscape's curve is the curve of time.

This place changes, but not quickly. The pools migrate, shifting position across seasons. A dead willow falls and becomes a log that will take fifty years to rot. A patch of sedge expands by inches each year. Channels might deepen after a wet season. But these are the smallest possible movements, the whisper-thin edge of process. The baseline state is stasis. The law here is patience.

There are no structures, no marks of intention. Nothing has been built because nothing needs to be built. This is a place that makes its own shelter in the form of shelter: the hollow spaces between roots, the reedy islands in the pools, the thick mud that holds heat and stays warm at night. The architecture is biological and pre-made.

The weirdness is minimal. This is simply what happens when water, time, and modest warmth are left alone to negotiate their terms. There's no strangeness, only the profound normality of a place that has settled into its final form and stays there, asking nothing of the world but to be left alone to drip and breathe and slowly, imperceptibly, remake itself grain by grain and year by year.

You could walk for hours and never feel that you've arrived anywhere new.
