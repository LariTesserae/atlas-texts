# The Tidal Flats of Kernn

Water moves through everything here, but not as you'd expect it. The landscape is a grammar written in moisture—not quite marsh, not quite something drowned. The ground beneath your feet is a slurry of ash-grey silt and something finer, something that feels organic but isn't quite. It gives way unevenly. Water doesn't pool so much as *hover* at the surface, bead and weep from between particles. The air itself tastes wet. Condensation runs down everything, even the sparse rock faces that emerge from the flats like the ribs of something very old.

The temperature sits at the edge of numbness. Not freezing—the water won't crystallize—but cold enough that your breath makes visible shapes, cold enough that the moisture precipitates faster than the flats can drain. Everything is perpetually damp. Your clothes don't dry. Nothing dries.

The space opens outward. You can see for miles across the flats, which means you can see the *emptiness* for miles. There are no forests, no grasses, no lichens that you recognize. The biology here—if it can be called that—works at scales you almost miss. The water itself shimmers sometimes with what might be motion, might be microscopic life, might be the light refracting through mineral solutions. There are bacteria. There are things smaller than bacteria. There is a thin, translucent film that grows across standing water in perfectly geometric patterns, like frost but warm to the touch.

The sound is what breaks the silence before you understand the silence was broken.

From somewhere across the flats—maybe a mile, maybe three—comes the *chorus*. It's not birdsong. Birds don't live here. It's the sound of creatures that have never needed to be quiet: vast, clicking, groaning presences in the water-channels that cut through the flats. They sound like mechanisms—like stone grinding stone, like metal expanding in heat, like something breathing through a filter of calcified matter. The clicks layer over one another. Some are deep enough to feel in your sternum. Others are so high they seem to come from inside your skull. They stop occasionally, sharply, as if attention has shifted. Then they resume elsewhere, often in a different register, as if the creatures are learning songs from each other—or hunting in synchronized patterns you almost understand.

Smaller sounds nest between the large ones: the constant *susurration* of water seeping, the wet *pop* of air pockets collapsing in the silt, the ticking of what might be carapaces against stone. Sometimes there's a sound like something sliding past something else, a scaled or chitin-armored body dragging across the flats with methodical purpose.

The creature life is visible if you know how to see it. The water-channels bloom with movement. There are things that look like horseshoe crabs but more geometric, their shells patterned with what might be barnacles or might be something else—growths, perhaps, or integrated technology older than memory. They migrate in slow waves, clearing channels, filtering, reorganizing the sediment. You see them in the water's surface tension, broken into segments by the ripples they create.

There are larger presences deeper in. You catch glimpses of them: shapes that move with deliberate slowness, some as long as a house, some impossibly thin and segmented. They don't surface often. Sometimes you see the wake they leave behind—channels widened, sediment redistributed. The water around them clouds with particulates. There's a sound then, closer, that makes your bones feel hollow.

The flats themselves are ancient beyond measure. Not eroded—that word suggests time was a force acting *on* them. Rather, they've *settled* into their final form through incomprehensible cycles of settling. The rocks that jut up—sparse, crystalline, some shot through with veins of something that catches light—have the quality of things that won't change. They've reached a state of equilibrium with the forces around them. When you touch them, they're warm, which seems wrong given the cool air, which suggests something is moving beneath the surface, some slow heat source, some deep current that hasn't stopped in millions of years.

Human structures are almost insultingly simple. Scattered across the flats are stone cairns—simple stacked rocks, human-height or shorter, built from materials at hand. They mark something: channels, perhaps, or safe passages. Some are old enough that the silt has begun to bury them; you have to know what to look for. Others seem recently maintained, topped with a flat stone or marked with a strip of worked leather tied to the peak. Are they waymarkers? Territorial claims? Prayer structures? There's no way to be sure. Each one has been placed with care, but the care is mute.

Near the center of the visible region—four or five miles into the flats—there's what might have been a settlement. The remains are minimal: a few post-holes, dark with rust; a large flat stone that could be a work surface or an altar; scattered objects of worked bone and simple metal, corroded beyond clear function. No walls. No clear arrangement. Whatever purpose held it together is long solved, or abandoned, or transformed into something the landscape doesn't name clearly anymore.

The weather moves through on a slow rhythm. Fog rises without warning, thick and mineral-tinted. Visibility drops to ten feet. The click-songs of the creatures become even more vivid in the fog—sonar, navigation, communication, all of it pressed close. When the fog clears, the light is never quite bright. It's diffused, shadowless, flat—the sun visible but distant, filtered through a permanent haze of moisture and fine particles. Rain comes intermittently, not refreshing, but rather thickening the damp that already exists. Afterwards, new channels form in the flats, the creatures rearrange themselves, and the markers on the cairns shift slightly, as if something has passed between them.

The rules here are slow rules. Time operates at the pace of silt redistribution, of creature migration, of water seeping incrementally deeper or rising back toward light. Nothing is born here in the way you'd recognize. Nothing dies cleanly. Instead, there is transformation—slow, patient, so ancient that it feels like law itself.

You exist in this place as a visitor who doesn't disrupt much. Your footprints fill with water within the hour. Your presence registers as a minor topographic change. The creatures acknowledge you without particular concern or interest. The cairns stand whether you're here or not. The sound continues—that vast, patient, grinding chorus—regardless.

And somewhere, deeper than you can see, the flats continue into distances your mind can't quite hold, stretching toward horizons that curve away, toward the same cool damp, the same ancient settling, the same creatures moving through the dark water with the inevitability of planets.
