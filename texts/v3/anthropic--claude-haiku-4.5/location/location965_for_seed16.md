# The Dry Terraces of Keth-Mora

You find it by descending through a narrow cleft in weathered stone—a passage so cramped your shoulders nearly touch both walls. The air changes as you go deeper, becoming thicker, warmer, with a mineral taste that coats your teeth. Then the cleft opens.

The terraces reveal themselves in tiers, each one perhaps twenty feet across, stacked like shelves cut into the living rock of what must once have been a much deeper cavity. The stone itself is rust-colored, banded with veins of a lighter mineral that catches what little light filters down from the opening above—a light so faint and so far up that it seems almost theoretical. Your eyes adjust by degrees.

What grows here should not.

The vegetation is dense, almost choking in its profusion, but wrong in every visible way. The plants are pale—bone-pale, cream-pale, the color of things that have never seen sunlight—yet they are thick with life. They cluster in towering stands: fronds that unfurl like hands, stalks that pulse almost imperceptibly, structures that seem less leaf than membrane. They crowd against each other in the terraces, their bases rooted in soil that is black and fine as silt. The air moves around them but the plants do not sway. They barely move at all, yet you sense growth happening in real-time—a patient, inexorable adding-on of mass and structure.

The temperature is punishing. The terraces hold heat like a closed fist. The stone beneath your feet radiates it back. There's no breeze, only a thick, still air that seems to have weight. Sweat begins immediately and doesn't stop.

But where is the water?

There is none. Not a drop visible, not a stream or seeping moisture. Yet the plants thrive in a way that speaks of constant feeding. The black soil is not wet, but it is not desiccated either—it has the compressed, mineralized quality of earth that has been worked over geologically, chemically altered into something that holds nutrients in a form these pale things can consume. The plants' roots must run deep, into cavities below, drawing from reservoirs that lie beneath the terraces themselves.

The human settlement clings to the mid-levels in a way that suggests long habitation and meticulous organization.

Stone structures—built from the terrace-stone itself—form a loose grid along the flat surfaces. Not many: perhaps thirty or forty buildings visible from where you stand, though more may lie on the terraces above and below. They are simple constructions, mostly: rectangular chambers with flat roofs, connected by channels carved into the stone. The channels are for something—irrigation, perhaps, but of what? There's no water. Unless it is something else. Something moving through those channels that isn't quite liquid.

The buildings show their age in the way old things do: weathered, settled, integral to their setting rather than imposed upon it. They've been here long enough for the stone to have formed a gentle patina around them, as if the landscape has been slowly digesting their presence. Doorways are low and narrow. There are no windows that you can see—just rectangular apertures, some covered with woven screens that might be plant-fiber, might be something else.

The people—if there are people here now—are not visible.

What you see instead is *work*. Evidence of continuous, methodical labor. The terraces are not wild. They are managed. The dense pale plants are not random: they grow in clusters separated by narrow paths, each cluster roughly uniform in its density and height. The paths themselves are worn smooth, darkened by regular foot traffic. Where clusters meet the built structures, there are drying racks—simple frames of carved wood or bone lashed together with cordage—and hanging apparatus where plants in various states of desiccation sway almost imperceptibly in the still air. Some are pale as the living ones; others have darkened to brown or deepened to a faint purple.

The soil between the plant clusters is regularly turned. You can see the marks of hand-tools—wooden implements, perhaps, or bone—creating furrows. The work is not recent; the marks have settled into the black soil like old scars.

The sound is constant but not loud in the way you might expect. There's no rushing water, no wind, no animal calls. But the terraces are not silent. It's a subtle thing: a faint creaking sound that might come from the stone contracting and expanding in the heat, or from the growth of the plants themselves—the whisper of fibers extending, membranes unfurling. Occasionally there's a soft *crack*, sharp and sudden, like something snapping or settling. It echoes oddly in the enclosed space, bouncing from terrace to terrace in a way that makes it impossible to locate the source.

Underneath it all, a low hum. Not sound exactly—you feel it more than hear it. It seems to emanate from the stone itself, or from the spaces beneath the terraces, a vibration that resonates in your bones if you stand still long enough.

The light—that faint illumination from the opening high above—is not constant. You notice it brightens and dims on a rhythm that might be clouds moving across the entrance, or might be something more: the opening itself seems to pulse gently, as if the entrance above is breathing. When it dims, the pale plants seem to grow more luminous, as if some internal light is activating in their tissues, a faint bioluminescence that keeps the terraces from falling into absolute darkness.

There are no animal tracks. No bones, no droppings, no sign of any fauna beyond the human structures and the dense, patient vegetation. This is a sterile ecosystem, or nearly so—the plants and the people, nothing else. The absence of scavengers, of insects, of any of the small life that usually attends human settlement, is profound.

The built structures themselves tell you something about how long this place has existed.

The stone isn't just used; it's understood. The way the buildings sit on the terraces suggests generations of structural knowledge—how to place a foundation on such a steep slope, how to build without mortar in such a dry environment, how to angle the roofs to shed... something. The channels connecting the structures descend at precise gradients. Someone designed this. Someone knew what they were doing.

And yet the technology on display is *simple*. Primitive, even, in the way that word really means: reduced to essentials. The tools lying near the drying racks are straightforward—digging implements, binding cordage, cutting edges of dark stone. There's a grinding stone near the largest structure, its surface worn into a depression by uncounted repetitions of labor. Everything is made by hand, fitted by hand, maintained by hand.

But there's an intelligence here that goes beyond simple tool-use.

The water-question bothers you as you move closer. The plants require moisture. A lot of it, to grow this densely in this heat. Yet there's no water. You approach the nearest cluster of pale fronds and examine the soil around the base. It's moist—you can tell by the way it holds together—but not wet. And as you press your hand into it, you realize something strange: the soil is warm. Warmer than the ambient air, warmer than the stone itself in some places. 

Is it generating heat? Or is the heat coming from below?

The channels connecting the buildings, on closer inspection, are not empty. They carry something—a thin, opalescent fluid that moves with the viscosity of something thicker than water. It flows downslope in the channels with no apparent source, no visible pump or mechanism to drive it. It simply *moves*, at a pace that suggests constant regeneration or constant circulation. Where it passes over stone, it leaves no mark, no stain or residue. Where it touches the soil of the plant clusters, the plants seem to orient subtly toward it, their fronds angling to catch the flow.

Not water. Something else. Something that rises from below and is distributed through the settlement in an ordered, purposeful way.

The weirdness accumulates.

The longer you remain in the terraces, the more your sense of time becomes unreliable. The light continues its gentle pulsing. The hum from below seems to slow and quicken in response to your movements, or perhaps that's only your imagination. The pale plants don't sway in the breezeless air, yet somehow their overall configuration seems to shift when you're not looking directly at them—a slow rotation, a gradual reorientation. When you turn your head quickly, you catch the afterimage of movement that wasn't there a moment before.

The structures themselves begin to seem less like buildings and more like architectural elements of a larger organism. The way the channels connect them, the way the doorways face inward toward the plant clusters, the uniform spacing—it speaks less of separate dwellings and more of integrated, coordinated function.

There's an *intentionality* to the arrangement that suggests this is not a settlement as you understand the word. Or it is—but the settlement and the cultivated plants are not separate things. They are part of the same system.

The heat accumulates. Your sweat darkens your clothes but does not evaporate; the still air holds all moisture close. Your throat becomes dry despite the dampness all around. The taste of minerals on your tongue sharpens into something almost like flavor—something the black soil adds to the air when it heats, when the warmth causes minerals to volatilize, to release their signatures.

You think of leaving. You know where the entrance is, only one direction to climb. But the terraces feel like they want to show you something. Or perhaps it's only the accumulated heat and the strange silence that makes you want to push deeper. The terraces above are barely visible in the dim light, but you can see their outlines, more tiers receding upward into shadow.

Below, the pathway continues downward, where the structures grow more densely packed, where the pale plants press closer, where the hum from the stone beneath grows perceptibly louder.

You haven't seen anyone yet. But you've seen the work—the *active* work—and you understand you are in a place that is continuously, methodically operating. The plants grow. The fluid circulates. The stone hums. The heat intensifies. Time moves here at a pace that is not your pace.

The settlement of Keth-Mora is empty and full at once. And it is waiting—or working—or both—in the dim, hot, airless space beneath the desert, where water is a ghost-concept and the only life that matters cares nothing for air or light or the usual rules of growth.
