# Kashen Platform

The air tastes of rust and minerals. It hangs thick—not quite mist, not quite humid exhale, but something between, a suspended heat-shimmer that never quite resolves into clarity. The ground beneath is stone, black-red, fissured in patterns that look deliberate until you realize they're just how basalt cracks when it cools over centuries: hexagonal, interlocking, fractured into tiles the size of a hand or a car.

Water moves through it obsessively.

Not visible at first. But you hear it—not flowing, but *seeping*: a low percussion beneath everything, stone drinking from within itself. Touch the ground and your palm comes away damp. The rock itself weeps from pores invisible to the eye. There are places where thin sheets of moisture run across flat expanses, barely enough to catch light, and they leave mineral rings—concentric circles of copper-green and sulfur-yellow where the water evaporates in the heat, precipitating salts that never quite dissolve again. The landscape is layering itself, salt strata building grain by grain.

In the depressions—and they're everywhere, shallow bowls and channels worn into the stone over time—water pools. These are the only places the eye can rest. The pools are warm to the touch. Alkaline. They smell of deep earth and something chemical, ancient. Nothing floats in them. Nothing swims. But they're not sterile: a faint film sometimes covers them, iridescent, thinner than an eyelash. Under the right angle of dim light, it catches and throws back colors that don't quite match the spectrum. Bacterial perhaps, or pre-bacterial, or something without a proper name—the kind of thing that exists only because the conditions are so specific, so extreme, that normal life never found a foothold to compete.

The city is built *into* this.

Structures rise from the black stone—not on top of it, but integrated, as if the buildings grew from the rock itself. Walls are faced with treated basalt, dark enough to absorb heat, and they steam in the early morning when the temperature differential is steepest. There are cooling towers, hundreds of them, cylindrical and rust-streaked, designed to manage thermal load. Water rises through them in engineered channels, evaporates, falls as condensation on coils, is recirculated. The whole system is a machine for moving heat away from the compact zones where people work.

The streets are narrow, running along the grain of the older fissures. They were built following the natural water-seepage channels—someone understood early that you don't fight hydrology here, you channel it. Gutters run constantly, a thin trickle even in what passes for drought. The water is collected, filtered through beds of—nothing organic, no plants—but through mineral traps: layered screens of copper mesh, settling tanks where salts drop out, cisterns cut directly into the living rock.

Industrial infrastructure dominates the middle-elevation zones. Factories with high, narrow windows (for minimal solar gain). Mines working downward, accessing deposits of rare earths and lithium, the stone itself being the resource. Refineries where ore is processed—the smokestacks are minimal; most of the operation is mechanical, grinding and crushing and chemical separation, happening in enclosed chambers. Everything is designed for heat management. There's no waste heat being vented carelessly; it's all captured, used, cycled. The city runs on its own thermal gradient like a heat engine: the blazing surface, the cool depths accessed by drilling.

The light here is strange—the sky dim even at midday, not from cloud cover, but from the saturation of dust and mineral aerosol in the air. The refineries contribute; the constant grinding of the mines contributes; the evaporative cooling systems contribute. The atmosphere is thick with particulate. It diffuses the light into a uniform gray-gold, sourceless, shadowless in the worst parts. The few shadows that do form are sharp, black, wrong—where buildings block the entire diffuse field.

Sound is sparse. The siren of a shift-change at the mines, twice daily, low and penetrating. The hiss and drip of water systems, omnipresent but easy to stop noticing. Occasionally, the shriek of heavy machinery moving on rails, a mining car being hauled upslope. But these are punctual, predictable, and the space between them is quiet—not peaceful quiet, but the absence of fauna, the absence of wind (the high elevation and the thermal inversions trap air), the absence of any living thing that moves and calls. It's the quiet of a space that's been emptied.

Rarely—and this is where the strangeness settles in—you'll see movement. An animal. A bird, perhaps, or something that resembles one. It moves with a jerky gait, as if orienting to heat sources rather than sight. The miners have theories: adapted species, mutated for the high salinity and heat. Or possibly just rare visitors from the cooler zones below, driven up by desperation or seasonal instinct. They don't last long. The city itself is inhospitable to conventional biology.

But there are patterns. The water systems themselves move in cycles: seasonal, thermal, driven by the planet's daily heating. The salts precipitate and accumulate—the mineral rings grow slightly every year, building up like tree rings in reverse. The mining operations follow the ore deposits, gradually moving across the plateau. The city is not static. It's in motion on a long, industrial timescale, reshaping itself, optimizing, spreading into new areas as the old ones deplete.

And deep beneath, there's heat. Geothermal, accessed through drilling. Regulated carefully through heat exchangers. It's the city's heartbeat—the reason it exists here, why anyone would build on such hostile stone. The ground itself is the resource and the infrastructure, the problem and the solution.

The high-elevation zone extends for miles—you can see it when you climb to the highest point, a region of regular, geometric installations giving way to more sparse infrastructure, then to wilderness that isn't quite wild: it's a degraded zone where mining operations have exhausted themselves and the companies have moved on. Tailings piles. Collapsed shafts. Water systems silted in. But even abandoned, even dead, the plateau continues its work: stone continues to weep, salts continue to accumulate, the strange microbial films continue to colonize the pools. The city is abandoning pieces of itself, but the stone remembers everything that was done to it.

And the heat remains. Constant. Oppressive. The kind of heat that makes air visible, that warps distance, that teaches you quickly that this is not a place for complexity. This is a place for extraction. For systems. For the grinding efficiency of mechanical process running on thermal energy and will.

The sun, when visible through the haze, is a blurred disk. It never quite sets—the plateau curves, the horizon is close, but the light lingers for hours in a dimness that feels almost nocturnal. And then it's gone, and the ground, still warm, begins to cool fractionally, and the mineral-heavy air begins to precipitate, and the weeping intensifies: the land drinking back what the heat took from it.

A cycle. Brutal. Efficient. Absolutely impersonal.
