# The Verdant Flats

At first, you think you've entered a greenhouse world without walls—but there's no glass, no boundary, just the weight of air itself.

The landscape sprawls in all directions until perspective collapses it into a haze of green. You're standing on a plain so flat and so vast that the horizon curves slightly, or you believe it does; the mind rebels against genuinely flat things at this scale. The ground beneath your feet is not soil in any traditional sense. It's a compressed mat of root-matter and decomposing plant-stuff, dark and springy, slightly warm to the touch even through leather. When you step, it gives. Everything gives. The earth here is still *becoming*.

The vegetation is not arranged but *stacked*. Low shrubs with waxy, heat-flushed leaves form a basal layer—their branches woven so thickly they form a living floor, continuous, with no gaps. Rising through and above them are taller growths: plants that defy easy categorization. Some are tree-like, but their bark seems unfinished, still developing texture. Their leaves are oversized, pleated, engineered for humidity they rarely receive. They don't stand in isolation but in clusters, masses, almost colonial structures where individual specimens have begun to fuse at the base, their root-systems tangling into knots that push the earth upward in gentle, permanent swells.

The air is warm—not hot, not suffocating, but consistently warm, as though this entire continent sits in the exhalation of something vast and slow. There's moisture, yes, but not water. It's vapor from the plants themselves. The vegetation breathes. When you stop moving, you notice a gentle, almost subliminal pressure against your skin: the exhalation of ten thousand species photosynthesizing at once, producing more oxygen than the air can hold, creating a thick, almost syrupy atmosphere that tastes faintly green when you inhale.

There is no river. There is no pond, no standing water, no visible hydrology. Yet the plants are profoundly, impossibly lush. The answer: the world is a closed system. Water cycles through the plants themselves, extracted from mineral compounds in the developing soil, pulled up through root networks that extend impossibly deep into strata still half-formed. Some of the larger growths have learned to store it, their trunks thick and waxy, their leaves capable of absorbing moisture directly from air saturated by their neighbors. The water here is *moving*, always moving, through biology rather than gravity.

Sound exists only in the margins of silence. There is no wind—the atmosphere is too still, too saturated to permit currents. The vegetation doesn't rustle. Occasionally, something moves through the dense layers: small creatures, scaled and soft-bodied, vertebrates that have adapted to navigating the compressed world. You hear a *whisper* of displacement, a gentle *click* of something small and clawed finding purchase on a leaf-surface. Then stillness reasserts itself. In the dense canopy patches where older growths have created semi-shade, you might hear the slow *creak* of a plant-trunk adjusting, growing, shifting its weight.

The animal life is abundant but *quiet*. Small vertebrates dominate—things with the basic architecture of lizards and small mammals, though specific evolutionary paths seem tangential to Earth's templates. They are browsers primarily, consumers of the endless green. They move carefully, as if the world is fragile. Some are nocturnal or crepuscular, existing in the rhythms of a day-night cycle this world definitely maintains but which feels secondary, almost quaint, compared to the deeper biological calendar of growth and decomposition that structures everything. Predators are present but lean, efficient, moving through the understory with purpose. The prey are numerous enough that predation is gentle, culling rather than crisis.

The ground itself reveals the core strangeness: this is a world still assembling itself. The "soil" is not ancient. Look closely at the exposed roots of fallen plants, and you see they extend into layers of mineral that are just beginning to oxidize, to color, to accumulate the chemistry of true earth. The geological processes are young. The chemistry is still experimental. Some of the deeper layers seem semi-fluid, as though the planet hasn't quite finished with its own interior arrangements. And yet—impossibly—life flourishes in this embryonic substrate. Life that seems designed for newness, for incompleteness, for a world that is still writing the rules of how to be.

The city-structure is not built but *grown*. Scattered across the flats are zones of denser organization—places where the vegetation has consolidated into formations that function as architecture. A rise of interwoven stems, thick as tree-trunks, creates a hollow chamber where creatures shelter. Tangles of root form natural corridors, vein-like, pulsing with a faint luminescence at night—some adaptive glow from nitrogen-fixing organisms that create a living geography. There are no tools, no manufactured substances, but there is *infrastructure*: the biology itself has organized into systems. Certain plants have specialized into nutrient distribution, becoming almost vascular for the ecosystem. Others form protective structures. The whole landscape reads, at a glance, as chaotic abundance. At a longer look, it's a city where every building is alive and still growing, still discovering what it should be.

Weather operates on its own physics. The sky, when visible through the canopy, is a pale, hazy blue-green—the color altered by the density of oxygen and vapor in the air. Rain comes infrequently; when it does, it's not dramatic but thorough, a saturation that takes hours. The plants absorb it entirely before it can run off. Between rains, the air's humidity sustains everything. There may be storms at continental scales—disturbances that the ecosystem is still learning to weather, still evolving protections against.

Temperature fluctuates around warm but constrained. Night falls to pleasantly cool. The heat of the day is moderated by the plants' own respiration, their collective shade, the moisture in the air. This climate is stable, perhaps unnaturally so, as though the ecosystem has begun to regulate its own thermal properties.

The light is clear and normal but filtered. Direct sunlight rarely reaches the ground level; instead, everything is illuminated by green-shifted, diffuse light, as though the entire landscape exists underwater, in a medium that is mostly air but acts, in terms of light, as something denser. At dawn and dusk, when the sun reaches the horizon, the color becomes amber-green, almost amber-gold. At night, there is darkness, but it's not complete—the bioluminescence in the deeper root-layers and in some of the plants themselves creates a faint, irregular starfield at ground level.

The vastness is the thing that finally overcomes rational assessment. This plain extends farther than walking could meaningfully traverse. It has weather of its own. It has an internal ecology so complex that understanding any one piece requires understanding the whole. It is a single ecosystem so large it becomes a climate, a geology, a world-unto-itself. And it is young. The patterns are still settling. The rules are still being written. Somewhere in the deep past of this landscape—only recently past, geologically speaking—this was not: this was bare mineral, this was inorganic. Something tipped it toward life, toward profusion, toward this green density, and now the system is accelerating into itself, bootstrapping complexity from its own biological heat.

You are small here. You are an observer in a world still *happening*.
