# The Moss Highlands of Keth'al

You emerge into a place that exists mostly in suggestion. The ground beneath your feet is a compressed mat of moss—countless species tangled into something between soil and living tissue, still damp from morning's drainage. Your boots sink slightly with each step, releasing the smell of deep earth and fermentation. The moss is everywhere, the foundational fact of this world. It clings to itself, multiplies in the minute spaces between older growth, creates a substrate so complete that you cannot see bare stone anywhere, though you know the rock exists underneath, ancient and jagged.

The elevation is immense. You stand somewhere near three kilometers up, where the air has thinned and carries a constant knife-edge of cold that makes your lungs ache. Your breath crystallizes in small clouds that dissolve before falling. The temperature hovers just above the point where water becomes ice—perpetually poised in that uncertain state. On your exposed skin, the damp feels like a slow theft of heat. It's not the sharp cold of snow-fields but something wetter, more persistent. The moss absorbs moisture without releasing it, and you realize the climate here exists in a state of permanent half-thaw: enough liquid water to sustain life, never enough heat to truly dry anything.

The vegetation fills the space. Not in the way a forest fills it—there are no trees here, not in any recognizable sense. Instead, there are *structures* made of plants. Moss builds upward into low mounds, some as tall as a person's waist, some reaching chest-height. Between these grow spore-bearing stalks, ferns with fronds that overlap like roof-tiles, ground-hugging shrubs with leaves the color of old bronze. The plants don't compete for space; they collaborate, build on each other. Where moss meets stone-outcrop, lichens have etched chemical signatures into the rock, softening edges, preparing the landscape for future growth. It's a system in early adolescence—young enough that sharp features still protrude, recently enough emerged from bare stone that you can imagine it happening, can almost see the stages of colonization still occurring.

The horizon dissolves into mist at a distance you can't quite judge. Weather moves across this height with its own physics. You've watched the same cloud mass for hours, reorganizing itself, revealing and concealing the far ridgelines. Occasionally, rain arrives not as drops but as a dense mist that seems to rise from the ground as much as fall from sky. The water cycle here is inefficient and endless—nothing flows away quickly. Moisture catches in the moss, in the cupped fronds, in shallow depressions between the mounds. There are small pools, perfectly still, reflecting the grey-white sky. Nothing evaporates rapidly at this temperature. Everything lingers.

The light is wrong. Even at what should be midday, the world exists in a permanent twilight. Clouds obscure the direct sun. The mist itself becomes a diffusing lens, spreading what little light penetrates into an even, sourceless glow. Shadows don't exist here in any meaningful way. There's only gradient—from slightly darker grey to slightly lighter grey. After hours in this landscape, you realize your eyes have adjusted to accept vastly less illumination than they normally require. Colors fade. The moss becomes monochromatic. You learn to see by the subtle variations in texture rather than tone.

The vast emptiness is deceptive. There is nothing here per cubic meter, or so it feels—you could walk for hours and meet only mist and moss-covered stone. But the land extends beyond perception in all directions. You cannot see where it ends. The horizon doesn't exist. The world simply fades upward into cloud. You have the sensation of standing on a platform within a much larger structure, but the structure is so immense that you perceive only the immediate surface. Weather systems develop their own character here, their own personalities. You've seen what you could only describe as a localized storm develop over a ridge system three kilometers away—the clouds rotating, darkening, the wind arriving in waves as if the weather were breathing.

There are traces of habitation, but they're subtle. You find the remnants of a structure at what must be an old settlement: walls of stacked stone, grass-grown now, sunk back into the landscape. The stones are roughly worked—chopped, not carved. No mortar, just friction and weight. The building is almost lost in the moss. Whoever built it lived simply. You find evidence of tool-use: marks on certain stones showing deliberate shaping with tools of stone or possibly early metal. There's a cleared area around what might have been a hearth, though fire leaves little trace in this damp climate. No smoke-blackened stone survives long here. The acid in the moss eats away at everything eventually.

The few people you've encountered are hardy, minimal in their needs. They wear clothes woven from plant fibers, dyed with pigments that leach from stone and lichen. They move through the landscape with the ease of those who've adapted their entire physiology to function in perpetual damp-cold. They speak, when they speak, in voices that carry oddly through the mist—dampened by moisture, swallowed quickly by the mass of vegetation.

Life populates this realm despite its harshness. Insects—countless varieties—emerge from the moss. You hear them constantly: a soft chittering, a rhythmic clicking, what might be some form of insect chorus at twilight (though time is uncertain here; twilight lasts for hours or days). There are birds, rarely seen but often heard—plaintive calls from somewhere in the mist, sharp sudden wings that vanish into grey. Something larger moves through the deepest moss-mounds at night: heavy, deliberate, leaving depressions that fill slowly with water. You've seen tracks but not the creature. The fauna maintains itself on whatever the moss produces, on the insects, on each other. It's a quieter ecosystem than you'd expect—the damp absorbs sound, but what does make sound is amplified by the confined air, the dense vegetation acting as both silencer and amplifier depending on frequency.

The soundscape is paradoxical. There's constant noise—the clicking of insects, the drip of water, the whisper of wind through the high fern-fronds—but it arrives muffled, as if through thick cloth. The moisture in the air carries sound strangely. Sometimes a distant call arrives clear as if from ten meters away; other times, something nearby is nearly inaudible. The wind, when it comes, sounds like it's moving through the moss as much as across it, as if the vegetation is an instrument being played. Rain on the fronds creates layers of sound: pattering on the outer leaves, then a muted drumming on inner layers, then the quiet trickle into the substrate below.

There is something off about the geometry here. Not in any way you can articulate precisely. The mist creates optical effects—distances collapse and expand. You think you see a structure in the fog, only to reach it after a ten-minute walk and discover it's a natural rock formation, unworked, barely emergent through the moss. The scale confuses you. Is that ridge system five kilometers away or fifty? Are those mushrooms the size of your hand or the size of a person, distorted by distance and haze? The landscape operates at a scale that troubles your intuition. The moss-mounds could be centimeters or meters tall. The erosion features are freshly carved by water and cold, sharp, unrounded—but so vast and few that they barely register as erosion at all. Instead, they read as essential topography, as if this is simply how the world is shaped here at this elevation in this climate.

The system is active. Everything moves, but slowly. The plants grow—you could almost watch it if you stared long enough. The water cycles endlessly, captured and released and recaptured. The insects emerge on their own schedule, appearing in waves. Something fundamental is happening, some ecological succession is occurring, some balance is being found and then re-found. You are witnessing youth, but vast youth—a landscape in the early stages of a process that will take millennia.

You could stay here and it would be possible to live. The moss provides insulation, the plants provide food (though you'd need to learn which ones), the water is constant if you know where to find it still and clear. But you would be changed by it. The cold would settle in your bones. The perpetual damp would make your skin soft in places and cracked in others. Your eyes would adjust to the grey. You would begin to move at the pace the landscape demands, not the pace you bring to it.

The moss highlands exist at the edge of habitability—not quite hostile, but demanding surrender. They are young, vast, empty, and somehow absolutely full.
