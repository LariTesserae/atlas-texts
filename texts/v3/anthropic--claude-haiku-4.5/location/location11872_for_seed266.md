# The Plateau of Exhausted Light

You arrive at altitude where the sky has learned to be still.

The plateau stretches in all directions—a vast, weathered table of rust-colored stone that has spent geological ages shedding itself. The rock face is not jagged. Eons of wind (slow wind, patient wind) have worn it into rounded ridges and gentle valleys, each depression filled with fine dust the color of dried blood. The stone itself has a particular dryness; it drinks light and gives nothing back except heat. When you touch it, it is warm—genuinely warm, not from touch but from the day itself, which has soaked into every surface and settled there like a memory that won't fade.

The emptiness is the first thing you understand. The second thing is that the emptiness is inhabited.

Across the plateau, scattered across distances that make individual features hard to resolve, stand structures. They are industrial in character but not in urgency. Tall latticed towers of dark metal—mining infrastructure, mostly. Extraction equipment frozen mid-operation, or perhaps still operating, but so slowly that movement registers only across hours or days. Conveyor systems run in shallow arcs between distant points, their belts moving with glacial patience. Some are dark. Some catch the light in ways that suggest they are still powered, still converting mineral from depth to surface, but the work happens at a rhythm you could almost mistake for geology itself.

The buildings—and there are buildings, scattered in loose clusters that might charitably be called towns—are constructed from the same stone as the plateau itself, quarried from it. This creates a visual confusion: structure and landscape become difficult to distinguish at distance. A long, low processing facility might be a natural ridge. A dormitory complex might be an outcropping. The architecture is functional, repetitive, without ornament. Corrugated metal roofs. Pipes carrying unknown substances between facilities. Windows rare and small.

The air is warm and carries a mineral taste. Not acrid—acidity would suggest volatility, and this world resists volatility. The air is inert, dust-laden, still. When dust moves, it moves in slow suspension, taking hours to settle from a height.

Sound exists here but is muted by the scale and the density of rock. The machinery produces noise—the deep thrum of distant motors, the grind of stone on metal, the occasional hiss of hydraulic systems—but these sounds travel poorly through the thin, dry air. They arrive at your ear diminished, as if already ancient when they reach you. Wind, when it comes, moves without urgency, and produces only a whisper through the lattice towers and across the open spaces.

The fauna are not what you expected.

They emerge slowly from your awareness. At first you mistake them for equipment malfunction—a ripple in the dust near a conveyor line. Then you see them in clearer form: creatures with segmented bodies, chitinous in appearance, moving with the same patience as the machinery around them. They are large—some spanning the length of a vehicle—but their movements are economical, almost meditative. They seem to be mining. They burrow into the soft dust, extracting something, moving it in slow deliberate paths toward the industrial structures. No one stops them. The workers in the distant facilities seem to accept their presence as part of the ambient industrial landscape.

Other creatures—you're less certain what to call them—hang from the lattice towers in clusters. Translucent, gelatinous, their bodies seem to absorb the ambient heat and convert it into a faint bioluminescence that only becomes visible in the shadow-sides of structures. They don't move often. When they do, they shift position with the sun, following shade. They might be filtering something from the air. The air here tastes of their presence, though whether that's taste or imagination becomes difficult to distinguish.

The light itself is strange. Not alien—the sun is recognizable, its spectrum normal—but it falls on this landscape in a way that produces very little shadow. The dry, dust-laden air scatters it evenly. Everything is illuminated but without contrast, as if you're seeing the world through old glass. The horizon is hazy. The sky is a pale, washed-out blue that seems to retreat from you rather than embrace you. The sun itself, when you look toward it obliquely, seems to be watching the plateau with the same patient indifference that the plateau shows it.

The temperature remains constant throughout the day—warm, never hot, never cool. This constancy is disturbing in a way you can't quite articulate. It suggests that the plateau has reached an equilibrium, that it has found its stable state and intends to remain there for a very long time.

The city—the main settlement, if you walk toward the largest concentration of structures—reveals itself gradually. Warehouses built into the stone itself, their openings dark and deep. Residential areas arranged in grids, the buildings identical, paint faded to the color of the stone that surrounds them. Industrial canals carry liquid—mineral solutions, perhaps, or coolant for machinery that generates no waste heat—in slow, steady flows toward collection points. Workers move between facilities at intervals. They wear protective gear, but it seems ceremonial rather than functional; no one appears distressed or urgent.

There is a central plaza. It contains a structure that might be administrative, or might be religious, or might serve no function at all. It is larger than the surrounding buildings, constructed from stone that is slightly darker than the plateau itself, and its surfaces are marked with patterns—either wear-marks from long erosion, or deliberate designs, or both simultaneously.

The place is not dead. It is not alive in any conventional sense. It exists in a state of slow transformation, of gradual extraction and collection and processing, of creatures moving and machinery grinding and dust falling and light spreading across stone. Change happens here, but on a timescale that requires patience to perceive. A worker who left this place for a decade and returned would find everything superficially identical, yet would recognize, in the deepening of certain channels and the shift of particular stone formations, that work continues.

The sky never darkens here to true night. It dims to a pale orange, then brightens again. The transition takes many hours. The stars, when they emerge, are dim and numerous, obscured by the dust-laden air. The industrial lights of the plateau—steady, non-flickering—come on, and the world takes on a different character: less like a natural formation, more like the interior of some vast machine.

You realize, after some time here, that you cannot hear the wind anymore. You have become acclimated to the scale. The distant hum of machinery has become the baseline of silence. Your own breath sounds loud in the stillness.

The plateau stretches on. It is old. It will be here long after you leave.
