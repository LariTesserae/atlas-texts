# The Tarn of Rendered Echoes

The water here is **almost solid**—not ice, but something closer to oil rendered from light itself. It fills a basin no more than three hundred meters across, cupped between walls of pale basalt that still bear the geometric fracture-marks of their violent cooling. The water doesn't move in waves. Instead it *breathes*, the surface rising and falling maybe half a meter per hour in a rhythm that has no name. When it rises, the air above shimmers. When it falls, you can hear it—a sound like a thousand glass bowls being filled simultaneously, very slowly, each one precise.

The water is cool. Not cold. The kind of cool that feels like holding something that hasn't been in sunlight for centuries. Your hand in it becomes numb over about twenty minutes. The sensation doesn't hurt; it just... stops.

**What lives here** is the puzzle.

The water writhes. Not with fins. The surface occasionally dimples where something rises to taste the air, and what rises is **not** a mouth—it's more like a knotted geometry, something with too many angles for its size. These creatures (the word sits wrong) are densely packed. In any given square meter of surface, three or four of these protrusions will appear in the span of an hour. They don't compete. They seem to occupy the same water without collision, as if the tarn is structured in more dimensions than sight permits.

You can see *into* the water down about fifteen meters before the light stops playing fair. In that band, shadows move in coordinated schools, except they're not schools—they're more like thoughts, patterns of intelligence moving through the water in long, patient geometries. They take hours to complete a circuit of the basin. Whatever they are, they are **old**.

The walls of basalt **sing**, but not metaphorically. There are cracks in the stone, fresh enough that their edges don't yet feel weathered, and through these cracks a sound emerges—a low, sustained tone that shifts in frequency imperceptibly slowly. It takes about three hours to move through a full octave. This is not biological. This might not be accidental either. The cracks are too regular, too evenly spaced around the basin's circumference. There are eleven of them. Each one sings a different pitch. Together they create a chord that resolves so slowly you can only hear it if you stay still for hours and let the change accumulate in memory.

The basalt itself is **young**—somewhere between millennia and millennia-and-a-half, perhaps. You can see the flows, the precise edges where one cooling layer met another. Some surfaces are glassy, almost mirror-like, reflecting the pale sky above in sections. Other walls are already beginning to peel, long sheets of darker stone separating from lighter stone beneath, but this process will take longer than most human timescales can hold. The peeling happens at a rate you could only measure with instruments designed to track centuries.

The atmosphere above the tarn **weighs**. The air is saturated—not with cloud, but with a kind of suspended aqueous potential. When you breathe, your lungs taste mineral salt. The humidity never quite precipitates into rain, but the air is so full of water vapor that speech becomes difficult; sound seems to travel differently here, absorbed and redistributed by invisible moisture. When the water breathes and rises, the air becomes even thicker, harder to process. When it falls, there's an almost shocking moment of relative clarity, but only relative.

The **light** is strange in a way that's difficult to articulate. The sun is present. The sky is visible. But something in the way light interacts with the saturated air creates a sensation of perpetual dawn or dusk, even at midday. Shadows are soft. Colors are slightly desaturated, as if you're viewing the world through a pane of very clean glass. The basalt walls seem to glow from within at certain angles, though this is just the interaction of light and the stone's internal structure.

**Sound** is *everywhere*. The breathing of the water. The singing of the cracks. The subtle squelch and whisper of the creatures moving beneath the surface—not loud creatures, but so many of them, and so densely distributed, that the cumulative effect is a kind of white noise made of intention. Occasionally, from deep down, something produces a clicking sound, very distant, very deliberate. The clicks come in patterns that might be language, or might be sonar, or might be something that doesn't translate to human categories of meaning. They echo off the basin walls and create interference patterns that sit in your chest.

The basin itself is a **closed system**. There's no visible inlet or outlet. The water came from somewhere—possibly from below, possibly from the air itself crystallizing into liquid form through processes you don't understand—and it remains here, cycling, breathing, supporting whatever lives in the spaces between spaces that the strange geometries of this place seem to permit.

**Time moves differently here.** Not faster or slower in a measurable way, but the pace of things is *patient*. The creatures develop their patterns across hours and days. The singing of the stone will take millennia to carve itself into anything audible to an unprepared ear. The edges of the basalt will not crumble in any human lifespan. You could sit here for a week and feel, at the end, as though you'd witnessed only the opening phrase of something much, much longer.

If you close your eyes, the sounds resolve into something almost musical. If you keep them open, you begin to see patterns in the way the creatures move, though you can never quite focus on them directly. The water breathes. The stone sings. The creatures think their long, deliberate thoughts. And the air, so thick and so full, holds it all suspended in a kind of crystallized patience that doesn't feel hostile so much as **utterly indifferent to human scales**.

This place is not dead. But it is not alive in any way you have names for.
