# The Hollow Garden

The cavity opens downward—you've descended into it along a hand-carved spiral, each switchback worn smooth by centuries of feet. The air doesn't grow colder as you descend; instead it becomes *thicker*, heavier with a warmth that holds moisture you can taste on your tongue, though there is no water here. Not water as you knew it.

The walls around you sweat a faint amber resin that smells of copper and fermented fruit. It seeps in slow rivulets from cracks in the stone, never pooling, never flowing far—it evaporates into vapor so fine it catches the light source you've brought in scattered fragments, each droplet catching and splitting the beam. The stone itself is pale limestone, but stippled with darker veins of something metallic-looking, something that doesn't erode the way stone erodes. These veins are *sharp*—your fingertip would draw blood if you touched them carelessly. They're young, newly exposed by some earlier shift or excavation.

The vegetation begins gradually.

At first, only a fuzz. Pale filaments clinging to the damp walls—not quite fungal, not quite plant, something that exists in the taxonomic space between. These threads glow faintly in darkness, a suggestion of bioluminescence that's barely present until you look away, then catches the corner of your eye with a faint greenish-white. They're silent. Everything living here moves without sound until suddenly—

A creature.

You hear it before you see it. A *clicking*—rhythmic, deliberate, not frantic. It echoes in the chamber. The creature itself is mantis-like but wrong: its carapace is translucent and whorled, chambers within chambers like a nautilus shell, and it moves through the vegetation on legs that are mostly joint, mostly angle. It's hunting the filaments, or harvesting them, or perhaps being harvested by them—the relationship is opaque. It clicks again, and three others respond from different quadrants of the hollow. The sounds layer over each other, creating rhythmic interference patterns that your ear struggles to parse.

The cavity widens.

As you move deeper inward, the space opens into something vast—but you cannot see the vastness. The ceiling is perhaps thirty feet above, but it might be three hundred; the warm air holds so much vapor that distance becomes theoretical. The limestone floor beneath you is uneven, studded with formations that are young enough to still have sharp points—stalagmites that never had time to smooth themselves, still aggressive with their own newness. Perhaps they've only been growing for decades.

The vegetation *proper* begins here.

It is not forest. It is not garden. It's something that only exists in the absence of light and water. The plants—and you must call them that for lack of a better word—are pale and strange. Some are mushrooms of towering proportions, their caps thin and papery, arranged in spirals that hurt to look at directly. Others are vine-like but with an articulated quality, jointed like insect legs, and they grow not downward but in all directions at once, creating three-dimensional lattices that your eyes cannot quite map. Still others are bulbous, translucent, filled with that amber liquid that seeps everywhere but never pools, never flows like water would.

The ground between the plants is not bare. It's covered with what appears to be a soft nap, a fuzz of something organic that gives slightly under your feet. It smells of warmth, of fermentation, of minerals being slowly converted into something else. There is *space* here—the plants are scattered, not crowded—but the space itself feels inhabited, dense with presences you can't quite name.

The sound is everywhere.

Not the clicking of the mantis-things (though that continues, sporadic, forming patterns that might be language or might be feeding). There is a deeper sound—a resonance that you feel more than hear, a vibration in your sternum. It comes from the plants themselves, or from the vapor, or from something vast and patient moving in the air. It's the sound of fermentation on a geological scale: gases moving through stems, liquids weeping from tissues, the slow conversion of nutrients into growth and heat.

Occasionally, something *sings*.

It's a sound like struck crystal, but warmer—a sustained note that rises and falls in microtones. It comes from deep within the vegetation, from something you never see. The first time you hear it, your instinct is to move toward it. The second time, you understand this instinct is not your own.

The light here is your own.

Whatever bioluminescence exists in the filaments and smaller organisms provides only a suggestion of illumination—enough to confirm that your lantern is necessary, not enough to feel redundant. Your light creates sharp shadows against the undulating landscape, and these shadows move with a strange elasticity, stretching and compressing as the vapor shifts. In the gaps between your lit circles, the cavity recedes into a darkness that seems to have *depth*, that seems to contain something patient.

The creatures are abundant but sparse.

You see them rarely and often. The mantis-things are most common, clicking through the vegetation at all hours (there are no hours here; time moves in fermentation's rhythm, not in sunrise-sunset). There are also things like hexapods made of overlapping plates, moving with a sinuous precision through the plant-lattices. Once, you glimpse something that might be molluscan—a mass of soft tissue and tentacle, clinging to a pale fungal cap, breathing in visible pulses. The creatures don't seem to perceive you as a threat or an intruder. They navigate around you with the same careful indifference they show the plants.

Nothing here hunts frantically. Nothing starves. The ecosystem has reached an equilibrium that is absolutely static.

This is perhaps the strangest thing: nothing changes.

The amber resin weeps at the same rate, day after day. The stalactites and stalagmites neither grow nor diminish in any perceptible way. The plants maintain their positions, their distributions, their slow metabolic processes. The creatures move through this landscape following routes that seem predetermined, as though they are grooves in the system itself. The vapor holds its density. The temperature never fluctuates.

Time here is not experienced as change but as *duration*.

You realize, after hours in the hollow, that you're not moving through a landscape—you're existing *within* a system, a closed ecology that has solved the problem of existence through perfect stasis. Nothing grows old because nothing changes. Nothing dies because there are no gradients, no pressures pushing toward dissolution. The creatures click their ancient rhythms. The plants maintain their strange geometries. The vapor holds the warmth like a hand around a glowing coal, neither warming nor cooling.

The resin still weeps. It still doesn't pool. It still disappears into vapor.

And in the farthest reaches of the hollow, where your light cannot quite reach, something continues to sing in half-tones that your ear was not shaped to fully comprehend.
