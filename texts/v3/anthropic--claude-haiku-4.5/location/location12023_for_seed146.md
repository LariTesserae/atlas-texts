# The Thermite Caverns

You enter through a shaft driven downward generations ago—rough, hand-widened, never straight. The walls are still sharp with the tool-marks that made them, angular and unhealed by time. Not old enough to smooth. Not old enough to settle into themselves.

Below, the cavern system opens into something vaster than should fit beneath the earth. The sensation of true scale arrives slowly: not through single landmarks, but through the gradual realization that the far wall—visible as a diffuse glow—is impossibly distant. Weather forms here. You can taste the difference in the air at different heights.

The temperature is warm. Genuinely warm, not the ambient warmth of depth, but a steady, even heat that comes from nowhere and everywhere. It hovers at a constant that never fluctuates—you notice this after an hour of walking, when you realize you haven't shifted your posture, haven't searched for shade or sought shelter. Thirty degrees. Always thirty degrees. It is a law of this place.

The ground beneath you is not stone in any conventional sense.

It is *glass*—but not the glassy surface of cooling lava. This is something more fundamental: the cavern floors and walls are coated in a substance that appears to be solidified not from melting, but from crystallization itself, as if the bedrock were in the process of transforming into something else and froze mid-becoming. Volcanic in origin, yes, but ancient volcanic. The light that gives the cavern its faint luminescence—deep orange, like coals banked beneath ash—seems to come *through* the glass itself, or from within the mineral's structure. It does not cast shadows properly. Things do not seem to occclude one another so much as to define layers of the same glowing medium.

The glass is sharp. Brutally so. Fresh edges everywhere, fracture-lines that have barely begun to age, surfaces that could draw blood from a careless hand. There is no dust—dust would require erosion, and erosion requires water or time or both. This place has had neither, or has resisted both. The breaks are clean. The geometry is terrible.

Structures are scattered throughout—too few to suggest settlement, too deliberate to be accident. Rough assemblies of stone and unfired clay, held together without mortar or engineering principle, just stacked and left to balance. Some are mere markers: cairns no higher than your knee. Others are small chambers, roofed with flat stones, their entrances dark even by the standards of this place. They are old enough to have settled into the ground, but not old enough to have begun crumbling. The clay is still firm. The stones haven't shifted. They are young ruins, if ruins they are.

Tools lie scattered—bone and stone implements, mostly, though there is crude worked metal here and there. Not rusted. The absence of water means no rust; oxidation happens, but slowly, and the oxide doesn't flake or powder. It sits on the surface like a thin dressing. The tools themselves show no signs of being abandoned in haste. They are simply *left*. Placed. Set down at specific intervals, as if marking a grid no living eye understands.

The silence is profound but not complete. It has texture.

The glass resonates. Not constantly, not loudly, but at intervals that follow no rhythm you can predict, the cavern floor and walls emit a very low hum—felt more in the teeth than heard through the ears—as if the material itself is breathing on a timescale of minutes. The note never changes. It is not unpleasant, only strange, and it makes navigation feel like movement through a thing rather than through space.

And the life.

You don't see it at first because it doesn't move much. But once your eyes adjust, you realize the glass itself is not empty. Within it—trapped, or embedded, or growing—are creatures of a type that suggests the deep-sea made of mineral instead of water. Translucent forms, some no larger than grains of salt, others the size of your hand, suspended in the glass matrix or moving through it with deliberate slowness. They are not bioluminescent exactly, but they *use* the light that comes from the glass itself, bending it, refracting it, creating the only patterns in this place besides the mathematical fractures of the stone.

The creatures have no names because they fit no category. Some possess what might be shells, chitinous or carbonate-based, but the boundary between creature and structure dissolves when you look directly. Are they eating the glass? Are they made of it? Are they symbiotic with it, generating the light together? The questions feel wrong here—too binary for what you're seeing.

Larger fauna move at the margins of sight. Sinuous, pale things that navigate the perimeter where the glass meets less crystalline stone. They too seem ancient and new simultaneously—perfectly adapted to this environment, yet still in the process of learning it. They don't hunt, or if they do, they take centuries between kills. You see no violence. No competition. The density of life is high—multiply present—but layered, distributed, each form occupying a separate frequency of the cavern.

Time doesn't touch this place the way it touches the world above.

The temperature never shifts. The light never changes intensity. The low hum continues at its steady pulse. The creatures do not age visibly. The structures do not further decay. Even you, after hours or days—and time becomes hard to measure here—realize that the sweat of arrival has not dried, that your thirst hasn't worsened, that your fatigue exists only in memory. The equilibrium is not hostile. It is merely *complete*. The place has found its form and will not change it.

Yet it is young. Everything about it screams youth—the sharpness, the newness of the stone-work, the sense that the creatures have only recently discovered they could move in this direction, that the glass itself is still settling into its final state. It is possible that given a thousand years, or a million, this will become something else. But the rate of change is so slow that you cannot imagine the mechanism. The cavern has achieved a stasis so perfect it is indistinguishable from permanence.

You could die here and become part of the stasis. The glass would accept you. The light would find you. And for longer than you could comprehend, you would continue.
