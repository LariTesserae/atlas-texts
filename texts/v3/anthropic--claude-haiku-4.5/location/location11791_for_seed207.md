# The Hollows

You descend through the stone mouth—a fissure barely wider than a shoulder—into a place that doesn't quite fit the geography of the world above. The walls are still wet where you squeeze through. Not dripping. Sweating. A steady, patient exudation that has glazed the limestone for centuries, making it smooth and cool as glass.

The passage opens.

The cavern is smaller than you'd expect from that vast cold sky you left behind. Perhaps twenty meters across. Perhaps ten. Distance becomes slippery here because of how the light fails. A single bioluminescent panel—old tech, the kind that hums at a frequency just below hearing—casts everything in a faint, greenish phosphorescence. It hangs in a titanium cage bolted to the wall, maybe forty years of installation wear showing in the rust that blooms around the bolts. Someone's work. Someone's choice to bring light here.

But that light barely reaches the far side.

The plants are what arrest you. They grow in the margins—in the damp stone pockets, along the walls where moisture runs, clustering where someone has scattered dark soil in discrete, careful mounds. Ferns: a dozen varieties at least, fronds so densely layered they form a kind of compressed tapestry. Mosses the color of old copper. Liverworts in burgundy and pale green, so small they read as a texture rather than individual organisms. There are fungi too—the real work of the ecosystem—pale, thin-stalked things that rise like ghost architecture from the nutrient-rich rot of the fern beds.

The vegetation is wrong for a cavern. Too lush. Too organized. It suggests decades of tending.

Temperature sits just above freezing. The stone sweats because of the differential—warm groundwater moving through the limestone from somewhere deeper, losing its heat gradually to the shaft above. The air smells of iron, of wet earth, of something faintly chemical that you can't name. Copper salts, perhaps. Or something in the water's ancient journey through bedrock. Your breath doesn't fog, but it would if you stayed long enough. The cold works slowly here, like everything else.

The moss is too soft to stand on. You've learned to walk on the bare limestone, where the moisture has worn paths. These paths are old—grooves worn by regular footfall, the edges rounded by time. Multiple routes. Someone has been coming here often, or many someones have, or one person came with devotion and left marks that will outlast them.

The water, when you locate it, is almost invisible. No stream. No pool. Instead, it exists as a thin film across one entire wall—a permanent seep that feeds the plants. Up close, you can see the stratification: where different minerals have precipitated, leaving pale banding like growth rings. These bands represent seasons, maybe years. The water never runs fast enough to groove the stone, but it never stops either. The pace of a geologic patience.

There are other remnants of human presence.

A metal canister, corroded nearly through, sits wedged in a crevice. Beside it, a laminated paper in protective plastic, pages yellowed. The handwriting is careful, dated in years you can still read: observations, pH measurements, species lists. *Polystichum acrostichoides, Asplenium platyneuron, Pteridium aquilinum subspecies var.—* The text degrades into illegibility, but the impulse is clear. Someone was documenting. Cataloging. Making the hidden visible through notation.

There's a bench—actual worked wood, now covered in a living skin of moss and liverwort. It faces the fern wall. Someone sat here. Sat in the cold and the green shadow-light and watched the slow processes. For how long? Long enough that wood returns to the forest it came from, metabolized by fungi, incorporated back into the cycle.

The light technology suggests something beyond standard: the bioluminescence isn't natural. The chemistry that powers it is elegant—self-sustaining, requiring no external feed, designed to work in cold environments. It's old enough that you wonder if it was salvage from something else, repurposed here. The cage it hangs in is older still—brass fittings, titanium core, an engineering standard you don't recognize but feel you *should*.

The sound here is the absence of sound, mostly. Your breathing. The almost-inaudible whisper of water across stone—not flow, but migration, the slow movement of moisture through molecular-scale spaces. Occasionally, a *tick*—the sound of condensation forming and dripping from the lowest points of the ceiling. Seconds apart. Minutes. The rhythm never quite settles into predictability.

No animal life. No insects. No sound of beetles, no smell of guano from bats. Just the plants and the fungi and the patient minerals and the evidence of a single human intelligence choosing, regularly, to come here and tend this pocket of green in absolute darkness.

The deepest unsettling comes when you realize: the bench doesn't face the ferns randomly. It's positioned exactly where the bioluminescent light pools most concentrated. Someone didn't come to explore. They came to sit. To watch this slow world operate at its own rhythm—a rhythm measured in the subtle precipitation of minerals, the imperceptible growth of moss, the patient seep of groundwater that has been finding this exact path through stone for longer than the human species has been aware of it.

You sit. The bench is damp. Cold. Your weight disturbs nothing. The water continues its seep. The ferns don't move. The light—ancient, patiently glowing—illuminates what was here before you arrived and will be here after you leave, changed by nothing you've done.
