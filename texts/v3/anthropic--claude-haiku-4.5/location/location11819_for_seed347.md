# The Sunken Market

The light down here is submarine. Not dark—a steady grey-green diffusion filtering through the water-table, muted and without shadows. Your eyes adjust after the first moments and the place resolves: an underground valley, perhaps a quarter-mile across, its ceiling lost in the vegetative canopy that fills the space between the ground beneath your feet and the pale stone roof somewhere fifty feet up. You've descended via a natural ramp—old, recently scoured by seepage—and now you stand on the valley floor where nothing is ever quite dry.

The vegetation is impossible in its density. Everywhere. Moss hangs in wet curtains from the exposed limestone walls. Ferns cluster so thickly at ground level that walking means stepping over their fronds rather than through open air. What you thought were boulders resolve, as you look closer, into hummocks of compressed growth—moss over moss over root systems that have knotted themselves into solid mass. The color is green, yes, but also silver where water clings. Everything appears *just-rained-upon*, though no rain falls here. The stone sweats. The air itself has weight and coldness: a few degrees above freezing, humid enough that breathing feels like drinking.

Water is everywhere and nowhere visible as discrete body. There are no pools, no streams in the conventional sense. Instead, the ground perpetually glistens. Water emerges from the stone face at a hundred small points—creeping from fissures, weeping from cracks, condensing on surfaces already sodden. Your boots sink slightly into the saturated moss with each step, releasing a faint exhalation of cold air. Small rivulets trace their way downhill, too subtle to follow, yet the floor has a grain to it—paths of slightly lesser moss where water has worn preference over millennia.

The human settlement is woven into this like a second ecology.

Stone structures rise in clusters: buildings, yes, but made not imposed but *integrated*. Walls follow the contours of the valley floor, built from the same limestone as the ceiling, so they seem less constructed than revealed—as if the builders merely uncovered rooms that had always been here, waiting. The stone is fresh-looking; the erosion features are sharp. A building face shows clean fracture planes where someone has quarried, perhaps only seasons past. The mortar between stones still shows crystalline glitter—not yet colonized by lichen.

The structures are packed densely. A narrow street—if it can be called that—winds between buildings, sometimes only four feet wide, the walls so close that you could touch both sides. This is not accidental. The density serves a purpose: every surface gains the benefit of neighboring warmth, every corner pools what little air-current moves through. Narrow passages slow the seep; water finds its level between the buildings and flows more deliberately, used, perhaps, for purposes you haven't yet understood. Stairs descend into doorways. A second level of structures sits half-built above the first, raw stone showing where wood frames—if there was ever wood—has given way. The settlement seems still in construction, not abandoned but incomplete. Forever becoming.

The structures have no regular faces. Windows vary in size and placement. Some buildings have multiple doorways; some seem sealed entirely, their purpose opaque. Roof lines are irregular, some natural stone, some shaped to slope for drainage. You see no chimneys. No clear evidence of fire. No ash, no charred stone. The cold would forbid it, perhaps. The dampness. The air doesn't smell of smoke.

Yet there are signs of organization. What might be a plaza opens among the buildings—a wider space, perhaps a hundred feet across, where the moss is beaten down from traffic. At its center, a stone platform rises slightly, worn smooth. Other features: what looks like a sorting yard, where objects are arranged in patient rows—stones graded by size, objects you can't identify at this distance. A water channel carved deliberately through the stone, wide and shallow, running from one end of the plaza toward the southern passage. Someone *tends* this space.

The life here is active, despite the quiet.

Small creatures move in the moss. Not insects, precisely—movement is too slow, too deliberate. Or perhaps they're insects so adapted to this place that they've taken on the rhythm of stone and water. A crab-like thing, pale and eyeless, emerges from under the moss fronds. Others follow. They work, perhaps, or graze. A sound like rustling, but so soft it barely registers—more like the memory of sound, or water moving through deep space. You hear the *absence* of bird-calls, of wind-noise, of the thousand small disturbances of the surface world. What you hear instead: the tick and drip of water-movement, irregular and scattered. A low grinding—perhaps still-settling stone. The hum of the place's own breathing, if places breathe.

The human settlement has no visible occupation. No figures move in the streets. No smoke, no sound of labor. Yet the order of the place argues against abandonment. The arrangements are too neat, too purposeful. The water channels too clean. Recently cleaned? It's impossible to say. Time moves differently here. Seasons don't penetrate. Decay is slow, held in abeyance by cold.

In the deeper passages, where the light dimmer becomes thin and the buildings thin out, you sense rather than see larger structures. Vaulted chambers. A sound from far down—a low note, resonant, like the valley itself is ringing. Whether it's mechanical or geological or biological remains unclear.

The cold presses down. Not painful—not quite. But it makes you aware of your own heat, the small furnace of you, leaking into the vast cool surrounding. The air wants you to be stone. To be still. To settle into moss and wait while water finds your cracks and the settlement above grows slowly, slowly into what it must become.
