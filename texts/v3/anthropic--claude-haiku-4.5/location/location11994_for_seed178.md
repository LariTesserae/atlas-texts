# The Shriek Marshes

The light here is almost gone. What little remains comes refracted—scattered through layers of mist and condensing breath, through water-beaded spider-silk that hangs in sheets between the hummocks. Everything exists in bruise-tones: purples fading to slate, slate fading to the absence of color. Your eyes adjust constantly and never quite succeed. You learn to navigate by other means.

The sound is what hits first and never stops hitting. It is *enormous*. The marsh generates a continuous shriek—not wind-sound exactly, though there is wind, cold wind that cuts at exposed skin. This is something else. The entire landscape is screaming.

The noise comes from the marsh-throat itself. Water—endlessly, impossibly water—drains upward through the low peat into the crystalline lattice-structures that push up from below. These structures are not stone. They are something that should not exist: geometric, sharp-edged, yet somehow organic in their proliferation, like frozen music or mathematics given physical form. As water passes through them, the structures resonate. They oscillate at frequencies that human ears can detect only as a sustained, building shriek that varies from moment to moment—sometimes a grinding howl, sometimes a needle-thin whistle, sometimes a bass rumble that you feel in your sternum. Nothing stops it. The resonance is the marshes' baseline condition, the note from which all other sounds emerge.

And there are other sounds. Many.

The vegetation here grows in impossible density despite the cold that should kill it. Frost-ferns. Ice-mosses. Things that are neither plant nor mineral but seem to incorporate both—crystalline fronds that still flex and photosynthesize in the weak, refracted light. They grow so thickly that the ground between the hummocks is less open space than compressed, layered growth: a three-dimensional thicket where it's difficult to see more than a meter ahead. The plants themselves emit sound. Clicking sounds. Creaks. What might be moisture-expansion or might be something closer to language. The frost-ferns produce a particular noise when the wind moves through them—a chittering, almost metallic scrape, as if millions of insect legs were trying to climb glass. The sound is unbearable for some visitors, the kind of noise that accumulates in the teeth and bones.

The creatures here are abundant but mostly small. You see them indirectly: movements in the compressed growth, sudden ripples in the standing water, shadows that don't quite match the light-source. There are no large predators—the temperature won't support them—but the small things are everywhere. Arthropods of a type that should not exist in such cold. Some are crystalline-bodied. Some seem to exist partially in water and partially in air, their bodies adapted to move between states. They move with frantic energy. The whole ecosystem appears to be in some state of permanent urgency, as if the marshes are a conversation happening too fast to follow, creatures responding to stimuli you cannot perceive, reacting to changes in the water-table or the resonance-frequency or the light-levels that shift across milliseconds.

The water itself is the real force here. It covers perhaps sixty percent of the ground-level, but "covers" is the wrong word. The water *moves*. Not in tides or currents, but in a kind of slow, deliberate circulation that seems almost purposeful. It wells up from below—from the deep places where those crystalline structures originate—and it saturates everything. The ground is not solid. It is a succession of saturated layers: peat, then clay, then something that is neither, then compressed frost and mineral-rich water that has been frozen and thawed so many times it has achieved a consistency between solid and liquid. Your feet sink. The water is so cold it burns the skin. Everything drips. Weeping rocks—actually formations of the mysterious crystalline material—leak water continuously, creating channels and micro-falls and pools that shift position from hour to hour as the underground circulation changes.

The temperature is brutal. Not the worst cold, but relentless. It numbs exposed skin within minutes. The moisture in the air means the cold penetrates deeper than it should; you can feel it layering against your lungs, building up in your chest. Breath freezes before it dissipates. The frost-ferns themselves are almost transparent with ice, yet they persist, fed by the water and the weak light, thriving in conditions that should be sterile.

The structures—those geometric impossibilities—rise in scattered clusters across the marshes. They are not buildings, though some have taken to calling them that. They are more like *machines*, but machines that predate human understanding of machinery. They oscillate. They hum. They seem to be *processing* something: water, temperature, the very composition of the air. Some are recent formations—sharp, glittering, covered in a kind of frost that reorganizes itself hourly. Others are ancient, coated in layers of ice and moss and the accumulated detritus of the ecosystem. All of them are active. All of them participate in the continuous transformation of the marshes.

And that transformation never stops. The dynamic is constant. New structures emerge from the water, pushing up over weeks or months, geometric and precise. Others collapse back into the peat, their materials breaking down and reabsorbing into the system. The water-table shifts. Channels open and close. Entire sections of the marsh reorganize themselves. The hummocks that form stable ground today may be dissolved into bog within a season. The frost-ferns spread and die back and spread again. The creatures move in waves: sometimes vast numbers of a single species appear, strip a region of vegetation, then vanish as suddenly as they came. The very composition of the air seems to change—sometimes it's breathable, almost sweet with mineral-taste; other times it's thick with particulates that sting the eyes and make the throat constrict.

The most disorienting aspect is the sense of being embedded in a system that is fundamentally *young*. These marshes are not ancient in the way most wetlands are. They are actively forming. The geological processes here operate on human timescales. You can watch the crystalline structures grow. You can see the ground rearrange itself. The landscape is still writing itself, still discovering what it is and can be.

There are no permanent settlements, though traces remain. A metal platform, sinking slowly into the peat, dating from perhaps two centuries ago. Some kind of observation post or research station, abandoned when the structures began to malfunction or the cold became unbearable or whatever understanding brought the researchers here proved inadequate. The platform is covered now in ice and frost-fern, but its mathematics are still visible: a grid, precise, inadequate to the task of comprehending what actually happens here.

The darkness is almost total. You navigate by sound and touch and the slow adjustment of the eye to absence. The refracted light provides almost no true illumination. What it does is make the environment visible in the weakest possible sense—you can see shapes, movements, the rough topography of the hummocks and the water-channels—but visibility beyond twenty or thirty meters is optimistic. The darkness presses in. It is not empty darkness. It is saturated with presence: the scream of the structures, the layered sound of the vegetation, the small frantic movements of countless creatures, the drip and splash of the perpetually weeping landscape. The darkness is dense with information that comes through every sense except sight.

The air itself tastes of minerals and frost. There is no sweet smell, no rot-smell despite the abundance of organic material. There is only the mineral-cold, the taste of water from depths, the faint metallic tang of the crystalline structures when the wind shifts and carries a fine powder of their erosion-products.

And the shriek never stops. It is the constant background against which all other existence here is arranged. It is the sound of a landscape in the process of becoming, moment by moment, never static, never complete.
