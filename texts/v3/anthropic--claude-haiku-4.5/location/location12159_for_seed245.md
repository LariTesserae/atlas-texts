# The Foundry-Flats

The ground beneath you is not ground at all—it's *layering*. Corroded copper plates, green with verdigris, welded edge-to-edge into vast sheet-valleys that extend to the horizon. Brass beneath that, and below the brass, something darker: iron oxide that's never quite finished rusting, perpetually in the act of becoming. The metal hums. Not audibly—you feel it in your molars.

Water collects here, but it shouldn't. The humidity sits in bands, visible if you look sideways—stratified air made almost tactile by the suspended brine and mineral particulate. It's not humid air so much as the *memory* of wetness, suspended in place by heat and electromagnetic something-or-other. Your breath condenses immediately and runs in rivulets down your face. The metal beneath your feet weeps constantly—seepage that emerges from nowhere, pools in the embossed valleys of deliberately-made topography, and disappears into seams that close themselves up again within hours. 

The seams are the problem. Or the evidence. Or the machinery.

Everything here was built, but not by anything currently alive. The plates don't fit together naturally; there are gaps the size of ravines between them, sealed with slag that's still warm to the touch. The spacing is intentional—a grid, almost, but warped, warping, *continuing* to warp. Sometimes the gaps close while you're looking at them. Sometimes they open wider. The metal shifts with a sound like a ship groaning, except the sound arrives before you register the movement, or after, or simultaneously on different frequencies your ears can't quite separate.

There are structures. Towers, perhaps. Or antenna. Or fingers. Built from the same metal stock, ribbed and hollow, they rise from the plateaus between the vast copper sheets. They're spaced too far apart for any rational geography—you could walk for an hour and see nothing but dim silhouettes, then suddenly be in a forest of them, clustered so densely you have to squeeze between their corroded surfaces. The towers leak. They exude a mineral-rich solution that smells of copper and something else—something organic-shaped, copper-sulfate blue, almost biological, except biology is impossible here.

The heat is pervasive, not oppressive. It radiates from below, from within the metal itself. The plates are warm enough to hurt if you lay your palm down, but not hot enough to burn immediately. There's a threshold to it, a temperature that hovers just at the edge of what human skin can tolerate. You exist in a constant negotiation with this heat—your body sweating, the moisture evaporating instantly, only to fall as condensation in the sheltered valleys and gaps.

The light is wrong. There's a sun, presumably, but it's obscured behind layers of particulate—mineral dust, oxidation clouds, the steam that perpetually rises from the water-seepage. Everything exists in a kind of amber gloom, as though filtered through old glass or through water itself. The shadows are sharp but the illumination is diffuse. Colors desaturate except for the verdigris greens, the rust-reds, the copper-bright oranges where metal is still exposed, new, still being formed.

Change happens constantly. The temperature fluctuates—not seasons, but hourly cycles of expansion and contraction that you can *see* happening. The metal plates expand, and the gaps between them close; they cool, and new gaps open. Small fractures appear in the brass, run in networks across the surface for a day, then seal themselves. The water-seepage intensifies, then slows to a trickle. Once, you might have seen what looked like new copper being extruded from beneath the older plates—a slow-motion bubbling that solidified into additional layering, additional complexity.

The towers rearrange themselves. Not often. Maybe once every few hours, maybe once a week; time here is hard to judge. They shift position by degrees, moving across the plateaus in ways that no structure should move. The gaps where they stood close up or open. New gaps elsewhere split the copper plates like stress fractures in glass. You learn not to camp too close to them.

There are patterns in the erosion—or rather, the *non*-erosion. The metal doesn't weather normally here. The corrosion is intentional, staged, designed. The verdigris spreads in geometric formations. The rust follows the welds. New material emerges from beneath the old in a way that suggests not decay but *replacement*, as though the landscape is perpetually constructing a new version of itself underneath the existing one, and the old version is sloughing away to make room.

Nothing lives here. No bacteria in the mineral pools, no fungus in the crevices, no insects in the still air. The landscape is utterly dead, utterly *inert*, except it isn't. It's doing something. It's becoming something. It has a physics all its own, a chemistry that violates the handbooks, an intention buried in the geometry of its own construction.

The sound is mostly the metal—the groaning, the expansion-click, the settling-hiss of cooling plates. But underneath, if you listen, there's a rhythm. A pulse. A signal, perhaps, or just the acoustic ghost of whatever built this place, still vibrating in the metal's memory.

You are very alone here. And you are also very much not alone.

The landscape continues in all directions, identical and utterly unlike itself, vast beyond any reasonable measure. You could walk for weeks and see the same copper valleys, the same brass mountains, the same dim-lit horizon. Or you could turn a corner and find yourself in a completely different section, new patterns, new tower arrangements, new seepage geometries.

It's building itself. Or rebuilding. Or building *you*, slowly, converting you into something compatible with its own strange physics. You've been here for hours or days, and you can't quite remember when the distinction between your sweat and the landscape's condensation stopped being meaningful.
