# The Rust Flats

The world here has already made its decisions and settled into them. There is a quality of exhaustion to the place, though not of recent collapse—more like the exhaustion of something that finished changing centuries ago and has been breathing the same breath ever since.

The ground is rust. Not rust on something else, but rust *as* ground: layered oxidized metal, compacted and crusted into a weathered plain that extends farther than visibility allows. The Flats. Beneath this crust, a deeper stratum of actual ore—magnetite, hematite—worked thin by ancient machinery that no longer exists here, leaving the subsurface in a state of permanent incompleteness. What remains is the oxidized dross, the refusal matter, spread across low terrain that rolls without urgency toward distant horizons.

The air is warm and carries a mineral taste. Here and there, long-dry channels cut through the plain—old drainage paths from when water ran through these spaces with some regularity. Moisture collects in these grooves now. Not flowing water, but standing moisture: the channels become weak reservoirs after the infrequent rains, and between rains, condensation beads on the undersides of mineral crusts. The humidity rises in certain pockets where the metal oxides hold their memory of chemical transformation. Breath moves differently in these damp zones—thicker, slightly sulfurous.

The scale is disorienting. The Flats don't hem you in. There are no structures, no walls, nothing built—the industrial work here was extraction, and extraction leaves absence. Yet the plain is not featureless. 

Distributed across the landscape at irregular intervals stand the *stacks*—massive, idle machines. Stamp mills. Concentrators. Massive cylindrical digesters, now silent, their mechanisms corroded beyond the point of recognizing function. They're built from that same metal that constitutes the ground, so they don't stand *on* it so much as rise from it, as if the landscape itself fractured upward into machinery and then forgot what the machinery was for. Some stacks are dense pillars, others open frameworks of corroded beam and cable. They cast shadows in the bright daylight, and those shadows never move.

The light here is even. The sun is high, though you're never entirely certain of the time—the sky is a uniform pale cream-gray, cleared of clouds but hazy with fine particulate that hangs suspended in the warm air. The particulate is rust dust, mostly, ground fine enough to drift, to settle in the channels, to coat anything that remains still too long. The light is industrial light: shadowless in its way, though shadows exist. What exists is visibility without warmth in the visual sense—you can see far, but the distance is pale, atmospheric, as if the dust creates its own weathered filter between observer and landscape.

Sound.

The Flats are *loud*, and the loudness is the strangest aspect because there is no clear source. The machinery is still. Nothing moves. Yet there's a constant, deep *vibration*, a kind of grinding hum that might be:

—Wind moving through the corroded channels in the metal framework of the stacks, creating resonance in the cavities.

—Something below, something in the subsurface still moving, still working. Tectonic. Hydrothermal. The earth's own machinery, clicking and grinding against itself.

—Or simply the sound of the place maintaining itself: rust oxidizing, metal crystals settling into their final orientations, mineral equilibrium asserting itself across centuries. The sound of a system that has achieved perfect, permanent balance and is eternally confirming that balance.

This sound has no rhythm. It's not periodic. But it's never silent either. Visitors report that after a few hours, the sound becomes indistinguishable from the texture of attention itself. You are not hearing it; you are becoming it.

The water here is strange. The channels fill after rain—genuine rain, not mist, though it's rare enough that months pass between storms. When water comes, it falls warm and heavy for a few hours, then ceases entirely. The channels briefly run, carrying rust-colored water that tastes of iron and something else, something mineral and almost sweet. This water does not pool for long. It drains, but not downward—or if it drains downward, something else fills the channels within days: condensation beading on the metal surfaces, moisture rising from subsurface reservoirs, dew concentrating in the early morning. The channels maintain a perpetual damp. The ecosystem, if ecosystem is the right word, exists in this damp— 

Because there is life here, though almost no one calls it that.

In the channels, in the condensation, in the moist grooves between corroded plates: fungal networks of a type that exists nowhere else. Pale, nearly colorless, they form thin branching structures that have learned to feed on metal oxides, on the very landscape itself. They're not an intrusion into the industrial world; they're a symbiosis with it, an organism that evolved—or emerged—to consume industrial by-product. Under magnification, they glow faintly with their own bioluminescence, a cold white light that means nothing to anything else that lives here. Because almost nothing else lives here.

Rarely, very rarely, a creature moves through the Flats. No birds—the air is too warm and stable for flight patterns, and there's nothing to eat on the wing. Occasionally a thing like a crab, heavily carapaced, walks the channels at night. It feeds on the fungal growth and is itself slowly being colonized by it, metal-tasting and armored. These creatures are sparse. You might walk for a day and see nothing. Then at dusk, two or three might cross your path, moving with absolute purpose toward channels you haven't noticed, as if the landscape reveals itself only to eyes that didn't evolve in the presence of green.

The Flats have been here long enough that they've become something other than a byproduct of industry. They're not post-industrial—that word suggests a before and after, suggests time passing. The Flats have stabilized. The chemical reactions have slowed to their minimum. The water has found its rhythm—rain, drainage, condensation, stasis. The fungal networks have colonized every available surface in perfect balance with their resource. The sparse fauna has adapted to perpetual hunger and moved only when necessary. The machines stand not as ruins but as fixtures, as much a part of the geology as anything else.

The rules of this place are simple: nothing changes, nothing decays further, nothing grows. Everything oxidizes to its final state and then holds it. The sound continues. The moisture cycles. The light remains even. The rust sleeps.

If you stand still in the Flats long enough—hours, perhaps a full day—you'll begin to understand that you are not observing an equilibrium. You are *in* it. The dust will coat your skin. The hum will become your heartbeat's echo. The moisture in the channels will be indistinguishable from the moisture in your lungs. The Flats don't resist presence; they absorb it, integrate it into their balance, and continue.

You will not decay here. You will simply stop changing, like everything else.
