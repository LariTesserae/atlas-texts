# The Cloth Gardens

You enter through a doorway in the hillside, and the air changes texture before your eyes adjust.

The first thing is the *smell*—vegetable mold, copper, something green pressed and fermenting. The space opens into a chamber no larger than a modest barn, but the chamber itself has almost ceased to matter. Every surface is *occupied*. The walls—if they are walls—have become a substrate. Thick moss, liver-colored and spongy, clings to what might be brick or stone beneath. Water seeps from cracks at a rhythm too slow to drip individually; instead the whole vertical face weeps. The moss drinks continuously. Droplets collect on the hanging fronds of things that aren't quite ferns, their fronds subdividing into geometries that seem wrong, too precise, as though designed rather than evolved.

The ground is not soil. It's a *mat*—woven, almost deliberately. Layer upon layer of plant material has compressed here over decades or centuries into something textile-like. Walking across it yields slightly. Your footprints fill with moisture almost as soon as you make them. Beneath the mat, you can feel the squelch and give of standing water, close enough to the surface that plant roots have learned to breathe it. The smell comes from here, from this pressed darkness where decomposition moves on its patient schedule.

Vines don't climb so much as *fill*. They don't grow upward to sunlight—there is ordinary light here, filtered gray through whatever opening feeds the space, diffuse and somehow damp-colored. Instead the vines have learned to tessellate. They loop and braid and create chambers within themselves, a skeletal architecture of living wood that forms half-rooms, partial corridors. Some are thin as cord. Others have thickened, become rigid. Dead sections hang among living ones, slow to rot here in the saturated air. Everything is covered in a fine film—lichen perhaps, or something unnamed. It makes the vines look silvered, like they've been soaked in weak solution and left to oxidize.

There is a structure. A single structure.

It sits in the center-back of the chamber, visible but not prominent, the way a bone might be visible in a tended garden. It's metal—industrial riveted metal, the kind that belonged to machinery. Factory-made. Sectioned. Perhaps it was a pressure vessel once, or a boiler, or a tank of some specific specification. Now it's become *environment*. The metal has oxidized to that particular orange-red that only iron oxide in high moisture achieves—not rust exactly, but rust in its full flower, blooming. The metal has cracked in places, and from those cracks vegetation emerges with the certainty of something that has been doing this work for a very long time.

The metal object is being *integrated*. Tendrils have threaded through the rivets. Moss clings to the exposed surfaces in patches of violent green. Something flowering—small pale blooms—has rooted along the upper curve. The structure maintains its form, but barely. It's become a trellis for something that no longer needs its original function. The slow rusting is part of the process. The decay and the growth are occurring at similar rates, a perfect balance. The machine is being *claimed*, has been claimed, will continue being claimed across a timeline that extends far beyond any human measure of usefulness.

Sound exists here, but it's muffled. Water sounds mostly—the slight trickle from wall-seeps, the drip-drip-drip from somewhere unseen, drops striking leaves with that thin plastic patter. Occasionally a creaking: the metal structure settling, a vine shifting under its own weight, the slow giving of the compressed mat-floor. These sounds arrive through the humid air as though traveling through cloth. They don't echo. Nothing echoes here.

The air *moves*, but barely. There's a slow circulation—perhaps air entering from the side passage, moving through the space with the patience of water, exiting somewhere you can't see. It carries humidity in both directions. Condensation forms on the cooler metal surfaces, gathers, runs down like tears. The warm aspect of this place is misleading; it's only warm relative to the deeper earth. The actual sensation is cool and damp and timeless.

Life here is sparse in terms of *creatures*. You see no insects moving except occasionally—a single fly, investigating. No birds. No larger animals. The absence of animals is notable, as though they've learned to avoid this place, or cannot survive in its particular saturation. What life exists is *botanical*, total, oceanic in its completeness. The plants have arranged themselves into an ecosystem so full that there seems no room for anything else. The plants *are* the place. They are its weather, its architecture, its purpose, its future.

Time moves differently here. You cannot see rapid change, though if you watched the metal structure long enough—across years, decades—you would witness its slow return to earth. The vines thicken by fractions visible only across seasons. The water seeps at a constant rate, patient as geology. A single fallen frond decomposes through phases that might take a season or a century; here they seem equivalent. The place exists in a kind of botanical stasis, not static but *slow*, operating at a rhythm that doesn't match human impatience.

You could stay. The warmth is adequate. The moisture is constant. The vegetation presses close enough to feel like walls, like safety, like a guarantee that outside is far away and held at distance by this green interface. The metal structure—whatever it meant to do, whoever built it, what purpose it served—is here only as memory, as material, as the last skeleton of a previous age being soft-ly consumed by a present that has no interest in speed.

The place has achieved its form. The balance holds. Tomorrow it will be almost identical. In fifty years it will be unrecognizable. In either timeframe, nothing here hurries.
