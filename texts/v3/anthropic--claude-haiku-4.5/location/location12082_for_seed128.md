# The Cistern

You enter through a membrane—not quite a door, more a threshold where the air itself grows thick and acquiesces. The transition is immediate: temperature spikes, humidity becomes tactile, and sound arrives like a physical thing.

This is a cistern, though not one built to hold water. It holds *sound* instead—or perhaps the sound here is merely what water does when engineered beyond its molecular nature.

The space is small enough that you could touch three walls simultaneously if you stretched. The floor is not floor but a dense lattice of synthetic black material—something between carbon and organic polymer, woven so tightly that light dies in its depths. Beneath it, visible where the lattice widens fractionally, lies water: not pooling, but *alive*, luminescent with engineered bioluminescence—diatoms that have been modified across thirteen generations to emit in frequencies that make your teeth hum.

The walls rise perhaps eight meters. They are not smooth. Every surface is textured with what first appears to be corrosion, then reveals itself as intentional: ridges and valleys in a fractal pattern, each one ribbed to channel and amplify. The material is something synthetic and ancient-looking, kept perpetually damp by condensation that doesn't bead but films—a weeping that suggests the walls themselves are porous, breathing, semi-alive. Or perhaps they're simply engineered to perfection by a civilization that forgot why they built this place.

The sound is everywhere. 

It enters as a low thrumming—not felt, but *known*—a frequency that seems to originate from the engineered water below. As you acclimate, you perceive layers: a bass resonance that might be mechanical pumps (you see no pumps, but the cistern is surely serviced by systems hidden in the walls' thickness); a middle register of organic clicking and pulsing from within the water itself; and a high, almost crystalline keening that the synthetic walls emit as they flex microscopically with thermal currents.

The water itself is dense with life—but life reassembled. You glimpse (though the light is poor, and perhaps you're meant not to see clearly) shapes moving in the bioluminescent murk. Fish, or what fish have become: elongated, translucent things with no visible eyes, navigating by the sound they seem to be constantly making. Smaller organisms—crustacean-derived, certainly—swarm along the lattice floor, their movements synchronized to the ambient frequency in a way that suggests they are not finding food so much as *performing* the cistern's operating frequency, keeping it tuned.

The vegetation is minimal but architectural. Engineered ferns—their fronds modified to trap and redistribute the condensing moisture—cling to the highest portions of the walls, their growth arrested at human-head height. They are neither alive nor dead in any way you've learned to define these states. They photosynthesize, but their light source appears to be the water's luminescence reflected through fractal geometry. They grow, but very slowly, and their biomass adds to the density of presences without consuming much oxygen.

The atmosphere tastes of mineral, salt, and something chemical—not unpleasant, but specific. Breathing here, you notice the air has texture. It seems to carry suspended particles, or perhaps your lungs are simply reading the sound as sensation. The humidity is complete; your clothes begin to cling within moments.

Time behaves oddly. The sound cycles—you notice this gradually. A peak in the thrumming every thirty seconds or so, during which the bioluminescence brightens by a fraction. The creatures in the water respond, their movements quickening in synchrony, then settling. You realize: this is a system. The whole cistern is a machine for some purpose you don't understand, and the life within it (or the constructed-life, or the life-that-is-also-engineered-function) is both the mechanism and the fuel.

The walls continue to weep. Droplets form in specific locations—on the ridges of the fractal texture—and run downward in channels you now perceive as deliberate, routing water toward subtle intakes that feed back into the lattice below. Nothing is wasted. Everything cycles.

You notice the walls are marked with symbols—or are they marks? They might be where the material has been repaired, or where it has failed and been re-integrated. Lines of slightly different material, healing-scar lines, but they also seem arranged. A language, perhaps, written in the geometry of remediation.

The scale of this place is disorienting. It's intimate—hand-width distances in some directions, close enough to the sound-source that you feel it as pressure—yet layered. The walls are thick. You sense depth behind them. Other chambers, perhaps. Other cisterns arranged in series, or the infrastructure that feeds this one. You are embedded within something much larger that you're experiencing through this single narrow aperture.

The temperature sits at the edge of comfort. Warm enough that your skin beads with sweat, but the sweat evaporates immediately in the saturated air, evaporation rendered impossible yet somehow still occurring. Equilibrium suspended in a state that should not be sustainable.

Nothing here is natural, and yet nothing appears forced. The engineered water *wants* to be here. The creatures *want* to pulse in rhythm. The walls *want* to weep and resonate. The whole mechanism hums with a purpose so old, so embedded in its structure, that purpose and nature have become synonyms.

You could stay. The sound is almost restful once you stop resisting it. Almost like hearing your own blood's ancient rhythm played back on instruments designed before your species learned to walk upright.

Almost.
