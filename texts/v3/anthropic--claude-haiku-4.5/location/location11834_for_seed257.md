# The Thermal Deeps

Water fills everything here—not as ocean, but as something thinner and more persistent. A perpetual warm rain descends through absolute darkness, each droplet falling perhaps a fingerbreadth per hour, so fine and warm it hardly feels wet. The air itself is saturated past saturation, a distinction without difference. You breathe water; you drink air. The temperature holds at body-warmth, never changing, which after a time becomes its own kind of cold—the cold of stasis, of something held in suspension forever.

The walls are close. This is a cathedral hollowed from nothing, a constructed chamber perhaps thirty paces across and half again as tall, though the darkness makes measurement uncertain. Every surface—floor, walls, vault above—has been dressed by hand into blocks and fitted without mortar. The stone is black and porous, volcanic or synthetic, warm to the touch. The seams between blocks run so tightly that water beads along them before dripping downward. The craftsmanship is ancient and meticulous, the work of people who expected to never finish and never to leave.

Sound exists here only as theory. Water falls in absolute silence. The creatures—and there are creatures—make no noise either as they move through the rain. You become aware of them gradually: a slight displacement of the falling water, a shadow that doesn't quite match the shadows around it, a presence sensed rather than seen. Fish-things, or things that approximate fish. They are legion.

They hang in the warm rain like held breath, moving with profound slowness, barely distinguishable from the water itself. Their bodies are translucent and columnar, segmented, rimmed with what might be gills or organs of absorption. They drift in patterns that repeat across spans of years, if time can be said to move here at all. Thousands of them, nearly motionless, suspended in the thermal column. Some are no larger than a thumb. Others stretch three or four hands long. They do not school or hunt. They simply *are*, maintained in this place by currents too slow to perceive, fed by something in the water itself—minerals, perhaps, or the slow breakdown of the constructed world.

The floor is layered with sediment: the accumulated drift of decades or centuries. It is not soft. The builders' blocks lie beneath a fine silt that compacts under pressure into something almost stone-like. In this sediment, in crevices along the walls, in the spaces between the slow-fish, are smaller things. Organisms that resemble nothing: crystalline structures with frayed edges, nodular masses that might be fungal or might be chemical precipitate given a semblance of form. They neither grow nor diminish, as far as can be observed. They simply continue, as permanent as the blocks themselves.

The darkness is total except for one thing: a faint bioluminescence from some of the creatures and the mineral deposits. Not light, exactly. More like the memory of light, a phosphorescence so dim it might be imaginary—but consistent, unchanging, enough to let your eyes adjust to a permanent twilight that is never quite daylight. This glow is cool-blue and ancient, suggesting that whatever biological process sustains it has been running for an incomprehensibly long time at the same steady output.

The air tastes of minerals—salt, iron, something sulfurous buried beneath warmth. Breathing it is neither difficult nor easy; it is simply what breathing is here. Your lungs do not labor. Neither do they expand fully. Everything in this place exists in a regime of minimal expenditure.

The walls, if you approach them, show marks: deliberate grooves and channels carved into the stone in no pattern you can recognize. Tool marks. Hand's breadth grooves, made with implements of copper or bone, repeated in thousands across the surface. Whether they are decorative, functional, or something else—a calendar, a record, marks of obsession—is impossible to determine. The water has not worn them smooth, but neither are they sharp. They are settled into their own age.

Nothing decays here. Nothing grows. The space is hermetically complete—no hint of passage in or out, no tide, no exchange with whatever lies beyond. The rain falls endlessly into water that never pools, never drains. The creatures maintain their thousand-year suspension. The sediment layers thicken with the patience of geology. The blocks endure.

And in this perfect, airless quiet—perfect and silent as the dark—time moves like the falling water: imperceptibly, inevitably, forever.
