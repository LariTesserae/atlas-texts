# The Ascending Dry

The place announces itself first as *elevation*—not as a mountain you climb toward, but as a fait accompli you're already inside. The ground here doesn't rise gradually. It exists at altitude as its base condition. When you look outward from any vantage point, the landscape extends horizontally across impossible distances, but all of it sits high. The air is thin enough that breathing requires consciousness. Your lungs know the difference.

The terrain is mineral. Not stone in the way you might imagine—not granite, not marble. This is something older and stranger: a matrix of crystalline salts layered with oxides that have oxidized so completely they've become inert, stable, resigned. The surface feels almost glassy, though not smooth. Weathering has carved it into a landscape of low ridges and shallow basins, all of it worn into a kind of stony equilibrium that has held for geological ages. The colors are muted—ochre, pale gray, hints of purple where the light catches iron compounds at certain angles. Nowhere is the ground bright. Everything has been leached of saturation by time and the absence of water.

Water left here a very long time ago. You can feel its absence the way you feel a missing tooth. The salts wouldn't exist this way if water hadn't moved through them once, hadn't crystallized them out, hadn't laid them down in strata. But that process is finished. There's no wetness here, no moisture in any usable sense. The air is desiccated—it pulls moisture from exposed skin, from the insides of your mouth. The rare times a mist rises from the ground (at what must be dawn, though the light cycle here is strange), it's a mineral mist, not water vapor. It carries the taste of salt and something else: old chemistry, the smell of elements completing their slow reactions.

The sky above is difficult to read. Not truly dark, but not bright either. The light has a quality of filtering—as though it passes through something fine and dispersing before it reaches the ground. There's a sun, certainly, but it casts no sharp shadows. The illumination is even, almost sourceless, which makes the landscape feel enclosed even though it extends to a genuine horizon in all directions. The color of the sky is a washed-out blue that sits somewhere between white and gray. At the edges of your perception, at the limits of the landscape, the sky seems to *pulse* very slightly—not rhythmically, but as though the boundary between air and space above is unstable, semi-permeable, deciding moment by moment whether to let light through.

And the settlements exist here.

They are not built. This is important. They are *arranged*—organized into geometric patterns that respect the terrain's own logic without imposing force against it. Structures rise in clusters across the lowlands between ridge-lines, and they are constructed from the material at hand: blocks of the same crystalline salt-stone, fitted without mortar, stacked into low towers and connecting walls that follow the contours of the ground. These buildings don't climb; they spread. The largest settlements sprawl across valleys in branching patterns, like frozen rivers of stone. The architecture is repetitive, modular, based on units that repeat. No two towers are identical, but they're all built from the same vocabulary. Windows—if they can be called that—are openings, not transparent, just openings. Doorways at ground level are rare. Access is by stairs, ramps, sloping surfaces carved into the bedrock itself.

No one is building anything new. The settlements have the quality of *completion*—not abandoned, but finished, settled into their forms like sediment that has finally stopped moving. The most remarkable structures are the oldest ones, where centuries or millennia of wind erosion have smoothed their edges, rounded their corners, made them look almost organic, as though the stone has learned to grow. Newer sections (though "newer" might mean merely four or five hundred years) still retain their sharp lines, their clear geometry. But nothing is under active construction. There are no loose materials, no scaffolding, no signs of repair. Things break down at the pace the mountain-plateau allows them to break down, and they are endured in their broken state.

The population is sparse beyond any natural distribution. You might walk for hours between encountering a human presence. When you do, it's usually singular: a figure at a vantage point, watching the landscape. Sometimes small groups gather at nodes in the settlement network—nodes that seem to be determined by some logic you can't immediately parse. Geometry? Resource location? An older infrastructure? The people themselves are tall, adapted—their skin has a translucent quality to it, almost pale, and they move without haste. They don't speak loudly. Their voices, when you hear them, seem to come from a distance even when they're close. They're engaged in activities that take time: slow fabrication of small objects, maintenance of the stone structures, arrangement of materials in patterns you suspect have significance. No one seems to rush. No one seems to have anywhere urgent to be.

This makes sense. Time here moves differently. Not faster, not slower—differently. The light cycle doesn't match any standard day. The seasons, if they exist, are imperceptible in any yearly span. Instead, time seems to operate on a longer scale. Changes happen, but they're geological. A building might slump slightly over decades. A salt formation might develop new crystalline patterns as its molecular structure equilibrates. Weather exists, but it's abstract: the mist rising in the morning, a wind that comes from no clear direction and carries mineral dust, occasional condensation that forms patterns on the glass-smooth surfaces of stone. Nothing you'd call rain. Nothing you'd call a storm. Just slow, methodical alteration.

The ecology here is the strange part. There's no plant life, no recognizable fauna in the traditional sense. But the place isn't dead. It's occupied by processes—by things that move through the mineral matrix, by reactions that are ongoing and complex. Sometimes the stone itself seems to shift when you're not looking directly at it. Small areas of the salt formations will change color imperceptibly, as though something is working through them, consuming the mineral structure and excreting subtly different compounds. There are creatures, possibly, but they're not animals as you'd recognize them. They might be symbiotic with the stone itself, or they might *be* a form of the stone, animated by some chemistry or process you don't have a framework for. You see evidence of them: tracks in the mineral dust that lead nowhere, marks on the formations that suggest something has fed, patterns that arrange and rearrange themselves over the course of days.

The silence is near-absolute. There's a hum sometimes, very faint, that might be the wind moving through hollows in the rock or might be something generated by the processes occurring within the mineral matrix itself. It's so quiet that your own breathing becomes overwhelming, and after a while you learn to regulate it, to make it part of the ambient sound rather than a disruption. Occasionally something *shifts*—a settling of stones, a crack appearing in a crystalline formation—and the sound travels far across the empty air, sharp and isolating. The people make almost no noise as they move through the settlements. No foot-falls, no conversation above whispers. It's as though the landscape has taught them that sound here is precious, that each noise carries too much weight.

The rules of the place are becoming clear only slowly. There's an equilibrium being maintained. The settlements exist at specific densities, in specific configurations. The population remains stable—no births you've observed, but no deaths you've witnessed either. The stone provides everything needed: shelter, material, and somehow food, though you can't determine how the inhabitants derive nutrition from the crystalline matrix. There's no agriculture, no storage of preserved foods, no evidence of hunting. Yet they persist. They maintain. They watch the landscape with an attention that suggests they're monitoring something crucial, keeping some balance that must not tip too far in any direction.

And sometimes, especially at the edge of sleep, you feel the wrongness that undergirds everything. The light doesn't behave quite right. The horizon has a texture to it that suggests it's not a natural curve but something *drawn*, something that's being actively rendered. The sky's pulsing becomes more pronounced when you're not looking at it directly—you see it in peripheral vision, a rhythm that has no clear frequency. The people's stillness isn't peaceful; it's patient in a way that suggests they're waiting for something, or maintaining something, or preventing something. The very structure of reality here feels thin, as though the minerals underfoot are doing more than just existing—they're holding something in place.

The place is old beyond measure. The place is stable beyond all natural stability. The place is occupied by processes that follow rules you haven't learned yet, that don't map to any ecology you've studied. But it is *active*. It is maintaining itself through some mechanism that's continuous, invisible, fundamental. And you're inside it now, and the longer you stay, the more you suspect that the settlement arrangements, the human presence, the entire culture that's organized itself here—it's all part of the same process. The people aren't separate from the landscape. They're maintaining it. Or the landscape is maintaining them.

Both, perhaps.

The cool air moves, carrying salt and something crystalline. In the distance, a structure gleams faintly as the light shifts. And time, on the Ascending Dry, continues at its own strange pace.
