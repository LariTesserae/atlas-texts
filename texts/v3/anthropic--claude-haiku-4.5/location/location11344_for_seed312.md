# The Aerie of Keth

The city clings to a plateau that shouldn't exist—a flat-topped mountain rising from mist-filled valleys, its edges so recently carved by water and weather that they still hold the fractured, almost aggressive geometry of fresh stone. The cliff faces are maybe two thousand feet of vertical limestone, pale and scored with parallel striations where water has only just begun its patient work of dissolution. From below, the city reads as a tawny scrawl across the plateau's face, but up here, standing in it, you understand: Keth is *layered*.

The buildings don't climb the slopes so much as they *stack*—stone upon stone, generation upon generation, with almost no vertical separation. Terraces merge into courtyards which become rooflines which become streets of a higher tier. The masonry is crude by choice or necessity; blocks are fitted tight but without mortar, relying on weight and geometry. You can see daylight through some gaps if the angle is right, and in the cold hours before dawn, thin mist curls through these gaps like the city is exhaling. The architecture shows no specialization: storage looks like dwelling looks like workshop. Everything serves multiple purposes because space is precious in ways it isn't elsewhere.

The moisture here is *contained*, *personal*, almost miserly. There's a river—you sense it before you see it, a ribbon of sound that never quite reaches full voice—that runs through a cut in the plateau's heart, dividing the city into western and eastern districts. The crossing is narrow, deliberate. Water wheels turn slowly there, their rhythm so patient they seem barely to turn at all, though they've been operating for long enough that the stone beneath them has worn into a shallow groove. The water itself is cold enough that steam rises from it even in midday; it carries a mineral smell, faintly sulfurous. The plateau sits on some threshold—too high for the warmth of the lowlands, too high for water to fall as rain with any frequency. Instead: dew. Fog that condenses on the city's many stone surfaces and collects in cisterns, in carved channels, in the depressions of centuries-old wells. The residents have designed everything to *catch* moisture. Roof angles guide it downward. Courtyards angle gently toward collection pools. Even the old stones are worn into subtle channels, so worn that you have to look carefully to know they're intentional.

The vegetation is almost incidental. Sparse, yes—but present with a kind of stubborn specificity. Lichen covers perhaps a third of the stone, running in bands where moisture is most reliable. There's a particular gray-green type that only grows on north-facing walls; it's so abundant there that the western district reads as pale from a distance, the eastern as gray-green. Grasses grow in the courtyards, tough stuff with deep roots, and in the spring they produce small flowers—white, efficient, joyless. The residents let them be. There are gardens, but they're maintained with an almost monastic discipline: herbs in stone beds, vegetables in raised plots with walls that double as seating. No waste of land on beauty. One district has fruit trees—ancient things, gnarled, that must have been planted before the current terracing system, because the city has built around them rather than removing them. They produce something like apples, small and dense and sharp.

The animals seem to outnumber the people, or at least to occupy space more conspicuously. Birds, primarily—cliff-nesters that have learned to use the city's ledges and cornices as substitute crags. Their calls are sharp in the thin air, echoing off stone, creating an impression of more birds than actually exist. There are pale raptors that hunt them. Insects swarm in the brief warm hours—gnats around the river, moths that emerge at dusk and seem to move through the city with purpose, heading somewhere. In the older terraces, where the stone is most eroded and weathered, there are animals that make homes in the gaps: rodents, small things that leave tracks in the dust. The plateau is ringed with goats, wild or semi-wild, that graze on the meager slopes beyond the city's edge. Their bells—simple bronze—create a background frequency that most residents probably stop hearing after childhood but which never really leaves the air.

The city is *populous*. Not crowded in the way a valley city might be, but dense with presence. You can't walk twenty paces without intersecting someone's path or seeing evidence of their recent passage: a child's toy in a corner, laundry draped on a line strung between buildings, fresh soot around a cooking fire. The streets are narrow—sometimes wide enough for two people if they're friendly, sometimes reducing to passages between walls where you have to turn sideways. The narrowness is deliberate: it keeps the warmth in, keeps the wind from pressing too hard, reduces the amount of sky you have to work against. The architecture reads as introvert, withdrawn, conscious of its own fragility.

There is work visible everywhere, but it's slow work. A mason repointing a wall; the work will take weeks. Someone mending a roof by replacing tiles one at a time—this is how it's done here, never wholesale repair, but incremental maintenance that might take a generation. You see spinners working wool in courtyards—the raw material comes from the goats beyond the plateau—and the wheels turn at a pace that suggests meditation rather than production quota. A blacksmith's forge burns with a steady flame, but the smith himself sits idle much of the time, waiting for work. There's no sense of hurry.

The silence is its own presence. The wind, when it comes—and it comes often, sliding over the plateau's edge like something testing the boundary—creates a sound like breath held. The river, again: present but not loud, a kind of white noise that becomes audible only when you consciously attend to it. Birdsong punctuates rather than fills. The sounds of human activity—hammering, voices, footsteps on stone—seem to disappear quickly into the thin air, absorbed by the altitude and the stone in equal measure. People speak quietly here, as though any raise in volume would be an offense against the landscape. Even children playing maintain a kind of restrained energy.

The light is *clear*. Thin air means that shadows hold detail rather than pooling into black; you can see into them. But the sun, at this elevation, has an edge that it lacks elsewhere—it doesn't warm so much as *illuminate*. The stone holds cold through most of the day. In the morning, frost still visible in the deepest recesses of the masonry; by afternoon, perhaps some warmth on a south-facing wall. The dusk comes early, the dawn late. The stars, when visible, are excessive in their number.

The city's rhythm is slow, synced to seasons and decades rather than days. This is a place designed to endure, not to thrive. The buildings are constructed as though they expect to last centuries without major intervention. There's no maintenance schedule written down anywhere, but there's a collective memory of when this or that section was last worked on: twenty years ago, fifty years ago, the generation before last. The cycles of stone and weather are longer here than in the world below. A cliff-face might shed a section once every two centuries. A building might require substantial repair once in three generations. The erosion is young—this plateau is still finding its final shape—but the people have already learned to live at the pace erosion sets.

The city tastes, when you put your tongue to your cheek, of minerals. The air is thin enough to make breathing conscious work for a newcomer; locals have chests adapted to it, and they move with a kind of efficient economy. They don't hurry because hurrying at this altitude is costly. The architecture, the pace, the quiet—all of it is an agreement with the place, a negotiation with the altitude and the sparse resources. It is not comfortable. It is sustainable.
