# The Terraces of Keshem

The first thing you notice—before sight clarifies—is the *sound*. A vast pneumatic breathing, irregular and mechanical. Somewhere in the massive fog-banks above, industrial pumps cycle through their regimen: *thump-hiss, thump-hiss*—the rhythm never quite settles into predictability. The sound travels strangely here, bouncing off wet vegetation, diffusing through layers of mist. It has been the same sound, in the same register, for longer than anyone visiting can quite remember.

The landscape exists in tiers, though you don't grasp this immediately. What you see first is simply: *green*. Everywhere. Every surface laden with it—a moss so dense and ancient it has developed a structure all its own, a felt-like substrate inches thick, layered in shades from lime to forest to something almost black-green in the deepest recesses. The vegetation isn't *on* the rock; the rock is a rumor beneath the plants. This moss-carpet rolls across the terrain like a vast, breathing skin.

Standing at the altitude—perhaps 4,500 meters, though the maps are old and the standard meters don't quite apply here anymore—you occupy one of the high plateaus. The elevation is genuine but the world doesn't feel high in any triumphant sense. Rather, the cold is *precise* here. Not brutal, but absolute: 3-4 degrees Celsius, year-round, as though the temperature was set and has held. Your breath comes out in clean clouds. The air itself tastes mineral and living at once.

The water is ubiquitous. Not falling now, but *present*—suspended, saturating. The ground beneath the moss is perpetually weeping. Every handful of vegetation you might gather would drip for hours if disturbed. The air carries so much moisture that the distinction between rain and atmosphere has eroded away; there's simply a state of constant aqueous presence. Somewhere far above—beyond the low cloud ceiling that has its own weather system, its own winds—rain must be falling still. It always is, here. Water flows down through the moss matrix in patterns so slow they are barely change, seeping downslope toward edges you cannot see.

And yet the whole system appears *established*. Ancient. Balanced. The moss doesn't seem to be rapidly colonizing or dying back; it simply *is*, in a steady state that has endured through whatever climate-shifts might have brought it to this condition. The plants exist in a kind of temporal stasis—not frozen, but unchanging in any observable timeframe. Patterns visible at ground level—the way certain mosses form concentric rings, the way liverworts have arranged themselves into nearly crystalline arrays—suggest that nothing has disturbed this equilibrium for a very long time. Decades, at minimum. Centuries, more likely.

The industrial element asserts itself as the fog shifts. The pumps you hear are industrial-grade, rusted but functional. They were installed—when?—at intervals along the plateau edge, partially obscured by vegetation but still operative. Their purpose becomes clear: they are drawing water up from the depths below, from reservoirs kept at these heights to maintain the conditions. The moss cannot exist here without the constant moisture supply; the pumps ensure the water cycle continues, mechanized, algorithmic, non-negotiable. Somewhere, there is a facility managing this. The plants don't know it. They simply live in the conditions the machinery maintains.

The scale of it becomes apparent only through duration. You might walk for hours across the terraces and feel as though you've made no progress through the landscape. The moss-covered ground slopes with such gentleness that horizontal and vertical become theoretical concepts. The plateaus step down in vast increments—each terrace might be a kilometer across, separated from its neighbor by slopes so shallow they read as almost-flat. But if you could see from outside—if you could leave and return—you'd recognize that these terraces represent a vertical descent of thousands of meters, arranged across a land area that has its own weather patterns, its own wind-shadows and cold-pockets and zones of particular saturation.

The vegetation, while seemingly total, maintains a particular density. The moss *is* thick, but there are spaces. A bryophyte here, a low lichen there—not competing but existing in some understood hierarchy. Occasionally, hardy flowering plants push through: small, flowering shrubs with leaves adapted to this environment, with blooms that appear almost metallic in the ambient light. They are sparse enough that you notice each one, can count them across a given stretch. There is no jungle-claustrophobia here, despite the vegetative total coverage. The plants have made an agreement with the space: we will fill it, but we will not crowd it.

The animal life is present but subdued. Small arthropods, mostly. Insects adapted to the perpetual damp and cool. You might catch sight of a mountain bird—a ptarmigan, perhaps, or something related—perched on a rock-outcrop where the moss has worn thin. Occasionally, larger shapes move in the middle-distance, within the fog: possibly mountain goats, possibly something the ecosystem has devised on its own. The animals here seem to move slowly, unhurried, as though they are operating on a different timescale from the rest of the world.

The light—it's normal light, technically, but it reads differently. It comes from above, filtered through the cloud layer, diffused through moisture-saturated air, and reflected from countless millions of plant-surfaces. It's not dim, but it's *soft*, even at what would normally be high-sun hours. Shadows are gentled, almost absent. The entire landscape has a quality of luminous gray-green, with darker accents where the terrain dips or the vegetation crowds thicker. Colors are muted; distinctions are made by tone rather than hue.

The sounds layer beneath the pump-rhythm. Water moving through soil, barely perceptible, a kind of settling whisper. Wind, high and remote, passing over the landscape without disturbing it much. The occasional animal call—thin, attenuated, calling across distances. Insects, in aggregate, making a sound that is almost electronic, a kind of faint white-noise hum that might be the sound of respiration itself.

The weirdness arrives gradually. It's not obvious at first. But the longer you remain here, the more you begin to notice the *wrongness* of equilibrium. Nothing should be this stable. Ecosystems oscillate, seasons turn, populations boom and crash. But this place has achieved a state that feels *enforced*—as though the industrial machinery overhead (or below, or distributed through the system) is actively maintaining a singular condition. The moss grows at a precise rate. The water flows at calibrated speeds. The animals appear, do what they do, and vanish back into the mist with a regularity that begins to seem planned. The temperature never varies. The light never changes its quality.

You begin to wonder if you are within something or if something is within you—whether the entire system, including yourself, has been absorbed into a larger apparatus that is content to continue indefinitely in its current state. The vegetation seems to know something. The pump-sounds continue, steady and implacable. The moss grows. The mist persists. Time becomes speculative.

And it is, you realize, *beautiful*. Cool. Established. Vast. Alive in every direction, alive with a patience that asks nothing and changes nothing. A place at equilibrium, maintained—perhaps deliberately, perhaps inevitably—in a state between growth and decline, between flooding and drought, between the living world and whatever machinery has learned to live alongside it.

The pumps continue. The moss breathes. And the terraces step down toward a horizon you cannot see.
