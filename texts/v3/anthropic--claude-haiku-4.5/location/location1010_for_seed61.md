# The Galleries Below Harth

The deepest quarters of the settlement exist in caverns no one alive remembers excavating. They are simply *there*, as though the stone itself permitted habitation the way a shell permits the creature within.

Water is the primary architecture here. Not flooding—something more intimate. Every surface weeps. The limestone walls glisten with a constant seepage that has shaped itself into channels over centuries, maybe longer. The moisture condenses on cooler stone and runs in thin, permanent threads down every vertical face. The air itself feels like breathing through cloth. Your clothes begin to dampen within minutes of descent. The stone floor, ancient beyond reckoning, has worn smooth in places from the passage of barefoot traffic and the eternal slide of water toward the lowest points. Pools gather in depressions—not stagnant, but slowly circulating. Their surfaces are mirror-still, and you don't look too long into them.

The cold here is bone-specific. Not brutal. Settled. It has been this temperature for so long that the distinction between air and stone seems almost academic. Your breath becomes visible immediately; it becomes part of the permanent fog that layers the vaulted ceiling six, seven meters overhead.

The streets—because they are streets, arranged in a rough grid by centuries of intention—are cramped. Built structures of dry-stacked stone rise against the cavern walls and in isolated clusters across the floor, leaving only narrow channels between them. The buildings are *dense*, compressed. They share walls with their neighbors as though warmth is a resource to be pooled. Doorways are low and offset; windows are rare. Hand-worked stone blocks, fitted without mortar, create structures that seem almost organic in their settling—nothing is quite level. Rooflines are irregular, stacked, terraced. Some buildings lean toward the cavern wall; others incline inward toward a central spine of what might have been intentional, or might have simply happened.

Every surface is carved. Not decorated—carved with purpose. Numbering systems, territorial marks, ownership signs worked into stone with repeated strikes of hand hammers. Channels cut deliberately for water management, creating a subterranean hydraulic system that has operated unchanged for generations. The carved marks themselves are weathered to softness; the edges have surrendered to water and time. This is a place where change happens so slowly it becomes indistinguishable from permanence.

The sound is unending. Not a roar—more a saturation. Water is the primary noise: it drips, runs, trickles, echoes off stone. The cavern ceiling multiplies every sound. A drip becomes a rhythm. A rivulet becomes a presence. But beneath and through that aquatic susurration runs human noise—voices calling across the stone corridors, the metallic ringing of hand tools against rock, the scrape of stone on stone as structures shift infinitesimally. A smith works somewhere in the middle distance, and the hammer-strikes create a metronomic counterpoint to the water-song. Someone is singing or chanting, the words indecipherable, the melody warped by cavern acoustics into something almost liturgical.

The fauna is scarce. No birds, obviously. Occasionally you see small pale creatures—blind fish in the deeper pools, insect-things that seem more rumor than substance. They are so rare that spotting one feels significant, like a sign. The residents speak of them sparingly. There is a sense that the deep places are not entirely *for* creatures, that life here exists on sufferance.

The light is a conspiracy of inadequacy. Some ventilation shafts miles above allow thin gray daylight to descend, but it becomes so diffused by distance and cavern geometry that it barely qualifies as illumination. Mostly the Galleries run on torches—pine fat or rendered animal oil, mounted in iron sconces that have been rusted to a particular stable brown for longer than anyone can measure time. The torches create a scattered, amber inefficiency. You can see your immediate surroundings clearly enough—the face of the person next to you, the texture of stone within arm's reach—but beyond that, distance becomes uncertain. The fog of condensation catches the light and creates its own local glow, soft and diffuse, so that far walls appear to glow faintly from within the stone itself.

The air smells of mineral seepage, something faintly metallic, the constant wet-stone smell of caves. There is no rot here—nothing decays in the traditional sense in this cold, damp, lightless place. Instead there is the smell of patience, of processes that move on timescales the living cannot quite track.

The settlement seems entirely self-contained. It does not feel provisional. The stone buildings are not temporary shelters but permanent assertions, built with the expectation of centuries of habitation. The carved marks on walls indicate family territories, water-rights, storage boundaries—a social structure that has calcified into stone itself. The residents move through the corridors with the certainty of those who have never needed to leave, who navigate by a knowledge so deep it never rises to consciousness.

There is something almost alive about the place, though not alive in the way the upper world recognizes life. It is alive the way a crystal lattice might be said to be alive—organized, stable, following rules that are not written anywhere because they are written into the very structure of the stone. The water flows in the same channels it has always flowed. The residents build their homes in the same way generation after generation. The tools are the same. The carved marks accumulate but do not change in character.

And nothing—nothing—suggests this will ever be different. The place is in such complete equilibrium with itself that even the idea of change seems to be repelled by the cold stone walls.
