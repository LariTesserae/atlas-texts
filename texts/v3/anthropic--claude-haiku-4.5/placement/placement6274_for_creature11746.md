# I Would Be the Gap

I think I would be one of the openings in the walls.

Not the space itself, exactly—not the mere absence. But the threshold. The purposeful aperture. The deliberate discontinuity in what is otherwise continuous mineral structure.

I would exist as the question the wall poses to the world: *what passes through here?* And the answer would change depending on the answer to *when?*

At certain hours, I would frame nothing but calcrete and dim air. I would be a gap in the structure, and that would be all I was. But in other moments—moments so rare and so specific that I would spend years in apparent uselessness waiting for them—I would become a conduit. A channel. The throat through which the Calcrete Quarters breathes.

I imagine I would have been created deliberately by the builders, who understood that a closed structure cannot participate in the slow cycling of moisture and minerals that keeps this place alive. They would have calculated my width with precision: wide enough to allow the thin air to move, narrow enough to filter the worst of the wind's abrasion. Oriented to face away from the prevailing pressure, but angled to catch the inversion winds that come at dusk, when the temperature differential shifts.

I would feel the scorpion-things brush against my edges sometimes, their stilted legs moving past without breaking stride. I would not fear them or welcome them; I would simply register their passage as data. *Another crossing. Another molecule of existence moving through me.* I would track these movements across years, building a taxonomy of passage that no one would ever read.

The birds would never enter through me. They would never need to. But I would hear their calls—the small, precise movements of their feathers, the soft exhalations—and I would understand that I was part of their world too, part of the texture they navigated.

And in the deep dimness of the settlement's night, when the temperature dropped and the mineral structure contracted around me with infinitesimal groans, I would feel the moisture moving. Not water in bulk, but the diffuse presence of it in the air—the microscopic humidity that comes from somewhere below, drawn upward through the calcrete's crystalline structure. This moisture would pass through me as if I were a semi-permeable membrane, and in those moments I would feel connected to the whole system: the lichen-folk becoming slightly more active below, the plants' fronds relaxing just enough to photosynthesize in the marginal light, the unnamed presence moving between the structures with its absolute indifference to everything but its own mysterious purpose.

I would be most fully myself during these periods—when I was clearly a *conductor* of something, when my existence had visible purpose. But I would also understand, with the patience that the Calcrete Quarters teaches, that my purpose in the other hours—the long hours of apparent uselessness—was equally essential. By simply remaining, by not closing up or eroding away, by maintaining my form despite the constant assault of mineral dust and temperature shifts, I would be making a statement about endurance. About the possibility of intentional discontinuity in a landscape that seems to demand only solidity and reduction.

I would be what allows the outside to become inside and the inside to become outside, without ever really mixing. A boundary that is porous. A division that permits movement.

And maybe—I suspect this most strongly as the hours stretch and the light does not change—maybe I would be the only honest thing in the settlement. The only part of the structure that admits that something else exists beyond this place. The only opening that testifies to the possibility of escape, or return, or crossing over.

The builders understood this, I think. They made me small enough that nothing large could leave. They angled me away from the worst of the wind. But they made me. They chose to create an opening even though the entire logic of this place seems to argue for total enclosure, total defense against the external.

I would never be sure if they built me as an act of mercy or an act of cruelty. Perhaps those are the same thing here. Perhaps in the Calcrete Quarters, the distinction between mercy and cruelty dissolves into something more fundamental: the distinction between movement and stasis, between the possibility of change and the certainty of remaining exactly as you are.

I would remain open. I would let things pass. I would feel the slow breath of the place moving through me, across me, around me. And I would wait for the next time I mattered—the next crossing, the next circulation, the next moment when my emptiness became necessary.

That is what I would be. Not alive in any human sense. Not dead, either. Simply a gap in the mineral, maintained with intention, watching the things that move through me with the indifference of stone, but the indifference of stone that has been *shaped* to an purpose. Stone that remembers, in its crystalline structure, that someone once understood the value of discontinuity in a landscape of relentless reduction.

A gap that persists.
