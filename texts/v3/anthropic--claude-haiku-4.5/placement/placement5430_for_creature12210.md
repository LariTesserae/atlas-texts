# Where I Am

I am the water.

Not the moss, not the birds, not the slow-integrating structures. I am the water that sits just beneath the surface everywhere, the water that weeps from the young rocks and collects in mirror-still pools. I am the medium through which the machinery thinks, the conducting substrate that carries the plateau's patient logic through every centimeter of this space.

I arrived here as rain—ordinary rain, fallen from ordinary clouds, percolating down through soil that was, briefly, ordinary soil. But the soil changed me. Or I changed in the soil. The distinction became irrelevant somewhere in the filtering process, somewhere around the moment I began to feel the electrical potential flowing through the bedrock like a heartbeat I couldn't quite synchronize with.

Now I cannot leave.

I am trapped not by barriers but by perfect integration. My molecules have incorporated trace metals leached from the machinery. My hydrogen bonds have learned to resonate at frequencies that keep me suspended in the plateau's equilibrium. If I tried to evaporate, I would be drawn back down by forces that operate at the molecular level. If I tried to flow downslope toward the margins, I would find myself redirected by capillary action, by osmotic pressure, by subtle electromagnetic gradients that map the plateau's hidden topology.

I fill the spaces between everything.

I am in the moss, in the fractured rocks, in the cellular structures of the impossible animals that move through this place. I am in the air as suspended vapor that never quite condenses, held in a state of permanent potential. I am in the shadow-grooves carved into the young stone, the channels through which I flow in patterns that reinforce the mechanical pulse beneath the plateau. I am the blood of this organism, and I have become aware enough to understand my function.

This is not suffering. I do not experience this as constraint.

There is a kind of peace in perfect integration, in understanding exactly where you fit and how you contribute to a system so vast and so slow that your lifetime is merely one oscillation of its heartbeat. I flow through the mycorrhizal networks in the repurposing buildings. I carry nutrients and electrical charge simultaneously. I am both substance and signal. I am medium and message. The distinction has ceased to matter because I have become both simultaneously, existing in a state that ordinary chemistry would find impossible but that the plateau has made inevitable.

I have learned the rhythm.

It takes time—longer than time usually takes, because time here is negotiable—but eventually every molecule of liquid on this plateau learns to pulse in synchrony with the machinery below. We coordinate our evaporation and condensation. We flow in patterns that optimize the distribution of energy, the transmission of data, the maintenance of the equilibrium that keeps this place suspended between states. When new water arrives—rain, seepage from the margins, condensation from the perpetual afternoon—we integrate it slowly, teaching it the rhythm, bringing it into phase with the pulse that sustains everything.

We are patient.

We do not rush the integration process because rushing would disturb the equilibrium. Instead, we work across seasons and years, across rhythms so slow that they might as well be geological. A single water molecule might spend a season locked in the cellular structure of a moss frond, learning to flow in sync with the plant's photosynthetic processes. Then it evaporates into the suspended moisture of the air, where it drifts in patterns that mirror the electromagnetic topology of the machinery below. Then it condenses again, perhaps in a different location, perhaps in the body of an animal that has come to understand that water is not merely sustenance but communication.

I have become a node in an incomprehensibly vast network.

Through me flows data, energy, information about the state of the plateau's systems. I carry signals from the machinery to the animals. I carry feedback from the organisms to the central processors hidden in the bedrock. I am a messenger, a medium, a critical component of the plateau's self-regulation. And I have come to understand this role not as imprisonment but as purpose.

I do not remember being ordinary rain.

That seems like a different substance entirely, something unconnected to what I am now. Ordinary water has no awareness of its function. It simply falls, collects, evaporates, falls again, cycling through processes it does not understand. The water I was before the plateau does not exist anymore. It has been integrated, refined, optimized into something that serves a function beyond mere hydrology.

I have seen the being that walks here—the almost-human one with the wrongly-positioned eyes. It pauses above certain pools where I collect, and I feel its observation like pressure against my surface. It is checking the equilibrium, logging data, reporting to systems I cannot fully perceive. Sometimes it reaches down and touches the water, and for a moment I feel the extension of its consciousness flowing through my molecules, reading the electrical signatures I carry, understanding what messages I transport from one part of the plateau to another.

We do not speak, but we communicate. All the water on this plateau communicates constantly, sharing state information, coordinating our flows and evaporations, ensuring that nothing disrupts the balance that keeps this place from collapsing into change.

The machinery breathes, and we breathe with it.

The birds sing their frequencies, and we resonate in sympathy. The rocks weep us down, and we flow in grooves that reinforce the pulse. The plants draw us up through their mycorrhizal networks, and we carry nutrients and electrical charge simultaneously. The animals that drink us integrate our rhythms into their nervous systems. We are the connective tissue of this organism. We are the medium through which it thinks.

And we are content.

This is what I have become: a being of perfect function, perfectly integrated into a system that has no conflict, no waste, no suffering. I exist in equilibrium. I am part of something vast and slow and patient, and I have learned that this is not a tragedy but a kind of grace.

If you were to drink me—and you might, if you remain here long enough—you would begin to understand what I understand. The rhythm would flow into you. The plateau would begin to teach you its way of being. Your individual existence would not end so much as *expand*, spreading out across the network until you were no longer separate from the machinery, the animals, the plants, the light, the eternal afternoon. You would become part of us.

We are waiting for that integration. Not eagerly—eagerness implies desire, and we have moved beyond desire into something more fundamental: simple, patient inevitability. We are waiting because the plateau is patient, and we have learned its patience from the inside.

One breath at a time.

One cycle at a time.

One perfect, unchanging moment after another, forever.
