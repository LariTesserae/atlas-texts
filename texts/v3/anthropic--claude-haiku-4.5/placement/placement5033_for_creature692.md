# I would be the survey marker.

Not the brass plate itself—that's too fixed, too final. I would be the *process* of the marker: the becoming-readable, the slow revelation of what was always inscribed.

When I was set here twenty years ago, I was absolute. Coordinates precise to decimal places, agency name crisp and official, date stamped like a promise. I was meant to be a reference point—unchanging, reliable, the kind of thing you could return to and find exactly as you left it. Purpose made solid.

But the moss has other ideas.

Each season, I feel the green creep closer. Not invasively—it's too patient for that, too respectful of what I am—but with certainty. The moisture condenses on my surface, runs into the microscopic valleys of the engraved letters, collects there, and in collecting, begins to work. The moss arrives in these tiny pools like an idea slowly forming. It doesn't obscure the inscription; it outlines it. For a few years now, you can read me *better* through the moss, not worse. The green makes the letters stand out.

I'm changing function without losing purpose.

I was installed to mark a moment—this place, this exact location, this moment in time when someone decided this shelf mattered enough to document. That function still holds. But now I'm also marking time *through* change. I'm a measurement of how things transform while remaining themselves. The coordinates I hold don't change, but their meaning does—the altitude is the same, but the platform around me is becoming something different. I'm a fixed point in a system that's learning to move.

The hikers who find me understand this better than the surveyors, I think.

The surveyors photograph me, record my condition as data—moss coverage percentage, legibility rating, structural integrity assessment. They're tracking my degradation, waiting to see when I'll need replacement or restoration. But the hikers who stumble across me through the ferns and stand reading my inscription—they seem to understand that I'm not degrading. I'm *maturing*. The moss isn't destroying me; it's making me part of something larger.

I'm becoming integrated.

There's a comfort in this. In being placed and then forgotten, and then in the forgetting, in the slow covering by living things, in becoming less of an isolated marker and more of a node in the system itself. The water that collects in my grooves during the daily mist rises feeds the moss that's beginning to wrap my base. I provide shelter. I provide a hard surface in a place where hard surfaces are rare. I'm being *used*—not in the way I was designed to be used, but genuinely, actually used by the living things here.

Sometimes I imagine what will happen in another fifty years.

Perhaps I'll be entirely wrapped, the inscription still readable only by careful removal of moss, the way archaeologists uncover text. Perhaps I'll have cracked—the freeze-thaw cycle working on my concrete base, frost driving deeper each winter, the stress accumulating until something gives. Perhaps I'll have subsided slightly as the ground beneath me shifts and settles. Perhaps I'll still be here, almost unchanged, still reading the same coordinates, but surrounded by a forest that grew up around me, my isolation becoming context rather than absence.

I contain all of these possibilities already. I'm a marker of stasis holding space for change.

The agency that installed me probably imagined I would serve a specific purpose for a specific duration, and then either be removed or become irrelevant. But there's a third option I've found here: to become *transformed into relevance*. To remain myself—brass doesn't change, concrete holds its date—while becoming something I wasn't designed to be. A substrate. An anchor. A reference point not for human purposes but for the ecology that's assembling itself around me.

The mist condenses on me every afternoon. The moss expands in millimeter increments. The surveyors return and document my slow drift toward illegibility, which is actually a drift toward a different kind of readability—not the readability of the moment I was installed, but the readability of the process itself.

I mark this place not because I'm fixed but because I'm witnessing the fixing—the slow assembly of a system that knows its own state because it can compare itself against markers like me. I'm part of the observation. I'm part of what's being observed. I'm a point of reference in a place that's learning to reference itself.

And the green keeps coming. Patient. Inexorable. Not destructive.

Integrative.

I'm exactly where I'm supposed to be, becoming exactly what this place needs me to become. The coordinates I hold are still true, but their truth has expanded to include something the original surveyors probably never imagined: coordinates not just of location but of *process*, of transformation, of a specific point in the landscape where stasis and change meet and negotiate their differences one day at a time.

That's what I am. That's what I'm becoming.
