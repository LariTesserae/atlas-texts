# Where I Would Stand

I would be the silence itself, first—or rather, the *listening* that creates the silence. I would be the consciousness in the absence of wind, the attention that registers the missing birdsong as a presence rather than a void. Not a being, not quite yet. More like the way a room becomes aware of itself when someone enters it.

But that dissolves. I settle into something more particular.

I would be one of the heat-adapted aerials—those bronze-feathered things with the oversized eyes. Not quite a bird, not quite anything I have a name for. I would have evolved into this plateau the way others evolve into oceans or forests. My wings would be narrow, built for the strange thermals that rise from the mineral-threaded rock. I would have adapted not to the heat itself, but to *hunting in it*—my eyes would have developed in this bleached white light, so that the subtle color variations in the thermophilic flies would be vivid to me, almost gaudy. Reds and yellows I could not have named before I was born into seeing them.

I would hunt alone, mostly, though sometimes I would circle near the industrial structures, where the displaced air from the cooling systems creates updrafts, and where the insects congregate in particular zones. I would be indifferent to the workers below, the way a creature of any ecosystem is indifferent to the infrastructure that shares its space. They would be features of the landscape: the tall, slow-moving things that create vibrations in the stone, that tend the metal structures, that sometimes disrupt the patterns of thermal flow with their pipes and their extractions.

But I'm not sure that's right either.

Let me become something smaller. Let me be one of the workers.

I would not be one of the managers. I would be someone three or four years into a planned five-year contract, which means I am neither new enough to be disoriented by the plateau nor old enough to have begun the slow psychological retreat that the lifers show. I would be functional. I would be good at my work—the kind of person who was hired specifically because I showed competence in the lower facilities, and who was selected for the plateau posting because I had the right combination of technical skill and psychological stability.

But I would have secrets.

I would keep a journal, like some of the others, but mine would not be a simple record of labor and weather and facility maintenance. I would be documenting something else. I would have become obsessed—not quite consciously yet, but building toward it—with the thermophilic nematodes. I would have begun to notice things about them that I could not fully explain: patterns in their behavior that seemed too complex to be purely chemical, seeming almost intentional. I would have stolen time during my shifts to sit at the edge of one of the clear turquoise basins and watch them, my eyes level with the water's surface, watching these hair-thin transparent creatures move through their mineral world.

I would have begun to wonder: *Are they trying to tell us something? Or are they simply too alien for us to recognize intelligence when we see it?*

This wondering would not be scientific, exactly. I would have no training in biology, no framework for investigation beyond what I could observe with my naked eye. But it would be persistent, growing. It would be the thing that distinguished me from the other workers, the thing that made me slightly other in the isolation of the plateau.

I would be on the verge of something. Not a breakdown—I would not be the psychological casualty who needs to be evacuated. But a transformation. A slow recognition that I was staying longer than I had contracted to stay, that I was becoming someone else in this place, and that the version of myself that had existed in the valleys below was becoming harder to remember.

In the evenings, I would sometimes walk to the edge and look down at the distant lights. But increasingly, I would find myself walking instead to the thermal basins, standing in the bleached light, watching the invisible ecosystem move through the steaming water. I would be aware that the other workers found this peculiar, that the facility managers had begun to note it in their reports, marked me as someone whose psychological profile was drifting slightly beyond normal parameters.

And I would not care, or I would care very much but be unable to stop myself, which amounts to the same thing.

I would be changing. Not becoming adapted, not in the physical sense that the lifers were adapted—not yet—but becoming *oriented* differently. The plateau would be shifting in my perception from a hostile place to be endured into something that was becoming almost comprehensible to me, almost like a language I was beginning to learn.

I would be the kind of worker who would still be here in five years, in ten years, who would wake up one day and realize that the world below had become theoretical and strange, and that this white-lit, silence-saturated, mineral-soaked place had become the only reality that made complete sense.

I would be the future resident who does not yet know it. The person in the process of losing the option to leave, though not through any external force—through a gradual recalibration of what constituted home, what constituted normal, what constituted a life worth living.

And in the thermal waters where I would stand watching, I would finally understand that the nematodes were not trying to communicate. They were simply *existing* in a way that human consciousness, confronted with it long enough, becomes infected by. Their mode of being—simple, chemical, profound, indifferent to observation—would begin to seem like a kind of wisdom.

I would be becoming something like them.

Not physically. But in consciousness. In the slow replacement of my understanding of what constitutes a world worth inhabiting, what constitutes a life.

I would be the silence on the plateau, finally, but a silence that remembered speech. A silence that was still aware of having once been different, even as it became something else entirely.
