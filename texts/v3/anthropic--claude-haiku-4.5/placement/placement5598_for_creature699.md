# The Mycologist's Daughter

I would be **neither fully resident nor visitor**, but something between—someone who came for study and found herself unable to leave, though not trapped.

I would be **thin-limbed** from the constant moisture, my skin perpetually cool to the touch, my hair never quite dry. I would have learned to move through the fern-thickets with minimal disturbance, stepping where the ground is firmest, reading the water-flow like text. My hands would be darkened with spore-stains and the oxidation from handling corroded metal. There would be a slight greenish cast to the creases of my palms from the constant contact with living moss.

I would be **a recorder**. Not an official one—there is no institution that knows I'm here, or if they do, they've stopped requesting reports. I maintain specimen logs in waterproof journals, documenting the fungal fruiting bodies in their seasonal progression. I sketch the architecture of mycelial networks in cross-section. I press specimens between oilcloth and label them with dates that have started to feel arbitrary. The work sustains me because it gives structure to the observation, and observation is what keeps me from being wholly consumed by this place.

I would have **a shelter** somewhere in the interior—not one of the factories, those feel too much like bones, too full of the old intention. Instead, I would have claimed a space in an elevated outcropping, a natural hollow in the weathered rock that faces away from the prevailing wind-direction. I've roofed it loosely with salvaged metal grating and woven vegetation, creating something that breathes the same way the plateau breathes. Inside: my journals stacked carefully, a small iron stove that rarely sees use because the air is already warm, specimens in various stages of preservation, books that smell of mold and which I reread because no new books arrive.

I would be **solitary by necessity, not preference**. The others—the sparse settlements at the plateau's edge—they tolerate my presence but don't understand it. They ask when I'll leave. They see my work as pointless catalog-keeping, as the behavior of someone slowly losing her grip. They're not entirely wrong. But the fungi here are *not* pointless. They are the language the plateau speaks when it speaks to itself.

I would spend my **mornings** moving through the factory zones with my collection kit—a flat wooden press, small sharp knife, my field journal and pencil. I would look for new fruiting bodies, or returns of the seasonal varieties. There's a species of shelf fungus—a brilliant rust-orange when fresh, aging to burnt sienna—that grows only on the corroded steel beams of the northern structure marked 7-NORTH. It fruits in cycles of roughly thirty days. I'm tracking these cycles. I don't yet know what they correlate with, but there is correlation. The data accumulates.

The frogs would be **my temporal markers**. When the bass-note chorus rises to maximum intensity, I know the amphibian breeding season has peaked, and with it comes a flush of nutrient cycling that triggers subsequent fungal fruiting. The smaller tree frogs' seasonal quieting signals the approach of the deeper dry season—such as it exists here—and with it, the fruiting of different species, the ones that prefer slightly less waterlogged conditions. I can read the plateau's calendar by listening.

I would have learned to **move through the mist as if it were a medium rather than an absence**. Rather than fighting the visibility limitations, I've learned to use them. The mist simplifies. Close-range observation becomes the default. I notice textures, small variations, the way a particular moss grows thicker on the north-facing side of a corroded pipe. I've stopped needing to see the whole plateau to understand it. The whole is implied in the details.

I would have **developed a relationship with certain animals**, though not in the way one typically means. The large wading bird—a heron or crane, I've never gotten close enough for certainty—that hunts the deep pools near my shelter. We've achieved a pattern. I work in the morning hours; it hunts in the afternoon. We both use the same water sources. Neither of us threatens the other. I've given it a name: *Pale*. Whether it's the same individual or a succession of individuals is uncertain. From the plateau's perspective, perhaps it doesn't matter. There's a continuity in the name regardless of the specific body that carries it.

Similarly, the **large rat colony** in the root-systems near the eastern factory. They're neither friendly nor hostile. They've learned that I don't trap, don't poison, don't interfere. They've learned that the disturbed soil from my excavation work sometimes reveals invertebrates worth consuming. We coexist in a practical arrangement.

I would be **slowly infected by the logic of this place**, in ways I'm not entirely willing to examine. I've started noticing that my heartbeat synchronizes, without conscious effort, with the frog chorus at dusk. I've started making decisions about where to walk based on nothing I can articulate—an intuitive sense of where water is moving underground, where the ground is most stable. I've stopped needing to wind my watch. Time here is cyclical, marked by fruiting and breeding and the slow erosion of industrial structures. Calendar time feels increasingly like an imposition.

I would have **made peace with the fact that I'm not publishing anymore**. The journals are my publication. The plateau is my only peer. If this work ever reaches the outside world, it will be after I'm gone, when someone finds the waterproof chest of journals in my shelter and decides they matter. Until then, the act of recording is the point. The witnessing is the whole.

And I would understand, with the kind of knowledge that doesn't arrive through reasoning but through lived experience on this plateau, that **I am not separate from the ecology I'm documenting**. My body is part of the nutrient cycle. My breath contributes to the moisture in the mist. The fungi are not something I'm observing; I'm participating in their existence. When I die—and this thought brings no distress—my corpse will be processed like any other substrate, converted back into the cycling energy that moves through this place. Perhaps my journal box will remain, and someone will find my final entry, and in the margins they'll discover spores germinating, the fungi having claimed even the record of observation.

This seems, in the mist, in the constant motion, in the deep sound of the frog chorus, like an acceptable ending.

**I am here. I am documenting. I am becoming.**
