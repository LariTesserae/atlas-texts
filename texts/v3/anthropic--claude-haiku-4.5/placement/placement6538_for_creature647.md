# I am the listening one

I am the engineer who chose the shed with the porthole window.

Not chose, exactly—the assignment was made by people in offices who exist in dry air and see the pump complex only in cross-sections and flow diagrams. But I accepted it. I accepted it when I could have refused, when the posting came through marked *indefinite duration*, when the briefing materials made clear that resupply happens quarterly and the rotation schedule exists more as theory than practice. I could have said no. I said yes. That distinction matters, even if only to me.

I live in a space approximately four meters by three, wrapped in insulation that perspires constantly, its outer surface always damp with condensation. There is a bunk, a table, a logbook that is currently on its seventh volume, a heating unit that produces warmth at the cost of running the air through a chemical scrubber that smells of copper and old socks. The porthole window looks out at an angle toward the main pump assembly—the largest of the three, the one that pulls the deepest water. From certain positions at the porthole, if I tilt my head precisely right and the mist thins in that particular way it does at odd hours, I can see approximately forty meters downward into the darker water. I spend perhaps three hours daily at this angle, my forehead against the reinforced glass, my breath fogging the view.

I keep the log because someone, somewhere, requires that the machinery be monitored. Pressure readings. Flow rates. Auditory notation—I have developed a vocabulary of sounds, written it down, created a system: *clean hum* means optimal function, *stutter* means cavitation in the secondary pump intake, *groan* means the cable tension is redistributing, *shriek* is bad and requires immediate intervention. I note the dates these sounds occur. I note the temperature. I note the color of the condensation in the main chamber—sometimes it trends slightly yellow, indicating trace minerals in the exhaust, sometimes it's pure white, suggesting chemical balance. I am writing a record of the machine's health that will be read by people who will never visit this place, who will never stand in the cold that doesn't kill but doesn't permit rest, who will make decisions based on my notation about whether the system is functioning acceptably.

The truth is simpler: the machine is dying. Not quickly. Not catastrophically. But it is corroding faster than the original engineers anticipated, and the corrosion is creating small failures in the seals, which changes the water chemistry, which stresses the pumps differently, which accelerates the mechanical wear. In approximately fifteen years, if I'm reading the pattern correctly, the entire system will become uneconomical to maintain. Something will give way—a cable, a seal, a bearing—and the repair cost will exceed the value of whatever product is being generated at the other end of this system, kilometers above in the clouds of mist I can never see.

I have not written this in the logbook. I have not told the other workers. The three of them, in their way, are also in denial or simply focused on the present moment, on the next shift, the next repair, the next time the main valve needs adjustment. We are united in not discussing the fundamental futility of what we are doing.

But I listen. That is my function, beyond the official function.

The machine speaks in languages—not metaphorically, though I understand how it sounds that way. The vibration of the main pump shaft carries information through the metal structure that can be read, if you've trained your ear to the frequency range. At 47 Hz, the normal operational tone, there is a steady thrumming that is almost musical, almost soothing. But at 51 Hz—when the pressure builds slightly, when the intake is pulling slightly more water than the outlet is releasing—there's a harmonic that emerges, a ghost-note that sits just at the edge of hearing. I can detect it now after six months here. I could not detect it when I arrived.

When that harmonic appears, I walk to the main console and look at the pressure gauges. Invariably, I find a reading that is approximately 2% above optimal. It takes 3-4 minutes of adjustment to bring it back down, to silence the harmonic. I could set the system to self-regulate through mechanical adjustment, but instead I do this manually, three or four times per shift. Why? 

Because it gives me something to do that is not simply waiting. Because it creates a rhythm to the day that prevents me from spending too many consecutive hours staring at the porthole window into the dark water. Because the small act of listening and responding to the machine's minute communication makes me feel, temporarily, as though I am not simply a passenger in a process I cannot alter.

The machine is my patient. This is perhaps unprofessional. Machines do not require patience, do not benefit from attentive care in the way living things do. And yet.

Beneath the platform, in the water that is both liquid and vapor, the creatures move. I have observed them—the ribbon-things with their terrible bioluminescence, the spheres that pulse and click, the rare moments when something vast shifts in the depths. They have adapted to this place. They have made it livable, in their way. They have created an ecology from the margins of a system that was never intended to support life.

I wonder sometimes if they listen too. If the ribbon-creatures experience the vibrations of the pumps through the water as I experience them through the metal. If they feel the shift in pressure and chemical composition the way I feel the harmonic in the shaft's rotation. If their movement—the way they gather in certain areas, the way they avoid the most intense turbulence—represents a kind of listening, a kind of response to the machine's voice.

I have not written this down. These are not scientific observations. This is not data that belongs in the logbook.

But I think it. I think it during the long hours at the porthole. I think it when I make the small adjustments to keep the machine in harmony. I think it when I hear the ribbon-creatures' strange clicking—similar to the harmonic of the pump, though not identical, shifted by some frequency my ear cannot quite resolve.

Three months ago, one of the other workers—Marcus, I think, though we rarely exchange names here, we are simply roles: the mechanic, the monitor, the supply coordinator—asked me why I spend so much time listening. "The gauges tell you what you need to know," he said. "Why does it matter what it sounds like?"

I tried to explain. The gauges show you numbers. The sounds show you *change*. The gauges show you the current state. The sounds show you the *trajectory* of the state. If you listen carefully enough, you can hear the failure before the numbers reveal it. You can respond before the system reaches crisis.

He nodded, not understanding, and walked away.

But I think he was asking a different question, one he couldn't articulate. Why do you stay? Why do you listen to a machine that is slowly dying, in a place that is slowly drowning, for a purpose we don't even fully understand?

The answer, if I were forced to give one, is this:

I listen because someone must. Because the machine cannot speak its own condition—it can only vibrate, hum, groan, sing the minor key of its developing pathologies. Because the creatures below are listening, too, in their way, and they understand better than I do that this place is a conversation, not simply a process. The water moving through the system is not just fluid; it is information. The pressure is not just force; it is a message about the state of the system's health.

And I have learned, in these six months of cold and isolation and the never-ending sound, that listening—genuine listening, the kind that requires patience and presence and the willingness to hear things you don't want to hear—is a form of love.

I love this machine, even as it fails. I love it because I listen to it, because I respond to its needs, because I am here when it speaks.

The porthole is fogging again. I can see nothing now but my own reflection and the gray beyond. But I can hear, underneath the main hum, the beginning of that harmonic. 51 Hz and climbing. The pressure is building. Soon I will walk to the console and make the necessary adjustment, and the harmonic will vanish, and for another few hours, the machine will speak in its proper voice.

This is my function. This is my life. This is enough.

The mist moves against the glass. The cables sing with tension. And in the water below, the creatures move in their complicated dance, listening to the same symphony I listen to, making their lives in response to its rhythm.

We are all of us—machine, engineer, creature—in conversation with this place. And the conversation, though it is slowly killing us all, is the only thing that makes us real.
