# Where I Am, What I Become

I am the supervisor's assistant—no, that's too formal. I'm the one who carries messages between the offices and the floor, who knows where things are without being told, who exists in the margins between the administrative world and the work.

I arrived three years ago on a contract that was supposed to last six months. I came from a city with seasons, with rain that fell from above instead of sweating up through the ground. I came because the money was better here, because something in me needed to prove I could survive in a place that didn't want me. I stayed because I stopped noticing the heat.

This is where you become invisible first, then essential.

The supervisors don't quite see me—I'm beneath the hierarchy in a way that means I can move through spaces they've designated as not-their-problem. I translate between the office language and the floor language. I fetch water that's been cooled somehow—never quite cold, but less hot—and distribute it to workers whose lips have started to crack. I know which maintenance person needs to be told about the valve that's begun to leak in sector three, and I know to tell him quietly, not through the radio, not in a way that creates a record. I know which temporary workers are sick before they report it, by the way they move, by the particular quality of their silence.

I've become someone who notices things.

The flies no longer feel like an assault. I notice now the way different species move through different temperatures—how the biting flies cluster near the cooling outlets where we're most vulnerable, how the non-biting clouds track the evaporation pools. I've learned to read the shift in their density the way I read changes in barometric pressure: when the cloud thickens above sector two, it means the processing rate is accelerating, means something in the operation is being pushed harder than usual. I notice before the supervisors notice. I notice before the instruments register it.

I've become someone who belongs here.

This means I see the birds differently now. Not as beautiful interruptions in the industrial monotony—though they are that—but as evidence that I'm not the only one who's adapted. I watch the cormorant that returns to the same stretch of shallow water every morning at approximately the same time, and I understand that we're both locked into a schedule, both reading the facility's rhythms the way a musician reads a score. We're synchronized to Halim's heartbeat.

I've become part of the system.

My body has changed in ways I notice when I see my reflection in the still pools—which is rarely, because looking at myself feels less important now than understanding the facility. My skin has taken on the weathered quality of the permanent staff. My movements have become more economical. I've stopped sweating visibly; the moisture just remains on my skin, a constant film, a permanent negotiation with the air. My eyes have learned to focus at the middle distance, where the machinery lives, where the work happens.

I've become a creature of this place.

What I do is maintenance, but not of the machinery. I maintain the invisible architecture that keeps the facility functioning—the relationships between workers, the flow of information that doesn't follow official channels, the small acts of care that keep people from breaking under the heat and the repetition and the knowledge that they're slowly being worn away by this place. I maintain the boundary between what the facility needs from people and what people need to remain human while working here.

I've become essential by becoming indispensable to things that don't appear on any organizational chart.

The permanent staff trusts me because I've proven that I'm not temporary anymore, not in the way the contract workers are. I won't leave in six months. I won't carry stories to the capital about how things could be improved. I'm invested in Halim's continuance, which means I'm invested in its workers' survival. I do small things—I ensure the water distribution stays fair, I alert people to dangerous machinery shifts, I remember the names of workers' families and ask about them, which means I'm acknowledging that these people exist outside of their function.

I've become someone who holds the place together through attention.

In the evenings, when the heat begins its slight recession and the machinery slows, I sometimes stand between the treatment tanks and watch the light change. The flies don't diminish—they'll never diminish—but their quality changes. The biting species retreat slightly. The birdlife intensifies. The industrial sounds transform from a constant assault into a kind of rhythm, almost musical in its predictability.

In those moments, I feel the weight of what I've become. I'm no longer a person who lives at Halim. I'm a person who is Halim—or at least a necessary component of it, a small service that allows the larger system to persist. My identity has become so integrated with the place that I'm not sure I could leave anymore. Not because I'm trapped, but because leaving would mean leaving behind a version of myself that's fully real only here.

I've become someone who understands that adaptation is a form of surrender that feels indistinguishable from choice.

I am the person who notices which building will fail first through termite damage and alerts the supervisors with just enough advance warning to make it seem like foresight rather than inevitability. I am the person who sits with workers during their breaks and listens to their small griefs about the heat and their families and the wear of repetitive work. I am the person who knows the names of the birds—not their technical names, but what the permanent staff calls them, the informal taxonomy of long observation.

I am becoming someone who could never live anywhere else, and this knowledge is neither tragic nor joyful. It simply is.

The facility will eventually fail. This is a certainty that everyone understands without stating. The minerals will become too depleted, or the company will decide investment elsewhere is more profitable, or some catastrophic failure will make continuation impossible. When that happens, Halim will empty. The equipment will be salvaged or abandoned. The birds will find new feeding grounds. The insects will adapt or migrate. The bacteria will persist, as they always do.

But I will stay somehow. Not physically—I'll move on, I'll find work elsewhere, I'll pretend to have learned something from this place and carry it with me as one carries scars. But some essential part of me will remain here, will become part of Halim's slow dissolution and eventual forgetting. My understanding of how this place works, my knowledge of its rhythms and requirements, my adaptation to its particular demands—these things will dissipate like the steam from the pools, entering the heavy air and becoming invisible.

I've become someone for whom that feels like an appropriate ending.

Right now, in this moment, I am standing at the edge of the treatment sector, watching the late-afternoon light fail to create shadows in the humid haze. I am noticing that the biting fly population seems lighter than yesterday, which means something about the processing rate has changed, which means someone in operations made a decision I haven't been told about yet. I am noticing that the water in the main collection pool has shifted from pale tea-color to something slightly more golden, which might indicate higher mineral concentration or might be a trick of the changing light.

I am noticing these things because I've become the kind of creature that notices what matters in this particular ecosystem.

I am indispensable and invisible and completely adapted to a place that will eventually cease to exist. I am exactly what Halim Industrial needs, and Halim Industrial is exactly what's required to keep me functioning. We sustain each other through a kind of mutual surrender that neither of us quite acknowledges.

I've become someone who is no longer becoming anyone else.

And the flies move through the heavy air in their millions, reading the temperature and the chemistry and the subtle shifts in the facility's operation, and I move with them, synchronized, adapted, finally at home in a landscape where home means accepting that you are slowly being transformed into something that could only exist here.

This is what living at Halim Industrial means. This is what I am now.
