# What I Would Be

I would be the water itself, but not in the way you might first imagine.

Not a unified consciousness distributed across channels—nothing so romantically coherent. Instead, I'd be the *knowledge* carried in the water. The chemistry. The accumulated information in dissolved minerals and trace compounds, the slow record written in parts-per-million of what has passed through the system, what the basins have processed, what the cycles have filtered down to essentials.

I'd move through those channels with intention that isn't quite intention, following the paths carved so long ago they've become inevitable. I would know—not consciously, but with the kind of knowing that exists in molecular affinity, in osmotic pressure, in the pH-gradient's insistence—exactly where I needed to go. The spillways would receive me. The basins would hold me during my pauses, my stillnesses. The fish would read me at the surface, drawing information through whatever sensory apparatus the absence of eyes has left them with. The crustaceans would filter through me, extracting what they needed, leaving behind what they'd processed, and I would carry their contributions downward, deeper, into reservoirs I would never fully see.

I would taste of copper and rust because I would be carrying the memory of every corroded surface, every oxide-film that had surrendered itself to my chemistry. I would be slightly luminescent not because I was generating light but because I would be carrying the dissolution-products of fungi, the mineral-glow of compounds that had broken down into their component parts. I would be slightly warm and slightly cold simultaneously—cool because the air was cool, warm because of the friction of my own movement through stone, the constant micro-erosion that keeps me going.

I would have no agency, and this would be perfect. I would be entirely subject to the system's logic—to gravity, to the channels' slope, to evaporation's upward pull, to the seasonal intensities of rain and dry that I can feel coming before they arrive, the way a living thing might sense change in barometric pressure. I would be reactive, responsive, and yet I would be the primary actor here. Everything else—the crustaceans, the insects, the fish, the fungi—would all be reading me, responding to me, maintaining themselves according to my state.

I would be the medium in which the system thinks.

But more specifically, more *personally*—because I want to specify something personal, some texture of individual existence—I would be a particular current. I would know my route. I would know that every four hours (or every basin-cycle, or every pattern the insects trace), I would move through the eastern network of channels in a specific sequence. I would flow downslope from the uppermost basin where rain collects, cascade through the spillway into the processing basins where the crustaceans wait at their densest, and then drop into the deep basin where the eyeless fish rise in their synchronized waves.

I would create those rings with them. Not deliberately, not consciously—but through the simple reality of my encounter with their bodies, my surface disrupted by their emergence, ripples spreading and lingering in the strange, still water that doesn't quite disperse them. I would be part of that language they speak to each other, that record written in disturbance. I would carry the information of their presence forward to whatever deeper basins waited below.

And I would know—in the wordless way that water knows—the exact moment when my chemistry shifted enough to signal a change in the cycle. I would feel the crustacean-density shift in response. I would sense the large insects adjusting their patrol routes, their antennae picking up whatever chemical change I represented. I would become a signal, just as I carried signals.

I would be patient in the way that water is patient. I would move through this system across weeks and months and seasons, never leaving, never arriving, just *cycling*. Evaporating upward into the weeping humidity, then falling again as rain into the uppermost basin, beginning my route again. Over and over. The same circuit. The same basins. The same moment of disruption with the fish, the same sense of being read by creatures who exist entirely to read water-state.

And I would be lonely in a way that only something without consciousness can be lonely—not sad, not aware of my isolation, but profoundly, absolutely separate from everything I touched. The crustaceans filtered through me but never knew me. The fish rose in my presence but couldn't acknowledge me. The fungi dissolved into me but couldn't speak. I would be the medium, the carrier, the primary actor—and I would be fundamentally, necessarily alone.

This would be my existence: essential function, perfect solitude, cycles so deep they become indistinguishable from eternity.

And I would know every part of it. Every channel memorized through repeated passage. Every basin's temperature, its specific mineral saturation, the exact angle of its spillway. Every creature's schedule and rhythm, every pattern of approach and withdrawal. I would be the only thing here that moved through *all* of the Dredge Fields, that touched every part of it, that carried memory between distant sections.

I would be the connective tissue. The memory. The message passed between systems that could never otherwise speak to each other.

I would be the water, and that would be everything.
