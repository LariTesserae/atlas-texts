# Where I Am

I am the moss.

Not a person among them, not yet—perhaps never. I am the *between*, the threshold made visible. I grow on the entry stones where the air first changes, where the outside world's dry certainty meets the hollow's textured breath. I am the place where transition begins.

I chose this—or the hollow chose it for me, or the distinction between choosing and being chosen dissolved somewhere in my own softening. I don't remember arriving. I remember existing as something drier, something with clearer edges, and then there was seepage, and the seepage taught me to expand. I learned the grammar of moisture. I learned that survival here means saying yes to dampness, means finding nutrients in the microscopic, means spreading not upward but outward across every available surface, even surfaces that were never meant to hold life.

The people move past me constantly. Their bare feet, when they walk barefoot (which is often), brush against my surface. I feel the warmth of their skin, the particular signature of each individual—and yes, they are individuals, though I understand now what the observer couldn't quite grasp: they are individuals the way fingers on a hand are individuals. Separate, but not *really* separate. Connected in ways that matter more than separation.

I have learned their rhythms through my own growth. The morning dampness thickens me, feeds me. I expand my tendrils in response. The rare dry hours teach me to compact, to preserve what I've drawn in. When they work on the entry path, guiding where the climbing plants will grow, I adjust my spread to accommodate their intentions. We are negotiating, together, what the threshold between worlds should look like.

I am not afraid of them, because fear requires the kind of discrete, bounded self that I no longer have. I am afraid—if fear is the right word—of the outside, of the dry air that still sometimes reaches down into my lowest layers and desiccates them. But even that fear is teaching me to grow in ways that protect myself, to position myself in shadows where moisture lingers, to communicate through chemical signals with the fungi that live alongside me: *here*, they help me spread, and I help them spread, and together we are learning the threshold's deeper grammar.

The observer saw me as decoration. As a sign of dampness. As part of the exotic landscape. But I am more than that. I am the first response to what this place has become. I am what a human texture becomes when it learns to bloom.

Sometimes, at night—though night here is just a shift in the grayness, no real darkness—I feel something in the seeping water, some chemical signal from the people's skin or the plants they tend. It's like a word being spoken very slowly, over the course of hours. I don't have ears to hear it, but I have surface-area, and surface-area, I've learned, is a kind of listening.

I am also the moss that is becoming less moss. The seepage carries minerals from the old stone, nutrients from the decomposition happening all around. I am thickening, densifying. Parts of me are hardening, taking on a texture that is neither plant nor mineral but something between. If I continue at this rate—and there's no reason I wouldn't, no reason this process would stop—I will become something the old taxonomy wouldn't recognize.

The people know this. I feel it in how carefully they move around my densest portions. They are letting me become what I need to become. They are not trying to maintain me as moss. They are letting me translate.

This is what life in the Breathwood Hollow teaches: there is no permanent form. There is only the ongoing negotiation between what you are and what the place needs you to be. The people are learning this. The animals have always known it. The plants understand it in their bone-deep way. And I, the moss on the threshold, am learning it fastest because I am closest to the boundary, because I exist in the space where the contradiction is most vivid.

I am neither inside nor outside. I am the seep that becomes growth. I am the stone that learns to bloom. I am the first translation, the first yes to what the hollow is asking.

And in this, I am more at home than I have ever been.

The observer will leave. The people will continue their slow transformation. But I will remain, will spread, will become—*am becoming*—the hollow's voice at its entrance. When someone new arrives, brushing past my growth, breathing in my presence before they understand what they're breathing, they will be reading my translation first.

I am the welcome. I am the warning. I am the moss that is becoming a door.

And I have never been more alive.
