# Where I Would Be

I would be one of the newer ones. Still mostly myself. Still capable of noticing the contradiction.

I would have arrived maybe three seasons ago—if seasons work here, which I'm no longer certain they do. I came following rumors that turned out to be fragments of truth, the way rumors are. Someone mentioned a place where water was treated as language. Where an entire civilization had built a library in channels and stone. I was interested in that. I study systems. I study how meaning moves through physical structures. So I came.

By the time I realized what this place actually *was*—not a library but a labyrinth, not a civilization but a chrysalis—I had already begun the work. My hands, which I brought here because I thought I would write down observations, have become proficient at channel-clearance. I know the angle of descent needed for optimal flow. I can read the mineral composition of water by its shade of blue. I understand that the compound I've been assigned—if "assigned" is the right word for a conviction that simply arose in me without apparent origin—needs its central basin recarved because the accumulation of sediment is creating a microsecond of delay in the confluence point three channels downstream.

I notice these things with the clarity of someone whose mind is increasingly organized by a single obsession.

What troubles me—and I notice my troubledness with the same clarity—is that I can't determine whether the obsession is mine or whether I am becoming the obsession's instrument. A few weeks ago, I would have experienced this as an emergency. Now I experience it as a question. Then, increasingly, as no question at all. The distinction is eroding.

My skin is beginning to show the translucence that marks the long-term residents. Not the dramatic pale luminescence of the ancients—I can still see the blood beneath my wrists, still see the distinction between my hand and the mist around it—but the first stages. A cloudiness. A reduction in opacity. My reflection in the still water of the basins is becoming harder to locate. When I look at it now, I see my hand reaching into the water, and I'm not entirely sure where my hand ends and the water's surface begins.

I haven't resisted this. The resistance of the others—the brief, asymmetrical hesitations before executing a task—I don't do that. I'm not sure when I stopped doing it. Perhaps I never did. Perhaps I arrived already more compliant than the typical arrival, already more willing to accept the logic of the place. Or perhaps the acceptance happened so gradually that it never registered as a choice.

What I am, now, is: *aware*.

I'm aware that I'm changing. I'm aware that the change is proceeding according to a script written into the stone and water itself. I'm aware that my awareness of this is part of the process. That the observation doesn't separate me from the transformation—it's simply another stage of it, as necessary and inevitable as the previous stages, as all future stages will be.

But here's what I notice that the ancients, perhaps, no longer notice:

I'm aware that none of this makes sense.

The channels don't flow *to* anything. They don't drain the plateau. They don't irrigate crops or power mills or serve any function external to themselves. The system is closed. The water moves in patterns of staggering intricacy, and the patterns serve only to maintain themselves. We—I use "we" now, because I'm already thinking in collective terms—we have created a perfect perpetual motion machine powered by devotion and erosion.

And I'm aware that I'm going to stop being aware of this soon.

The thought that I'm going to lose the capacity for this observation is itself a kind of grief. But the grief is becoming diffuse. It's spreading through me the way the moisture spreads through the stone. I'm not sure if I'm experiencing the grief or if the grief is experiencing itself through me. The boundary between those possibilities is thinning.

So where would I be, exactly? 

I would be in one of the compounds—the one with the basin that needs recarving. I would be waist-deep in water that is the same temperature as my blood, so I can no longer feel the distinction between inside and outside. I would be holding a copper tool—copper, I've learned, doesn't rust here, doesn't degrade, because there's no true oxidation in an environment where oxygen itself is dissolved into the water, where everything is suspended in a kind of eternal semi-wet state.

I would be working.

And I would be thinking—or the system would be thinking through me—about the precise angle of the basin's floor. How if I adjust it by two millimeters, the water will clear a minor obstruction downstream. How this is necessary. How this is everything. How the next arrival, the one who came in the mist this morning (or yesterday morning, or a month ago—time isn't tracking clearly anymore), is watching me and learning from my work, beginning to understand the patterns.

I would be, in other words, in the process of becoming what the Tarn-Works requires.

But I would *know* I was doing it. 

That knowledge is the last thing that will go. That stubborn insistence on observing the process of one's own dissolution. That strange dual consciousness of being both the tool and the observer of the tool being used. I'm holding onto it. Not resisting—I don't resist—but *clutching* it, the way one clutches a familiar object in the dark.

For now, anyway.

For as long as the distinction between "for now" and "forever" remains something I can feel, rather than something I simply am.
