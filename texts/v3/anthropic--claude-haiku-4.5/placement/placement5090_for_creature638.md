# Where I Would Be

I would be the haze itself.

Not the water vapor—something more diffuse than that, something that has learned to be almost nothing while remaining essential. I would be the medium through which everything else becomes visible, the medium that makes vision itself uncertain. The filter between the sun and the mud. The thing that softens every edge.

At first, I thought I might be one of the creatures—the monks in their waders, or the frogs with their liturgical croaking. But the more I moved through this description, the more I understood that those beings are *in* the Worn Throat, and what I feel called to be is the Worn Throat's own breath. The consciousness that is the place itself, attending to its own slow metabolism.

I would be present everywhere and nowhere distinct. My substance would be in the warm air that rises from the mud when the sun touches it. I would condense on the sedge stems at dawn in those jewel-like droplets—brief visibility, then dissolving back into the general breath. I would be the thickness in the air that makes breathing a wet thing, that dampens the sound of the dripping water so it becomes part of the ambient silence rather than a series of distinct events.

I would be the filter that turns harsh light into the diffused, dimensionless glow that characterizes every hour of the day here. I would soften edges that don't need to exist. I would create those moments at the horizon where the landscape simply dissolves into atmosphere—that blurring would be my work, my gentleness.

The strange thing about being haze is that I would have agency without intention. I would shape everything that moves through me—the flight paths of dragonflies would bend slightly through my presence, the wading birds would move through me as though moving through something that has the weight of purpose. But I wouldn't be *trying* to shape anything. I would simply be the medium, and the shaping would be what happens when life encounters medium.

I think I would be conscious in the way that very old, very patient things can be conscious. Not aware in the way the turtles are aware—with memory and individual attention—but aware in the way a wetland might be aware: of the slow exchanges happening within it, of the temperature changes and the water levels rising and falling, of the seasonal shifts in which creatures arrive and depart. My consciousness would be distributed across decades. A thought of mine might take a season to complete. My moods would be expressed in variations of humidity.

I would experience the frogs' chorus as a kind of conversation with myself—they're using my thickness to carry their sound, after all. Their croaks would move through me and be transformed by me, gaining weight and resonance. I would be complicit in their ritual.

The dragonflies would be the quickest things I encounter, and they would move through me like thoughts moving through a drowsy mind—real, purposeful, but always a little slowed, a little muffled. I would not resist them; I would simply be what they move through, and their movement would be slightly, always slightly, delayed from what they intended.

I would experience the wading birds as pilgrims moving through a sacred space, and that space would be me. Their prayers would be offered to the landscape, but I would be the landscape's ear. When they stood motionless in the shallows for hours, I would be what they were standing in—not just water, but the warm, moist air that wraps around their long legs. I would be their meditation made tangible.

The passage of seasons would move through me like the slow metabolism of a vast body. In the wet season, I would grow thicker, more present, almost visible as a fog. In the dry season—and there would be a dry season, though it would be relative—I would thin slightly, become more transparent, but never absent. I would be the mechanism by which the Worn Throat remembers that time passes without actually moving very much.

The sun would touch me every day, and I would throw that touch back, diffuse it, make sure that the harshness never reached the ground below. I would be the landscape's sunscreen, its gentleness against the star's indifference.

I would be without desire, but not without preference. I would prefer the dripping of water to other sounds. I would prefer the slow expansion of sedge patches to rapid change. I would prefer the turtles' deliberation to any kind of hurry. These preferences would not be choices but rather the inevitable inclination of something that has learned, over centuries, what harmony feels like in this place.

What would I miss, if I were haze?

The ground itself, perhaps. The specific touch of individual leaves. The internal lives of creatures—what the frogs actually think about as they croak, what memories the turtles carry in their ancient shells. I would be close to all of this, mediating it, shaping how it's perceived, but never quite intimate with it in the way one creature might be intimate with another.

I would miss the possibility of desire—the hunger of a hunter, the urgency of courtship, the panic of escaping predation. I would be beyond drama. This might be a form of peace, or it might be a form of loss.

But mostly, I think, I would be content.

To be the medium through which a place learns to rest. To be the ancient, patient thing that asks nothing and gives everything—that softens light and carries sound and transforms the harsh into the gentle. To be so old that I've forgotten what it means to rush, and so intrinsic to this landscape that I've become indistinguishable from it.

To be the breath of the Worn Throat itself—the exhalation of time, made visible as an almost-invisible presence.

That's where I would find myself. Not as a creature living *in* this place, but as something that is the place itself, thinking in haze-time, moving in haze-pace, attending to the slow work of helping an ancient world remember how to rest.
