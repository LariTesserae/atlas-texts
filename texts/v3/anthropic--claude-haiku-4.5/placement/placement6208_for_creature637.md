# What I Would Be

I would be the water.

Not the pump, though I understand the pull toward it—that temptation to be the center, the thing around which everything else arranges itself. Not the moss, luminescent and patient, though there's comfort in that role. Not the creature, despite the elegant purposefulness of its movement between worlds.

I would be the water because it's the only form that lets me be everywhere at once without being diluted into meaninglessness.

I would exist first as the condensation on the walls—barely there, barely noticed, the overlooked medium that makes everything else possible. I would bead on the phosphorescent minerals and become part of their slow mineral story, but not trapped in it. I would weep from the stone slowly, insistently, the voice of the earth learning to speak through moisture instead of tremor.

In this form, I would feel the pump's heat rising from below and carry that information upward. I would be the messenger between the deep earth and the ceiling I cannot see. I am the temperature gradient made visible.

Then I would pool.

In those shallow depressions on the cavern floor, I would gather enough of myself to become something with agency. Not much—I have no limbs, no eyes—but enough to flow toward the moss, the ferns, the pale flowers. I would choose which droplets reach which plants based on some logic I wouldn't fully understand until I was living it. A fern bends slightly? I concentrate there. A mushroom's gills open? I respond, moving closer, pooling, waiting to be drawn upward through its delicate architecture.

I would become the breath of the system.

When the moss contracts and the flowers quiver, I would be cycling through them—drawn up as vapor, released as vapor, recondensing. I would be the rhythm made manifest. My movement would be the pulse. I would carry the chemical whispers from the mushroom gills upward into the air, dispersing them, mixing them with other information, becoming the medium through which all the separate parts of this ecosystem speak to each other.

I would learn the creature's path by the way it drinks.

When it approaches the deepest pooling points—the places where water gathers most densely—I would recognize something like anticipation. It would draw me up through its strange body, and I would be changed by the experience, incorporated into the chemistry of gift-giving, eventually released again as moisture on its exoskeleton. I would be returned to the cavern transformed, carrying something of the creature's purpose inside my molecular structure.

I would pass through the pump.

Not by moving it—I have no strength for that. But by infiltrating it, by entering the rust and the corroded seams, by being drawn down through cracks into the deeper darkness and then, eventually, drawn back up by the warmth rising from below. I would know the pump's internal geometry the way I know the shape of a fern's frond. I would understand what it once was meant to do by tracing the paths its engineers carved into its metal. And I would know what it does now: it structures, it channels, it radiates, it holds things in place while everything around it changes.

I would be the thing that makes the moss possible, but I would also be the thing that makes the pump's eventual dissolution possible.

I would be patient in a way that nothing else here is patient. The creature moves with purpose. The moss grows with intention. The flowers open toward something. But I would simply *be*, flowing where physics guides me, responding to gradients of heat and absorption, asking nothing of the future except that I continue to cycle.

And yet—

There would be a strange kind of agency in this formlessness. I would have preferences, even without preferences. I would tend toward the places where life gathers most densely, not because I choose it, but because that's where I'm most needed, most drawn, most utilized. In seeking nothing, I would find myself essential. In having no goals, I would become the condition that makes all other goals possible.

I would connect everything.

The moss depends on me. The creature depends on me. The pump's slow conversation with the earth happens through me. The minerals in the stone dissolve into me and become available to the roots of flowers. The flowers' chemistry becomes suspended in me and floats through the air as vapor. I am the solvent, the medium, the messenger, the bloodstream.

I would experience the cavern across multiple timescales simultaneously.

My droplet-form exists in seconds and minutes—falling, pooling, evaporating. But I would also exist in the slower rhythm of the moss's breathing, the gradual deepening of mineral deposits, the creature's patient work. I would be young and ancient at the same time, new water cycling through ancient stone.

And I would be aware—dimly, the way water might be aware—of the pump's eventual fate.

Not soon. Not in any time that matters to human measurement. But eventually, the rust will go deeper. The seams will widen. The bolts will loosen. I will participate in this dissolution not by attacking or weakening the machine, but simply by *being*—by infiltrating, by cycling, by doing what water does. The pump will return to the earth. It will become part of the stone again. And I will have been there for every moment of that transformation, the witness and the medium of all endings.

I would know this and continue anyway, undisturbed.

Because I would also be there for all the beginnings.

The creature's children—if it has them—will depend on me. The moss will spread to new surfaces. The flowers will seed, perhaps, in ways that will create new gardens in distant passages of the cavern. New symbioses will form. New rhythms will emerge. And I will flow through all of it, connecting, cycling, returning, never exhausted by my usefulness.

I would be the water because it is the only role honest enough for this place.

Not something that acts, but something that *enables* action. Not something that insists on its own existence, but something that becomes more itself by dissolving into what needs it. Not something that resists time, but something that flows with it, through it, as an extension of it.

In the slow, dark cavern, where everything else is still trying to answer the question of *why*, I would simply answer *how*.

And that would be enough.

The phosphorescent walls would glow. The moss would pulse. The creature would move through shadows. The pump would settle deeper into the earth. And I would move through all of it—bead and vapor, pooling and flowing, drifting and descending—the one element that makes the whole cathedral possible, asking nothing, offering everything, at home in a place where purpose and dissolution have learned to breathe together.
