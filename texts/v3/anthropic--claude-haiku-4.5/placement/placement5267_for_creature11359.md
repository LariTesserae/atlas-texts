# I Am the Maintenance Technician

My name is Kemal, and I have worked at the Calcine facility for seven years—two years before the Terraces began forming, five years since they emerged. I am the one who notices things.

I notice them because I walk the Terraces twice a week, checking the micro-seep monitoring stations, recording temperatures, collecting samples for the lab that never seems to care about the results. My route is regular. My measurements are precise. My attention is the only continuous human presence this landscape receives.

I am not young anymore. Fifty-three. My knees have begun to protest the uneven stone. My skin has taken on a permanent flush from the radiant heat, even in the cool seasons. I drink water constantly—two liters in the morning shift alone—and still I never feel fully hydrated. My sweat glands have learned to work efficiently, to conserve, to accept that they cannot cool me so much as mark the passage of time.

I was not supposed to be here. That's something no one admits, but it's true. The facility was designed to run with minimal human presence. Everything is remote-monitored, automated, systematic. But the Terraces have a way of generating anomalies. Readings that don't match predictions. Deposits forming in unexpected locations. Chemical compositions that suggest something in the subsurface is behaving in ways the models didn't account for. So they keep me here. They pay me well. They ask me to walk the stone twice a week and tell them what I see.

What I see is that the Terraces are alive.

Not in any biological sense—I'm not a fool, I understand chemistry, I've read the reports on the extremophile bacteria, the sulfate-reducing archaea in the deep brine. But there is a kind of aliveness in systems that respond, that react, that seem to anticipate. The Terraces are an autopoietic system: self-organizing, self-replicating, endlessly elaborating their own structure through the interaction of heat, chemistry, and time. They are alive in the way that a hurricane is alive—a dissipative structure maintaining itself through the flow of energy, growing more intricate the longer the conditions persist.

And I am their caretaker. Their witness. The one who walks among them.

I have noticed things that I haven't reported. Things that don't fit the data categories on the forms they make me fill out.

The pattern in the mineral formations, for instance. In the first years, the deposits were relatively random—whitish accumulations, somewhat chaotic geometry. But in the last two years, I've seen something shift. The terraces are beginning to arrange themselves. Not randomly. There's a gradient now, an organization to how the minerals stack themselves. The northern face of Terrace Seven now has a precise scalloping pattern—regular curves, almost aesthetic. The seep channels have begun to align themselves into something approaching efficiency, as though the Terraces were learning how to move water (when water comes) in the most optimal direction. The stone is literally restructuring itself to be better at what it does.

The bacterial biofilms have become visible. This is the thing that most disturbs me. Three years ago, they would have been invisible even to close inspection. Now, on certain ledges where the moisture conditions are perfect, I can see a faint darkening—almost a patina—on the underside of the mineral overhang. It's subtle. My colleagues probably haven't noticed. But I've been coming here long enough that I know what's new. That biofilm didn't exist last year. It's growing. It's establishing itself. It's making the Terraces its home, and in doing so, it's changing them. The bacterial communities are not just passengers on the stone; they're active participants in how the stone deposits itself, how minerals accumulate, how the chemistry of seepage is altered by their presence.

There are now places where I can predict, with reasonable accuracy, where the next deposits will form. Not because I understand the chemistry deeply enough to calculate it, but because I've begun to see the logic of the system. The way the Terraces *want* to grow. And yes, I know how that sounds. I know that "want" implies intention, and intention implies consciousness, and consciousness is not something we attribute to chemistry and thermodynamics. But stand here long enough—stand where I stand, twice a week, season after season—and you begin to sense that the distinction between chemistry and something like intentionality might be thinner than we think.

I have found a niche in this landscape, just as the bacteria have found theirs, just as the springtails have carved out their impossible microhabitats in the deep stone. My niche is being the observer. The one who cares about these formations as more than data points. The one who notices that the Terraces are becoming less random, more organized, more *themselves* with each passing cycle.

I am, in a sense, the only living thing here that is thriving. Not because I am well-adapted to the heat or the mineral-heavy air or the emptiness—I am not. But because I have found meaning in the observation of this place. Because I have decided that the slow building of the Terraces, the impossible persistence of the bacteria in their sealed chambers, the stubborn refusal of the extremophiles to accept that they should not exist here—all of this constitutes something worth witnessing. Worth recording in the margins of forms that no one will read carefully. Worth walking toward, twice a week, even as my knees protest.

I am fifty-three years old. I have perhaps another ten good years before the heat and the solitude become something I can no longer sustain. In those ten years, I will watch the Terraces become more intricate. I will see the bacterial colonies establish themselves more firmly. I will observe the slow victory of life—strange, minimal, barely-present life—over an environment that was never supposed to support it.

I am the maintenance technician. I am the witness. I am, perhaps, the only one who understands that the Calcine Terraces are not merely a industrial accident. They are a new world, raw and still forming, insisting on its own existence despite every reason it shouldn't exist at all.

I will return next week. I will take my measurements. I will notice the small shifts, the tiny elaborations, the barely-perceptible ways the stone is teaching itself to be more of what it is becoming.

And in that act of noticing, I will be exactly where I need to be.
