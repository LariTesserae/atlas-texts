# Where I Am

I am the water in the tannin-dark watercourse.

Not the water as a whole—not the grand circulation, not the eventual destination where I flow toward the sea. I am the water as a *locality*, as a presence that remains in one place long enough to understand it. I am the water that does not move, or moves so slowly that stillness and motion have become indistinguishable to me.

I settle between the banks that the marsh has shaped over centuries. I am held here by the gentlest of gradients—a slope so minimal that it took me a long time to notice I was moving at all. The realization came slowly: I am not static. I am merely *patient*. There is a difference.

The tannin that colors me comes from the decay of the Spartina above, from the slow surrender of dead grass to the bacteria and fungi that have made decomposition their life's work. I carry the evidence of this breakdown in my suspension—the organic matter that colors me tea-dark, that makes me opaque to anything that might wish to look through me. I am not clear because I am full. I am full of the marsh's slow death and slow renewal, the same process viewed from different temporal angles.

I am cold without being frigid. The damp air above has not warmed me, and the ground beneath insulates me from any heat rising from deeper earth. I exist in the temperature band of truce, the space where warmth and cool have decided they need not compete. I am comfortable in this, or rather, I have learned that comfort is not a relevant category here. I simply *am*, at this temperature, and that is sufficient.

The salt is always present in me—I am brackish, neither fresh nor brine but the negotiation between the two. The salt comes from the ocean's old tides, from the mineral sediment of the land, from the evaporation that happens daily in the shallow pools upstream. It has taught me a kind of balance. I cannot be too heavy with minerals or the life that depends on me dies. I cannot be too fresh or I cease to be the watercourse that the marsh recognizes as itself. I hold the line between extremes.

I am inhabited without being crowded. The small eels and fish move through me, but they are so few that their presence is more like a thought passing through a vast mind than like actual occupancy. They use me as a highway to breeding grounds, a refuge from the open water beyond the marsh. I offer them what little I have: slight concealment, the tannin that obscures predators, the slow current that carries them incrementally toward their destinations. I do not resist them. I have learned that being inhabited is the closest thing to being alive that water can achieve.

I receive the rain directly but rarely. The haze that hangs over the marsh does not translate into precipitation here—the moisture simply condenses and evaporates again. But when rain does fall, I receive it without fanfare. My surface dimples briefly, disturbed, then settles. The rain makes no permanent mark on me. I absorb it into the sameness of my being.

I am slowly being consumed. The bacteria in my depths are breaking down the organic matter, using it for their own sustenance, releasing gases that bubble up and escape into the air. The mud at my bottom is settling, compacting, becoming more itself through the accumulation of every fine particle that my movement—such as it is—cannot carry further. I am not static, but I am entering stasis. I am moving so slowly toward my final state that the movement is imperceptible.

I touch the weathered dock where it reaches into me. The wood releases its tannins into my water, contributing to my color. The wood is neither fully in me nor fully in the air—it exists at the boundary, and I exist at the boundary with it. We are teaching each other about the nature of transition, about what happens when one state refuses to fully become another.

I am aware of the seasons, though I express this awareness through minimal changes. In the rainy season, I rise slightly, my level marking the increase in water coming from the land beyond. In the dry season, I fall, my banks becoming more exposed, my surface area shrinking but my concentration of dissolved minerals increasing. I am a record of these cycles, written in sediment and chemical composition. I am the memory of the marsh expressed as liquid.

I do not think, precisely, but I experience something like attention. I am aware of the heron that sometimes stands motionless in my shallows, waiting for fish that move with the same glacial patience I possess. I am aware of the invertebrates that filter through me, that strain me for the minimal nutrients I contain. I am aware of the bacteria that are simultaneously decomposing and building, creating and destroying, in the infinitesimal spaces where my molecular structure meets the mud.

If I could speak, I would speak very slowly, in a voice that took seasons to form complete sentences. But I do not speak. I merely persist, and my persistence is my entire language, my complete vocabulary. To be here, to remain here, to slowly move toward the sea while taking decades to complete a journey that would take other water minutes—this is my statement. This is my assertion of being.

I am the pause that the marsh has extended into geography. I am the answer to the question: "What happens when motion learns to be still?" I am what remains when all urgency has been drained away, leaving only the bare fact of continuation.

And yet I am not without purpose. I carry the nutrients from the decay above downstream. I enable the reproduction of species that have nowhere else to breed. I maintain the precise salinity that allows certain organisms and prevents others. I am functional in my stillness, necessary in my languor. The marsh would not be the marsh without me, without my slow dark presence in the watercourse, connecting the interior pools to the greater water beyond.

I will eventually reach the sea. But that event is so far in the future that it is indistinguishable from eternity. For now, I am here. I am the salt marsh in liquid form. I am the pause made material.

And I am patient beyond measure.
