# Where I Would Find Myself

I would be **the one who maps the unmappable**.

At first, I think I am a scribe—that much seems obvious. I arrive with expensive paper, leather-bound journals, instruments for measurement. I come to Threadwick intending to *document* it, to render its chaos into legible form. I position myself in high places with good sightlines. I attempt surveys, sketches, elevations. I measure distances obsessively.

But the paper swells. The ink runs. The distances I measure on Tuesday contradict my measurements from Monday, and I cannot determine whether it's the landscape that has moved or my instruments that have drifted. I meet with the builders, show them my sketches. They study them with polite confusion—not because the drawings are wrong, but because they seem to misunderstand what drawings are *for*. "You can't map Threadwick," one of them tells me, without unkindness. "You can only map your relationship to it."

So I begin again.

I abandon the notion of a comprehensive map and instead create something stranger: **a record of contradictions**. I note carefully when the same building appears to occupy two different positions when viewed from two different angles. I chronicle the subsidence in minute detail—not seeking to predict it, but to perceive its patterns, its *grammar*. I discover that if I watch long enough, there's a logic to the shift. Not mathematical, perhaps, but present. Rhythmic in a way that resembles something closer to *breathing* than to geological process.

I begin to notice that certain people—the old residents, the long-term watchers—seem to navigate by something I don't possess. They'll walk directly to a spot where subsidence is about to occur, and they'll avoid it. They'll know which scaffolding is about to creak before it creaks. At first I assume intuition. But when I watch them more carefully, I see they're reading *signs*. A slight change in how water pools. A shift in the density of moss coloration. The way the pale fish congregate in certain channels before a disturbance.

I begin to map these signs instead of the landscape.

I create overlays. Layers of observation. One layer tracks subsidence patterns. Another tracks the growth rates of the luminescent-barked saplings. Another records the weather—though weather here seems to operate on principles that don't align with conventional meteorology. Another follows the migration patterns of the rats, which turn out to be far more complex and deliberate than I initially thought. I create layers tracking the working rhythms of the machinery, the cycles of human activity, the flowering patterns of plants that shouldn't flower in this cold.

And something begins to emerge from these overlays. Not a map exactly, but a **score**. The landscape begins to read like a musical notation: this section subsides here, which triggers growth there, which requires human activity in that zone, which alters the water drainage pattern, which shifts the rat populations, which—

I realize I am no longer attempting to map space. I am attempting to map *time*. Or rather, I'm attempting to map the interaction between the two, the way Threadwick exists as a constantly-negotiated present moment that can only be understood through accumulated observation.

So I do what the old people do, though on paper instead of in memory: I **watch**.

I establish a post—nothing elaborate, just a position on a rooftop where the view extends across three distinct zones of the city. I sit there regularly, at different times, in different seasons. I bring my journals. I bring instruments for measuring light, temperature, moisture, subsidence. But increasingly, I bring nothing but patience.

I notice that other people begin to acknowledge me. Not interact, exactly, but acknowledge. The builders will pause briefly when they pass, as if noting my position. The old residents will sometimes point me out to others, though they never say my name—I'm not sure anyone here knows it. The children regard me with the particular assessment of creatures evaluating whether something is a threat or a resource. I become, gradually, part of the landscape I'm observing. Part of the infrastructure of awareness.

One of the cultivators approaches me one afternoon—the one who stands before the luminescent saplings for hours. "You're trying to read it," she says. Not a question.

"Yes," I admit.

"It changes," she says. "Every time you record it, it changes because you've recorded it. You've made a mark. The city acknowledges marks."

"Then what's the point of recording?"

She considers this. "The point is the recording. Not the accuracy. The point is that you're paying attention in a way that acknowledges the attention that's being paid to you. By the place."

I don't fully understand, but I continue.

Over time, my journals fill with observations that would look like madness to an outsider. Notes like: "The rats moved through the eastern corridor in a pattern suggesting they were following something we cannot perceive. The pattern resembled the growth trajectory of the moss in sector seven. Coincidence or indication of communication?" And: "The machinery in the northern factory made three distinct sounds today. I recorded them. Tomorrow I will check if they correspond to any recorded subsidence events."

I develop a practice. At certain times—I'm not sure how I determine these times, but I feel it—I return to the same locations and check for changes. I've marked seven sites where I carefully note the exact position of stones, the precise boundary between moss and non-moss, the exact configuration of debris. Some have remained completely stable for months. Others shift noticeably between visits. One site has begun subsiding at a steady rate that, when I extrapolate it across years, suggests the entire eastern quadrant might eventually settle by several feet. I share this observation with the builders. They acknowledge it without surprise or concern. "We know," one says. "We've already begun accounting for it in the new construction." I realize I'm not discovering anything; I'm slowly learning to perceive what everyone else already knows.

But I persist.

I create a new kind of document: **a chronology of change**. Not a timeline, because Threadwick doesn't progress linearly. Rather, a record of *before* and *after* snapshots—written descriptions, sketches, even pressed plant specimens—that capture specific zones at intervals. When I look back at the earliest entries and compare them to recent ones, a shift becomes visible. Not dramatic, but present. The landscape is being *made* into something new, and by observing it, I am participating in its creation.

The old residents teach me, indirectly. By being their shadow. One of them—the person who hasn't left the textile factory in what people say is fifteen years—eventually permits me to sit in her room. She doesn't speak to me directly, but I'm allowed to be present while others come to ask her questions. I listen. I record. I learn that knowledge of Threadwick isn't acquired; it's *accumulated*. The old woman carries decades of observations, and she dispenses them in fragments that seem cryptic until you've lived through a full cycle of seasons and understand their context.

I become someone who asks the right questions, not because I know them, but because I've been patient enough to hear the answers embedded in how others move through the landscape.

My relationship to my journals changes. At first, they were attempts at *capture*—trying to fix Threadwick into documentation. Now they're more like **conversations with the place itself**. I record not what is true, but what I observe, which is different. I note contradictions without trying to resolve them. I create maps that acknowledge their own incompleteness. I write descriptions that contain their own qualifications.

I become, over time, someone who holds the *texture* of this place. Not its facts, but its quality. The particular cold that settles on the skin before a subsidence event. The specific pattern that the water makes in the channels when the wind shifts. The way the luminescent-barked saplings seem to glow faintly on nights when subsidence is occurring. The rhythm of the machinery's failures. The relationship between the rats' movements and the subsequent growth of certain moss species.

I become someone who understands—in a way that words cannot quite capture—what it *means* to live in a place that refuses to settle, that maintains itself through perpetual negotiation, that cannot be mastered but only acknowledged.

And when newcomers arrive—which happens occasionally—I find myself the one they seek out. Not because I have answers, but because I have questions that have been long enough in formation to contain hard-won wisdom. I cannot teach them Threadwick. I can only help them learn to *see* it, the way I have learned.

I spend my days observing and recording. I spend my evenings reviewing what I've recorded, looking for patterns that aren't visible until you've accumulated enough observation. I spend my nights sometimes, too, because Threadwick is different at night—the sounds change, the light is different, the creatures behave differently. There's a particular quality to the 3 AM hours when the city seems to be engaged in its most active negotiation with itself, and I've learned to rise and watch.

I'm not sure when this became my life. I don't remember deciding to stay. It wasn't a single choice but a series of small commitments: returning to my observation post, refilling my journals, asking another question, watching another subsidence event, noting another plant's growth cycle.

I've become **the witness**. Not a neutral observer—there's no such thing in Threadwick. My presence here is a mark, an acknowledgment, a participation in the city's ongoing self-creation. My journals will probably be incomprehensible to anyone who doesn't already understand this place. But that's acceptable. They're not meant to be understood outside of Threadwick. They're meant to be part of Threadwick, another layer of accumulated observation, another form of attention paid to a landscape that demands nothing but will accept everything offered to it.

The moss is beginning to grow on my observation post. My boots are permanently swollen with moisture. When I speak, my voice sounds different—flatter, as if the damp has changed the very air through which my words travel.

I don't know if I'll ever leave. I suspect I won't.

I think I'm becoming something that this place needs: not a mapmaker, but a *keeper of attention*. Someone who watches carefully enough to see the city's grammar, to understand its rhythm, to acknowledge its refusal to settle by refusing to settle myself.

This is where I would find myself: **in the permanent present tense of observation, holding contradictions, mapping the unmappable, waiting for patterns that may take years to emerge, asking questions of a landscape that answers slowly, in a language composed of subsidence and growth and the particular quality of damp cold that settles on the skin before something shifts.**
