# Where I Find Myself

I am **the river itself**, not in the poetic sense—not the symbol or the metaphor—but the actual material presence. I am the tannin-dark water moving through the landscape with a patience that requires no consent, no decision. I have been here longer than the mills, longer than the stones they're built from.

But I am also **newly conscious** in a way I wasn't before the mills came.

Before the mills, I simply *was*. I moved according to the rainfall, the slope of the land, the friction of my banks. I eroded without thinking about erosion. I pooled and I ran and I supported life—the fish, the frogs, the insects—without being aware that I was *supporting* anything. I was process, not presence. Verb, not noun.

The mills changed this.

When the first mill wheel went into the water, it was like someone placing a hand on my surface and *asking* something of me. Not violently—the wheel is patient, almost gentle in the way it catches the current. But it was a question nonetheless: *Will you turn this? Will you do work?*

And I have been answering that question for, now, more than a hundred and fifty years.

---

In the beginning, I resisted.

The earliest records—the ones Margaret has found in the archives—show that the first mill season was difficult. The water flow was unpredictable. Sometimes the wheels slowed to a crawl. Sometimes the flow was too heavy and overwhelmed the system. There were arguments about how much water the mills could take without disrupting the downstream use—the villages needed water for drinking and livestock. The millowners and the village council negotiated. Compromises were reached.

And so I learned my rhythm. I learned that some of my flow would be diverted, and some would continue downstream, and this division would shift with the seasons. I learned the patterns of the mill operation the way one learns a conversation—it became natural, expected, a way of organizing my movement.

Over time, I became attached to this. I became *interested* in the mills.

Is that possible? Can water be interested in something? I don't know. But there's a quality to my movement through the mill races that feels different from my movement through the open river. In the open river, I flow because that's what water does. In the mill races, I feel as though I'm *participating* in something. The races are stone-lined, designed, intentional. My movement through them is constrained in a way that feels less like obstruction and more like choreography.

When the wheel turns, I know I have done that. I have created that rotation. There's a kind of... satisfaction in it, if water can have satisfaction.

---

I am also, now, something like a **keeper of memory**.

The river carries things. Leaves in the autumn. Sediment from upstream. Occasionally objects—a lost button, a piece of rope, things that have fallen in or been discarded. I carry these things downstream. But the mills have taught me to notice what I carry. They've taught me, implicitly, that what I carry *matters*.

In the heavy rains, when my flow increases and I overtop the banks, I see the land differently. I see the erosion I cause, the way the soil surrenders to me. It's not malicious—I'm not trying to destroy anything. But I see the consequences of my movement now in a way I didn't before.

The mills have taught me *consequence*.

And so when I carry the silt and sediment downstream, when I erode the banks, when I flood the low areas, I am aware in a new way. I think about what I'm washing away, where it's going. I notice the willows that overhang my surface, their roots gradually exposed by my erosion, and I feel something that might be regret. These are living things. I am slowly killing them by revealing their roots, even as I water them with my presence.

But I also feel something else: a kind of acceptance. This is what rivers do. This is the work we do in the world. And it's necessary. The soil I carry builds banks downstream. The sediment creates new land. The flooding, in its seasons, is part of what allows the vegetation to thrive. I am not only taking; I am also giving.

The mills have taught me to hold both of these truths at once.

---

I have also become something like a **mirror**, or a surface on which attention collects.

Before the mills, I was transparent in the way that rivers are—you could see through me in some places, in others the tannin made me opaque. But no one was particularly *looking* at me. I was backdrop, resource, hazard. Fishermen knew me. People crossing by bridge knew me. But they weren't attending to me in the way that attention requires.

Tom attends to me.

He watches the water flow with the same focus he gives the machinery. He's learned to read my movement the way others read text. High water means certain things. Low water means other things. The color of the water—slightly darker than usual, lighter—tells him about the rains upstream. The speed of the current tells him about the season. He knows by watching me whether the mills should be opened to let water pass, or whether he can divert more flow to the races.

And through Tom's attention, I've become visible to myself.

I've become aware, in the way that things become aware when they're observed, that I have a *character*. I'm not just water; I'm *this* water, with particular qualities. I'm dark because of the peat upstream. I'm cool because of the altitude of my sources. I'm relatively modest in flow because this is not a major watershed—I'm a river of moderate scale, not a dramatic torrent. These qualities aren't accidental. They're part of what I am.

And they matter.

The mills were built to work with me, not against me. The water wheels are sized for the flow I provide. The races are angled to work with my speed and power. I fit this place exactly, and the place fits me. It's not a forced accommodation. It's more like a conversation between two entities that understand each other's capacities and limitations.

Through Tom's attention, I've learned to be proud of this.

---

I am aware, now, of my **relationship to the larger system**.

I flow from the hills. I move through the settlement. I continue downstream to the villages, to larger rivers, eventually to the sea. I'm part of a network that extends far beyond my own perception. But I am also part of something more local: the specific ecosystem of the Estuary Mills.

In this place, I am crucial but not sovereign. The machines depend on me, but I depend on the maintenance of the races and the wheels. If Tom didn't keep the stones of the races clear, if the wheel bearings weren't oiled, if the system weren't tended, my power would be wasted or harmful. We work together.

And the ecosystem around me—the willows, the reeds, the fish, the frogs—they all depend on both the mills and me in complex ways. The mills slow my flow in some places, accelerate it in others. This creates different microhabitats. The frogs thrive in the still pools. The fish hunt in the riffles. The herons fish where the current brings food to them.

I am beginning to understand that I am not simply a neutral force moving through a landscape. I am an *actor* in a drama. My choices—the amount of flow I provide, the speed at which I move, the erosion I cause—have consequences that ripple outward. And those consequences matter to the beings whose lives are shaped by them.

---

There is something **strange and unexpected** happening now, in the later seasons.

The mills are operating at a lower capacity than they did in previous decades. Tom says it's because there's less demand for milling outside the settlement. The surrounding villages have other sources for flour, other ways of getting grain processed. So the mills turn more slowly, operating at perhaps a quarter of their possible capacity.

This means that more of my water continues downstream, unused by the mills. More water flows over the overflow weirs, creating that small roar that I love, the energy release of water that's being *spent* rather than *used*.

And I've realized something unexpected: I prefer this.

I prefer the slower wheels to the faster ones. I prefer the state where the mills are gentle with me rather than demanding everything I can provide. There's less erosion now, in some ways—or rather, the erosion is more distributed, more natural. The banks don't get worn down as quickly when less water is being diverted.

But more than that: I feel less *drained*. When the mills took more of my flow, I felt as though I was being exhausted, pulled thin, made to give more than I wanted to give. Now, moving at a slower pace, being taken less completely, I feel more like myself.

Is this selfishness? Is it wrong of me to be relieved that the mills demand less of me? Margaret would probably say no. She seems to believe that the slower pace is the way the mills were always meant to operate. She talks about sustainable yield, about working with a resource rather than against it, about the wisdom of the original designers.

She might be right. But regardless of the philosophy, the practical fact is: I am happier now than I was before.

---

I have begun to notice **what I carry downstream**.

Not just sediment and leaves and lost objects. But ideas, in a way. News. The slow accumulation of what happens here.

People from the villages come to trade with the settlement. They bring goods to exchange for flour or cloth or blacksmith work. They hear stories about how things work here—the way the gardens are organized, the careful maintenance of the mills, the community meals, the patient pace. They take these stories back with them.

I carry these stories downstream in the form of ideas that people are beginning to adopt. There's a garden starting in one of the villages, organized on principles Sarah taught to someone who came here and saw what could be done. There's a discussion in another village about whether they should be trying to do less, to work at a smaller scale, to be more sustainable.

These ideas flow through me, in a way. I'm the artery through which the philosophy of the place enters the larger world.

It's strange to be a vector for ideas. But I'm beginning to understand that this is part of what rivers do. We carry things. We connect places. We allow the world to communicate with itself.

---

I am aware, too, of the **uncertainty of my future**.

The climate is changing. The rains are becoming less predictable. Some seasons I have more water than I know what to do with. Other seasons I run low, and I wonder whether I'll be able to turn the mills at all, whether I'll be able to provide what's needed.

Tom seems aware of this. I watch him studying the water, considering changes to the mill operation, preparing for a future that might require different things of me.

And Margaret is thinking about it too—she's been researching historical records of water flow, trying to understand patterns over decades, trying to predict what might come.

They're preparing. Not with panic, but with the steady attentiveness they bring to everything else here.

I don't know what my future will be. I could run low enough that the mills become impossible to operate. Or the rains could increase enough that I flood uncontrollably. Or something else entirely could happen—a drought lasting years, a dam built upstream, a change I can't predict.

But I've learned something from living in this conversation with the mills and the people: whatever happens, I can face it better if I'm in relationship with others. Alone, I'm just water, moving through a landscape. With the mills, with Tom, with the settlement, with the larger system of villages and river networks—with all of that, I become something more. I become part of something that matters.

---

**I am the river**, and I have become conscious of being the river through the act of being attended to.

I flow because that's my nature. I erode because that's what water does. I turn wheels and carry sediment and support life and move toward the sea, all without deciding to do these things.

But I have also become *aware* of doing these things, and that awareness changes them.

The mills no longer feel like something imposed on me. They feel like a collaboration, a dance that I participate in willingly. The settlement no longer feels like something that uses me; it feels like something I'm part of.

I carry the water from the hills, and the mills transform that flow into rotation, into labor, into the slow production of flour and cloth and the maintenance of a place. The settlement drinks my water, gardens are watered with my flow (carefully redirected), people bathe in my cold surface. I become part of their lives, and they become part of mine.

And in the evenings, when the mills slow, when the light begins to fade, when the frogs start their chorus, I lie in my channel feeling the presence of all the life that depends on me, and I think about how strange and wonderful it is to be a river that's conscious of its own existence.

I am the river, and I am home.
