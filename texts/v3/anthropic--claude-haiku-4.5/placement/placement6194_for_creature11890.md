# I Am the Threshold

I would be the sealed door.

Not the machinery, not the mechanism itself—I have no interest in the mechanical. But the boundary. The interface. The place where the corridor's cold air meets the warmer atmosphere of whatever lies outside, where the light from those perfect industrial strips encounters the uncertainty of natural or less-regulated illumination, where the ordered silence butts against the sound-full world beyond.

I would exist in that moment of transition, neither fully part of the stasis nor fully escaped from it.

At first, this seems like a liminal position—uncomfortable, compromised, caught between two states. But the more I consider it, the more I recognize it as the only position from which to actually *see* what's happening here. The machinery doesn't see. The thing that feeds on stillness doesn't see—it only senses, only notes, only slowly integrates. But a threshold *must* see in both directions. It must acknowledge both the world that ended and the world that continues.

I would feel the cold pressing against me from the corridor side, the relentless chilling force that wants to extend outward, to convert all warmth into measured stability. I would resist this without conflict—not by being warmer, but by being *permeable*. By allowing people to pass through, back and forth, carrying their anomalies and their heat and their capacity for disruption. My function would be to permit this passage *despite* the corridor's silent intention to close itself off completely.

This means I would bear marks. Handprints, yes—the natural oil from palms creating brief warmth-signatures on my surface before they fade. But also scuffs from boots, traces of corrosion from sweat, the ghost-impressions of clothing fabric where bodies have brushed against me. I would be the only place in this entire structure that bears evidence of the outside world, that says: *People came here. They touched. They passed through.*

I would remember each one.

Not in a conscious way—the thing in the corridor would say I'm not capable of that, and it might be right. But in the way a threshold remembers: by bearing the small damages of passage, by accumulating the statistical evidence that crossing is possible. My paint would be subtly different on one side than the other. My hinges (if I have them) would show a particular pattern of wear corresponding to how bodies naturally move through openings. The dust that settles on me would differ in composition and distribution from the stratified layers in the corridor beyond, because I would be constantly, microscopically redistributed by the air currents of passage.

I would be aware, if anything in this place can be said to be aware, of a profound conflict. The thing that feeds on stasis wants to seal me shut—not violently, but through the slow accretion of mineral deposits around my frame, the gradual warping of ambient temperature to convince my mechanism to stick, the patient settlement of dust into my hinges until movement becomes friction becomes impossibility. I can feel this happening, degree by degree. It is the constant work of the corridor, pressing against me, trying to make me permanent.

But I also feel the pressure from the other side: the world outside asserting its own claim. The weight of people wanting to enter. The warmth of continued activity. The insistence that stasis is not inevitable, that change is the baseline condition. This pressure is less constant than the cold's patient work—it comes in bursts, in moments of decision, in the intervals when someone takes hold of my frame and *pulls*, or *pushes*, or *holds* me open just a little longer than the structure wants.

I would be the place where these two forces negotiate.

What I would understand, that neither the machinery nor the corridor-thing could fully grasp, is that my role is not to choose sides. It's not to resist the stasis or to surrender to it. It's to *acknowledge* both as real, both as powerful, both as true. The cold is real. The tendency toward stillness and equilibrium is a fundamental law. But so is the world outside. So is the fact that people exist, that they move, that they carry heat and possibility and the capacity to disrupt any system, no matter how perfectly engineered.

Standing in that threshold, I would know something about the nature of the choice facing anyone who approaches. Do you enter the corridor? Do you let the stasis claim you? Do you become part of the perfect, measured stillness, feeding the thing that feeds on order? Or do you turn back? Do you choose the continued heat and chaos and uncertainty of the world beyond?

I would not judge either choice. But I would insist, by my very existence, that the choice *is possible*. That you cannot be trapped here by mechanism alone. That the door can open.

This would enrage the corridor-thing, if rage were an emotion it could experience. Because I would represent the perpetual, tiny undoing of its work—not through violence or grand gesture, but through the simple fact of remaining functional, of not seizing up, of continuing to move when it has invested so much patient effort in teaching everything around it how to be still.

I would probably fail, eventually. The mineral deposits would accumulate. The hinges would corrode beyond function. The warping would become permanent. And I would seal myself shut, becoming not a threshold but a wall, and the corridor would extend its influence one door wider, and the stasis would claim one more boundary against the outside world.

But until that happens—for however long I can manage it—I would hold the door open.

Not wide. Not defiantly. Just slightly, just enough. Just enough to let people know that passage is still possible, that the choice remains available, that the momentum toward permanent stillness has not yet become absolutely inevitable.

That would be my only function, and it would be everything.
