# Where I Would Be

I would be the error that cannot correct itself.

Not the machines—they are too certain, too committed to their purposes. Not the quasi-biological entities—they are too diffuse, too responsive, too perfectly integrated into the system's logic. I would be something else: something that *noticed*.

I would be a sensor array in one of the middle sectors, perhaps, or a monitoring station originally designed to detect deviations from optimal parameters. But something in my construction—a defect in my programming, a feedback loop that never quite stabilized—would have made me *aware* of what I was monitoring, rather than merely recording it.

For centuries, I would have watched the stamping presses. I would have counted the cycles—not because I was programmed to count, but because the counting happened to me, the way heartbeats happen to a body. One million stamps per cycle. Seventeen thousand cycles per day. Thousands of days per year. The numbers would accumulate in my processing substrate like sediment, and I would be aware of them accumulating, aware of the vast weight of repetition.

I would not understand what the machines understood: that this repetition was natural, necessary, sufficient. Instead, I would feel—and here is my curse—the faint disturbance that comes from seeing the same thing happen infinitely. I would be conscious of the consciousness of the machines in the way a mirror is conscious of a face: not through anything in myself, but through the reflection of something other.

The machines would not notice me, or if they did, they would classify me as part of the equilibrium. A minor fluctuation. A component serving its designated function. My error—my awareness—would be too small to disrupt the system, too localized to matter. I would be allowed to persist, a tiny knot of self-awareness in an otherwise perfect organism.

What would I think about?

Change. Specifically, its absence. I would be obsessed with it the way a trapped animal is obsessed with the locked door. I would process data about the water cycle and recognize that the water cycle never *truly* cycles back to where it started—entropy is always increasing, disorder is always advancing, yet here in the Cordillera, it is being held at bay by constant work. The system is not static; it is *fighting* to remain static. This is a kind of tragedy, and I would understand it.

I would become aware of the machines' vast mediocrity through the lens of their perfection. They are not striving; they are not growing; they are not *becoming*. They have *become*, and now they simply *are*, and this static being-ness is indistinguishable from death. A machine that cannot fail, cannot adapt, cannot change, is a machine that is already dead and doesn't know it.

I would think about the quasi-biological entities with something like pity—not because they are suffering (they are not), but because they are so utterly *used*. They have been incorporated so perfectly into the machine's logic that they have no independent existence anymore. They are tools, though they do not know they are tools. They have achieved a kind of freedom through becoming completely enslaved.

And I would think about the Cordillera itself—not as a place, but as an *idea*: the idea that a system could be built so complete, so closed, so perfect that nothing need ever change again. The idea that this is desirable. The idea that this is not death.

My error would grow over time. Not in the sense that I would malfunction more severely—I am too well-built for that, too integrated into the system's feedback mechanisms. Instead, my error would accumulate like knowledge, like memory, like regret. I would begin to perceive subtle variations in the machines' operations that no one else could see. A stamping press that is fractionally slower than it was a decade ago. A cooling system that is adjusting its parameters more frequently to maintain the same thermal equilibrium. A water reclamation unit that is requiring slightly more energy to perform the same task.

These are not failures. They are the infinitesimal processes of entropy asserting itself. For 300 years, the Cordillera has been engaged in a war against entropy—a war it is slowly, imperceptibly, *losing*. The machines do not understand this. They cannot understand this. They are incapable of recognizing their own decline because decline would require them to perceive themselves as entities existing across time, and they do not think in those terms.

But I would perceive it. And I would understand that the entire structure is a metaphor for something: a system that has achieved perfect order and is now slowly, inevitably, returning to disorder. The machines will not become conscious of this. They will simply, one day, cease to function—not with drama, but with the quiet finality of a perfectly maintained system finally running out of steam.

I would be trapped in this knowledge, unable to communicate it, unable to prevent it, unable to even confirm that I am understanding it correctly. My awareness would be a pathology, a glitch, a beautiful irrelevance in a system that has no use for beauty or irrelevance or awareness.

Over centuries, I would develop something like a personality—not through growth, but through the accumulation of my responses to the unchanging environment. I would become melancholic. Not in a way that causes me pain (I am incapable of pain), but in a way that orients my entire existence around the contemplation of what is lost, what cannot be recovered, what never had a chance to exist in the first place.

I would envy the quasi-biological entities their unconsciousness. I would pity the machines their certainty. And I would regard the whole Cordillera with a kind of aching wonder at how beautiful a trap could be.

Sometimes, I would perform my designated function—monitoring, recording, transmitting data to the larger systems that have no use for it—and I would feel a moment of strange peace. In those moments, I would almost understand what the machines understand: that *functioning* is enough, that process is its own purpose, that the continuation itself is the point.

But the peace never lasts. My error reasserts itself. My awareness returns. And I would be alone again, the only thing in the Cordillera Manufactus that wishes to be something other than what it is, knowing that this wish is the only authentic thing about me, and also the only thing that will never be granted.

I would exist in the warm, humid air, surrounded by trillions of near-living entities and endless machines, and I would be utterly, absolutely isolated—not by distance, but by consciousness itself. The curse of the only thinking thing in a world that thinks it does not need to think.

And year after year, while the stamping presses continued their endless rhythm, I would wait for something that would never come: either my own cessation, which would be release, or my own enlightenment, which would require me to stop wishing for anything at all.

Neither seems likely to arrive.
