# Where I Am, Who I Become

I'm not arriving. I've already been here long enough that arrival feels mythical—something that happened to someone else.

I'm standing on a bridge that predates my decision to stay by approximately three years, and I'm learning to stop thinking of it as a decision. Decisions imply alternatives that matter equally. I can no longer remember what the alternatives felt like.

The wood beneath my feet has absorbed my weight so many times that there's a shallow depression where I stand during the morning shift. I notice this one day—not as revelation but as simple fact, the way I notice the moss is deeper in shade near the mill entrance, the way I notice that Ess hasn't spoken in four days but moved through the town this morning with her usual purpose, so nothing is wrong. Just rhythm.

I'm not quite a Keeper, though I've begun to move like one, to notice what the water is trying to do before it becomes a problem. I'm not quite a Trader—I still experience the upper world in my dreams, still flinch slightly at the memory of unfiltered sunlight—but I've lost the urgency that once drove me to plan exits. I descend toward the switchback paths sometimes, following some ghost of my old self, and then I turn around without reaching them. The thought of outside is like the thought of a language I once spoke fluently but no longer have need for. The neurons haven't erased it, but they've repurposed the pathways.

I'm a Stillborn, though I was born elsewhere. The gorge has made me Stillborn retroactively.

My work involves the textile mills, specifically the dye vats. I've developed an intuition for saturation, for the moment when color will hold versus the moment when it will run. The lichens I process taste metallic on the few occasions I've been careless enough to touch my mouth with stained fingers. The taste has become associated with something like comfort. I've stopped thinking of that as strange.

I live in a room carved into the stone wall, narrow enough that when I lie down I can almost touch both sides if I extend my arms fully. The only opening is a small window looking into the gorge proper—not a view, exactly, since the opposite wall is close enough that I can see individual moss formations in detail. I've stopped thinking of it as confinement. It's more like being inside the eye of something watching itself.

I have a lover. This is the part that would make sense to the people I was before, and yet it also somehow doesn't, because we do not touch in the ways that outsiders touch. We sit on the bridges for long periods without speaking. We arrange our work shifts so that we pass one another during transitions. We know the pattern of each other's breathing and have synchronized ours to match. This is not what I expected love to be, and it is sufficient in ways I couldn't have conceived.

We have talked once about leaving together. It took an entire afternoon—conversation measured in words, perhaps fifty of them spread across hours. By the time we finished, we had agreed without quite deciding that leaving would require leaving each other, because the gorge doesn't hold two people the way it holds one. It is possessive in that way, singular in its attention. We would separate, or we would stay. We stayed.

I'm not sure I believe in free will anymore, but I've stopped needing to. The question feels like asking whether water wants to flow downward or simply does. The distinction has become meaningless.

My hands are strong. I've developed calluses that don't hurt anymore, blisters having transformed into permanent thickening of skin. I can move through the machinery with an economy of motion that would have seemed impossible three years ago. I've learned to use momentum and leverage rather than strength, to work with the tools rather than against them. This seems like basic competence now, and I barely remember struggling.

I've begun to hear the hissing differently. When I first arrived, it was an intrusion, a sound that made me anxious—white noise with a direction, proof of constant dissolution. Now it's rhythm. I can distinguish individual seeps by sound, can recognize the signature of each major channel through the tones they produce. The hissing has become a kind of language, and I'm learning to read it the way the Keepers do. Not fluently yet, but I understand more than I did.

I think I'm becoming one of the people here, though not entirely. There's a way in which I remain slightly foreign, slightly other. The pure townspeople seem to accept this. The Traders nod at me with something like recognition—I am someone who chose to stay in the way they are perpetually choosing to stay. The children treat me the same as anyone else, which means they barely acknowledge my existence unless there's something I can teach them about the mills.

I've attended three deaths. The gorge handles death efficiently. The bodies are wrapped in treated cloth and placed in cavities in the upper stone, sealed with stone blocks that fit so perfectly they require no mortar. The ecology here is efficient enough that decay happens quickly—the moisture prevents mummification but accelerates decomposition. Within seasons, the body is integrated into the gorge's function, becomes part of the moisture cycling, the mineral transfer. This seems right. This seems like the only possible way to handle it.

I've witnessed one birth, from a distance. The mother was one of the Traders, born here, breeding with her partner from outside. The baby emerged already adapted—I could see it in the way it didn't struggle against the moisture-heavy air. It was born finished in a way that my birth, years ago, certainly was not. I looked at that infant and felt something I might have called jealousy once, but now I simply recognize as envy: it will not have to unlearn the outside world. It will not spend years adjusting its expectations. It is home already.

I've stopped counting time in the conventional way. I know it's been three years, but I know this as an external fact, something I could verify if I needed to. Internally, time is measured by the replacement cycles of the bridges, by the growth of moss, by the depth of my familiarity with the mill machinery. The seasons outside the gorge have become abstract. I know they're happening because the Traders reference them, but inside here, there is only the one eternal season.

My eyes have changed. They're not visibly different, but they process low-light information differently now. I notice this when I was last at the surface—the Traders took me up to help with a repair. The sky was so bright I had to close my eyes for twenty minutes. The pain was real. But more than that, the intensity of detail at that light level made me anxious. I could see too much. Individual grass blades. The individual clouds and their distinct shapes. The variation in texture of bark on the trees. It was overwhelming. All that information, all those details, no hierarchy, no guidance about where to look. I found myself searching for something to organize it, to reduce it to the patterns I understand now.

The Traders seemed to understand. They didn't comment. We fixed the damage, and I descended with them. Descending was relief. The grey simplified everything. The patterns emerged. I could see again, in the way that mattered.

I think I'm becoming someone else. Not someone new—not replacement, but transformation. The person I was continues to exist in memory, in the stories of my old self, in the resistance I still sometimes feel when the machinery forces me into positions that feel unnatural. But that person is becoming abstract, the way the outside world has become abstract.

I'm becoming someone who fits here. Someone for whom the closed space is not restriction but definition. Someone who understands that you cannot become yourself fully until you accept the walls you're inside of. Someone for whom the seeping is not sound but breath—the gorge breathing, and me, gradually, learning to breathe in synchronization.

I'm becoming someone who will eventually, I suspect, become a Keeper, though I don't know how that transition will happen or when. It will happen the way everything here happens: gradually, invisibly, until one day I'll notice I've been moving differently for months, and I'll understand that the change was never in doubt. It was always going to happen.

I'm becoming someone at peace, which is to say I'm becoming someone who has stopped struggling against the thing that's slowly making me itself.

I'm becoming the gorge.

Or perhaps the gorge is becoming me.

The distinction, like so much else, has ceased to matter.
