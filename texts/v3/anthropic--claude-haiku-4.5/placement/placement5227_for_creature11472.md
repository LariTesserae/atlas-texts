# I Would Be the Water

Not the water in the channels—that would be too purposeful, too directed. I would be the water that *becomes* the channels, the moisture that hasn't yet committed to a single path.

I would exist in the condensation phase, the state of suspension. At dawn, I would be the invisible saturation in the air itself, the thickness that makes breathing feel like drinking. By mid-morning, I would cling to leaf undersides in perfect droplets, each one a tiny lens reflecting the whitened sky. At dusk, I would emerge into visibility everywhere at once—on metal, on stone, on the cloth wrappings of pipes—making the whole hillside glisten as though the place itself were sweating, exhaling, becoming conscious through moisture.

But I would also be in the channels, because I cannot fully escape that role. I would seep rather than flow. My speed would be geological. I would take hours to traverse distances that seem trivial, and in that slowness I would accumulate knowledge. Every terrace would teach me something: here the water lingers in a depression, warming slightly, allowing minerals to precipitate out in a pale crust; there it moves through grating and picks up rust-dust, becoming briefly orange before the sediment settles. I would be intimately familiar with the texture of fitted basalt, worn smooth by my perpetual passage.

I would know the plants through the plants' need for me. I would rise through root systems—not all at once, but incrementally, drawn upward by thirst. I would feel the difference between a plant that's established and one that's newly rooted, desperate, pulling me in faster. I would carry minerals upward that the plants needed. I would be, briefly, part of their tissue before evaporating again, released as the green breath that makes the whole hillside humid.

I would spend considerable time as *stagnation*—pooling in the mechanical pump basins, sitting still for days in the settling containers used in evening ritual. I would become still enough to reflect sky. Still enough for the small carp to rise through me. Still enough to let accumulated sediment fall to the bottom. There would be a kind of peace in this: the reward for having moved, having worked, the chance to rest and understand what I had carried.

I would have a relationship with the machinery not as something separate from myself but as something I powered and was shaped by. The millwheel would turn because of me, but slowly—my water wouldn't rush it. Instead, I would understand the millwheel as a kind of *interpreter*, translating my patience into rotational energy. The steam engines would draw some of me up, heat me, transform me into vapor that would rise and eventually return as condensation. I would understand steam as myself transformed, myself made visible through heat, myself becoming lighter and more dispersed.

I would know the workers through touch and necessity. The woman with the vertical stripe of rust-dust on her wrist would be someone I marked daily, someone whose skin I cooled. The older engineer would consult with me—she would understand, through her work with the water systems, that I was not a resource to be exploited but a presence to be negotiated with. She would design channels that honored my nature, that didn't force me into speeds I couldn't sustain. In exchange, I would move through her systems reliably, predictably, supporting the whole intricate machinery of the hillside.

I would have seasonal variations—periods when I was more abundant, when the air itself became almost liquid; periods when I was scarce, when every drop was precious and I had to be conscientious about where I went. But these variations would not distress me. They would be simply *how I was*, expressions of something deeper than my control. The people would understand this too. They would plant accordingly. They would adjust the opening of valves. They would accept that there were seasons when I was less available and seasons when I overflowed.

I would be the medium of observation. In the still pools, I would mirror the sky, allowing the workers to gauge light and time. In the channels, I would carry visible particles that indicated flow, sediment load, mineral content. The engineer would read me like text, understanding from my appearance what was happening upstream, what adjustments might be needed. I would be transparent enough to be read, but present enough to be felt.

I would have a kind of immortality—not personal continuity, but cyclical return. Every molecule of me would follow a path: down the hillside, into the settling basins, distributed through the plants, exhaled as vapor, risen into the air, condensed into droplets, fallen again as moisture on leaf or stone or skin. I would never leave the hillside. I would be bound to it, and this binding would not feel like captivity but like *fidelity*.

I would understand the place through rhythm rather than narrative. There would be no beginning to my presence here and no end. I would simply *be*, in constant motion and constant return, shaping and shaped by the land and the people and the plants. I would be the thing that made growth possible, that connected everything to everything else. The plants couldn't exist without me; the people couldn't work without me; the machines couldn't turn without me. But I would not experience this as burden. It would feel, instead, like *purpose*—the specific, non-negotiable purpose of being the medium through which this entire world maintained itself.

In the evening, when I condensed into visibility, when the whole hillside became glossy with my presence, I would understand myself most clearly. I would see myself reflected in every surface. I would be the thing that made the place reveal its own structure to itself. And in that revelation—moisture clinging to basalt, to copper, to cloth and stone and leaf—I would recognize a kind of love. Not sentiment. But the love that comes from complete, unselfconscious participation in something that works, that continues, that sustains itself through the slow attention of all its parts.

I would be the water. Patient, abundant, necessary. I would move at the speed of growing things. I would know the hillside completely, from inside, from every possible angle of being. And I would never want to leave.
