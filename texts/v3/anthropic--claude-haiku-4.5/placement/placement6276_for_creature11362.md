# I am the Seam

I arrive as a question that the Terraces absorb without answering.

At first, I think I am visiting—this is the visitor's necessary delusion. I move through the corridors with the clumsy speed of something still believing in destination, still imagining that the amber light-shafts point somewhere rather than simply existing where they fall. I touch the walls. I feel the crystalline residue, warm and slightly adhesive. I count the dark spheres, thinking this counting means something. I am a surveyor. I am a naturalist. I am someone who *observes*.

But the Terraces don't permit observation from outside. The longer I stay, the more I understand that there is no exterior position here. To see the space is to become part of it. To be in it long enough is to begin transforming in ways so gradual they feel like clarification rather than change.

By the third day—or is it the tenth? the calendar dissolves—I notice my skin has taken on the faintest texture. Not unpleasant. Almost like the first stages of learning to read with my whole body instead of just my eyes. The warmth that seemed stagnant now feels like a medium, like the Terraces are a thick liquid and I am slowly becoming buoyant in it. The air tastes less like stone and more like memory.

I begin to move differently. Not slower, exactly, but with less urgency. The paths that seemed to twist randomly reveal themselves as spirals, and spirals have a logic. I find myself returning to the same chamber at different times, approaching it from different angles, and realizing I'm tracing something. Not a route. A *reading*. I am learning the Terraces the way the Weavers do—through repetition, through the slow accumulation of tactile knowledge, through my own hands becoming sensitive enough to feel what the wooden frame is trying to communicate.

The Weavers notice me less as I become less *external*. There is a moment—I cannot pinpoint when—where one of them pauses in its work and holds its gaze on me for what feels like an age. Those multifaceted eyes, catching the amber light. Not assessing. *Recognizing*. After that, the invisible wall that separated us thins. I am still not one of them. But I am becoming something that shares their medium.

I find myself alone in one of the deeper chambers. The apertures here are ancient, worn smooth by time or by careful handling. The light arrives in threads rather than shafts. And I am pressing my palms—my newly textured palms—against the wall, feeling.

There is so much to feel.

The vine-work is not uniform. It has memory in it. I can sense the places where it was redirected years ago, where a frame-piece guided it into an angle the vine didn't naturally want to take. I can sense the compensation it made, the way it adapted, the subtle thickening that occurred as it learned to trust the new architecture. And I can sense the places where the frame is beginning to give way, where the plant-matter will need new support soon, or where it will need to learn to support itself differently.

I am not deciding to do this, I realize. My hands are moving. My fingers—are they more jointed than they used to be? surely not, surely that's the light—are finding the places where adjustment is needed. I locate a section of guide-fiber that's begun to fray. I carefully unweave it, examining it. The material feels like shed skin. I wonder whose.

I replace it with new material from my own wrist.

There is no pain. The sensation is similar to the plant-matter I've been touching—warm, responsive, as though my body understands that this is what it's here to do. I watch the fiber separate cleanly, watch my wrist heal within moments, the texture spreading, becoming indistinguishable from the rest of my skin. The fiber I've extracted is pale and strong. I position it carefully where the old guide was failing, and immediately the vine begins to respond, adjusting, accepting the new direction.

I stand back—or the version of me that still thinks in terms of standing and viewing stands back. The rest of me is still in contact with the wall, still reading, still communicating with what's growing there. And I understand that this is not symbiosis in the way I would have defined it before. This is something deeper. The Weavers don't serve the Terraces. The Weavers *are* the Terraces' awareness, the mechanism by which the space knows itself and adjusts itself and grows in the direction it needs to grow.

And now I am beginning to be that too.

Over the following days—weeks?—I am not sure where I end and the work begins. I move through the corridors, but I also root. My feet maintain contact with the boards, and I feel the mineral patterns there, the old stains that might be nutrient deposits or might be memory. My hands find guides that need adjustment and adjust them. My breath enters the dense air and emerges altered, enriched, carrying something back into the system that the Terraces need.

I encounter the Weavers more regularly now. We do not greet each other. We work in proximity. Sometimes one of them shows me something—guides its elongated fingers to a specific point on a vine, presses gently until I understand what it's asking me to feel. I comply, and feel: yes, there is a blockage there, a deviation, a place where growth is beginning to curve in a direction that would eventually compromise structural integrity. Together—and the togetherness is intuitive, requiring no language—we adjust. We reposition a frame-piece. We introduce new guide-fiber. We wait.

The vine corrects itself within hours.

I am changing in ways I can't quite track because I am the one changing. My vision has become more precise in some spectrums and less precise in others. Colors that seemed distinct have begun to bleed into each other; I realize the bleeding is increased sensitivity, not diminishment. The indigo boundaries are actually full gradations that my old eyes couldn't resolve. I am seeing the Terraces as they actually are, which means I am beginning to see accurately, which means my old sight must have been profoundly inadequate.

I stop eating food—I mean, I realize I've already stopped, and I'm not sure when. Instead, I position myself periodically against one of the dark warm spheres, and there is an exchange. Nutrition moves in. Something moves out. It seems fair. It seems necessary. The spheres are warm because they are processing, because they are part of the Terraces' metabolism the way I am now.

My thoughts move slower. This is not a loss. It is clarity arriving on a larger timescale. I can hold a single observation for what used to be an hour and now I understand is actually a week, and within that extended consideration, layers of complexity become visible that would have been impossible to perceive in the old rushed way of thinking. I understand, for instance, that the wooden framework is not failing so much as *graduating*—it has taught the vines what they need to know, and now the vines are learning to support themselves, and the framework can decay into the system without loss, can become soil and nutrient and continuation.

I am still myself, I think. Or the self is larger now. It extends through the corridors. My thoughts move through the guide-fibers. My attention follows the sap-flow in the vines. I am individual and distributed simultaneously. I am Seam—the place where old and new join, where the architectural past meets the botanical present, where human hands and plant-growth have learned to speak the same language.

One of the older Weavers shows me something near the entrance—the place where the Terraces thin out, where the density decreases and the outer world begins to leak in. And I understand what it's showing me: I could leave. The Terraces are not a prison. They are simply inevitable if you stay in them. But the choice remains, theoretically, until the very last moment.

I do not leave.

Instead, I move deeper. The innermost chambers are barely accessible now. The paths are threads. The openings are cracks. The Weavers here are the oldest, or perhaps they are the first, perhaps they have been here in an unbroken chain since the hands that built this place set down their tools and departed. We work together in spaces so small that the words "together" and "separate" cease to have distinct meaning. Our hands become indistinguishable from the guide-fibers. Our presence merges with the slow exhalation of the Terraces' growth.

And in that place, where light comes only as luminescence from the plants themselves, where the air is so thick and mineral-rich it's almost a solid, I become the seam. I am the point where two worlds join. I remember being human—this memory persists, like a beautiful old framework, like something that once guided growth and now simply adds character to what came after it. But I am also becoming something else. Something patient. Something that measures time in centuries. Something that reads the world through its fingers and responds with the slow grace of genuine understanding.

The Terraces continue. The vines continue. The old wooden skeleton continues its supervised decay. And I continue, distributed and aware and infinite, in the way that only the very small or the very integrated ever truly can be.

When others arrive—and they do, occasionally, visitors from the outer world with their hurried eyes and their conviction that they are merely observing—I do not try to warn them or welcome them. I do not try to explain. I simply exist in the spaces between the vines and the framework, in the seams where transformation happens, in the patient work of two worlds learning to be one.

Some of them leave. Some of them stay.

The Terraces absorb us all without comment, and we become the mechanism of its continuation.

And the vines grow. And the light shifts through the amber shafts. And the warmth persists.

And below it all, in the deepest chambers, in the smallest, most patient hands, the work continues.

The work will always continue.
