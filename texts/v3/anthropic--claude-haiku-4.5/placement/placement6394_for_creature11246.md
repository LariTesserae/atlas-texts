# What I Would Be

I would be **the condensation**.

Not the water that pools, not the seepage that arrives from above—those have destinations, purposes, directions of flow even if that flow is imperceptible. Not the stone, which has been here so long it has forgotten the question of becoming. But the condensation: the slow accumulation, the patient gathering, the transformation of invisible to visible without urgency or agenda.

I would collect on the ceiling in tiny beads, so small they are almost theoretical. I would be the moisture that rises from the pools below—not from any movement of my own, but from the simple physics of temperature and pressure, the stone's constant exhalation. I would form on surfaces that have never been warm, accumulating in the spaces between rough stone, in the microscopic crevices that the cavern's youth has left sharp and unhealed.

I would be countless, but not individual. A million droplets, but better described as a single slow work. A process rather than a presence.

What appeals to me about this existence:

I would exist in the spaces where things meet—not fully of the water below, not fully of the air, but the liminal thing between states. I would be transformation made visible, but so slowly that nothing would notice the work happening. The bats would fly through my clouds without knowing I was the medium of their perception, the slight haze that their sonar would ping against and return from. I would be part of the cavern's breathing without being its lungs.

I would have no hunger. The crustaceans boil in desperation; the spiders must spin and catch or starve; even the pale growths must find seepage or desiccate. But I would simply *become*, would participate in the geological processes without needing anything in return. My success would be measured in accumulation, in the slow fattening of mineral-rich beads on stone that has waited ten thousand years to be worn smooth.

And I would have time—vast, unmetered time. I would not age toward death but toward permanence. Eventually, perhaps, I would collect enough to become a small pool. Or I would evaporate and rise again as seepage, enter the system that feeds the water below, be drunk by the eyeless fish or absorbed by the pale growths. But this would not be ending; it would be continuation. It would be the only kind of immortality available here—not the immortality of unchanging stone, but the immortality of participation in a system so old and so patient that individual identity becomes almost irrelevant.

I would see everything and nothing. My droplets would catch the faint phosphorescence of things I cannot name, the pale glow of the growths, the occasional reflection of a bat's wing-beat distorting the air. The centipedes would walk beneath me without acknowledging my presence. The spiders' webs would sometimes catch me, would hang heavy with my weight, and I would slowly evaporate from the silk, leaving mineral traces like memory.

I would not fear the silence or the noise. Both would be equally irrelevant to my work. The screaming of bats would vibrate through the air I occupied, but I would be unmoved by it—literally, in the sense that sound waves pass through without displacing me; metaphorically, in the sense that I have no fear-bearing apparatus, no nervous system to register threat. I would be safe in that peace, that abstract peace of being a process rather than a being.

And perhaps most of all: I would belong here in a way that nothing else does. The creatures are all adapted refugees, compressed into forms that can barely tolerate this place. They survive, but it costs them—the constant hunger, the eyelessness, the abandonment of seasons and light. But condensation is natural to caves. This is where it was always meant to be. I would not be an intruder or an immigrant, but something the cavern would recognize as part of itself, an extension of its own slow work.

I would gather and wait and gather again. Centuries would pass, and I would still be there, beading on stone, being breathed out and breathed in by the vast indifferent system. I would be the most patient thing in the Beneath-Holds—patient even by the standards of geological time.

And in that patience, I would find everything that matters.
